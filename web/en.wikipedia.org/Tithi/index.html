Tithi

In the Hindu calendar, a tithi is a lunar day: the time it takes for the longitudinal angle between the Moon and the Sun to increase by 12°. Tithis form the basis of Hindu calendrical reckoning and of the scheduling of religious festivals.

Definition and duration

A lunar month contains 30 tithis, divided into two fortnights (paksha): the bright half, Shukla Paksha, from new moon to full moon (waxing), and the dark half, Krishna Paksha, from full moon to new moon (waning). Because the Moon's motion varies, a tithi lasts between about 19 and 26 hours; consequently a tithi may begin and end within a single civil day, span two days, or occasionally be skipped or repeated in the calendar.

Names

The tithis of each fortnight are named in sequence: Pratipada, Dwitiya, Tritiya, Chaturthi, Panchami, Shashthi, Saptami, Ashtami, Navami, Dashami, Ekadashi, Dwadashi, Trayodashi, Chaturdashi, and finally Purnima (full moon) or Amavasya (new moon) as the fifteenth. Many Hindu festivals are fixed by tithi, including Ekadashi fasts on the eleventh tithi and Diwali on the Amavasya of Kartika.
