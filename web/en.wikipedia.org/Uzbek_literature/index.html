Uzbek literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Classical Uzbek literature was written in the Chagatai Turkic language, reaching its peak with Alisher Navoi in the 15th century.

Uzbek literature refers to the body of written works produced in the Uzbek language and the traditions of the Uzbek people of Central Asia, together with the earlier Turkic literary heritage of the region written in Karluk and Chagatai Turkic. It encompasses a rich oral epic tradition, classical court poetry of the Timurid era, and the prose, poetry and drama of the modern period.

Oral tradition

Before and alongside written literature, the Uzbeks preserved heroic epics (doston) performed by bakhshi storytellers to the accompaniment of the dombra. The Alpamysh cycle — the tale of the hero Alpamysh and his beloved Barchin — is the most celebrated, shared with neighbouring Turkic peoples and running to thousands of lines in some versions. Other cycles include Gorogly (Koroghlu) and the romantic epics of Yusuf and Zulaykha.

Early written literature

Turkic writing in Central Asia goes back to the Orkhon inscriptions of the 8th century. In the Islamic period, Yusuf Khass Hajib's Kutadgu Bilig ("Wisdom of Royal Glory", 1070), a Karakhanid mirror for princes, and Mahmud al-Kashgari's Diwan Lughat al-Turk, a dictionary and anthology of Turkic dialects, became foundational texts. Ahmad Yasawi's Sufi verse in the 12th century made Turkic a vehicle of mystical poetry.

Chagatai golden age

Under the Timurids in the 15th–16th centuries, the literary language known as Chagatai (Old Uzbek) reached its classical height with Alisher Navoi (1441–1501), minister at Herat and the greatest poet of the tradition. Navoi's five epic poems (the Khamsa), his lyric divans in Turkic and Persian, and his Muhakamat al-Lughatayn ("Judgment Between the Two Languages", 1499), defending the expressive power of Turkic against Persian, made him the national poet of the Uzbeks. His contemporary Zahiriddin Muhammad Babur (1483–1530), founder of the Mughal Empire, wrote the Baburnama, a candid autobiography that remains a masterpiece of Chagatai prose, as well as poetry in both languages.

Later Chagatai literature flourished in the khanates of Bukhara, Khiva and Kokand, with poets such as Mashrab, Turdi, and the Khivan court historian-poets Munis and Agahi; in the 19th century, women poets including Nadira and Uvaysi wrote notable divans in Kokand.

Jadid and Soviet periods

In the early 20th century the Jadid reformers — Mahmud Khoja Behbudiy, Munawwar Qari, Abdulla Avloni, Abdulhamid Cholpon, Abdurauf Fitrat and Abdulla Qodiriy — modernized Uzbek letters, founding newspapers, theatre and the Uzbek novel; Qodiriy's O'tgan kunlar ("Bygone Days", 1925) is regarded as the first Uzbek novel. Most of the Jadid generation perished in the Stalinist purges of 1937–38. Soviet-era Uzbek literature, constrained by socialist realism, nevertheless produced major figures such as the poet G'afur G'ulom, novelist Oybek (Navoi, 1945), Askad Mukhtar, and the poets Erkin Vohidov and Abdulla Oripov, author of the national anthem's words.

After independence

Since 1991 Uzbek literature has grappled with the recovery of suppressed history — the Jadids, the purges, and religious themes — alongside the transition of the language's official script from Cyrillic to Latin. Poetry remains the most prestigious genre, with writers such as Muhammad Yusuf achieving mass popularity.
