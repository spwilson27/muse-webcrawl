The Langstroth hive is a vertically modular beehive whose defining feature is a set of wooden frames, held within stacked boxes, in which honey bees build comb. It was designed by the American apiarist Lorenzo Lorraine Langstroth, who patented it in 1852, and it has become the standard equipment of modern beekeeping around the world.

The bee space

Langstroth's crucial discovery was the "bee space": a gap of about 3/8 inch (roughly 6–9 mm) that bees neither fill with comb nor seal with propolis. By designing a hive in which every frame is surrounded by exactly this space, he made each comb individually removable without cutting or destroying neighbouring comb — allowing the beekeeper to inspect the colony, manage disease, split colonies and harvest honey by removing frames and extracting it centrifugally, returning the empty combs to be refilled.

Construction

A Langstroth hive consists of a floor (bottom board), one or more deep boxes ("brood boxes") in which the queen lays and the colony raises brood, and shallower boxes ("supers") added above for surplus honey storage — often separated from the brood nest by a queen excluder. An inner cover and a telescoping outer cover cap the stack. Frames usually hold a sheet of beeswax or plastic foundation embossed with a hexagonal pattern that guides comb building.

Impact and variants

The movable-frame hive displaced skeps and fixed-comb hives and underpins commercial honey production and migratory pollination. Related designs include the British National hive, the Dadant hive and various regional standards, most retaining Langstroth's frame-and-bee-space principle.
