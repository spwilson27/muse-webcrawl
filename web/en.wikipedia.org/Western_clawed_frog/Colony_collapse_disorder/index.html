Colony collapse disorder (CCD) is an abnormal phenomenon that occurs when the majority of worker bees in a honey bee colony disappear, leaving behind a queen, plenty of food and a few nurse bees to care for the remaining immature bees. The phenomenon was first recognised in the winter of 2006–2007, when American beekeepers reported losses of 30–90% of their hives with this distinctive pattern, and it drew global attention to the declining health of managed honey bees and other pollinators.

Signs

In a collapsed colony, the queen and capped brood are usually present and food stores are untouched, but adult workers are almost entirely absent — and, mysteriously, their bodies are not found in or near the hive. Robbing by other bees and invasion by pests such as wax moths are delayed, suggesting that collapsed colonies' stores may be avoided or contaminated. Surviving colonies nearby are often unaffected.

History

Similar episodes were recorded as early as 1869 and under names such as "May disease" and "disappearing disease" through the 20th century. The modern outbreak was reported by Florida and Pennsylvania beekeepers in late 2006; annual surveys subsequently recorded winter losses around 30% or more in the United States for several years, far above the 15% beekeepers considered normal. Reports of CCD-like losses came from Europe and elsewhere, though some high-profile cases were later attributed to other causes.

Causes

No single cause has been established, and scientific consensus holds that CCD results from a combination of stressors. The leading factors are infestation by the parasitic mite Varroa destructor and the viruses it spreads (especially deformed wing virus); exposure to pesticides, notably neonicotinoid insecticides, which can impair bees' navigation and immunity; pathogens such as the gut fungus Nosema ceranae and Israeli acute paralysis virus; malnutrition from monoculture forage; and stresses of migratory beekeeping. The relative weight of these factors remains debated.

Consequences and response

Because roughly one in three bites of food in a typical Western diet depends on pollination, CCD raised fears for agriculture and food prices. It spurred major research programmes, government action plans for pollinator health, and restrictions on neonicotinoids, including an EU ban on outdoor uses of the three main compounds in 2018. While classic CCD symptoms became less frequently reported after the early 2010s, beekeepers continue to report unsustainably high annual colony losses from the interacting causes behind it.
