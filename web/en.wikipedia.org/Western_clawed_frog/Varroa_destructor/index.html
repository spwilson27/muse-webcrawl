Varroa destructor, the Varroa mite, is an external parasitic mite that attacks and feeds on honey bees and is one of the most damaging pests of the western honey bee (Apis mellifera) worldwide. The mite can reproduce only in a honey bee colony: an adult female enters a brood cell just before it is capped, lays eggs on the developing bee pupa, and the mite offspring feed on the pupa's fat body tissue. Heavy infestation weakens and shortens the lives of bees, spreads viruses, and, if untreated, usually kills the colony within one to three years.

Biology

Adult females are reddish-brown, flattened and oval, about 1.1 by 1.6 mm — visible to the naked eye. A foundress mite hides in a cell containing a five-day-old larva; after the cell is capped she lays a male egg followed by several female eggs at roughly 30-hour intervals. The offspring mate inside the cell, and the mature, mated females emerge with the young adult bee to begin the cycle again. Mites prefer drone (male) brood, where the longer development time lets more daughters mature. Between brood cycles, mites ride on adult bees, feeding on fat body tissue — long misidentified as feeding on haemolymph (blood), until research published in 2019 showed the fat body is the primary food.

Spread

The mite originally parasitised the Asian honey bee Apis cerana, with which it coexists without usually killing colonies. It jumped hosts to A. mellifera in the first half of the 20th century as European bees were brought into Asia, and then spread with the movement of bees and queens: through Russia and Europe, to South America in the 1970s, the United States in 1987, New Zealand in 2000, and Australia — long the last major mite-free beekeeping region — in 2022.

Damage and viruses

Feeding mites weaken individual bees, reduce their weight, lifespan and immune competence, and inject viruses directly into the body. Varroa is the principal driver of deformed wing virus (DWV) epidemics: bees emerge with shrivelled wings, unable to fly. The mite–virus complex is implicated as a leading contributor to elevated colony losses and to colony collapse disorder.

Control

Management relies on monitoring (for example by alcohol-wash or sugar-roll counts) and integrated pest management: synthetic miticides such as amitraz and fluvalinate (against which resistance has evolved), "soft" treatments including oxalic and formic acids and thymol, drone-brood removal, brood breaks, screened bottom boards, and breeding bees for hygienic and mite-resistant traits such as Varroa Sensitive Hygiene (VSH).
