Neonicotinoids are a class of neuroactive insecticides chemically similar to nicotine. Developed from the 1980s onwards, they became the most widely used insecticides in the world because they are systemic — absorbed and distributed throughout a treated plant — effective against sucking pests, and comparatively low in acute toxicity to mammals. Common members include imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam, acetamiprid, thiacloprid and dinotefuran.

Mode of action

Neonicotinoids bind to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors in the insect central nervous system, causing overstimulation, paralysis and death. Their selectivity for insects over vertebrates arises from differences in receptor subtypes, although they are highly toxic to many invertebrates, including aquatic insects and bees.

Use

The dominant use is as seed treatments on crops such as maize, oilseed rape (canola), soybeans and cereals, protecting seedlings throughout early growth. They are also applied to soil and foliage, used on turf and ornamentals, and in veterinary products against fleas on pets.

Environmental controversy

Because the compounds are water-soluble and persistent, they are found widely in soils, waterways, and the nectar and pollen of treated and neighbouring plants. Laboratory and field studies have linked sublethal exposure in bees to impaired navigation, learning, reproduction and colony performance, and implicated them, together with parasites and habitat loss, in pollinator decline. The European Union restricted three neonicotinoids on flowering crops in 2013 and banned nearly all outdoor uses in 2018; some other jurisdictions have imposed partial restrictions, while regulators elsewhere continue to permit broad use. Research also links neonicotinoid contamination to declines of aquatic invertebrates and insectivorous birds.
