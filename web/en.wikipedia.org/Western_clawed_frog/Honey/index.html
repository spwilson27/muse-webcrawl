Honey is a sweet and viscous substance made by several species of bees, the best-known of which are honey bees. Bees produce honey from the nectar of flowers (or from honeydew secreted by aphids), refining it by regurgitation and evaporation inside the hive, where it is stored in wax honeycomb as the colony's long-term food supply. Honey gets its sweetness from the monosaccharides fructose and glucose, and has about the same relative sweetness as sucrose (table sugar).

Production

Foraging bees collect nectar in their crop ("honey stomach"), where enzymes begin breaking down its sugars. Back at the hive, house bees repeatedly regurgitate and fan the nectar to evaporate water until its moisture falls below about 18%; the finished honey is then sealed in cells with a wax cap. To make one pound of honey, a colony's bees may visit millions of flowers and fly tens of thousands of miles. Beekeepers harvest the surplus by removing frames, cutting the caps and spinning the comb in a centrifugal extractor.

Properties and storage

Honey is hygroscopic, acidic (average pH about 3.9) and so low in available water that bacteria and fungi cannot grow in it; sealed pots of honey found in ancient Egyptian tombs remained edible after some 3,000 years. Most honeys eventually crystallise as glucose separates out; gentle warming restores the liquid state. Because honey may contain spores of Clostridium botulinum, it should not be fed to infants under one year old.

Varieties

Honey's flavour and colour depend on its floral source. Monofloral honeys — clover, acacia, orange blossom, buckwheat, manuka (from New Zealand's mānuka tree, prized for its antibacterial activity) — command premium prices, while most commercial honey is a blend. Honeydew honey, gathered from aphid secretions on conifers, is dark and less sweet.

History and uses

Humans have collected honey since the Stone Age; it was the principal sweetener of the ancient world, used in cooking, in medicine (as a wound dressing, a use supported by modern evidence for some applications), in religious offerings, and fermented into mead, perhaps the oldest alcoholic drink. Today honey is used as a food and sweetener, in cosmetics, and as a traditional cough remedy.
