Pollinator decline

Wild bees, including bumblebees, have declined in range and abundance across Europe and North America.

Affected groupsBees, butterflies, moths, hoverflies, and other pollinating insects; some birds and bats
Main driversHabitat loss, pesticides (incl. neonicotinoids), pathogens and parasites, climate change, invasive species
At-risk servicesPollination of ~75% of leading food crops; reproduction of ~90% of wild flowering plants
Key reportsIPBES Pollinators Assessment (2016)

Pollinator decline is the observed reduction in abundance and diversity of insect and other animal pollinators in many ecosystems worldwide during the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Pollinators — chiefly bees, but also butterflies, moths, flies, beetles, wasps, and some birds and bats — are essential to the reproduction of most flowering plants and to global food production; about three quarters of leading crop types benefit from animal pollination.

Evidence

Long-term monitoring, particularly in Europe and North America, documents range contractions in bumblebees, declines in butterflies and moths, and widespread losses of insect biomass. The 2016 assessment of the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) concluded that many wild pollinator species face elevated extinction risk regionally, while data for most species and regions remain poor. Managed honey bee colony losses have risen in many countries, though total managed colony numbers globally have increased.

Causes

Declines are driven by interacting stressors: loss and fragmentation of flower-rich habitat and nesting sites due to agricultural intensification and urbanisation; exposure to pesticides, especially systemic neonicotinoid insecticides; diseases and parasites, notably the Varroa mite and associated viruses in honey bees and pathogen spillover to wild bees; climate change shifting ranges and phenology; and invasive species and commercial trade in pollinators.

Responses

Responses include national pollinator strategies, agri-environment schemes restoring flower-rich habitats, integrated pest management, restrictions on the most harmful insecticides (such as the EU's 2018 outdoor ban on three neonicotinoids), improved beekeeping and pathogen control, and citizen-science monitoring schemes.
