Deformed wing virus (DWV) is an RNA virus of the family Iflaviridae that infects honey bees and other insects. It is one of the most widely distributed and economically important viruses of the western honey bee. The virus was first isolated from symptomatic bees in Japan in the early 1980s.

Transmission and symptoms

DWV persists at low, covert levels in many colonies, transmitted vertically from queen to offspring and horizontally between bees. The arrival of the ectoparasitic mite Varroa destructor transformed its impact: the mite feeds on developing pupae and on adult bees' fat body tissue, injecting virus directly into the haemolymph and suppressing bee immunity. When mite infestation is heavy, overt infections emerge — emerging adults have shrivelled, crumpled wings, shortened abdomens, discolouration and paralysis, and die within days. High viral loads also shorten the lifespan of apparently normal bees and are strongly associated with winter colony losses.

Variants

DWV exists as a complex of closely related master variants, principally types A and B (type B is also known as Varroa destructor virus-1), which can recombine. Type B has become dominant in many regions and may outcompete type A within mites and bees.

Control

There is no treatment against the virus itself; control depends on keeping Varroa mite populations below damaging thresholds through monitoring, chemical treatments (such as oxalic and formic acids or synthetic miticides), and biotechnical methods such as drone brood removal. Breeding of mite-resistant bees is an active research goal.
