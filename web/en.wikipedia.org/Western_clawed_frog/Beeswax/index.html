Beeswax (Latin: cera alba) is a natural wax produced by honey bees of the genus Apis. It is secreted as thin scales from glands on the abdomens of worker bees, roughly 12–18 days old, and chewed and moulded by the bees into the hexagonal cells of the honeycomb, in which the colony raises brood and stores honey and pollen.

Composition and properties

Beeswax consists mainly (about 70%) of esters of long-chain fatty acids with long-chain alcohols — principally myricyl palmitate — together with free fatty acids and hydrocarbons. It is inert and insoluble in water, has a melting point of 62–65 °C, and burns cleanly with a mildly sweet odour. Fresh wax is nearly white; it yellows as it incorporates pollen oils and propolis.

Uses

Humans have used beeswax for thousands of years: in lost-wax casting of metals, encaustic painting, candles (long prized for church use), cosmetics and pharmaceuticals (as a stiffener and emulsifier), food glazing (E901), wood and leather polishes, and in beekeeping itself, where pressed sheets of wax foundation guide comb construction in frames. Beeswax candles and blocks were historically valuable trade goods; ancient Egyptians used the wax in embalming and shipbuilding.

Production

Beekeepers obtain most wax from cappings cut from honey frames during extraction, and from old combs, melting and filtering the crude wax into blocks. Bees consume an estimated 4–8 kg of honey to secrete 1 kg of wax, making it considerably more valuable per kilogram than honey in some markets.
