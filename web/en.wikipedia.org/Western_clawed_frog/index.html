The western clawed frog (Xenopus tropicalis, formerly Silurana tropicalis) is a species of frog in the family Pipidae, native to the rainforests and forest-savanna mosaics of West and Central Africa, from Senegal to Cameroon and eastwards. Like its better-known relative the African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), it is wholly aquatic, with a flattened body, webbed hind feet bearing small black claws, and a lateral-line system used to detect water movements.

Description and ecology

Adults reach about 4–5 cm in body length, smaller than X. laevis. They inhabit slow streams, pools and flooded forest, feeding on invertebrates and small vertebrates detected by scent and touch, since the tongue-less Pipidae cannot flick a tongue to capture prey; instead they shovel food into the mouth with their forelimbs. Males call underwater to attract females, which lay eggs that develop through a free-swimming tadpole stage.

Model organism

Xenopus tropicalis has become an important model organism in developmental and cell biology and genetics. Unlike the allotetraploid X. laevis, it is a true diploid with a smaller genome (about 1.7 billion base pairs), a shorter generation time (roughly four to six months) and large, externally developing eggs suited to experimental embryology and transgenesis. Its genome was published in 2010 — the first amphibian genome sequenced — enabling genetic screens and studies of vertebrate development, regeneration and disease. Together with X. laevis, which served in the historical "Hogben" pregnancy test, the species underpins much of modern amphibian experimental biology.
