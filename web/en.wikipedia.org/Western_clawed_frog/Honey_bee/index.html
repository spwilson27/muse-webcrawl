A honey bee is a eusocial flying insect within the genus Apis of the bee clade, all native to mainland Afro-Eurasia. Honey bees are known for their production of honey and beeswax, for their construction of perennial colonial nests from wax, and for the large size of their colonies. The best-known species is the western honey bee (Apis mellifera), domesticated for honey production and crop pollination and now found on every continent except Antarctica.

Colony life

A colony consists of a single fertile queen, tens of thousands of sterile female workers, and (seasonally) male drones. Workers progress through tasks with age — nursing brood, building comb, guarding the entrance — before becoming foragers. Returning foragers communicate the location of rich food sources by the waggle dance, which encodes distance and direction. Colonies reproduce by swarming: the old queen leaves with part of the workforce to found a new nest while a daughter queen takes over.

Products and pollination

Honey bees gather nectar and convert it into honey, stored in wax comb as the colony's energy reserve; they also produce beeswax, royal jelly, pollen and propolis, all harvested in beekeeping. Their greatest economic value, however, is pollination: managed honey bees pollinate a large share of the world's crops, while wild honey bees and other pollinators also contribute.

Threats

Honey bee colonies face numerous stressors, including the parasitic mite Varroa destructor and viruses it spreads (notably deformed wing virus), other pathogens and pests, pesticide exposure — including neonicotinoids — habitat loss and poor nutrition, and the still incompletely explained phenomenon of colony collapse disorder. Declining pollinator health has made honey bee research a major scientific and agricultural priority.
