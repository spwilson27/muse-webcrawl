Western honey bee

Western honey bee
Scientific name: Apis mellifera
Family: Apidae
Native range: Europe, Africa, western Asia
Known for: Honey production; crop pollination

The western honey bee or European honey bee (Apis mellifera) is the most common of the honey bee species worldwide. Native to Europe, Africa and western Asia, it has been domesticated for honey production and crop pollination and has been introduced to every continent except Antarctica. It is of enormous economic importance as the world's premier managed pollinator.

Biology and society

Western honey bees live in colonies of tens of thousands of individuals organised into castes: a single fertile queen, female workers, and male drones. Workers communicate the location of food sources through the waggle dance. Colonies reproduce by swarming, in which the old queen leaves with a large group of workers to found a new nest. Numerous subspecies are recognised, including the Italian bee (A. m. ligustica) and the Africanised "killer bee", a hybrid with A. m. scutellata in the Americas.

Beekeeping and threats

Humans have kept honey bees for thousands of years; modern beekeeping uses movable-frame hives such as the Langstroth hive. Since the mid-2000s, beekeepers have reported severe losses attributed to colony collapse disorder, the parasitic mite Varroa destructor, viruses, pesticides and habitat loss. The western honey bee's genome was sequenced in 2006.
