Manuka honey (Māori: miere mānuka) is a monofloral honey produced by bees that pollinate the flowers of the mānuka tree, Leptospermum scoparium, which grows uncultivated in New Zealand and southeastern Australia. It commands premium prices because of its distinctive antibacterial activity, which is attributed largely to the compound methylglyoxal (MGO), formed in the honey from dihydroxyacetone present in mānuka nectar.

Properties and grading

Most honeys are mildly antibacterial through hydrogen peroxide production, enzymes and acidity, but mānuka honey shows strong "non-peroxide" activity that persists and can be quantified by its MGO content. Grading systems include the industry UMF (Unique Mānuka Factor) rating and MGO-number labels; New Zealand's Ministry for Primary Industries introduced a scientific definition and testing regime in 2018 to authenticate export honey. Medical-grade mānuka honey is used in licensed wound dressings, for which there is clinical evidence of benefit in some types of wounds; evidence for many broader health claims made for eating the honey remains limited.

Production and fraud

Mānuka flowers for only a few weeks each year, and the honey is correspondingly scarce and expensive — several times more "mānuka" honey has historically been sold worldwide than New Zealand produces, prompting widespread concern about adulteration and mislabelling, and disputes between New Zealand and Australian producers over the name "manuka".
