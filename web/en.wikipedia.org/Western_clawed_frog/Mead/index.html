Mead

Traditional mead ranges from dry to sweet and typically contains 3.5% to over 20% alcohol by volume.

TypeFermented honey beverage ("honey wine")
Alcohol content~3.5–20.5% ABV
Main ingredientsHoney, water, yeast
VariantsMelomel (fruit), metheglin (spices), braggot (malt), cyser (apple), pyment (grape)

Mead is an alcoholic beverage made by fermenting honey mixed with water, and sometimes with added ingredients such as fruits, spices, grains, or hops. It is one of the oldest known fermented drinks: residues consistent with a fermented honey beverage have been identified in Chinese pottery vessels dating to around 7000 BC, and mead features prominently in the mythology and literature of many cultures — the Norse "mead of poetry", the mead-halls of Anglo-Saxon verse such as Beowulf, and the drink of the Vedic and Greek gods' counterparts.

Production

The honey–water mixture (must) is inoculated with yeast and fermented; because honey is low in yeast nutrients, modern meadmakers add nutrients to ensure a healthy fermentation. Meads may be still or sparkling, dry or sweet ("sack" meads are especially strong and sweet), and are often aged for months or years. Named varieties include melomel (fruit mead), metheglin (spiced), cyser (apple), pyment (grape), braggot (brewed with malt like a beer–mead hybrid) and acerglyn (maple).

History

Mead was widespread in ancient Europe, Africa and Asia. In medieval northern Europe it was a prestige drink, the centrepiece of feasts in the mead hall; its expense relative to ale and beer, and later competition from imported wine and cheap sugar, pushed it into decline. The word "honeymoon" is popularly — if dubiously — connected to a custom of giving newlyweds a month's supply of mead. Since the late 20th century, a craft revival, especially in the United States, has seen hundreds of new meaderies open.
