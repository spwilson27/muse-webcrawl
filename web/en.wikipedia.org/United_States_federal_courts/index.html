United States federal courts

The United States federal courts are the courts of the federal judiciary of the United States, established under Article III of the Constitution and by acts of Congress. They decide cases arising under federal law and the Constitution, disputes between states, cases involving ambassadors, admiralty cases, and — under diversity jurisdiction — disputes between citizens of different states above a monetary threshold. The system's apex is the Supreme Court of the United States.

Structure

The Judiciary Act of 1789 created the three-tier structure that persists today. District courts are the trial courts of general jurisdiction, at least one in every state. The courts of appeals sit in twelve regional circuits plus the Federal Circuit, which hears specialized appeals in patent, claims, and trade cases from panels of three judges. The Supreme Court, with nine justices, exercises mostly discretionary review by writ of certiorari — granting a few dozen of the thousands of petitions filed each term.

Specialized and Article I courts

Congress has also created courts outside Article III, whose judges serve fixed terms: the Tax Court, the Court of Federal Claims, bankruptcy courts (units of the district courts), the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, and military courts. Article III judges, by contrast, hold office "during good behaviour" — effectively for life — and their salaries cannot be reduced, safeguards the framers intended to secure judicial independence.

Jurisdiction and workload

Federal question and diversity cases form the bulk of the civil docket; federal courts also try all federal crimes. Roughly 300,000–400,000 cases are filed in the district courts in a typical year, the great majority terminating without trial. Federal courts run parallel to the separate state court systems, which handle the overwhelming majority of American litigation.
