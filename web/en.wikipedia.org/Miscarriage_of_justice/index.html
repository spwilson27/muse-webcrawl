Miscarriage of justice

A miscarriage of justice occurs when a person is wrongfully convicted of a crime, or when a legal process produces an outcome that is contrary to justice, such as the acquittal of a guilty person due to procedural error or the conviction of an innocent person through flawed evidence. The term is used in common-law jurisdictions such as the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, and Australia.

Causes

Common causes of miscarriages of justice include false eyewitness identification, false confessions obtained under pressure, unreliable forensic science, prosecutorial misconduct, perjured testimony, and ineffective legal representation. Advances in DNA testing since the late 1980s have led to the exoneration of many wrongly convicted prisoners, particularly in the United States, where organisations such as the Innocence Project have documented hundreds of cases.

Remedies

Legal systems provide mechanisms to correct miscarriages of justice, including appeals, judicial review, and bodies such as the Criminal Cases Review Commission in England and Wales, which investigates suspected wrongful convictions and refers cases back to the Court of Appeal. Compensation schemes exist in many jurisdictions for those who have been wrongfully imprisoned, although eligibility rules vary widely.

Notable cases

Well-known miscarriages of justice include the Birmingham Six and Guildford Four in Britain, whose convictions for IRA bombings were quashed in 1991 and 1989 respectively, and the case of Stefan Kiszko. In the United States, cases such as that of the Central Park Five drew attention to coerced confessions by juveniles.
