Cohen's d

Cohen's d is a statistic used to indicate the standardised difference between two means. It is widely used as a measure of effect size in the social sciences, accompanying reporting of a t-test or ANOVA, and in meta-analysis. It is defined as the difference between two means divided by a standard deviation for the data:

d = (M1 − M2) / s

where the denominator is commonly the pooled standard deviation of the two groups. Jacob Cohen introduced the measure and suggested conventions for interpretation: a d of 0.2 is a "small" effect, 0.5 a "medium" effect, and 0.8 a "large" effect — guidelines Cohen himself offered cautiously and which are often misapplied as rigid thresholds.

Variants and corrections

Related statistics include Hedges's g, which applies a correction factor that reduces a slight bias in d for small samples, and Glass's Δ, which uses the standard deviation of a single (control) group. Versions of d also exist for one-sample and paired-samples designs, where the denominator is the standard deviation of the difference scores.

Interpretation

Because it is measured in standard deviation units, Cohen's d can be translated into other intuitive metrics, such as the probability that a randomly chosen person from one group scores higher than a randomly chosen person from the other. Critics note that practical importance depends on context, and recommend reporting d with confidence intervals rather than relying on the small/medium/large labels.
