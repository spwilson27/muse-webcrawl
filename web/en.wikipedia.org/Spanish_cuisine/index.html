Spanish cuisine

Paella and tapas are among the best-known elements of Spanish cuisine

Spanish cuisine comprises the cooking traditions of Spain, shaped by the peninsula's varied geography and climate, its Mediterranean setting, and a long history of cultural exchange — Phoenician, Roman, Moorish, and, after 1492, the ingredients of the Americas. It is built around olive oil (Spain is the world's largest producer), garlic, cured ham (jamón), seafood, rice, and an enormous regional diversity.

Signature dishes

Best-known dishes include paella, the saffron rice dish of Valencia traditionally cooked with rabbit, chicken, and beans, and widely varied with seafood; the tortilla española, a thick potato-and-egg omelette; gazpacho, the cold tomato soup of Andalusia; jamón ibérico, acorn-fed cured ham; and tapas, small plates ranging from olives and patatas bravas to grilled prawns and croquettes, eaten in bars as a social ritual. Churros with chocolate, pulpo a la gallega (Galician octopus with paprika), and Basque cheesecake are also widely known.

Regional cuisines

Catalan cuisine features sauces such as romesco and dishes like escalivada; the Basque Country is famed for pintxos and a dense concentration of Michelin-starred restaurants; Galicia for seafood; Asturias for fabada bean stew and cider; and Andalusia for fried fish and sherry-based cooking. Spain's avant-garde chefs, notably Ferran Adrià of elBulli, revolutionised world fine dining in the 1990s and 2000s with "molecular gastronomy".

Meals and customs

Spanish meal times are late by northern-European standards: lunch, the main meal, is typically eaten between 2 and 4 p.m., and dinner from 9 p.m. onwards. The traditional siesta break, though declining in cities, historically structured the day around the long midday pause.
