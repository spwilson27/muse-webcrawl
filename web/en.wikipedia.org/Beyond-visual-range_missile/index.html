Beyond-visual-range missile

TypeAir-to-air missile
Typical range30 km to over 150 km
GuidanceInertial/datalink midcourse, active radar terminal
ExamplesAIM-120 AMRAAM, Meteor, R-77, PL-15

A beyond-visual-range missile (BVRAAM) is an air-to-air missile capable of engaging targets at ranges beyond the pilot's visual identification distance, typically 20 nautical miles (37 km) or more. Modern BVR missiles combine long-range rocket propulsion with radar guidance, allowing fighters to attack enemy aircraft before being seen.

Development

Early BVR weapons such as the American AIM-7 Sparrow relied on semi-active radar homing, requiring the launching aircraft to illuminate the target throughout the engagement, and performed poorly in early combat use. The AIM-54 Phoenix, carried by the F-14 Tomcat, pioneered active radar homing at very long range. The decisive advance came with "fire-and-forget" missiles — above all the AIM-120 AMRAAM, introduced in 1991 — whose onboard active radar seeker takes over in the terminal phase, freeing the shooter to maneuver or engage other targets.

Guidance and propulsion

Typical engagement profiles use inertial navigation with mid-course updates via a datalink from the launch aircraft or an AWACS, followed by active radar terminal homing. Solid rocket motors power most BVR missiles; the MBDA Meteor instead uses a ramjet, sustaining thrust to the target and greatly enlarging the "no-escape zone" — the envelope within which a target cannot outrun the missile.

Effect on air combat

Since the 1990s the majority of air-to-air kills in major conflicts have been scored at beyond-visual range. BVR capability depends as much on radar performance, identification rules of engagement, electronic warfare, and networked sensors as on the missile itself; stealth and long-range sensors have pushed first-shot ranges still further with weapons such as the Chinese PL-15 and the American AIM-260 under development.
