Fallow deer

Scientific nameDama dama (Linnaeus, 1758)
FamilyCervidae

Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The fallow deer (Dama dama) is a species of deer native to the Mediterranean region of Europe and Asia Minor, and one of the most widely introduced deer in the world. It is celebrated for the buck's broad, palmate (moose-like) antlers and for its variable coat colour. The name "fallow" refers to the pale, yellowish-brown colour of the common colour phase; other phases include white, black (melanistic), and the spotted "menil" form.

Description

Bucks stand 85–110 cm at the shoulder and weigh 60–100 kg; does are smaller. Only males carry antlers, which are shed each spring and regrown by late summer; in mature bucks the beam flattens into a broad palm with points along its edge. The summer coat is typically reddish-brown with white spots; a dark stripe runs down the back and the rump shows a distinctive white patch bordered in black, with a long tail.

Distribution and history

The species survived the last ice age in Mediterranean refuges, principally Anatolia. Phoenicians, Romans, and later the Normans spread it across Europe for hunting parks; the Normans established it in Britain, where it became the classic deer-park species. Introduced populations now thrive in North and South America, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand.

Behaviour and ecology

Fallow deer are grazers and browsers of open woodland and parkland, feeding in herds whose composition changes through the year. During the autumn rut (October–November), bucks establish rutting stands or leks, groaning to attract does and fighting rivals, sometimes fatally. A single fawn (rarely twins) is born in June after a gestation of about 33 weeks. Fallow deer venison is highly regarded, and the species is widely farmed.
