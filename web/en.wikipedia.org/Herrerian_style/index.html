Herrerian style

The Herrerian style of architecture (Spanish: estilo herreriano or arquitectura herreriana) was the last stage of Spanish Renaissance architecture, dominant in Spain during the late 16th and early 17th centuries. Named after its chief exponent, the architect Juan de Herrera (1530–1597), it is characterised by severe, unornamented classicism, rigorous geometry, and monumental scale, closely associated with the reign of Philip II and the ideology of the Spanish Counter-Reformation.

Characteristics

The Herrerian style stripped away the decorative exuberance of the preceding Plateresque and the classical ornament of the earlier Renaissance phase. Its hallmarks include:

  • Complete absence or near-absence of surface ornament
  • Strict horizontality and symmetrical, cubic volumes
  • Buildings conceived as pure geometric compositions, often designed on a grid
  • Slate-covered spires and pyramidal pinnacles topped with spheres, influenced by Flemish architecture
  • Smooth stone (especially granite) facades articulated only by simple cornices and platbands
  • Austere interiors in keeping with the decrees of the Council of Trent

Principal works

The archetype of the style is the Royal Monastery of San Lorenzo de El Escorial (1563–1584), designed by Juan Bautista de Toledo and completed by Herrera for Philip II — a vast palace-monastery-mausoleum whose gridiron plan evokes the martyrdom of St Lawrence. Other major Herrerian works include Valladolid Cathedral (begun 1582 to Herrera's design), the Aranjuez palace works, the Casa de la Moneda in Segovia, the Lonja de Sevilla (now the General Archive of the Indies), and the Alcázar of Toledo's south facade.

Spread and decline

As the official style of the Habsburg monarchy, Herrerian architecture was exported across the Spanish Empire, influencing colonial building in Latin America and the Philippines. In Spain it gradually gave way during the 17th century to the Spanish Baroque, whose Churrigueresque ornament represented a deliberate reaction against Herrerian austerity, though the style was revived in the 20th century under the Franco regime for monumental state buildings such as the Air Ministry and the Valley of the Fallen.
