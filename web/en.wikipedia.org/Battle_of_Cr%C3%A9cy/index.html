The Battle of Crécy took place on 26 August 1346 near Crécy-en-Ponthieu in northern France. It was one of the first and most famous battles of the Hundred Years' War, in which a smaller English army under Edward III decisively defeated a much larger French force under Philip VI.

Background

Edward III had landed in Normandy in July 1346 and marched toward Flanders, devastating the countryside. Caught against the Somme, he forced a crossing at Blanchetaque and took up a strong defensive position on a ridge near Crécy, dismounting his men-at-arms and deploying thousands of longbowmen on the flanks.

The battle

The French army arrived piecemeal late in the day and attacked uphill without proper preparation. Genoese crossbowmen, hampered by wet bowstrings and lacking their protective pavises, were driven back by the rapid shooting of the English longbows. Successive charges by the French chivalry were shot down in heaps; King John of Bohemia was among the many nobles killed, and Philip VI was wounded and led from the field. English losses were remarkably light by comparison. Some accounts also record the first battlefield use of cannon by an English army.

Significance

Crécy established the tactical dominance of the dismounted men-at-arms combined with massed longbow archery and damaged the prestige of French chivalry. Edward went on to besiege and capture Calais, which remained in English hands for more than two centuries.
