Danish language

Danish (dansk, pronounced [ˈtænˀsk]) is a North Germanic language spoken by about six million people, principally in Denmark and in the region of Southern Schleswig in northern Germany, where it has minority language status. It is also spoken on the Faroe Islands and Greenland as a compulsory school subject.

History

Danish descends from Old Norse, the common language of Scandinavia in the Viking Age. During the Middle Ages it diverged from Swedish, absorbing heavy Low German influence through Hanseatic trade. Danish was once spoken across southern Scandinavia and in the Danelaw of England. A standard written language based on the Copenhagen dialect, influenced by the Danish Bible translation of 1550, gradually emerged.

Phonology

Danish is noted for its exceptionally large vowel inventory — around 20 distinct vowel qualities distinguishing length — and for the stød, a form of glottal prosody that distinguishes word pairs. Spoken Danish reduces consonants and vowels extensively, making it famously difficult for learners and slower for Danish toddlers to acquire than neighbouring languages. Written Danish remains close to Norwegian Bokmål, and the three mainland Scandinavian languages are largely mutually intelligible in writing.

Grammar and alphabet

Danish has two grammatical genders (common and neuter), a suffixed definite article (huset, "the house"), and forms questions and verb tenses analytically. Its alphabet has 29 letters, ending with Æ, Ø, and Å (adopted in 1948; formerly written Aa). Numbers are partly vigesimal: 50 is halvtreds and 70 is halvfjerds, based on multiples of twenty.

Dialects

Traditional dialects include Insular Danish, Jutlandic, and Bornholmish, though regional dialects have receded in favour of a Copenhagen-based standard (rigsdansk).
