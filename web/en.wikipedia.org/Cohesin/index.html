Cohesin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cohesin is a protein complex that mediates sister chromatid cohesion, homologous recombination, and DNA looping. Cohesin holds sister chromatids together after DNA replication until they are separated in mitosis and meiosis, ensuring accurate chromosome segregation. It is a multi-subunit ring-shaped complex of the SMC (structural maintenance of chromosomes) family.

Structure

The cohesin complex consists of four core subunits: two SMC proteins, SMC1 and SMC3, whose long coiled-coil arms fold back on themselves to form a heterodimer with ATPase head domains; a kleisin subunit (SCC1/RAD21 in mitotic cells, or REC8 in meiosis) that bridges the SMC heads; and a HEAT-repeat protein (STAG1/2, or STAG3 in meiosis) associated with the kleisin. The resulting structure can form a ring that topologically entraps DNA, a model supported by experiments in which cleavage of the kleisin or linearization of DNA releases cohesion.

Function and regulation

Cohesin is loaded onto chromosomes by the SCC2–SCC4 loader complex, and cohesion is established during S phase, a process requiring the acetyltransferase ECO1/ESCO1-2, which acetylates SMC3. In vertebrate mitosis, most cohesin is removed from chromosome arms in prophase by the WAPL-mediated "prophase pathway", while centromeric cohesin is protected by shugoshin–PP2A until anaphase, when the protease separase cleaves the kleisin subunit, opening the ring and allowing sister chromatids to part. In meiosis, cohesin is removed in two steps to permit the reductional and equational divisions.

Beyond cohesion, cohesin helps organize chromatin into topologically associating domains and loops together with CTCF, and it participates in DNA double-strand break repair by homologous recombination.

Clinical significance

Mutations in cohesin subunits and regulators cause developmental disorders known as cohesinopathies, including Cornelia de Lange syndrome (often involving NIPBL, the human SCC2 homologue) and Roberts syndrome (ESCO2). Somatic mutations in cohesin genes such as STAG2 are also recurrent in cancers including bladder cancer and myeloid malignancies.
