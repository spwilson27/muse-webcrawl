Rodolfo Graziani, 1st Marquis of Neghelli (1882–1955), was an Italian military officer who served as a prominent commander in Mussolini's colonial wars and in the Second World War. He became notorious for brutal campaigns in Libya and Ethiopia — where he earned the name "the Butcher of Ethiopia" — and he remains one of the most controversial figures of Fascist Italy.

Colonial campaigns

A career officer wounded in the First World War, Graziani led the brutal "reconquest" of Libya in the 1920s and early 1930s, deporting the nomadic population of Cyrenaica into concentration camps where tens of thousands died, and capturing and hanging the Senussi resistance leader Omar al-Mukhtar in 1931. In the Second Italo-Ethiopian War (1935–36) he commanded the southern invasion from Italian Somaliland, authorising the use of poison gas. As Viceroy of Italian East Africa he survived an assassination attempt in Addis Ababa on 19 February 1937, which triggered three days of reprisal massacres (Yekatit 12) in which thousands of Ethiopians were killed.

Second World War

As governor-general of Libya and commander in North Africa, Graziani advanced into Egypt in September 1940 but was routed by the British counteroffensive Operation Compass, which destroyed his Tenth Army; he resigned in early 1941. After the 1943 armistice he joined Mussolini's German-backed Italian Social Republic as Minister of Defence, organising its armed forces and threatening death for draft resisters.

Trial and death

Tried after the war, Graziani was sentenced in 1950 to 19 years' imprisonment for collaboration with the Germans but was released after a few months; Ethiopia's efforts to have him tried for war crimes were blocked, and he was never prosecuted for his African campaigns. He became honorary president of the neo-fascist Italian Social Movement (MSI) and died in Rome in 1955. A mausoleum built in his honour at Affile in 2012 with public funds provoked lasting controversy.
