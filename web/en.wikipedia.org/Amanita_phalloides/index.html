Amanita phalloides

Amanita phalloides (/æməˈnaɪtə fəˈlɔɪdiːz/), commonly known as the death cap, is a deadly poisonous basidiomycete fungus, one of many in the genus Amanita. Widely distributed across Europe, it spreads by associating with various deciduous and coniferous trees. It is responsible for the majority of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide.

Description

The death cap has a large fruiting body with a cap 5–15 cm across, usually greenish-yellow to olive in colour, white gills, a white stem with a skirt-like ring, and a prominent bulbous base enclosed in a sac-like volva. It has no distinctive smell when young, and is said to taste pleasant, which contributes to accidental poisonings when it is mistaken for edible species such as the paddy straw mushroom or Caesar's mushroom.

Toxicity

The principal toxins are the amatoxins, chiefly α-amanitin, which inhibit RNA polymerase II and thereby halt protein synthesis, causing liver and kidney failure. As little as half a cap can be lethal. Symptoms are delayed 6–24 hours after ingestion and begin with severe gastrointestinal distress, followed by a deceptive remission and then hepatic failure. Treatment is supportive; silibinin and liver transplantation are used in severe cases. Historical deaths attributed to death cap poisoning include those of Roman emperor Claudius and Holy Roman Emperor Charles VI.

Distribution

Native to Europe, the species has been introduced to North America, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and South America through imported trees, and it is spreading. It forms ectomycorrhizal associations with oaks, beeches, chestnuts, pines and other trees.
