Andrew Jackson (March 15, 1767 – June 8, 1845) was an American lawyer, general, and statesman who served as the seventh president of the United States from 1829 to 1837. Before his presidency, he gained fame as a general in the United States Army and served in both houses of Congress. Jackson sought to advance the rights of the "common man" against a "corrupt aristocracy" and to preserve the Union.

Born in the colonial Carolinas to Scots-Irish immigrant parents, Jackson was orphaned during the Revolutionary War, in which he served as a courier and was captured by the British. He read law and became a prosecutor in frontier Tennessee, was elected to Congress and the Senate, and built a plantation, the Hermitage, worked by enslaved people.

Military career

Jackson rose to national prominence in the War of 1812. He defeated the Red Stick Creeks at Horseshoe Bend (1814), and on 8 January 1815 his outnumbered force decisively defeated the British at the Battle of New Orleans, making him a national hero. In 1818 he invaded Spanish Florida during the First Seminole War, an action that helped lead to the Adams–Onís Treaty ceding Florida to the United States.

Road to the presidency

Jackson won a plurality of the popular and electoral votes in the election of 1824, but the House of Representatives chose John Quincy Adams. Denouncing a "corrupt bargain", Jackson's supporters built the modern Democratic Party, and he defeated Adams in a rematch in 1828.

Presidency

Jackson's presidency ("Jacksonian democracy") expanded suffrage among white men, rotated federal officeholders (the "spoils system"), and strengthened the presidency. He signed the Indian Removal Act of 1830, which forcibly relocated Native American tribes from the Southeast — including the Cherokee on the Trail of Tears — to lands west of the Mississippi. He confronted South Carolina in the Nullification Crisis, threatening force while accepting a compromise tariff, and in 1832 vetoed the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States, withdrawing federal deposits and contributing to the speculative boom that preceded the Panic of 1837. His Specie Circular of 1836 required gold or silver for public land purchases.

Later life and legacy

Jackson retired to the Hermitage, remained influential in the Democratic Party, and died in 1845. Historians' assessments are deeply divided: he is praised as a champion of democracy and the Union and condemned for Indian removal and his defence and practice of slavery. His portrait appears on the U.S. twenty-dollar bill.
