Situated knowledge

Situated knowledge holds that all knowledge is produced from a particular embodied standpoint.

Situated knowledge is a concept in philosophy of science and feminist epistemology holding that knowledge is always produced from a particular position — embodied, located and partial — rather than from a detached, universal viewpoint. The term was given its canonical formulation by Donna Haraway in her 1988 essay "Situated Knowledges: The Science Question in Feminism and the Privilege of Partial Perspective".

The argument

Haraway criticised what she called the "god trick of seeing everything from nowhere": the claim, associated with unmarked scientific objectivity, to a vision that is infinite, disembodied and unaccountable. Against both this view and a relativism that treats all positions as equivalent, she argued for a feminist objectivity built from "partial, locatable, critical knowledges" — embodied perspectives that acknowledge their limits and can be held accountable, and that may yield stronger, more adequate accounts of the world, especially when the perspectives of subjugated standpoints are taken seriously.

Context and influence

The concept is closely related to standpoint theory in feminist philosophy (as developed by Sandra Harding and Nancy Hartsock) and to earlier pragmatist and sociology-of-knowledge themes that learning and knowing are situated in practice. It has been widely taken up in science and technology studies, anthropology, geography and feminist theory, and is sometimes discussed alongside "situated cognition" and "situated learning" in psychology and education, which hold that knowledge is inseparable from the activity and context in which it is developed.
