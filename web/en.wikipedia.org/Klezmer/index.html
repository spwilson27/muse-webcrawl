Klezmer

Klezmer (Yiddish: קלעזמער) is an instrumental musical tradition of the Ashkenazi Jews of Central and Eastern Europe. Developed from the music of Jewish wedding bands (kapelye), it blends melodies from synagogue chant, Balkan, Ottoman, Greek, and Romani music, and is characterised by expressive ornamentation imitating the human voice — laughter, sobs, and cantorial wails.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Hebrew kley zemer, "vessels of song," originally referring to musical instruments and later to the musicians themselves. Professional klezmorim played at weddings, fairs, and celebrations across the Pale of Settlement, absorbing local repertoires wherever they travelled.

Musical features

Klezmer uses modes such as the Freygish (Phrygian dominant) scale. Common dance forms include the freylekhs, bulgar, sher, khosidl, and the slow, improvisatory doina. The clarinet — popularised by virtuosos such as Naftule Brandwein and Dave Tarras — produces characteristic effects including the krekhts (a sobbing catch) and tshoks (laugh-like figures).

Emigration and revival

Mass emigration carried klezmer to the United States, where it was recorded extensively in the 1910s–30s and absorbed influences from jazz and theatre music. The tradition nearly vanished after the Holocaust and assimilation, but from the 1970s a revival led by musicians such as Giora Feidman, the Klezmer Conservatory Band, the Klezmorim, and later the Klezmatics and Andy Statman restored and renewed the repertoire. In Europe, klezmer festivals and bands have flourished since the 1990s.
