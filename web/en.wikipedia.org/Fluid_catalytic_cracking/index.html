Fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) is the most important conversion process used in petroleum refineries. It is widely used to convert the high-boiling, high-molecular-weight hydrocarbon fractions of crude oils into more valuable gasoline, olefinic gases, and other products. Cracking of heavy petroleum fractions was originally done by thermal cracking, which has been almost completely replaced by catalytic cracking because it produces more gasoline with a higher octane rating and produces byproduct gases with more carbon–carbon double bonds (olefins), which are more valuable than the paraffins produced by thermal cracking.

The feedstock to an FCC is usually that portion of the crude oil that has an initial boiling point of 340 °C (644 °F) or higher and an average molecular weight ranging from about 200 to 600 or more. This portion of crude oil is often referred to as vacuum gas oil (VGO). In the FCC process, the feedstock is injected into a heated stream of fluidized catalyst, where it vaporizes and breaks down into smaller molecules. The catalyst is then separated from the cracked vapors, regenerated by burning off deposited coke, and recycled.

History

The first commercial use of catalytic cracking occurred in 1915, but the modern process grew out of the work of Eugene Houdry in France in the 1920s and 1930s. His fixed-bed Houdry process was commercialized in the United States in 1936. The fluid catalytic cracking process itself was developed by a consortium of American and British companies — including Standard Oil of New Jersey, Standard Oil of Indiana, Shell, and the Kellogg Company — working through the Catalytic Research Associates group, formed to circumvent the Houdry patents. The first commercial FCC unit, called PCLA #1 (Powdered Catalyst Louisiana), began operation on May 25, 1942, at the Baton Rouge refinery of Standard Oil of New Jersey (now ExxonMobil). At that time America was involved in World War II, and the new process dramatically increased production of aviation gasoline.

Process description

A modern FCC unit consists of a reactor section, a catalyst regenerator, and a fractionator. Hot catalyst from the regenerator (at roughly 700 °C) contacts the preheated feed in a vertical or inclined pipe called the riser. As the mixture travels up the riser, the heavy hydrocarbons crack into lighter products at temperatures of about 480–540 °C and near-atmospheric pressure. Cyclones in the reactor vessel separate the catalyst from the product vapors. Spent catalyst, coated with coke, flows to the regenerator, where air is blown through it to burn off the coke at temperatures around 700–760 °C. The heat released in the regenerator supplies the energy needed for the endothermic cracking reactions, and the hot regenerated catalyst returns to the riser.

Catalyst

Modern FCC catalysts consist of four major components: a crystalline zeolite, a matrix, a binder, and a filler. The zeolite — typically a rare-earth-exchanged Y zeolite (faujasite) — is the primary active component responsible for cracking activity and selectivity. Catalysts are manufactured as microspheroidal particles with an average size of about 60–80 micrometres, fluidizable in the vapor stream. Additives such as ZSM-5 are blended in to increase gasoline octane and light olefin yields, especially propylene.

Products

A typical FCC produces, by volume, roughly 45–55% gasoline (cracked naphtha), 10–20% light cycle oil, plus LPG (liquefied petroleum gas rich in propylene and butenes), light gases, slurry oil, and coke that is burned in the regenerator. Because of growing demand for petrochemical feedstocks, many FCC units are now operated in "maximum propylene" mode.
