Grimdark is a subgenre of speculative fiction with a tone, style or setting that is particularly dystopian, amoral or violent. The term is inspired by the tagline of the tabletop wargame Warhammer 40,000: "In the grim darkness of the far future there is only war." Grimdark works typically reject clear moral binaries in favour of flawed or ruthless protagonists and worlds where hope is scarce and victory costly.

Characteristics

Scholars and critics associate grimdark with graphic violence, moral ambiguity, political cynicism and a rejection of the heroic quest structure of traditional epic fantasy. Adam Roberts characterised it as fiction "where nobody is honourable and Might is Right". Others distinguish grimdark from "grim realism" by its degree of exaggeration and bleakness for its own sake.

History and examples

Precursors include Michael Moorcock's Elric stories and Glen Cook's The Black Company (1984). The label gained currency in the 2000s with writers such as Joe Abercrombie (The First Law), Steven Erikson, Mark Lawrence and R. Scott Bakker. George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire is frequently discussed alongside the subgenre, though its categorisation is debated.

Criticism and responses

Critics argue grimdark can confuse brutality with realism and exclude other perspectives; the "hopepunk" movement emerged partly as a counterpoint. Defenders see the subgenre as a reaction against formulaic, sanitised fantasy.
