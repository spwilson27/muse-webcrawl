Pollination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bee transferring pollen between flowers

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther (the male part of a flower) to a stigma (the female part), enabling fertilisation and the production of seeds. Most flowering plants (angiosperms) depend on pollination for reproduction, and the process may occur within the same flower, between flowers of the same plant, or between different plants of the same species.

Mechanisms

Pollination occurs by two broad mechanisms: abiotic pollination, in which pollen is carried by wind or water, and biotic pollination, in which animals carry the pollen. Wind pollination (anemophily) is common in grasses, conifers and many temperate trees; water pollination (hydrophily) is rare and mostly confined to aquatic plants.

Biotic pollination is carried out by insects (entomophily) — including bees, butterflies, moths, flies and beetles — as well as by birds (ornithophily), bats (chiropterophily) and other vertebrates. Pollinators typically visit flowers in search of nectar or pollen as food; pollen grains adhere to their bodies and are deposited on the stigma of the next flower visited.

Self-pollination and cross-pollination

Self-pollination occurs when pollen from a flower fertilises the same flower or another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination (also called xenogamy or allogamy) is the transfer of pollen between different plants, which promotes genetic diversity. Many plants have evolved mechanisms to encourage cross-pollination, such as self-incompatibility, separation of male and female flowers, or different flowering times for anthers and stigmas (dichogamy).

Importance

Pollination is a critical ecosystem service. Roughly three quarters of the world's flowering plants and about one third of global food crop production depend to some extent on animal pollinators. Crops such as apples, almonds, coffee, cocoa and many fruits and vegetables rely on insect pollination. Declines in wild and managed pollinator populations — driven by habitat loss, pesticide use, disease and climate change — are a significant concern for agriculture and biodiversity.

Co-evolution

Flowers and their pollinators have co-evolved over more than 100 million years. Floral traits such as colour, scent, shape and nectar reward are adapted to particular pollinators: bee-pollinated flowers are often blue or yellow with ultraviolet guides, while bat-pollinated flowers tend to be large, pale and night-scented. Some orchids mimic female insects to achieve pollination by pseudocopulation.
