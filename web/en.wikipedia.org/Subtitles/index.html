Subtitles are text derived from a transcript or translation of the dialogue in a film, television program, video game, or similar medium, displayed — usually at the bottom of the screen — in synchrony with the corresponding speech. They serve two main audiences: viewers who do not understand the language spoken, and viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing (for whom subtitling also describes relevant sounds and speaker identification, overlapping with captions).

Types and conventions

Subtitles may be intralingual (same language as the audio, for accessibility or language learning) or interlingual (translated). Because viewers read more slowly than actors speak, subtitles are condensed: typically one or two lines of about 40 characters, shown for one to six seconds, at reading speeds conventions place around 12–20 characters per second for adults. "Open" subtitles are burned into the image, while "closed" subtitles are carried as separate data the viewer can toggle.

History and production

Subtitling grew from the intertitles of silent film; when sound arrived in the late 1920s, subtitling became one of the two main ways to export films across languages, the other being dubbing — countries historically divided into "subtitling" and "dubbing" markets. Broadcast teletext systems in the 1970s–80s brought optional subtitles to television. Modern workflows use timecoded file formats — SubRip (.srt) is the ubiquitous plain-text standard, and WebVTT is the web-native counterpart — and increasingly machine translation and automatic speech recognition, with human editors checking quality. Unofficial fan-made subtitles ("fansubs") have played a notable role in the global spread of anime and other media.
