Arica is a commune and a port city with about 220,000 inhabitants in northern Chile, capital of the Arica Province and of the Arica y Parinacota Region. It lies 18 km (11 mi) south of the Peruvian border, at the northern end of the Atacama Desert, and is known as the "City of Eternal Spring" for its mild, extremely arid climate — Arica is among the driest inhabited places in the world, with average annual rainfall around 1 mm or less.

History

The area was inhabited for at least 10,000 years; the nearby Azapa Valley preserves the Chinchorro mummies, among the oldest in the world. Founded by the Spanish in 1541 as San Marcos de Arica, the port shipped silver from Potosí and endured attacks by English and Dutch privateers. Arica belonged to Peru after independence but was captured by Chile in the War of the Pacific at the Battle of Arica (7 June 1880), the assault on the Morro headland; sovereignty was settled in Chile's favour by the Treaty of Lima (1929), which returned Tacna to Peru. Under treaty obligations, Arica's port serves Bolivian trade, and the Arica–La Paz railway opened in 1913.

Geography and economy

The city sits where the Lluta and San José (Azapa) river valleys meet the Pacific; the Azapa Valley produces olives, tomatoes and citrus in an otherwise barren landscape. Economy rests on the port, fishing and fishmeal, agriculture, mining services, commerce with Peru and Bolivia, and tourism drawn by beaches such as Chinchorro and La Lisera and by surfing at El Gringo. Landmarks include the Morro de Arica with its historical museum, the San Marcos Cathedral and the former customs house, both associated with the workshops of Gustave Eiffel.
