Insular area


The insular areas of the United States, in the Caribbean and the Pacific.

In United States law, an insular area is a territory of the United States that is neither part of one of the fifty states nor part of the District of Columbia. The term derives from the Insular Cases, a series of Supreme Court decisions of 1901 and after concerning territories acquired in the Spanish–American War. The principal inhabited insular areas are Puerto Rico, Guam, the US Virgin Islands, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands, together with a number of minor outlying islands.

Legal status

Insular areas are classified as organised or unorganised, and as incorporated or unincorporated. An incorporated territory is one to which the US Constitution applies in full and which is considered an integral part of the United States; the only current example is Palmyra Atoll. Unincorporated territories are possessions in which only fundamental constitutional provisions apply automatically. Puerto Rico and the Northern Mariana Islands are commonwealths; Guam and the US Virgin Islands are organised unincorporated territories; American Samoa is unorganised and unincorporated, and its people are US nationals rather than citizens by birth, a status unique among the territories.

Administration

The Office of Insular Affairs in the Department of the Interior coordinates federal policy for most insular areas. Residents generally cannot vote in US presidential elections and are represented in Congress by non-voting delegates (a resident commissioner in Puerto Rico's case), though they may vote in party primaries and pay varying forms of federal tax.
