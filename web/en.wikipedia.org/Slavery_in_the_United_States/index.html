Slavery in the United States

Enslaved people picking cotton on a plantation in the American South

Slavery in the United States was the legal institution of human chattel slavery that existed in the United States of America from its founding in 1776 until the passage of the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865. Slavery was established in the English colonies from the early 17th century and was practised in all of the original thirteen colonies at independence.

Development

The first enslaved Africans arrived in Virginia in 1619. Over the following two centuries, slavery became central to the agricultural economy of the South, especially after the invention of the cotton gin in 1793 made short-staple cotton hugely profitable. The 1808 ban on the international slave trade did not end the institution; instead, a vast domestic trade moved roughly one million enslaved people from the Upper South to the Deep South. By 1860 the enslaved population approached four million.

Civil War and abolition

Slavery was the central cause of the American Civil War (1861–1865). President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 declared freedom for enslaved people in the rebellious states, and Union victory made abolition nationwide. The Thirteenth Amendment, ratified in December 1865, abolished slavery throughout the United States except as punishment for crime. The legacy of slavery — in wealth disparities, racial inequality and American law and politics — remains a subject of extensive scholarship and public debate.
