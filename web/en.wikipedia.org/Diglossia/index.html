Diglossia

Diglossia
FieldSociolinguistics
Defined byCharles A. Ferguson (1959)
Varieties"High" (H) and "Low" (L)
Classic casesArabic, Swiss German, Greek (historically), Haitian Creole/French

In linguistics, diglossia is a situation in which a single language community uses two distinct varieties of a language (or two languages) in different social contexts: a "high" variety (H), learned formally and used for literature, formal speech, education, and official purposes, and a "low" variety (L), acquired at home and used for ordinary conversation. The term was defined in its modern sense by Charles A. Ferguson in a landmark 1959 article.

Features

Ferguson listed characteristic features: a sharp functional separation between H and L; higher prestige for H; a literary heritage in H; acquisition of L natively and H through schooling; standardization and grammars for H; long-term stability of the arrangement; and structural differences in grammar, lexicon, and phonology, with H often more conservative and complex.

Examples

Classical cases include Arabic (Modern Standard/Classical Arabic versus regional colloquials), German-speaking Switzerland (Standard German versus Swiss German dialects), and historically Greek (Katharevousa versus Demotic, resolved in favour of Demotic in 1976). Joshua Fishman extended the concept to bilingual situations where the two codes are unrelated languages, and distinguished diglossia with and without bilingualism.

Related phenomena

Diglossia shades into bilingualism, code-switching, register variation, and language standardization. Political debates over H and L — over education, media, and national identity — have been significant in the Arab world, Greece, and elsewhere.
