Coat of arms

A coat of arms is a heraldic visual design on an escutcheon (i.e., a shield), surcoat, or tabard. The coat of arms on an escutcheon forms the central element of the full heraldic achievement, which in its whole consists of a shield, supporters, a crest, and a motto. Coats of arms have been used since the High Middle Ages to identify individuals, families, states, organizations, and corporations.

History

Heraldic designs came into general use among European nobility in the 12th century, probably as a means of identification in battle and tournaments when faces were hidden by helmets. Systematic, hereditary heraldry developed as designs passed from father to son, and heralds kept records of arms to settle disputes. By the late Middle Ages, arms were used by towns, guilds, universities, and clergy as well as by armigerous families.

Elements

The shield bears charges (animals, objects, geometric devices) on a field divided by ordinaries such as the chief, fess, bend, and cross. Colours follow the rule of tincture, which discourages placing colour on colour or metal on metal. Above the shield sit the helmet and crest; mantling flows from the helmet; supporters may flank the shield; a motto scroll is placed below or above. Arms are described in a technical language called blazon.

Modern use

National coats of arms appear on passports, currency, and official buildings. Heraldic authorities — such as the College of Arms in England and the Court of the Lord Lyon in Scotland — grant and regulate arms in some jurisdictions, while in others arms may be freely assumed.
