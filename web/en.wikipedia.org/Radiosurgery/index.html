Radiosurgery

Radiosurgery is a medical procedure that uses precisely targeted ionising radiation to treat tumours and other disorders, typically in a single session (when delivered in a few sessions it is often called stereotactic radiotherapy). Despite the name, it involves no incision; instead, multiple converging beams deliver a high dose to a small, well-defined target while sparing surrounding healthy tissue. It is most commonly used for brain tumours, arteriovenous malformations and functional disorders such as trigeminal neuralgia.

History

The concept was pioneered by Swedish neurosurgeon Lars Leksell, who in 1951 described using stereotactic localisation with focused radiation. Leksell and physicist Börje Larsson developed the Gamma Knife, first used clinically in 1967–68. Linear accelerator (LINAC)-based radiosurgery followed in the 1980s, and later dedicated systems such as CyberKnife added robotic, image-guided delivery.

Techniques

Gamma Knife units use many cobalt-60 sources arranged so their beams intersect at the target. LINAC-based systems shape photon beams with arcs and cones. CyberKnife uses a compact accelerator on a robotic arm with real-time image guidance, enabling frameless treatment and tracking of moving targets. Proton beam systems exploit the Bragg peak for dose deposition. Patient positioning is fixed with a stereotactic frame or mask, and treatment is planned from MRI and CT imaging.

Indications

Common indications include vestibular schwannomas, meningiomas, pituitary adenomas, brain metastases, arteriovenous malformations, and trigeminal neuralgia. Radiosurgery elsewhere in the body (stereotactic body radiotherapy) treats selected lung, liver and spinal lesions.

Risks

Effects may be delayed months; risks include radiation necrosis, swelling, and injury to nearby critical structures, which careful dose planning seeks to minimise.
