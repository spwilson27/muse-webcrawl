Secret ballot

The secret ballot, also known as the Australian ballot, is a voting method in which a voter's identity in an election or referendum is anonymous. This forestalls attempts to influence the voter by intimidation, blackmailing, and potential vote buying. The system is one means of achieving the goal of political privacy, and is regarded as a cornerstone of modern democracy; the Universal Declaration of Human Rights guarantees elections by secret ballot.

History

Ancient Athens and Rome used forms of secret voting for some decisions, including voting with pebbles or shards (ostracism). In the modern era, France adopted secret voting in its constitution of 1795. The Chartist movement in Britain demanded the ballot as one of its six points in 1838. The modern system of government-printed ballots cast in private was pioneered in the Australian colonies: Tasmania, Victoria, and South Australia introduced it in 1856, and it spread to Britain with the Ballot Act 1872, and to the United States beginning in the 1880s and 1890s — hence the name "Australian ballot".

Methods

Key features include ballots printed at public expense listing all candidates, voting in an enclosed booth, and depositing the marked ballot in a sealed box, so that no vote can be traced to a voter. Modern variants include postal voting and electronic voting, which raise their own secrecy challenges.

Arguments and criticisms

Supporters argue secrecy is essential to prevent coercion and bribery, since a bought or coerced vote cannot be verified. Critics, from John Stuart Mill onward, have argued that voting is a public trust best exercised openly, and that secrecy enables uninformed or selfish voting. Secret ballots are also standard in legislatures for electing officers and in jury-like bodies, though ordinary legislative votes are usually public.
