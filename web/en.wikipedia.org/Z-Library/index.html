Z-Library

Z-Library (abbreviated as z-lib, formerly BookFinder) is a shadow library project that provides access to scholarly journal articles, academic texts, and general-interest books. It is described as the world's largest ebook library and the world's largest scientific articles store. Z-Library is a mirror of Library Genesis, from which most of its books and articles are sourced.

The project has been the target of various legal actions over copyright infringement. In November 2022, its domains were seized by the United States Department of Justice following an indictment by the U.S. Attorney for the Eastern District of New York, and two Russian nationals alleged to be its operators were arrested in Argentina. Despite the seizures, the site has continued to operate, later becoming accessible through personal domains issued to individual registered users.

History

Z-Library began around 2009 as a mirror and borrowing interface for Library Genesis. Over the following decade it grew its own collection and user base, and by the early 2020s it claimed to host more than ten million books and over eighty million articles. The site relied on volunteer donations and offered limited free downloads per day, with additional downloads and features available to donors.

Legal status and blocking

The site has been blocked by internet service providers in numerous countries, including the United Kingdom, France, India, and Italy, following court orders obtained by publishers' associations. Publishers and authors' groups regard the service as a major source of ebook piracy, while supporters argue it provides access to knowledge for students and researchers who cannot afford or obtain texts through legal channels.

2022 domain seizure

On 3 November 2022, several Z-Library domains displayed a seizure banner from the U.S. Department of Justice. Two Russian citizens, Anton Napolsky and Valeriia Ermakova, were arrested in Córdoba, Argentina, and charged with criminal copyright infringement, wire fraud, and money laundering. The operators later announced the site would continue through alternative access methods, including a Tor mirror and personal single-user domains distributed via email.
