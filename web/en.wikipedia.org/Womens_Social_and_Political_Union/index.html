Women's Social and Political Union

The Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU) was a women-only political movement and leading militant organisation campaigning for women's suffrage in the United Kingdom from 1903 to 1918. Its members were known as the suffragettes. The WSPU was founded in Manchester on 10 October 1903 by Emmeline Pankhurst, together with her daughters Christabel and Sylvia and other women of the Independent Labour Party milieu, under the slogan "Deeds, not words".

Militancy

From 1905, when Christabel Pankhurst and Annie Kenney were arrested after heckling a Liberal meeting, the WSPU adopted escalating tactics to force the government's hand: interrupting political meetings, mass marches such as the 1908 Hyde Park rally, window-smashing raids on Whitehall and Oxford Street, and, from 1912, an arson and bombing campaign against empty buildings, pillar boxes, and works of art. Its colours were purple, white, and green, and its newspapers — Votes for Women and later The Suffragette — publicised the cause.

Prison and hunger strikes

Hundreds of suffragettes were imprisoned, where many went on hunger strike and were subjected to force-feeding. The government's 1913 Prisoners (Temporary Discharge for Ill Health) Act — the "Cat and Mouse Act" — allowed hunger strikers to be released and re-arrested once they recovered. In June 1913, Emily Davison was fatally injured at the Epsom Derby after stepping in front of the King's horse, becoming the movement's most famous martyr.

Dissolution

At the outbreak of the First World War in 1914, the WSPU declared a truce and threw its energy into the war effort, renaming its paper Britannia. When women over 30 won the vote in 1918, the Union's purpose was achieved and it was wound up; equal franchise followed in 1928.
