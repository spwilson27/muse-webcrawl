Transition metal

In chemistry, a transition metal (or transition element) is an element whose atoms have an incompletely filled d subshell, or which can form cations with an incomplete d subshell. The transition metals occupy the central d-block of the periodic table — groups 3 to 12 (older numbering IIIB to IIB), in periods 4 to 7 — and include such familiar metals as iron, copper, nickel, silver, gold, platinum, zinc and titanium. Depending on the definition used, group 12 elements (zinc, cadmium, mercury) are sometimes excluded because their d subshell is full in their common oxidation states.


Transition metal compounds are often vividly coloured

Characteristic properties

Because their (n−1)d and ns electrons are close in energy, transition metals typically show:

  • Multiple oxidation states — e.g. iron(II) and iron(III), manganese from +2 to +7.
  • Coloured compounds — arising from d–d electron transitions, as in blue copper(II) sulfate and purple permanganate.
  • Paramagnetism in many compounds, due to unpaired d electrons.
  • Complex formation — they readily bind ligands to form coordination complexes such as haemoglobin's iron centre.
  • Catalytic activity — iron in the Haber process, nickel in hydrogenation, vanadium(V) oxide in the contact process, platinum-group metals in catalytic converters.
  • High melting points, hardness and good electrical and thermal conductivity, with notable exceptions such as mercury, a liquid at room temperature.

Importance

Transition metals underpin industry and biology alike: steel (iron with carbon and alloying metals such as chromium and manganese) is the world's most important structural material; copper carries electricity; titanium serves in aerospace; and iron, copper, zinc, cobalt and molybdenum are essential trace elements in enzymes. Their rich chemistry of complexes and oxidation states makes them central to catalysis, pigments, batteries and medicine (e.g. the anticancer drug cisplatin).
