The Aksumite Empire, or Kingdom of Aksum, was an ancient kingdom centred in the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands, centred on the city of Aksum. Flourishing from roughly the 1st to the 10th century AD, it was one of the great trading powers of the ancient world, ranked alongside Rome, Persia and China, and controlled trade routes between the Roman Empire and India through its Red Sea port of Adulis.

Rise and expansion

Aksum grew from earlier cultures of the northern Horn of Africa, including the kingdom of D'mt. By the late 1st century AD the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea described Aksum as a thriving market for ivory. King Endubis (c. 270–300) was among the first African rulers to mint his own coins, in gold, silver and bronze. Under King Ezana in the 4th century, Aksum conquered the Kingdom of Kush and, following the mission of Frumentius, became one of the earliest states to adopt Christianity.

Height of power

In the 6th century King Kaleb intervened in South Arabia, conquering the Himyarite kingdom in Yemen around 525 AD to protect Christian communities, extending Aksumite influence across the Red Sea. The kingdom exported ivory, tortoiseshell, gold, emeralds, spices and enslaved people, importing silk, glass and manufactured goods.

Culture

Aksum is famous for its monumental carved stelae, including the great fallen stele and the still-standing obelisk of Aksum, as well as palace complexes and royal tombs. The Ge'ez language, written in the Ge'ez script, became the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. Ethiopian tradition holds that the Ark of the Covenant resides in Aksum's Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion.

Decline

From the 7th century, Aksum declined as Arab expansion disrupted Red Sea trade, Adulis silted up, and internal and environmental pressures grew. The kingdom gradually retreated into the highlands, and by around 960 AD it had effectively ended, though its Christian and cultural legacy shaped the later Ethiopian Empire.
