Smoking cessation

Quitting smoking substantially reduces the risk of lung cancer, heart disease, and stroke

Smoking cessation, usually called quitting smoking, is the process of discontinuing tobacco smoking. Tobacco contains nicotine, a highly addictive drug, which makes cessation difficult; withdrawal symptoms can include irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, increased appetite, and cravings. Nevertheless, a majority of smokers express a desire to quit, and roughly half attempt to do so each year in many countries.

Health benefits

The health benefits of quitting begin almost immediately: within hours, carbon monoxide levels in the blood fall; within weeks, circulation and lung function improve; and over years the risks of coronary heart disease, stroke, and lung cancer decline substantially compared with continuing smokers. Quitting before the age of 40 avoids more than 90% of the excess mortality associated with continued smoking.

Methods

Approaches include quitting unaided ("cold turkey"), behavioural counselling and quitlines, nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gum, lozenges, inhalers, and sprays), prescription medications such as varenicline and bupropion, and combination treatments, which evidence reviews find most effective. Electronic cigarettes are used by some smokers as a cessation aid; health authorities differ in their assessments of their effectiveness and safety relative to other methods.

Relapse and support

Most quit attempts end in relapse within the first weeks, and smokers typically make multiple attempts before succeeding permanently. Social support, coping strategies for triggers, and pharmacotherapy all improve long-term abstinence rates. Many governments fund cessation services and run public campaigns such as the United Kingdom's Stoptober.
