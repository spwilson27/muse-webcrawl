Italo-Turkish War

The Italo-Turkish War (Italian: Guerra italo-turca), also known in Italy as the Libyan War, was fought between the Kingdom of Italy and the Ottoman Empire from 29 September 1911 to 18 October 1912. Italy annexed the Ottoman provinces of Tripolitania and Cyrenaica (modern Libya) and occupied the Dodecanese islands in the Aegean.

Course of the war

Using an ultimatum over alleged mistreatment of Italian interests as pretext, Italy declared war in September 1911 and quickly seized the coastal cities of Tripoli, Benghazi, Derna, Tobruk, and Homs. Ottoman regulars and local Senussi and Arab fighters resisted stubbornly in the interior; a battle at Sciara Sciatt in October 1911 saw heavy Italian losses and reprisals against Arab civilians. Italy extended the war in 1912, shelling Beirut, bombarding the Dardanelles forts, and occupying Rhodes and the Dodecanese.

Firsts in warfare

The war saw the first use of aircraft in combat: Italian pilots flew reconnaissance missions from October 1911, and on 1 November 1911 Lieutenant Giulio Gavotti dropped the first aerial bombs on Ottoman positions at Ain Zara. Armored cars and radio were also employed in early roles.

Settlement and consequences

Facing the outbreak of the Balkan Wars, the Ottomans signed the Treaty of Ouchy (also called the First Treaty of Lausanne) on 18 October 1912, withdrawing from Libya. The conflict demonstrated Ottoman weakness, encouraged the Balkan League, and began Italy's colonial rule in Libya, which lasted until the Second World War.
