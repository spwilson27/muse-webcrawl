Experimenter effect

FieldPsychology, research methodology
TypeObserver bias
Key researcherRobert Rosenthal
CountermeasureDouble-blind experimental design
Expectations can be transmitted to participants through subtle, unintended cues.

The experimenter effect, also called the experimenter expectancy effect, refers to the influence that a researcher's expectations can have on the outcome of an experiment, typically through subtle cues transmitted to participants or through biased recording and interpretation of results. It is a form of observer bias and a threat to the internal validity of a study.

Research

Robert Rosenthal systematically studied the phenomenon in the 1960s. In a well-known series of studies, students running maze experiments with rats obtained performance differences depending on whether they had been told their rats were bred to be "maze-bright" or "maze-dull," even though the animals were assigned randomly. Rosenthal's work with Jacobson on teacher expectations (the Pygmalion effect) extended the idea to classrooms. The classic historical illustration is Clever Hans, the horse whose apparent arithmetic ability reflected unintentional cueing by his questioners.

Prevention

The standard safeguard is blinding: in a double-blind design, neither participants nor the experimenters interacting with them know the condition assignments. Automated data collection, standardized scripts, preregistered analysis plans, and independent replication further reduce expectancy effects. See also demand characteristics, placebo, and Hawthorne effect.
