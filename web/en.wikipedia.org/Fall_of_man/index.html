The fall of man, the fall of Adam, or simply the Fall, is a term used in Christianity to describe the transition of the first man and woman from a state of innocent obedience to God to a state of guilty disobedience. The doctrine of the Fall comes from a biblical interpretation of Genesis, chapters 1–3. At first, Adam and Eve lived with God in the Garden of Eden, but a serpent tempted them to eat the fruit from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, which God had forbidden.

The Genesis narrative

According to Genesis 3, the serpent — described as more crafty than any other beast — persuaded Eve that eating the forbidden fruit would make her like God, knowing good and evil, rather than causing death. She ate, and gave some to Adam, who also ate. Their eyes were opened; aware of their nakedness, they made coverings of fig leaves. When God confronted them, Adam blamed Eve and Eve blamed the serpent. God pronounced curses: pain in childbearing, toilsome labour, enmity between the serpent and humankind, and a return to dust at death. The pair were expelled from Eden, whose entrance was guarded by cherubim and a flaming sword, lest they also eat from the tree of life and live forever.

Theological interpretation

In Christian theology the Fall is linked to the doctrine of original sin, developed especially by Augustine of Hippo: all humanity inherits a corrupted nature from Adam's disobedience, requiring salvation through Christ, sometimes framed as a "second Adam". The narrative is traditionally read during Lent and Holy Week, and the doctrine of a fortunate fall (felix culpa) holds that redemption makes the outcome ultimately blessed. Judaism and Islam have related Adam-and-Eve narratives but do not share the doctrine of inherited original sin.

In art and literature

The Fall has been depicted innumerable times in Western art, from Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel ceiling to Masaccio's Expulsion from the Garden of Eden, and retold in Milton's epic Paradise Lost (1667). See also: Adam and Eve, Original sin, Garden of Eden, Tree of the knowledge of good and evil.
