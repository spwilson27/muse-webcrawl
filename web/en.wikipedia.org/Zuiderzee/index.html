The Zuiderzee ("Southern Sea", in contrast to the North Sea) was a shallow bay of the North Sea in the northwest of the Netherlands. For centuries it was one of the country's great fishing and trading waters — and a source of catastrophic floods — until the Zuiderzee Works closed it off in the 20th century.

Formation and history

In Roman times the area held a lake the Romans called Lacus Flevo (Lake Flevo, later the Almere). A series of storm surges, culminating in the St. Lucia's flood of 1287, breached the coastal dunes and merged the lake with the sea, creating the Zuiderzee. Around its shores grew prosperous ports such as Kampen, Enkhuizen, Hoorn, and Amsterdam, and fishing villages — Urk, Marken, Volendam, Spakenburg — famed for herring and eel.

The Zuiderzee Works

After the disastrous flood of 1916, the Netherlands adopted the plan of engineer Cornelis Lely. The centrepiece, the Afsluitdijk (Enclosure Dam), was completed in 1932, separating the Zuiderzee from the Wadden Sea and converting the southern part into the freshwater IJsselmeer. Large areas were then drained as polders — the Noordoostpolder and Flevoland — creating new land, towns such as Zeewolde, and the province of Flevoland.

Legacy

The vanished sea is commemorated by the Zuiderzeemuseum at Enkhuizen, and its former coastline, dialects, and traditional costume remain distinctive features of the towns that once fronted the salt water.
