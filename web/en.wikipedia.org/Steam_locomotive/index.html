Steam locomotive

A steam locomotive is a locomotive that provides the force to move itself and other rail vehicles by means of the expansion of steam. It is fuelled by burning combustible material (usually coal, oil, or, rarely, wood) to heat water in the locomotive's boiler to the point where it becomes gaseous and its volume increases 1,700 times. Functionally, it is a steam engine on wheels.

How it works

Steam generated in the boiler is delivered to cylinders, where it pushes pistons back and forth. The pistons are connected by driving rods to the locomotive's driving wheels, converting reciprocating motion into rotary motion. Exhaust steam escapes up the chimney through a blastpipe, which also draws the fire to burn hotter. Most locomotives carry their water and fuel in a separate tender.

History

The first full-scale working railway steam locomotive was built in 1804 by Richard Trevithick in the United Kingdom. In 1825 George Stephenson built Locomotion No. 1 for the Stockton and Darlington Railway, the first public steam railway, and his Rocket won the Rainhill Trials in 1829. Steam traction powered the railway age of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, growing in size to giants such as the Union Pacific Big Boy of 1941.

Decline

Steam locomotives are inherently inefficient (typically under 10 percent thermal efficiency) and labour-intensive to maintain. From the 1930s they were progressively displaced by diesel and electric traction; regular mainline steam ended in the United States around 1960 and in the United Kingdom in 1968. China was the last country to build mainline steam locomotives, ending production in 1988.

Heritage

Thousands of steam locomotives survive in preservation, operating on heritage railways worldwide, and a few newly built examples — such as Britain's Tornado (completed 2008) — have been constructed to historic designs.
