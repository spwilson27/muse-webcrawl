Soyuz (Russian: Союз, meaning "Union") is a series of spacecraft which has been in service since the 1960s, having made more than 140 flights. It was designed by the Korolev Design Bureau (now RSC Energia) for the Soviet space program and remains in service today, operated by Roscosmos. The Soyuz succeeded the Voskhod spacecraft and was originally built as part of the Soviet crewed lunar programs. It is launched on the similarly named Soyuz rocket from the Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan.

Following the retirement of the American Space Shuttle in 2011, the Soyuz served as the only means to ferry crews to and from the International Space Station (ISS) until the first crewed flight of SpaceX's Crew Dragon in 2020. Although China operates its own crewed spacecraft, the Shenzhou, no other nation offers a competing option.

Design

A Soyuz spacecraft consists of three parts (from front to back):

  • A spheroid orbital module, which provides accommodation for the crew during their mission.
  • A small aerodynamic reentry module, which returns the crew to Earth. It has a heat shield.
  • A cylindrical service module with solar panels attached, which contains the engines and most of the spacecraft's systems.

The orbital and service modules are single-use and are destroyed upon reentry; only the descent module returns intact. This design sacrifices reusability for lower mass. The spacecraft is designed for a crew of three wearing Sokol pressure suits, and seats recline the crew in a knees-up position to better withstand reentry acceleration.

Variants

The spacecraft has evolved through many generations, including the original 7K-OK version, the Soyuz-T, Soyuz-TM, Soyuz-TMA, and the current Soyuz MS series, introduced in 2016. An uncrewed cargo variant, Progress, shares much of the Soyuz design.

History

The first Soyuz flight, Soyuz 1, ended in tragedy when cosmonaut Vladimir Komarov was killed during landing in April 1967. The Soyuz 11 mission in 1971 ended with the loss of its three-man crew when a valve opened during descent, depressurizing the cabin. After these early failures, the design was repeatedly improved, and the Soyuz family became the longest-serving and most reliable crewed spacecraft in history.
