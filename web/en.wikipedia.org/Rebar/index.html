Rebar

Rebar (short for reinforcing bar or reinforcement steel) is a steel bar used as a tension device in reinforced concrete and masonry structures to strengthen and aid the concrete under tension. Concrete is strong under compression but weak under tension; embedding rebar, whose ribs mechanically bond with the concrete, lets the composite material resist bending and tensile stresses. The combined material is called reinforced concrete.

Materials and grades

Rebar is usually made from carbon steel hot-rolled with surface deformations (ribs) for bonding. It is specified by diameter — in the US by bar numbers (#3 to #18, in eighths of an inch) and in metric systems by millimetres — and by grade or yield strength, such as ASTM A615 Grade 60 (420 MPa) or European B500B. For corrosive environments, epoxy-coated, galvanised or stainless-steel rebar is used, and fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) bars are an alternative where steel's magnetic or corrosion properties are problematic.

Use and placement

On site, bars are cut, bent to schedules, and tied with wire into cages or mats, held at the correct cover distance from the formwork by spacers so the steel is protected from corrosion and fire. Welded wire fabric provides light reinforcement in slabs. Rebar must be lapped or mechanically coupled to transfer forces at joints. Because steel and concrete have similar coefficients of thermal expansion, the composite behaves well across temperature changes.

History

Reinforced concrete was pioneered in 19th-century France by Joseph Monier and François Hennebique; steel-bar reinforcement became standard in the early 20th century and now consumes a large share of world steel production.
