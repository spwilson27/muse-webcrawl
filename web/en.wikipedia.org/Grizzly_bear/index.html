Grizzly bear

The grizzly bear (Ursus arctos horribilis) is a large population, or subspecies, of the brown bear inhabiting North America. The name "grizzly" refers to the grizzled, silver-tipped appearance of its fur. Grizzly bears are apex predators and omnivores, and are a symbol of the North American wilderness.

Description

Adult grizzlies typically weigh 180 to 360 kilograms (400 to 790 lb), with males substantially larger than females. They are distinguished from black bears by their concave facial profile, pronounced shoulder hump of muscle used for digging, small rounded ears, and long straight claws. Colour ranges from blond to nearly black, often with pale-tipped guard hairs.

Distribution and habitat

Grizzly bears historically ranged across western North America from Alaska to Mexico. Today their strongholds are Alaska and western Canada; in the contiguous United States, populations persist around Yellowstone and Glacier National Parks and in parts of Montana, Wyoming, Idaho, and Washington. They occupy habitats from coastal rainforest and salmon streams to alpine tundra and prairie.

Diet and behaviour

Grizzlies are omnivorous: they eat grasses, sedges, roots, berries, whitebark pine nuts, insects, fish — especially migrating salmon — and mammals from ground squirrels to moose calves. They accumulate fat through the summer and autumn and hibernate in dens for five to seven months. Females give birth to one to four cubs during hibernation, and cubs stay with their mother for about two years.

Conservation

In the lower 48 states the grizzly is listed as threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. Recovery in the Greater Yellowstone and Northern Continental Divide ecosystems has brought numbers there to several hundred bears each, though the species remains absent from most of its former range. Conflicts with humans over livestock, food storage, and hunting remain central management issues.
