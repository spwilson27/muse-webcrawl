Molecular gastronomy

Molecular gastronomy is the scientific approach to cooking and the study of the physical and chemical transformations of ingredients that occur in cooking. Its program includes three areas, as cooking was recognized to have three components: social, artistic, and technical. Molecular cooking is a subdiscipline of food science that seeks to investigate the physical and chemical transformations of ingredients that occur in cooking.

The term is commonly used to describe a style of cuisine in which chefs explore culinary possibilities by borrowing tools from the science lab and ingredients from the food industry. Formally, the term molecular gastronomy refers to the scientific discipline that studies the physical and chemical processes occurring while cooking.

History

The term "molecular gastronomy" was coined in 1988 by Hungarian physicist Nicholas Kurti and French physical chemist Hervé This. Kurti, a low-temperature physicist at the University of Oxford, had long been interested in applying scientific knowledge to cooking; in 1969 he gave a lecture at the Royal Institution titled "The Physicist in the Kitchen." This, working at INRA in Paris, studied the chemistry of cooking from the early 1980s.

In 1992, a series of international workshops began at Erice, Sicily, bringing together scientists and professional cooks to discuss the science behind traditional cooking preparations. These meetings helped establish molecular gastronomy as a recognized discipline.

Chefs associated with the style include Ferran Adrià of elBulli in Spain, Heston Blumenthal of The Fat Duck in England, and Grant Achatz of Alinea in Chicago, though several of these chefs have distanced themselves from the label.

Objectives

The objectives of molecular gastronomy, as defined by Hervé This, include seeking the mechanisms of culinary transformations and processes in three areas: the chemical, the physical, and the social. More recent goals include studying culinary definitions and "culinary precisions" — traditional sayings, proverbs, and old wives' tales about cooking — and testing them scientifically.

Techniques

Techniques popularized by chefs working in this style include:

  • Spherification — forming liquids into spheres using sodium alginate and calcium salts.
  • Gelification — turning liquids into gels with agents such as agar or gellan gum.
  • Emulsification — creating light foams and airs, often with soy lecithin.
  • Sous-vide cooking — cooking food sealed in plastic at precisely controlled low temperatures.
  • Use of liquid nitrogen — for rapid freezing and dramatic presentation.
  • Powderizing — converting high-fat ingredients into powders using maltodextrin.

Criticism and terminology

Many chefs associated with the movement reject the term "molecular gastronomy," finding it pretentious or misleading, and prefer terms such as "modernist cuisine," "avant-garde cuisine," or "technoemotional cuisine." In a 2006 open letter, Adrià, Blumenthal, Thomas Keller, and Harold McGee stated that their cooking was not defined by the term, though they embraced new technology and science.
