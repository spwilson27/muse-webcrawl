DNA supercoil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

DNA supercoiling refers to the over- or under-winding of a DNA strand, and is an expression of the strain on that strand. Such strain may be due to the helical nature of the double helix itself being twisted beyond its relaxed state, or to the coiling of the helix axis in space. Supercoiling is important for DNA packaging within cells and for regulating access to the genetic code, since underwound DNA is more easily melted.

Terminology and mathematics

The topology of a closed circular DNA molecule is described by the linking number (Lk), which is conserved unless the strand is broken, and which decomposes into twist (Tw) and writhe (Wr): Lk = Tw + Wr. DNA whose linking number is lower than that of the relaxed molecule is said to be negatively supercoiled; the converse is positive supercoiling. The superhelical density (σ) expresses the deviation relative to relaxed DNA; chromosomal DNA in bacteria typically has σ ≈ −0.06.

Forms

Supercoiled DNA adopts two main geometries: the plectonemic form, an extended right-handed helix of the duplex axis typical of long DNA in solution, and the solenoidal (toroidal) form, in which DNA wraps around proteins — as it does around histones in the eukaryotic nucleosome.

Biological roles

Negative supercoiling stores energy that helps drive strand separation in replication initiation, transcription, and recombination. As polymerases advance, positive supercoils accumulate ahead of them and negative supercoils behind (the twin-supercoiled-domain model). Topoisomerase enzymes manage this strain: bacterial DNA gyrase introduces negative supercoils, topoisomerase I relaxes them, and topoisomerase IV decatenates daughter chromosomes.

Measurement

Supercoiling is commonly assayed by agarose gel electrophoresis, in which topoisomers of a plasmid migrate at different rates depending on their degree of supercoiling, or by intercalating dyes such as ethidium bromide and chloroquine that shift the distribution.
