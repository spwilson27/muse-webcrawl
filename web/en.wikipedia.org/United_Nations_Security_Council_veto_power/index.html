United Nations Security Council veto power

The United Nations Security Council veto power is the power of the five permanent members of the UN Security Council — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States (the "P5") — to block ("veto") any substantive resolution. Derived from Article 27 of the UN Charter, which requires the "concurring votes of the permanent members" for non-procedural decisions, it means a single negative vote from a permanent member defeats a draft even if all fourteen other members support it. The word "veto" does not itself appear in the Charter.

Origins

The veto was the price of great-power participation agreed at the Yalta Conference in February 1945 and confirmed at San Francisco: the major Allied powers insisted they could not be bound by enforcement decisions against their will. Smaller states objected but accepted the arrangement as necessary to keep the great powers inside the organisation — a lesson drawn from the League of Nations, which the United States never joined.

Use

The Soviet Union (and later Russia) has cast the most vetoes, followed by the United States, which has frequently vetoed resolutions critical of Israel; China has used the veto increasingly, often jointly with Russia, notably over Syria; the United Kingdom and France have not vetoed since 1989. Custom has softened the rule in three ways: abstention does not count as a veto; absence does not block action (which allowed the Korean War authorisations of 1950 during a Soviet boycott); and the veto does not apply to procedural matters, though the "double veto" question — whether deciding if a matter is procedural is itself vetoable — was settled in practice against the great powers.

Criticism and reform

Critics call the veto anachronistic and a cause of paralysis, as in Syria and Ukraine, where permanent members blocked action on conflicts in which they were involved. Reform proposals include enlarging the Council, voluntary veto restraint in mass-atrocity situations (the French–Mexican initiative and the ACT Group code of conduct), and the 2022 General Assembly "veto initiative" (Resolution 76/262), which requires the Assembly to meet whenever a veto is cast. The General Assembly's "Uniting for Peace" procedure provides a non-binding route around a deadlocked Council.
