Browning (cooking)

Browning meat develops flavour and colour. (Image placeholder)

Browning is the process of partially cooking the surface of food — usually meat — in order to develop flavour and colour, typically before it is finished in a mixed method such as braising or stewing. The brown colour and much of the flavour come from chemical reactions on the food's surface at high temperature, principally the Maillard reaction between amino acids and reducing sugars, together with caramelisation of sugars and the breakdown of fats.

Technique

Browning is usually done in a hot pan with a small amount of oil or fat, or under a grill or broiler. For good browning the surface of the food should be dry, the pan properly preheated, and the food left undisturbed long enough for a crust to form; overcrowding the pan lowers the temperature and causes the food to steam instead. Ground meat is often browned by cooking it until its water evaporates and it begins to sizzle in its own fat. Vegetables such as onions also brown, becoming sweeter as their sugars caramelise.

Chemistry and health

The Maillard reaction, named after Louis-Camille Maillard, generates hundreds of flavour compounds and brown pigments called melanoidins. At very high temperatures, browning starchy foods can also form acrylamide, a compound monitored by food-safety authorities; heavily charred meat forms heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, which is why moderation of extreme charring is advised.
