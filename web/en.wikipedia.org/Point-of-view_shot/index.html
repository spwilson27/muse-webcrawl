Point-of-view shot

A point-of-view shot: the camera becomes the character's eyes

A point-of-view shot (often abbreviated POV shot) is a film shot that shows exactly what a character sees, by placing the camera at the character's position and aligning its angle with their gaze. The POV shot is usually edited in a sequence: a shot of the character looking at something (often glancing off-camera), followed by the POV shot of what they see, and frequently a reaction shot. This structure, part of the grammar of continuity editing, lets the audience share the character's perception and is a principal mechanism of identification with characters in classical cinema.

Use in film

POV shots are used across genres. In horror and thriller films, the camera frequently adopts the viewpoint of a stalking killer — famously in the opening of Halloween (1978) — or of a victim perceiving a threat. In the "shark's-eye view" of Jaws (1975), underwater POV shots make the unseen predator present. Alfred Hitchcock refined subjective camerawork in films such as Rear Window (1954), told almost entirely from the protagonist's apartment-bound viewpoint, and Vertigo (1958), which visualised the hero's acrophobia with the dolly zoom.

Some films sustain the device for their entire length. The 1947 noir Lady in the Lake was shot almost wholly from the detective's point of view; the 2015 action film Hardcore Henry was filmed entirely in first person on wearable cameras, imitating the perspective of first-person shooter video games. Such sustained use remains rare because it restricts the audience's view of the protagonist and complicates staging.

Related techniques

The POV shot is distinct from the subjective shot, in which the camera imitates a character's mental state — blurred vision, dreams, intoxication — and from the "free indirect" point of view identified by Pier Paolo Pasolini, in which the director's style colours a nominally objective shot. Eyeline matching and shot/reverse-shot editing normally establish and maintain POV relationships in dialogue scenes. In video games, first-person perspective is the direct interactive analogue of the POV shot, and sports broadcasting uses helmet- and body-mounted cameras to similar effect.
