Hilbert's problems

Hilbert's problems are a set of twenty-three mathematical problems presented by the German mathematician David Hilbert in 1900 at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris. Framed as an agenda for twentieth-century mathematics, the problems influenced the direction of mathematical research for decades; some were solved quickly, others remain open, and a few were shown to be undecidable or too vague to admit a definite solution.

Selected problems

The first problem concerned the continuum hypothesis, later shown by Gödel and Cohen to be independent of the standard axioms of set theory. The second asked for a proof of the consistency of arithmetic, a goal undermined by Gödel's incompleteness theorems. The third, on the equal-area dissection of polyhedra, was solved within the year by Hilbert's student Max Dehn. The tenth problem, asking for an algorithm to determine whether a Diophantine equation has integer solutions, was answered negatively by Yuri Matiyasevich in 1970, completing work by Davis, Putnam and Robinson.

Legacy

Notable still-open problems include the eighth, which encompasses the Riemann hypothesis, and the sixteenth, on the topology of algebraic curves. The problems are widely regarded as a landmark in the history of mathematics, and later collections — such as the Millennium Prize Problems of 2000 — consciously echo Hilbert's example.
