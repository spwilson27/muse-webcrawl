Chaos (cosmogony)


Chaos (Greek: Χάος), the primordial void in Greek cosmogony.

Chaos (Ancient Greek: χάος, kháos) is the primordial state of existence in Greek mythology from which the first gods emerged. In Hesiod's Theogony, Chaos is the first entity to come into being, followed by Gaia (Earth), Tartarus and Eros. The word derives from a Greek verb meaning "to gape open", and in Hesiod it likely denotes a dark, yawning void or chasm rather than disorder in the modern sense.

In Hesiod

The Theogony opens its cosmogony with the line "In truth at first Chaos came to be". From Chaos sprang Erebus (Darkness) and Nyx (Night), who in turn produced Aether and Hemera (Day). Chaos is sometimes described as the lower atmosphere or the gap between heaven and earth.

Later interpretations

The Roman poet Ovid, in the Metamorphoses, reimagined Chaos as a formless, unordered mass of conflicting elements — "a crude, undeveloped mass" — and it is largely through Ovid that "chaos" acquired its modern meaning of complete disorder. Orphic cosmogonies instead placed a cosmic egg or the god Chronos at the origin.

Comparisons

Similar primordial voids or waters appear in other traditions, such as the Babylonian Apsu and Tiamat, the Norse Ginnungagap and the unformed earth and darkness over the deep in Genesis. In modern usage the concept has been borrowed, often loosely, by chaos theory in mathematics and physics.
