Ripe plums. Plums are stone fruits in the genus Prunus.

A plum is a fruit of some species in Prunus subgenus Prunus. The fruit is a drupe with smooth, often waxy skin, juicy flesh and a single hard stone. Mature plum fruit may have a dusty-white waxy coating, called "wax bloom", that gives them a glaucous appearance. Dried plums are called prunes. Plums are among the most widely cultivated temperate fruits, ranking behind only apples among deciduous tree fruits.

Species and history

The European plum, Prunus domestica, is a hexaploid believed to derive from hybridisation involving the cherry plum (P. cerasifera) and sloe (P. spinosa); it spread from the Caucasus region and was cultivated by the Romans. East Asia developed the Japanese or Chinese plum, P. salicina. China is by far the world's largest producer, growing more than half the global crop of roughly 12 million tonnes annually. Plum remains found in archaeological sites indicate human use since Neolithic times.

Cultivation and uses

Plum trees are small to medium deciduous trees flowering in early spring, frequently requiring cross-pollination. Fruits ripen from green through yellow and red to deep purple-black depending on variety; well-known cultivars include 'Victoria', 'Santa Rosa', greengages, mirabelles and damsons. Plums are eaten fresh, dried as prunes, canned, made into jams, juices and wines; they are fermented and distilled into slivovica in Central and Eastern Europe and umeshu (from the related P. mume) in Japan. The kernel contains amygdalin and the fruit's sorbitol content explains the prune's laxative effect.

Nutrition

Fresh plums are a good source of vitamin C, vitamin K and dietary fibre with modest calories; prunes are richer in fibre, vitamin K and boron but also more concentrated in sugars.
