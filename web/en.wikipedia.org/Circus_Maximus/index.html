The Circus Maximus (Latin for "largest circus") was an ancient Roman chariot-racing stadium and mass entertainment venue in Rome. Situated in the valley between the Aventine and Palatine hills, it was the first and largest stadium in ancient Rome, measuring 621 m (2,037 ft) in length and 118 m (387 ft) in width and accommodating, by modern estimates, around 150,000 spectators.

Races were run around a central barrier, the spina, decorated with statues and two Egyptian obelisks, and marked at each end by turning posts (metae). Chariots — most famously the four-horse quadrigae of the Red, White, Blue and Green factions — completed seven laps, counted by large wooden eggs and later bronze dolphins. Starting gates (carceres) at the Forum Boarium end released up to twelve chariots at once.

History

Racing on the site was traditionally dated to Rome's kings, and Julius Caesar enlarged the circus around 50 BC. After fires, Augustus and later Trajan rebuilt it in stone and brick-faced concrete, reaching its final form in AD 103. The last recorded chariot races were held in AD 549 under the Ostrogothic king Totila. The site was later quarried, farmed and built over; today it survives as a large public park retaining the track's outline, with excavated remains at its curved south-eastern end.
