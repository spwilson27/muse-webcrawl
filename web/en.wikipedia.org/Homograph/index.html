Homograph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A homograph (from the Greek homós, "same" and grápho, "write") is a word that shares the same written form as another word but has a different meaning. When spoken aloud, homographs may also be homophones — pronounced the same — or heteronyms, pronounced differently. Examples in English include bear (the animal) and bear (to carry), pronounced alike, and lead (the metal, pronounced "led") and lead (to guide, pronounced "leed"), pronounced differently.

Related concepts

Homographs belong to a family of terms describing relations between word forms. Homonyms, in the strict sense, are words spelled and pronounced the same but differing in meaning (both homographs and homophones). Homophones sound alike but may be spelled differently (to, too, two). Homographs in a broader usage include words that are merely spelled the same by coincidence — whether their sameness results from polysemy, where meanings diverged from a common origin, or from true etymological accident, as with bat (the mammal) and bat (the sporting implement).

Examples and complications

English is rich in homographs because of its layered vocabulary and relatively conservative spelling: bow (weapon), bow (bend at the waist), bow (front of a ship); wind (moving air) and wind (to coil); desert (arid land) and desert (to abandon). Some apparent homographs depend on capitalisation — polish and Polish — and on word class, as in record the noun versus record the verb, which differ in stress. In logographic scripts such as Chinese, homography is pervasive, since many characters share readings; in Japanese, kanji with multiple on and kun readings create numerous homographic forms. Homographs pose practical problems for text-to-speech synthesis, dictionary design, and machine translation, all of which must resolve the intended sense from context.
