Coronal mass ejection

Coronal mass ejection
TypeSolar phenomenon; plasma eruption
Typical mass~1012 kg of coronal material
Typical speed250–3,000 km/s
EffectsGeomagnetic storms, aurorae, satellite and grid disruption

A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a significant ejection of plasma mass from the Sun's corona into the heliosphere. CMEs are often associated with solar flares and other forms of solar activity, but a broadly accepted theoretical understanding of these relationships has not been established. CMEs release large quantities of matter and electromagnetic radiation into space; if directed at Earth, the arriving plasma and its embedded magnetic field can drive geomagnetic storms.

Characteristics

Observed with white-light coronagraphs such as those aboard SOHO/LASCO, a typical CME has a three-part structure: a bright leading edge, a dark cavity, and a bright core (often an erupting prominence). Ejected speeds range from about 250 to 3,000 km/s, and the average mass is on the order of 1.6 × 1012 kg. Near solar maximum several CMEs occur per day; near minimum, roughly one every few days. When the CME's magnetic field is oriented southward on arrival at Earth, it reconnects efficiently with the magnetosphere, producing strong storms.

Historical events and impacts

The largest recorded geomagnetic storm, the Carrington Event of 1859, was caused by a powerful CME and disrupted telegraph systems worldwide. A comparable CME in July 2012 missed Earth, an event estimated to have had the potential to cause trillions of dollars of damage to satellites, power grids, and communications.
