Battle of Los Angeles

The Battle of Los Angeles, also known as the Great Los Angeles Air Raid, was an anti-aircraft artillery barrage fired over Los Angeles, California, on the night of 24–25 February 1942, directed at a supposed enemy air attack that almost certainly never existed. The incident, coming weeks after the attack on Pearl Harbor and a day after a Japanese submarine shelled the Ellwood oil field near Santa Barbara, remains a famous case of wartime jitteriness.

The incident

In the early hours of 25 February, radar picked up an unidentified contact approaching the coast, air-raid sirens sounded across Los Angeles County, and a total blackout was ordered. From about 3:16 a.m., anti-aircraft batteries fired over 1,400 shells at reported aircraft illuminated by searchlights. Newspapers described objects moving slowly over the city; witnesses variously reported planes, balloons or nothing at all.

Casualties and damage

No enemy aircraft were downed and no bombs were dropped, but the barrage itself caused real harm: several people died in traffic accidents and of heart attacks during the blackout, and buildings were damaged by falling shells and unexploded ordnance. The incident made front pages across the United States.

Explanations

Secretary of the Navy Frank Knox initially called it a false alarm caused by "war nerves," while Army statements suggested real aircraft might have been involved. The most likely cause is a stray meteorological balloon that triggered the initial alert, after which shell bursts and searchlight beams were mistaken for enemy planes. The event has since become a staple of UFO lore and inspired the film 1941.
