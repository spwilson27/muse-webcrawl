William I (1120/21–1166), called William the Bad, was the fourth Hauteville king of Sicily, ruling from 1154 to his death. The younger son of Roger II, he succeeded to a rich, centralised, multilingual kingdom encompassing Sicily and southern Italy, founded by his family after the Norman conquests of the County of Apulia and Calabria.

Reign

William's reign was dominated by his chief minister, Maio of Bari, the "emir of emirs", whose low birth, power and supposed ambitions enraged the baronage. William faced simultaneous pressure from the Byzantine emperor Manuel I Komnenos (grandson of Alexios I), who invaded Apulia with the support of rebellious barons in 1155, and from Pope Adrian IV, who recognised the rebels. William recovered dramatically: his victory over the Byzantines at Brindisi (1156) and the Treaty of Benevento with the papacy confirmed his kingdom and ended Byzantine ambitions in Italy. Meanwhile his African possessions (Mahdia and the Ifriqiyan coast) were lost to the Almohads by 1160.

Revolt and later years

In 1160 Maio was assassinated by the baron Matthew Bonello; the following year conspirators briefly seized the palace and the king himself, murdered his young son Roger, and massacred Muslim courtiers before William regained control and punished the rebels savagely. Later tradition, shaped by hostile chroniclers such as "Hugo Falcandus", gave him his epithet "the Bad" and the reputation of an indolent, pleasure-loving tyrant; modern historians judge him more moderately, noting the survival of Roger II's administration and his patronage — al-Idrisi continued his geographic work under William, and the king began the pleasure palace of La Zisa in Palermo. He died in 1166, leaving the throne to his young son William II "the Good" under the regency of Margaret of Navarre.
