Near-death experience

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A near-death experience (NDE) is a profound personal experience associated with death or impending death, reported by people who have survived a life-threatening episode. Researchers describe common elements including feelings of peace, detachment from the body (an out-of-body experience), entering darkness, moving through a tunnel, encountering a bright light, meeting deceased persons or beings, a panoramic life review, and reaching a point of no return.

Research

The term was popularized by psychiatrist Raymond Moody's 1975 book Life After Life. Bruce Greyson developed the Greyson NDE Scale to measure depth of the experience, and Kenneth Ring proposed a five-stage model. Prospective studies of cardiac-arrest survivors, such as the Dutch study led by Pim van Lommel published in The Lancet (2001) and the AWARE study led by Sam Parnia, found that a minority of survivors report NDEs; the AWARE II study examined awareness during resuscitation.

Explanations

Explanatory models range from spiritual interpretations—evidence of an afterlife—to neuroscientific accounts invoking cerebral hypoxia, temporal-lobe activity, REM-sleep intrusion, endorphin and NMDA-receptor effects (the ketamine model), and the physiology of the dying brain. No single model accounts for all reported features, and the topic remains contested.

Aftereffects

Researchers report lasting changes in many experiencers, including reduced fear of death, increased spirituality and compassion, and changed life priorities; some also report difficulties readjusting, including strained relationships.
