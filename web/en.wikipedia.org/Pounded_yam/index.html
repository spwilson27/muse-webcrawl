Pounded yam is a staple food in West Africa, particularly in Nigeria, Ghana, Togo, Benin, and Côte d'Ivoire. It is made by boiling yams and pounding them into a smooth, elastic dough-like consistency. The dish is traditionally prepared using a large wooden mortar and pestle, a physically demanding process that gives the food its distinctive stretchy texture.

Preparation

Peeled white yam tubers are cut into pieces and boiled until soft. The hot pieces are then pounded in a mortar, with small amounts of the cooking water added, until the mixture becomes smooth, lump-free, and elastic. The finished pounded yam is shaped into balls or mounds and served immediately, since it hardens as it cools. Modern shortcuts include yam flour mixes and electric food processors, though purists maintain that mortar-pounded yam is superior.

Serving

Pounded yam is a "swallow" food: diners pinch off a small piece, roll it into a ball, make an indentation with the thumb, and use it to scoop up soup. Classic accompaniments in Nigeria include egusi soup, ogbono soup, efo riro (vegetable soup), and okra soup. Among the Yoruba it is closely associated with festive and ceremonial meals; among the Igbo, a closely related dish is akpu, made from fermented cassava (fufu).

Cultural significance

Yam holds deep cultural significance in West Africa, celebrated in festivals such as the New Yam Festival (Iwa ji) among the Igbo. Pounded yam's labor-intensive preparation has traditionally made it a food of hospitality and celebration, offered to honored guests.
