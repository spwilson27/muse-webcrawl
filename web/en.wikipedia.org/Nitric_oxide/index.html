Nitric oxide

Nitric oxide (nitrogen oxide or nitrogen monoxide) is a chemical compound with the formula NO. It is a colourless gas and a free radical, with an unpaired electron. In the atmosphere it is an air pollutant generated by combustion, but in mammals — including humans — it is an important cell signalling molecule involved in many physiological and pathological processes.

Biological role

Nitric oxide is produced from the amino acid L-arginine by enzymes called nitric oxide synthases (NOS). In blood vessels, NO released by endothelial cells diffuses into smooth muscle and causes vasodilation, regulating blood pressure; this discovery, which explained the action of nitroglycerin, led to the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for Robert Furchgott, Louis Ignarro, and Ferid Murad. NO also functions as a neurotransmitter and as a weapon of the immune system, being produced in quantity by macrophages.

Medicine and environment

Inhaled nitric oxide is used to treat persistent pulmonary hypertension in newborns, and drugs such as sildenafil (Viagra) act by amplifying NO signalling. In the environment, NO rapidly oxidises to nitrogen dioxide and participates in the formation of acid rain and photochemical smog; it is therefore regulated as a component of NOx emissions.
