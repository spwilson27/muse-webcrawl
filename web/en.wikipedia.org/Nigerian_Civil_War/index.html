The Nigerian Civil War (6 July 1967 – 15 January 1970), also known as the Biafran War, was fought between Nigeria and the Republic of Biafra, a secessionist state which had declared its independence from Nigeria in May 1967. The war followed the 1966 military coups and counter-coup and massacres of Igbo people in northern Nigeria, which drove hundreds of thousands of Igbo to flee to their homeland in the east, governed by Lieutenant Colonel Odumegwu Ojukwu.

Course of the war

Federal forces under General Yakubu Gowon quickly captured Biafra's coastal ports and the oil installations of the Niger Delta, while Biafra briefly advanced towards Lagos before being thrown back. Encircled and blockaded, Biafra shrank steadily. The federal blockade produced a catastrophic famine: images of starving children with kwashiorkor moved world opinion and inspired one of the largest civilian airlifts in history, as church groups and aid agencies flew relief into the enclave by night. The crisis spurred the founding of Médecins Sans Frontières.

Outcome and legacy

Estimates of the dead range from one to three million, the majority civilians who died of hunger and disease. In January 1970 Biafra's leaders fled or surrendered, and Gowon proclaimed a policy of "no victor, no vanquished", followed by a programme of reconciliation, reconstruction and rehabilitation — though the promised reintegration was imperfect and the war's memory remains a live issue in Nigerian politics.
