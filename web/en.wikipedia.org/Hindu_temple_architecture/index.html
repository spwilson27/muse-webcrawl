Hindu temple architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The gopuram of a South Indian temple

Hindu temple architecture as the main form of Hindu architecture has many varieties of style, though the basic nature of the Hindu temple remains the same, with the essential feature being an inner sanctum, the garbhagriha or womb-chamber, where the primary deity is housed in a simple bare cell. Hindu temple architecture is governed by the ancient Vāstuśāstra texts and reflects ideas of dharma, the cosmic order, and sacred geometry centred on the vastu-puruṣa-maṇḍala.

Basic elements

A typical temple consists of the garbhagriha (sanctum), a hall (maṇḍapa) where devotees gather, a porch or vestibule (antarāla), and a tower over the sanctum. The plan is based on a square grid symbolising the universe, and the temple is conceived as the house and body of the deity and a meeting place of gods and humans.

Nagara and Dravida styles

Two great classical traditions emerged. The northern Nagara style is characterised by the curvilinear śikhara, a beehive-shaped tower rising over the sanctum, as at Khajuraho and Konark. The southern Dravida style features a stepped pyramidal tower called the vimāna over the sanctum and monumental gateway towers (gopurams) covered in sculpture, as at Madurai and Srirangam. A third, Deccan tradition, Vesara, blends features of both.

History

Surviving structural temples begin in the Gupta period (4th–6th centuries CE); earlier shrines were probably built of perishable materials, and rock-cut temples developed alongside. Temple building flourished under dynasties such as the Pallavas, Cholas, Chalukyas and Vijayanagara, and the idiom spread with Hinduism to Southeast Asia, producing monuments such as Angkor Wat in Cambodia and Prambanan in Java.
