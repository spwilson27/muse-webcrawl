Group homomorphism

In mathematics, given two groups (G, ∗) and (H, ·), a group homomorphism from G to H is a function h : G → H such that for all u and v in G it holds that h(u ∗ v) = h(u) · h(v). A bijective homomorphism is called an isomorphism; a homomorphism from a group to itself is an endomorphism, and a bijective endomorphism an automorphism. Homomorphisms are the structure-preserving maps of group theory, analogous to linear maps between vector spaces.

Properties

Every homomorphism maps the identity element of G to the identity element of H, and maps inverses to inverses: h(u−1) = h(u)−1. The kernel of h is the set of elements of G mapping to the identity of H; it is always a normal subgroup of G, and h is injective if and only if its kernel is trivial. The image of h is a subgroup of H.

Isomorphism theorems

The first isomorphism theorem states that the quotient group G/ker(h) is isomorphic to the image of h. Further theorems describe correspondences between subgroups of G containing the kernel and subgroups of the image, and the structure of double quotients. These results make homomorphisms a principal tool for classifying and decomposing groups.

Examples

The exponential map from the additive group of real numbers to the multiplicative group of non-zero reals, x ↦ ex, is an isomorphism onto the positive reals. The sign of a permutation defines a homomorphism from the symmetric group onto {±1} whose kernel is the alternating group. The determinant is a homomorphism from the general linear group to the multiplicative group of the field.
