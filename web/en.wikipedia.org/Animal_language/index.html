Animal language

Animal language refers to communication systems used by non-human animals and to research into whether such systems share the defining properties of human language. Studies range from natural systems — the honeybee waggle dance, vervet monkey alarm calls, whale song — to experiments teaching apes, parrots, and dolphins elements of human-designed languages.

Natural communication

Karl von Frisch showed that honeybees encode the distance and direction of food in the waggle dance, an example of displaced reference. Vervet monkeys give acoustically distinct alarm calls for leopards, eagles, and snakes, eliciting appropriate escape responses. Prairie dogs and chickens likewise have functionally referential calls, and birdsong displays learned, hierarchical structure.

Language-trained animals

Famous subjects include the chimpanzees Washoe and Nim Chimpsky, taught American Sign Language; the bonobo Kanzi, who acquired lexigram symbols and some comprehension of spoken English; the grey parrot Alex, studied by Irene Pepperberg, who labelled objects, colours, and quantities; and dolphins trained on artificial gestural and acoustic languages by Louis Herman. Critics, notably Herbert Terrace's analysis of Nim, argued that ape signing reflects imitation and cueing rather than grammar, sparking enduring debate.

Design features

Charles Hockett's design features — including productivity, displacement, duality of patterning, and recursion — provide criteria for comparison. Most researchers conclude that no animal system exhibits the full open-ended syntax of human language, though many show semanticity, learning, and cultural transmission, notably in cetaceans and songbirds.
