Wide-angle lens

In photography and cinematography, a wide-angle lens is a lens whose focal length is substantially smaller than the focal length of a normal lens for a given film plane. This allows the photographer or cinematographer to include more of the scene in the photograph and emphasizes the difference in size or distance between objects in the foreground and the background.

Characteristics

For 35 mm film, a normal lens has a focal length of about 50 mm; lenses of 35 mm and shorter are generally considered wide-angle, and those of 24 mm and shorter are often called ultra-wide-angle. Wide-angle lenses produce strong perspective distortion at close range: near objects appear disproportionately large and straight lines near the frame edges may bow, especially in fisheye lenses, which can achieve angles of view approaching or exceeding 180 degrees.

Uses

Wide-angle lenses are favoured in landscape, architecture, and interior photography, where space is limited and a broad field of view is desirable. In photojournalism they allow the subject to be shown in context. Cinematographers use them to exaggerate movement toward the camera and to create a sense of depth in confined sets.

Design

For single-lens reflex cameras, whose mirror box requires clearance behind the lens, wide-angle lenses use a retrofocus design in which the rear elements sit farther from the focal plane than the effective focal length would suggest. Rangefinder and mirrorless cameras avoid this constraint and permit more compact symmetric designs.
