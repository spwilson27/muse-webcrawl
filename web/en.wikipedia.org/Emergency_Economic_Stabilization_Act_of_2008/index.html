The Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008, commonly known as the bailout of the U.S. financial system, is a law enacted in response to the subprime mortgage crisis. It authorized the Secretary of the Treasury to spend up to $700 billion to purchase distressed assets, especially mortgage-backed securities, and supply cash directly to banks through the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP).

Background and passage

Following the bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers on 15 September 2008 and the near-collapse of insurer AIG, credit markets seized up and stock markets fell sharply. Treasury Secretary Henry Paulson proposed a three-page plan for sweeping authority to buy troubled assets. After days of negotiation, the House of Representatives rejected an initial bill on 29 September 2008, sending the Dow Jones Industrial Average down 777 points in its largest single-day point drop to that date. The Senate passed a revised version with added provisions — including an increase in FDIC deposit insurance from $100,000 to $250,000 per depositor — and the House approved it on 3 October, when President George W. Bush signed it the same day.

Implementation

The Treasury, advised by the Federal Reserve, soon shifted emphasis from buying toxic assets to recapitalizing banks directly. Under the Capital Purchase Program, the government bought preferred shares in hundreds of institutions, including $25 billion each for the largest banks. Funds later supported AIG, the automobile industry (General Motors and Chrysler), and foreclosure-mitigation efforts. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 reduced TARP's authority to $475 billion. Most bank investments were repaid with interest, and the Congressional Budget Office has estimated the net lifetime cost of TARP at roughly $30 billion, concentrated in the auto and housing programs.

Reaction

The bailout was fiercely controversial across the political spectrum, condemned as corporate welfare by some and defended by officials, including Federal Reserve chairman Ben Bernanke, as necessary to prevent a second Great Depression. It helped fuel both the Tea Party movement and, later, Occupy Wall Street, and remains a central case study in debates about moral hazard and "too big to fail".
