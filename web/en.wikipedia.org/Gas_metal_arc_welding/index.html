Gas metal arc welding

Gas metal arc welding (GMAW), commonly known as MIG welding (metal inert gas), is a welding process in which an electric arc forms between a consumable wire electrode and the workpiece, heating and melting the metals so they join. A shielding gas fed through the welding gun protects the weld pool from atmospheric contamination.

Process

The wire is fed continuously at a controlled speed from a spool, while a power source maintains the arc. Depending on voltage, wire speed, and inductance, metal transfers from wire to workpiece in one of several modes: short-circuiting, globular, spray, or pulsed-spray transfer. Shielding gases include argon, carbon dioxide, and mixtures of the two; for aluminium and stainless steel, argon-rich gases predominate.

Equipment

A basic GMAW setup comprises a constant-voltage power source, a wire feeder, a welding gun, a shielding-gas supply, and a work clamp. The process is semi-automatic or fully automatic and is widely used in manufacturing and construction because of its high deposition rate and relatively easy learning curve.

Applications and limitations

GMAW welds carbon steel, stainless steel, aluminium, and other alloys in all positions. It is less suited to windy outdoor work, where the gas shield can be blown away, and it produces little slag, so it may offer less protection on dirty or rusty metal than flux-based processes. Welders must protect against ultraviolet radiation, fumes, and electric shock.
