Sand

DefinitionGranular material, grain size 0.063–2 mm
Commonest componentQuartz (silica, SiO2)
FormationWeathering and erosion of rock
UsesConcrete, glass, mortar, moulding, sandblasting
ScaleWentworth scale; ISO 14688

Sand is a granular material composed of finely divided rock and mineral particles. By definition its grains are larger than silt and smaller than gravel: geologists using the Wentworth scale class sand grains as 0.0625 to 2 millimetres in diameter. Sand is a non-renewable resource on human timescales, and it is one of the most consumed raw materials on Earth, second only to water.

Composition

The most common constituent of sand in inland continental and non-tropical coastal settings is silica in the form of the mineral quartz, which is abundant and highly resistant to weathering. Other sands differ strikingly: white tropical beach sands are often gypsum or ground coral; the green sands of Hawaii contain olivine; black sands consist of magnetite and other heavy minerals; and the pink sands of Bermuda derive their colour from foraminifera shells. Biogenic sands, formed from shells, coral and skeletons, dominate many tropical coasts.

Formation and transport

Sand is produced by the weathering and erosion of bedrock, and is transported by water and wind. Rivers carry eroded grains downstream, rounding them as they go; waves sort beach sand by size; and in deserts, wind-blown grains accumulate into dunes. Desert sand grains are typically well rounded and frosted by constant collisions. Given burial, heat and pressure over geological time, sand lithifies into sandstone.

Uses

Sand is indispensable to construction: it is the fine aggregate in concrete and asphalt, an ingredient of mortar and plaster, and the basis of foundations and roadbeds. Melted silica sand yields glass. Foundries cast metal in sand moulds; engineers use sand for filtration, for abrasive blasting, and for hydraulic fracturing. Because river and marine sand have suitably rounded, graded grains, the construction industry consumes an estimated 40–50 billion tonnes of sand and gravel per year, and demand has driven extensive legal and illegal mining that erodes coastlines and riverbeds — prompting concern about a global "sand crisis". Sharp manufactured sand produced by crushing rock is increasingly used as a substitute. Desert sand, smoothed by wind, is generally too rounded to bind well in concrete.

In culture

The immeasurability of sand grains made them a classic image of vast number — Archimedes' treatise The Sand Reckoner estimated the grains needed to fill the universe — and the hourglass, in which sand measures time, became an emblem of mortality. Singing sands, which emit sound when disturbed, occur on certain beaches and dunes.
