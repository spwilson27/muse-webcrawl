The Bakken Formation is a rock unit from the Late Devonian to Early Mississippian age occupying about 200,000 square miles (520,000 km²) of the subsurface of the Williston Basin, underlying parts of Montana, North Dakota, Saskatchewan and Manitoba. It consists of three members: an upper shale, a middle siltstone/dolomite, and a lower shale. The Bakken is one of the largest continuous tight oil accumulations in the world and the focus of the North Dakota oil boom of the 2000s–2010s.

Geology

The Bakken's two organic-rich black shale members are the source rocks; the fractured middle member acts as the reservoir. Because the rock has very low porosity and permeability, the oil became economically producible only with the combination of long horizontal wells and hydraulic fracturing, first demonstrated commercially in the Elm Coulee field in Montana in 2000 and then applied widely in North Dakota from about 2006.

Production and impact

USGS assessments have estimated billions of barrels of technically recoverable oil; the 2013 assessment put the mean at about 7.4 billion barrels for the Bakken and underlying Three Forks Formation. North Dakota's production surpassed one million barrels per day in 2014, transforming towns such as Williston and Watford City, spurring debates over infrastructure, flaring of associated gas, housing, and environmental effects including wastewater spills and increased seismic and road hazards. Oil from the Bakken is transported by pipeline and rail, the latter becoming controversial after the 2013 Lac-Mégantic rail disaster involving Bakken crude.
