Subdominant

In music theory, the subdominant is the fourth scale degree of the diatonic scale, and the chord built upon it. It is so called because it is the same distance below the tonic as the dominant is above it — that is, a perfect fifth below (or a perfect fourth above) the tonic. In Roman numeral analysis the subdominant chord is indicated by IV in a major key and by iv in a minor key.

Function

The subdominant has a predominant (or "pre-dominant") function: it typically prepares or leads toward the dominant chord. The progression I–IV–V–I is among the most common in Western music. The subdominant chord also appears in the plagal cadence (IV–I), sometimes called the "Amen cadence" because of its use at the ends of hymns.

Related chords

Chords sharing the subdominant function include the supertonic chord (ii) and the subdominant seventh. In minor keys the subdominant is usually minor (iv), though the raised sixth degree in melodic minor can produce a major IV. The Neapolitan chord is a major chord built on the flattened supertonic that likewise serves a predominant role.
