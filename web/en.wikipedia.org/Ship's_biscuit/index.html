Ship's biscuit

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: a hardtack biscuit from the age of sail]

A ship's biscuit (also called hardtack, sea biscuit, or pilot bread) is a simple type of cracker or biscuit made from flour, water, and sometimes salt. Inexpensive, dry, and long-lasting, it was once used for sustenance on long sea voyages, land expeditions, and military campaigns, in the absence of perishable foods or as a cheap substitute.

Ship's biscuits were baked up to four times to drive out moisture, and could remain edible for years if kept dry. Sailors often had to soak them in water, brine, tea, or coffee before eating, and they were notorious for infestation by weevils. A Royal Navy victualling yard at Gosport, Royal Clarence Yard, produced biscuits on an industrial scale in the 19th century.

In other contexts

During the American Civil War, hardtack formed a staple of Union and Confederate rations and was the subject of the soldiers' song "Hardtack Come Again No More". A biscuit from Scott's Antarctic expedition and other historic voyages survive in museum collections, including the oldest known ship's biscuit from 1852 held in Denmark.

The modern descendants include commercial pilot bread sold in Alaska and Hawaii, and various crispbreads.
