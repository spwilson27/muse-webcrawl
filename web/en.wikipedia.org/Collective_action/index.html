Collective action

Collective action refers to action taken together by a group of people whose goal is to achieve a common objective, such as providing a public good, changing a policy, or defending shared interests. The study of collective action asks how groups overcome the temptation for individuals to "free ride" on the efforts of others.

The collective action problem

In The Logic of Collective Action (1965), Mancur Olson argued that large groups with diffuse interests struggle to organize because each member's contribution is small and non-contributors cannot be excluded from the benefit. Small, concentrated groups — or groups offering selective incentives — organize more easily.

Solutions and research

Elinor Ostrom showed that communities often govern common-pool resources successfully through local rules, monitoring, and graduated sanctions, work that earned her the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences. Other mechanisms include repeated interaction, social norms, leadership, and institutions that lower coordination costs. Digital communication has dramatically reduced those costs, enabling new forms of mobilization.

Examples

Trade unions, social movements, open-source software communities, and crowdfunding campaigns are all forms of collective action. The concept is foundational in political science, sociology, and economics.
