Synapse

Neurotransmitter molecules cross the synaptic cleft to signal the next neuron.

In the nervous system, a synapse is a structure that permits a neuron (or nerve cell) to pass an electrical or chemical signal to another neuron or to a target effector cell. Synapses are essential to the transmission of nervous impulses from one neuron to another, and the human brain contains on the order of 100 trillion of them.

Chemical synapses

At a chemical synapse, an action potential triggers the presynaptic terminal to release neurotransmitter molecules from synaptic vesicles into the synaptic cleft. The molecules bind to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, exciting or inhibiting the target cell. Common neurotransmitters include glutamate, GABA, acetylcholine, dopamine, and serotonin.

Electrical synapses

Electrical synapses pass current directly between cells through gap junctions; they are faster but less modifiable than chemical synapses. Changes in synaptic strength — synaptic plasticity — underlie learning and memory, as in long-term potentiation.
