Consumption tax

Consumption tax
TypeIndirect tax
Tax baseSpending on goods and services
Common formsValue-added tax (VAT); sales tax; excise tax
Contrasted withIncome tax; wealth tax

A consumption tax is a tax levied on spending for goods and services rather than on income or wealth. The most widespread forms are the value-added tax (VAT), used by more than 160 countries, retail sales taxes, and excise duties on specific goods such as fuel, alcohol and tobacco.

How it works

Under a VAT, each business in the production chain charges tax on its sales but credits the tax it paid on purchases, so the net burden falls on the final consumer in proportion to consumption. A retail sales tax achieves a similar result by charging tax only at the final sale. Because the tax is embedded in transactions, it is relatively hard to evade and generates large, stable revenues.

Economic arguments

Supporters argue that taxing consumption encourages saving and investment, is simpler to administer than income tax in some respects, and does not penalise work directly. Critics note that consumption taxes tend to be regressive: poorer households spend a larger share of their income, so they pay a larger share in tax, unless essentials such as food are exempted or zero-rated, or credits are used to offset the burden.

Proposals

Some economists and politicians have proposed replacing income taxes with broad consumption taxes — for example a national sales tax (such as the proposed US "FairTax") or a consumed-income tax in which saving is deductible. Economists also study border adjustments and the treatment of financial services under VAT systems.
