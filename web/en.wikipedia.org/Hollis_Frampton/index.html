Hollis Frampton

Hollis Frampton
Born: 11 March 1936, Wooster, Ohio, US
Died: 30 March 1984 (aged 48), New York City, US
Occupation: Filmmaker, photographer, writer

Hollis William Frampton Jr. (March 11, 1936 – March 30, 1984) was an American avant-garde filmmaker, photographer, writer and theoretician. He is regarded as one of the central figures of structural film, a movement that emphasised the material properties of the film medium itself. His best-known works are Zorns Lemma (1970) and the unfinished epic cycle Magellan.

Early life

Frampton was born in Wooster, Ohio, and attended Phillips Academy in Andover, where he formed a friendship with the future minimalist sculptor Carl Andre and the painter Frank Stella. He studied at Western Reserve University in Cleveland but left without a degree. In the mid-1950s he corresponded with the poet Ezra Pound and visited him at St. Elizabeths Hospital in Washington, D.C., an experience that marked his intellectual formation. He moved to New York City in 1958.

Photography and early films

In New York, Frampton initially worked as a still photographer, documenting the sculptures of Carl Andre and other artists. He began making films in the early 1960s. Short early works such as Manual of Arms (1966) and Surface Tension (1968) explored fixed-camera setups, flicker and systematic structures. The seven-film cycle Hapax Legomena (1971–72) includes (nostalgia), in which photographs burn one by one on a hotplate while a narration describes — deliberately out of step — the photograph to come, and Poetic Justice.

Zorns Lemma and structural film

Zorns Lemma (1970), Frampton's most celebrated film, is an hour long and structured in three parts: a reading of a colonial primer text; a long central section of one-second shots of New York street signs, in which words beginning with successive letters of the alphabet are gradually replaced by wordless images, cycling through the alphabet many times until only images remain; and a final scene of a couple walking through a snowy field while voices read a medieval text on light. The film's title refers to Zorn's lemma in set theory, equivalent to the axiom of choice. Critic P. Adams Sitney grouped Frampton with the "structural filmmakers" who made the shape of the whole film the dominant content.

Later work and Magellan

From 1973 Frampton taught at SUNY Buffalo, where he helped build one of the first media study programmes and experimented with early digital imaging and computers. His final project, Magellan, was conceived as a vast film cycle — "an attempt to construct a total work" — to be screened over 371 days in portions keyed to the calendar, named after the first circumnavigation of the Earth. Only fragments were completed before his death from cancer in 1984, aged 48. He also wrote influential essays, collected posthumously in Circles of Confusion (1983), on photography and cinema.

Legacy

Frampton's films have been restored and are widely taught in film studies. His rigorous, witty explorations of language, time and the alphabet influenced generations of experimental filmmakers, and Zorns Lemma is routinely listed among the most important avant-garde films ever made.
