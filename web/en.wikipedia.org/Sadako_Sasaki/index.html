Sadako Sasaki

Thousands of paper cranes are offered each year at the Children's Peace Monument in Hiroshima.

Sadako Sasaki (Japanese: 佐々木 禎子, 7 January 1943 – 25 October 1955) was a Japanese girl who became a symbol of the innocent victims of nuclear warfare. She was two years old when the United States dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945. Though she survived the blast at her home about 1.6 kilometres (1 mile) from the hypocenter, she developed leukemia — the "atom bomb disease" — a decade later.

Paper cranes

While hospitalized in 1955, Sadako began folding paper cranes, inspired by the Japanese tradition that anyone who folds one thousand origami cranes (senbazuru) will be granted a wish. According to the popular account popularized by Eleanor Coerr's book Sadako and the Thousand Paper Cranes, she completed 644 cranes before her death at age twelve, and her classmates folded the remainder so that she was buried with a thousand. Her family has stated that she in fact folded well over a thousand cranes, continuing past the goal in hope of recovering and living.

Legacy

Sadako's classmates campaigned for a memorial to her and all child victims of the bomb. The Children's Peace Monument in Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park, topped by a statue of Sadako holding a golden crane, was unveiled in 1958. Schoolchildren around the world send paper cranes to the monument, and her story became a cornerstone of peace education. The paper crane itself is now an international symbol of peace and nuclear disarmament.
