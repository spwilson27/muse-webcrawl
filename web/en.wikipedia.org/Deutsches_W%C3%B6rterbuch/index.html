Volumes of the Deutsches Wörterbuch, the Grimms' dictionary.

The Deutsches Wörterbuch (German Dictionary), often called the Grimm Dictionary (Grimmsches Wörterbuch), is the largest and most comprehensive dictionary of the German language. Begun by Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, the Brothers Grimm, it documents the vocabulary of German from the early modern period onward, with detailed etymologies and quotations drawn from centuries of literature.

History

The Grimms contracted with a Leipzig publisher in 1838 and assembled a vast slip archive of quotations with the help of dozens of contributors. The first volume appeared in 1854; Jacob Grimm died in 1863 while working on the letter F, and Wilhelm had died in 1859 having completed the letter D. The work was continued by successive generations of scholars under the Prussian and later Berlin academies, and the first edition was finally completed in 1961—123 years after the project began—comprising 33 volumes and some 330,000 headwords.

Content and method

Each entry traces a word's forms, meanings, and development, supported by dated quotations from sources ranging from Martin Luther to Goethe and beyond. The dictionary shaped the historical study of German and Germanic philology, and Jacob Grimm's entries embed much of his broader linguistic research, including the sound correspondences known as Grimm's law.

Revisions and digital access

Parts of the dictionary with the oldest treatment were revised in a second edition begun in the 1960s. The complete work has been digitized and is freely available online, notably through the Trier Center for Digital Humanities, making the Grimm dictionary a standard reference for German historical lexicography.
