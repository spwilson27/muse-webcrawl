Adenosine triphosphate

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)
FormulaC₁₀H₁₆N₅O₁₃P₃
Molar mass507.18 g/mol
StructureAdenine + ribose (adenosine) with three phosphate groups
HydrolysisATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ, ΔG°′ ≈ −30.5 kJ/mol
RolePrincipal energy currency of the cell

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is a nucleoside triphosphate that provides energy to drive and support many processes in living cells, such as muscle contraction, nerve impulse propagation, condensate dissolution, and chemical synthesis. Found in all known forms of life, it is often referred to as the "molecular unit of currency" of intracellular energy transfer.

Structure and hydrolysis

ATP consists of an adenine base attached to a ribose sugar (together adenosine) and a chain of three phosphate groups. Hydrolysis of the terminal ("gamma") phosphoanhydride bond releases energy — about 30.5 kJ/mol under standard conditions and considerably more under cellular conditions — because the products, adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate, are stabilised by resonance and solvation and suffer less electrostatic repulsion than ATP itself.

Production

Cells regenerate ATP chiefly by cellular respiration: glycolysis yields a small net gain by substrate-level phosphorylation, while the great majority is produced by oxidative phosphorylation, in which the enzyme ATP synthase uses the proton-motive force across the inner mitochondrial membrane (chemiosmosis). In plants and photosynthetic bacteria, photophosphorylation performs the analogous process in chloroplasts using light energy. The human body recycles roughly its own mass of ATP each day.

Functions

Beyond energy transfer, ATP is a building block for RNA synthesis, a phosphate donor in signal-transduction phosphorylation cascades, an extracellular signalling molecule acting at purinergic receptors, and a hydrotrope that helps keep proteins soluble.

History

ATP was discovered in 1929 by Karl Lohmann and, independently, by Cyrus Fiske and Yellapragada Subbarow; Fritz Lipmann proposed its central role in energy transfer in 1941. The binding-change mechanism of ATP synthase was developed by Paul Boyer, and its rotary catalysis demonstrated in work recognised by the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
