Cartesian circle

The Cartesian circle is an alleged circular reasoning fallacy in the philosophy of René Descartes, first raised by critics including Antoine Arnauld in the Objections to Descartes's Meditations on First Philosophy (1641).

The problem

In the Third Meditation, Descartes argues that clear and distinct perceptions must be true because God exists and is no deceiver; yet his proof of God's existence itself rests on clear and distinct perceptions of the idea of God. Critics charge that this is circular: the reliability of clear and distinct perception is guaranteed by God, whose existence is known only through clear and distinct perception.

Responses

Defenders of Descartes have offered several replies: that the guarantee applies only to remembered demonstrations rather than currently intuited truths; that the cogito needs no guarantee; or that God validates only the general rule while particular perceptions in present attention are self-evident. The debate remains a standard topic in the study of foundationalism and epistemic circularity.
