Postmodern theatre

Postmodern theatre

Emergence: 1960s–1970s

Key practitioners: Robert Wilson, Richard Foreman, The Wooster Group, Pina Bausch

Traits: Fragmentation, multimedia, deconstruction, non-linear narrative

Postmodern theatre is a theatrical movement that emerged in the second half of the twentieth century, applying postmodern ideas — scepticism toward grand narratives, fragmentation, intertextuality and the blurring of high and popular culture — to stage practice. It reacts against the conventions of modernist and realist drama, in which a coherent plot, stable characters and a unified authorial meaning were assumed.

Postmodern productions frequently abandon linear storytelling in favour of collage-like structures assembled from text, image, movement, sound and video. Meaning is treated as something produced in performance and in the encounter with the audience, rather than something delivered by the playwright.

Characteristics

Common devices include the deconstruction of classic texts, the mixing of media (live performers alongside film and recorded sound), direct address and metatheatricality, repetition and simultaneity, and the refusal of psychological characterisation. Dance theatre, notably the work of Pina Bausch, is often grouped with the movement.

Practitioners

Robert Wilson's visually driven "theatre of images", the ontological-hysteric theatre of Richard Foreman, the media-saturated work of The Wooster Group and Elizabeth LeCompte, and companies such as Forced Entertainment in Britain exemplify the style. Playwrights including Heiner Müller and Caryl Churchill produced texts suited to postmodern staging.

Legacy

Postmodern theatre helped dissolve the boundary between theatre, performance art and dance, and its techniques — projection design, fragmented dramaturgy, devised creation — have been widely absorbed into mainstream practice.
