The Iban are an Austronesian ethnic group native to the island of Borneo, principally in the Malaysian state of Sarawak, with communities in Brunei and West Kalimantan, Indonesia. Historically known to Europeans as "Sea Dayaks" for their riverine and maritime raiding, the Iban are the largest single ethnic group in Sarawak, numbering over a million people. They are renowned for their longhouse communities, their agricultural skill in shifting rice cultivation, and rich oral and textile traditions.

Culture and society

Traditional Iban society is organised around the rumah panjang (longhouse), in which individual family apartments (bilik) open onto a long communal gallery under the leadership of a headman (tuai rumah). Customary law (adat) governs land, marriage, and ritual. The Iban language, closely related to Malay, is widely spoken and used in Malaysian broadcasting and education. Iban women are celebrated for the pua kumbu, an intricately patterned warp-ikat ceremonial textile, and Iban tattooing traditions have influenced modern tattoo art worldwide.

History

During the 19th century the Iban expanded through the river systems of Sarawak, sometimes in alliance with Malay chiefs and the Brooke "White Rajahs," who employed Iban forces while suppressing their headhunting and piracy. In the Second World War many Iban served with Allied forces against the Japanese occupation, and Iban trackers and scouts—the Sarawak Rangers and the famed Iban trackers—were employed by British and Commonwealth forces during the Malayan Emergency and the Indonesia–Malaysia confrontation. The Gawai Dayak festival, held on 1 June, celebrates Iban and wider Dayak identity.
