Linoleic acid is a polyunsaturated omega-6 fatty acid with the formula C18H32O2. It is an 18-carbon chain with two cis double bonds, the first located at the sixth carbon from the methyl (omega) end. Linoleic acid is an essential fatty acid for humans: it cannot be synthesized by the body and must be obtained from the diet. Its name derives from the Greek linon (flax) and oleic, relating to olive oil.

Biological role

In the body, linoleic acid is converted through gamma-linolenic acid and dihomo-gamma-linolenic acid to arachidonic acid, a precursor of prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and other eicosanoid signalling molecules. It is also a structural component of cell membranes and of ceramides in the skin barrier. Deficiency, rare outside artificial feeding or severe malabsorption, causes scaly skin and poor wound healing.

Dietary sources and health

Vegetable oils — sunflower, safflower, soybean, corn, and grapeseed — are the richest sources, and linoleic acid is typically the most abundant polyunsaturated fatty acid in Western diets. Its effect on cardiovascular risk has been extensively debated: replacing saturated fat with linoleate lowers LDL cholesterol, while critics note the potential for increased susceptibility to oxidation and competition with omega-3 fatty acids for metabolic enzymes.

Industrial uses

Linoleic-acid-rich oils are drying oils used in oil paints, varnishes, and linoleum manufacture, oxidizing and polymerizing on exposure to air. The acid is also a feedstock for soaps, emulsifiers, and cosmetics.
