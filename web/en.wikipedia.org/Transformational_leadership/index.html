Transformational leadership

Transformational leadership is a theory of leadership in which a leader works with teams or followers beyond their immediate self-interests to identify needed change, creating a vision to guide the change through influence, inspiration and intellectual stimulation, and executing the change in tandem with committed members of a group. It is contrasted with transactional leadership, which is based on exchanges of rewards and punishments.

Origins

The concept was introduced by sociologist James V. Downton (1973) and developed by political scientist James MacGregor Burns in his 1978 book Leadership, which distinguished "transforming" leadership — in which leaders and followers raise one another to higher levels of motivation and morality — from merely transactional leadership. Bernard M. Bass extended the theory to organisational settings from 1985 onwards.

Components

Bass and his collaborators identified four components, often called the "Four I's":

  • Idealized influence — the leader acts as a role model who is admired, respected and trusted.
  • Inspirational motivation — the leader articulates a compelling vision that inspires followers.
  • Intellectual stimulation — the leader encourages creativity and challenges assumptions.
  • Individualized consideration — the leader attends to each follower's needs for achievement and growth, acting as coach or mentor.

The Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ) is the standard instrument used to measure transformational leadership behaviours. Research generally finds positive correlations between transformational leadership and follower satisfaction, motivation and organisational performance, though critics note overlaps with related concepts such as charismatic leadership and the risk of manipulative "pseudo-transformational" leadership.
