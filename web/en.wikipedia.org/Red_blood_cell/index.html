Red blood cell

Red blood cells (RBCs), also called erythrocytes, are the most common type of blood cell and the vertebrate's principal means of delivering oxygen to the body tissues via blood flow through the circulatory system.

Structure

Human red blood cells are biconcave discs about 7–8 μm in diameter lacking a nucleus and most organelles. Their cytoplasm is rich in haemoglobin, the iron-containing protein that binds oxygen and gives blood its red colour. The flexible shape lets them squeeze through capillaries narrower than themselves.

Production and lifespan

Erythrocytes are produced in the red bone marrow through erythropoiesis, regulated by the hormone erythropoietin. They circulate for about 120 days before being removed by macrophages in the spleen and liver. Adults have roughly 20–30 trillion red blood cells. Disorders include anaemia (too few or dysfunctional cells) and polycythaemia (too many).

History

Red cells were first described by Jan Swammerdam in 1658 and observed in detail by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek.
