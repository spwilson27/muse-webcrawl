Videocassette recorder

A videocassette recorder (VCR), also known as a video recorder, is an electromechanical device that records and plays back analog audio and analog video from magnetic tape contained in a removable videocassette. VCRs allowed television viewers to record programmes for later viewing ("time shifting") and to play prerecorded tapes, transforming home entertainment from the 1970s through the 1990s.

History

Professional videotape recorders using reel-to-reel tape appeared in the 1950s, beginning with Ampex's Quadruplex system. Consumer formats followed: Sony's U-matic (1971), Philips' VCR format (1972), and the famous "format war" between Sony's Betamax (1975) and JVC's VHS (1976). VHS prevailed by the late 1980s due to longer recording times and wider licensing, becoming the dominant home video format. Later developments included S-VHS, Video8/Hi8 camcorders, and digital formats such as D-VHS.

Technology

VCRs use helical scan recording: a rotating drum with video heads writes diagonal tracks across the tape as it moves past, greatly increasing effective head-to-tape speed. Audio and control tracks are recorded along the tape edges. Most VCRs tune broadcast television, can be programmed with timers, and output to a TV via RF or composite video.

Impact and decline

The VCR spurred the home video rental industry (e.g. Blockbuster) and was at the centre of the 1984 U.S. Supreme Court case Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios, which held home time-shifting to be fair use. The DVD player surpassed the VCR in the early 2000s, followed by DVRs and streaming. Funai, the last manufacturer, ended VCR production in 2016.
