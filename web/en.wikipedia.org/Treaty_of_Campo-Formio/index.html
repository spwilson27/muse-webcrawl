The Treaty of Campo-Formio (today Campoformido) was signed on 17 October 1797 by Napoleon Bonaparte, representing the French Republic, and Count Ludwig von Cobenzl, representing the Habsburg Monarchy. The treaty followed the armistice of Leoben (18 April 1797) and ended the War of the First Coalition, leaving Great Britain as the only major power still at war with France.

Terms

Under the public articles, Austria recognised French control over the Austrian Netherlands (Belgium) and the Ionian Islands, which France took from the dissolved Republic of Venice. Austria received in compensation the city of Venice itself and Venetian territory up to the Adige River, including Istria and Dalmatia, thus partitioning the thousand-year-old Venetian Republic out of existence.

Secret articles provided that Austria would support French annexation of the left bank of the Rhine at a forthcoming congress, held at Rastatt, and would receive further compensation in Germany, including the Archbishopric of Salzburg.

Consequences

The treaty confirmed the dramatic gains of Bonaparte's Italian campaign of 1796–97 and established the Cisalpine Republic and the Ligurian Republic as French client states in northern Italy. It also redrew the map of Italy and Germany in ways that foreshadowed the end of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806. Austria's resentment at the settlement contributed to the formation of the Second Coalition in 1798.
