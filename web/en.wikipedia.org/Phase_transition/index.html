Phase transition

ExamplesMelting, boiling, condensation, magnetisation, superconductivity
ClassificationFirst-order / continuous (Ehrenfest); modern: discontinuous / continuous
Key quantitiesOrder parameter, latent heat, critical exponents
FrameworkStatistical mechanics; Landau theory; renormalization group

In physics, chemistry, and thermodynamics, a phase transition (or phase change) is the physical process of transition between one state of a medium and another. Commonly the term is used to refer to changes among the basic states of matter — solid, liquid, and gas — but phase transitions also include changes of crystal structure, magnetic ordering, superconductivity, and many other qualitative transformations of a system's properties.

Classification

Paul Ehrenfest classified transitions by the order of the derivative of the free energy that first becomes discontinuous. In first-order transitions (melting, boiling), the first derivatives — entropy and volume — jump, and the transition absorbs or releases latent heat, with the two phases coexisting. In continuous (second-order) transitions, such as the ferromagnetic transition at the Curie temperature or the onset of superconductivity, entropy and volume are continuous but quantities like heat capacity and compressibility change discontinuously or diverge.

Order parameters and critical phenomena

Landau theory describes transitions in terms of an order parameter — zero in the disordered phase and nonzero in the ordered phase (e.g. magnetisation for a ferromagnet, density difference for liquid–gas). Near a continuous transition, systems show critical phenomena: diverging correlation lengths and universal critical exponents, explained by Kenneth Wilson's renormalization group, which earned the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physics. Many dissimilar systems share the same exponents, forming universality classes determined mainly by dimension and symmetry.

Examples

Everyday examples include ice melting, water boiling, and frost forming. Technological and scientific examples include the liquid-crystal transitions used in displays, superconducting transitions in magnets and particle accelerators, the QCD transition in the early universe, and structural transitions in minerals deep within the Earth.
