Hardboiled (or hard-boiled) fiction is a literary genre that shares some of its characters and settings with crime fiction (especially detective stories and noir fiction). The genre's typical protagonist is a detective who battles organised crime during Prohibition in the United States and aftermath of World War I, while dealing with a legal system that has become as corrupt as the criminals he fights. In contrast to the more cerebral classic detective, the hardboiled hero is tough, unsentimental and laconic, and the prose style is spare and concrete.

History

The style emerged in the 1920s in pulp magazines such as Black Mask. Carroll John Daly's Race Williams stories (from 1923) are often cited as the first examples; Dashiell Hammett, a former Pinkerton operative, gave the form literary weight with Red Harvest (1929) and The Maltese Falcon (1930), featuring the Continental Op and Sam Spade. Raymond Chandler, with Philip Marlowe (beginning with The Big Sleep, 1939), articulated the genre's code in his 1944 essay "The Simple Art of Murder".

Influence

Hardboiled fiction profoundly influenced cinema: the film noir cycle of the 1940s drew directly on Hammett, Chandler and James M. Cain. The genre persists in modern crime novels, neo-noir films and video games, and the word "hardboiled" has entered general usage for a tough, unsentimental attitude.
