National Security Agency

Agency overview

Formed: November 4, 1952

Headquarters: Fort George G. Meade, Maryland, U.S.

Parent agency: Department of Defense

The National Security Agency (NSA) is a national-level intelligence agency of the United States Department of Defense, under the authority of the Director of National Intelligence. The NSA is responsible for global monitoring, collection, and processing of information and data for foreign intelligence and counterintelligence purposes, specializing in a discipline known as signals intelligence (SIGINT). The NSA is also tasked with the protection of U.S. communications networks and information systems.

The NSA relies on a variety of measures to accomplish its mission, the majority of which are clandestine. The existence of the NSA was not revealed until the 1970s. The agency's motto is "Defending Our Nation. Securing The Future."

History

The origins of the NSA can be traced to a codebreaking organization established during World War I. Its direct predecessor, the Armed Forces Security Agency (AFSA), was established in 1949. On November 4, 1952, President Harry S. Truman established the NSA by classified memorandum, consolidating cryptologic activities under a single agency.

During the Cold War, the NSA grew substantially, building its headquarters complex at Fort Meade, Maryland. The agency operated programs such as ECHELON, a surveillance network operated in cooperation with the other signatories of the UKUSA Agreement, which became the Five Eyes alliance.

Organization

The NSA is headed by the Director of the National Security Agency (DIRNSA), who also serves as chief of the Central Security Service and commander of United States Cyber Command. The director is the highest-ranking military officer in the U.S. signals intelligence community.

Controversies

The NSA has been involved in debates about privacy and the balance between national security and civil liberties. In 2013, disclosures by former NSA contractor Edward Snowden revealed details of global surveillance programs, including the PRISM program and bulk collection of telephone metadata, sparking worldwide debate.
