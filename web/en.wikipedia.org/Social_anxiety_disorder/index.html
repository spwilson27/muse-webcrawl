Social anxiety involves marked fear or anxiety about social situations

Social anxiety disorder (SAD), also known as social phobia, is an anxiety disorder characterised by intense fear or anxiety of social situations in which the individual may be scrutinised by others. Feared situations include public speaking, meeting new people, eating in front of others, and everyday interactions. The fear is out of proportion to the actual threat, leads to avoidance or endurance with intense distress, and interferes with daily life.

Signs and symptoms

Physical symptoms can include blushing, sweating, trembling, rapid heartbeat, nausea, and difficulty speaking. Cognitive features include anticipatory worry before events and detailed post-event rumination ("post-mortems"). Many people with the disorder use safety behaviours — such as avoiding eye contact or rehearsing sentences — which maintain the anxiety over time.

Causes

Social anxiety disorder typically begins in adolescence and arises from a combination of genetic vulnerability, temperament (such as behavioural inhibition in childhood), and environmental factors including negative social experiences and parenting styles. Twin studies suggest a moderate heritable component.

Treatment

Cognitive behavioural therapy, particularly exposure-based approaches, is the first-line psychological treatment and has the strongest evidence base. Medications include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs). Combined approaches may benefit some patients. Untreated, the disorder is often chronic and is associated with educational underachievement, occupational impairment, depression, and substance misuse.
