Pharmacovigilance

Monitoring medicines after approval is the core task of pharmacovigilance.

Pharmacovigilance (PV, or PhV), also known as drug safety, is the pharmaceutical science relating to the "collection, detection, assessment, monitoring, and prevention" of adverse effects with pharmaceutical products. The etymological roots for the word "pharmacovigilance" are: pharmakon (Greek for medicinal substance) and vigilare (Latin for to keep watch).

As such, pharmacovigilance heavily focuses on adverse drug reactions (ADRs), which are defined as any response to a drug which is noxious and unintended, including lack of efficacy of the medicine. Medication errors such as overdose, misuse and abuse of a drug, as well as drug exposure during pregnancy and breastfeeding, are also of interest.

History

Modern pharmacovigilance arose from disasters such as the sulfanilamide elixir tragedy of 1937 in the United States and the thalidomide tragedy of the late 1950s and early 1960s, in which a sedative marketed to pregnant women caused severe birth defects in thousands of children. These events led to strengthened drug regulation, including the US Kefauver–Harris Amendment of 1962 and the creation of the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring in 1968, coordinated from Uppsala, Sweden.

Methods

The mainstays of pharmacovigilance are spontaneous reporting systems, in which healthcare professionals and patients report suspected adverse reactions to regulators — examples include the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) in the US, the Yellow Card scheme in the UK, and EudraVigilance in the European Union. Reports are analysed for signals — statistical disproportionality or clinical patterns suggesting a previously unrecognized risk. Other methods include prescription-event monitoring, active surveillance through electronic health records and claims databases, and pharmacoepidemiological studies.

Regulatory action

When risks are confirmed, regulators may require label changes, restrict indications, issue safety communications, or withdraw a product. High-profile withdrawals driven by post-marketing surveillance include rofecoxib (Vioxx) in 2004, removed after evidence of increased cardiovascular risk.
