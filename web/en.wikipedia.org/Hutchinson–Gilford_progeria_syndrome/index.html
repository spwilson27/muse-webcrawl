Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome

Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), often called simply progeria, is an extremely rare genetic disorder in which symptoms resembling aspects of aging are manifested at a very early age. It affects an estimated 1 in 4 to 8 million newborns; only a few hundred cases have been documented worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

Children with progeria usually appear normal at birth but begin to show profound growth failure within the first year. Characteristic features include a disproportionately large head, prominent eyes, a small beaked nose, loss of subcutaneous fat and hair (alopecia), stiff joints, hip dislocation, and aged-looking, wrinkled skin. Intelligence is typically unaffected. Severe progressive cardiovascular disease — atherosclerosis of a kind otherwise seen in the elderly — is the usual cause of death, at an average age of about 14 years.

Cause

Progeria is caused by a de novo point mutation in the LMNA gene on chromosome 1, which encodes the nuclear envelope protein lamin A. The mutation activates a cryptic splice site, producing an abnormal protein called progerin that remains permanently farnesylated and anchors to the nuclear membrane, distorting nuclear shape and disrupting gene regulation and DNA repair. The condition is not usually inherited, arising sporadically, although the mutant gene acts dominantly.

Treatment

In 2020 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved lonafarnib (Zokinvy), a farnesyltransferase inhibitor originally developed for cancer, as the first treatment for progeria; clinical studies associated it with reduced mortality. Supportive care addresses cardiovascular complications, nutrition, and joint mobility. The disorder was first described by Jonathan Hutchinson in 1886 and Hastings Gilford in 1897, from whom it takes its name.
