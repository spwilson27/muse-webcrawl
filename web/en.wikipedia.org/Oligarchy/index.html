Oligarchy

Oligarchy
EtymologyGreek: oligarkhía — "rule by few"
Key theoristsPlato, Aristotle, Robert Michels, Jeffrey Winters
Related formsAristocracy, plutocracy, timocracy

Oligarchy (from Greek ὀλιγαρχία, oligarkhía, "rule by few") is a form of government in which political power rests with a small number of people. These people may or may not be distinguished by one or several characteristics, such as nobility, fame, wealth, education, or corporate, religious, political, or military control.

Classical theory

Plato and Aristotle used the term for the corrupt form of aristocracy: Aristotle defined oligarchy as government by the wealthy few in their own interest, in contrast to aristocracy, government by the best few for the common good. Ancient Greek oligarchies, such as the Thirty Tyrants at Athens (404–403 BC) and the constitutional order of Sparta, were frequent subjects of this analysis.

Iron law of oligarchy

In 1911 the German sociologist Robert Michels formulated the "iron law of oligarchy": all organizations, even democratic ones, inevitably develop into oligarchies because leaders acquire interests and resources distinct from those of the members. The idea has been influential in political science, though its universality is debated.

Wealth and oligarchy

Political scientist Jeffrey A. Winters has defined oligarchy specifically as "the politics of wealth defense" by materially endowed actors ("oligarchs"), distinguishing forms by how oligarchs are involved in government and how wealth is defended. The term is also commonly applied to post-Soviet Russia's business magnates of the 1990s, and in debates about the influence of wealth on politics in democracies such as the United States. Where rule by the rich is the defining feature, the related term is plutocracy.

Examples

Historical examples of oligarchic government include the Republic of Venice under its closed patriciate, the Dutch Republic's regent class, and ancient Sparta. Modern usage often attaches the label to states where a small military, corporate, or party elite dominates decision-making.
