TypeSerpent deities / beings

The nāga (Sanskrit: नाग) are a class of serpent beings in Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain traditions. Usually depicted as great hooded cobras, or as humans with serpent features or multiple snake heads, nagas inhabit an underground and underwater realm, Nagaloka, and are associated with waters, fertility, treasure, and protection — but can be dangerous when offended.

In Hindu tradition

Famous nagas include Shesha, the cosmic serpent on whom the god Vishnu reclines; Vasuki, used as the churning rope in the myth of the churning of the ocean of milk; and Takshaka, whose bite killed King Parikshit in the Mahabharata. Serpent worship remains widespread in India, notably at the festival of Naga Panchami.

In Buddhism and Southeast Asia

In Buddhist legend the naga king Mucalinda sheltered the meditating Buddha from a storm by spreading his hoods over him. Nagas pervade the art and folklore of Southeast Asia: the multi-headed naga balustrades of Angkor in Cambodia, the Phaya Naga of the Mekong in Thai and Lao belief, and temple guardian statues throughout the region. In Jainism, the tirthankara Parshvanatha is shown sheltered by the naga Dharanendra.

Symbolism

Nagas are generally regarded as guardians of water sources, thresholds, and hidden knowledge, mediating between the human and divine worlds.
