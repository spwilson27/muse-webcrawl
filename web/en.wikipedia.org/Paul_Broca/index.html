Pierre Paul Broca (28 June 1824 – 9 July 1880) was a French physician, anatomist, and anthropologist. He is best known for his research on the region of the frontal lobe now called Broca's area, which established the localisation of speech production in the brain and founded modern neurolinguistics.

Speech localisation

In 1861 Broca examined Louis Victor Leborgne, a patient nicknamed "Tan" because that was nearly the only syllable he could utter. After Leborgne's death, autopsy revealed a lesion in the posterior inferior frontal gyrus of the left hemisphere. Studying further cases, Broca concluded that articulate language is localised in this region — usually in the left hemisphere — a landmark demonstration that specific mental functions occupy specific brain areas. The resulting condition, expressive aphasia from damage to this region, is called Broca's aphasia.

Other work

Broca was a founder of modern physical anthropology: he established the Society of Anthropology of Paris in 1859 and developed instruments and methods for craniometry and the study of the brain, also describing the limbic lobe. He worked on tumours, aneurysms, and trepanation, and trained as a surgeon under leading Parisian clinicians. Some of his anthropological views — including attempts to rank human groups by skull measurements — are now regarded as scientific racism, a much-discussed part of his legacy.

Recognition

Broca became a senator for life in 1880, the year of his death. His name is one of the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower, and the preserved brains of his famous patients remain in Paris museum collections.
