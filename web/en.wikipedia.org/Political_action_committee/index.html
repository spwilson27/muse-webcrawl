In the United States, a political action committee (PAC) is a tax-exempt organisation that pools campaign contributions from members and donates those funds to campaigns for or against candidates, ballot initiatives or legislation. PACs are regulated by the Federal Election Commission under the Federal Election Campaign Act. The first PAC was formed in 1943 by the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) to raise money for the re-election of President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Types and limits

Traditional (non-connected and connected) PACs may receive contributions of up to $5,000 per year from individuals and may contribute up to $5,000 per candidate per election and $15,000 per year to a national party committee. Corporations and labour unions may not contribute directly from their treasuries to federal candidates, but may sponsor "separate segregated fund" PACs that solicit their employees or members. Leadership PACs are established by politicians to fund allies and build influence. Since the Supreme Court's decision in Citizens United v. FEC (2010) and the subsequent Speechnow.org ruling, Super PACs (independent expenditure-only committees) may raise unlimited sums from corporations, unions and individuals, provided they do not contribute directly to, or coordinate with, candidates.

Role in elections

Industry, trade-association, ideological and labour PACs are major sources of campaign funding, particularly for incumbents and members of relevant congressional committees. Critics argue PACs amplify the political influence of organised moneyed interests; defenders describe them as vehicles for collective political participation by ordinary members.
