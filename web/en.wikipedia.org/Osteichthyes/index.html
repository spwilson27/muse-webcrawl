Osteichthyes


Common name: Bony fishes
Diversity: about 29,000+ species
Classes: Actinopterygii (ray-finned), Sarcopterygii (lobe-finned)

Osteichthyes, commonly referred to as the bony fish, is a diverse superclass of vertebrate animals that have skeletons primarily composed of bone tissue. It comprises the overwhelming majority of fish species — more than 29,000 described species, roughly half of all known vertebrates — and is divided into the ray-finned fishes (Actinopterygii), by far the larger group, and the lobe-finned fishes (Sarcopterygii), which include the coelacanths, lungfishes and the ancestors of tetrapods.

Characteristics

Bony fish possess an endoskeleton of bone, a skull with a fixed set of bones, bony scales in most groups, and typically a swim bladder used for buoyancy control. Their fins are supported either by bony spines and soft rays or by fleshy lobes containing bones. Gills are covered by a protective bony flap, the operculum, whose pumping action allows many species to breathe without swimming.

Evolution and diversity

The earliest osteichthyans appeared in the Silurian period, over 425 million years ago. Ray-finned fishes radiated spectacularly and now dominate nearly all aquatic habitats, from deep-sea trenches to mountain streams. The lobe-finned lineage, though species-poor today, is of special evolutionary importance: tetrapods (amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals) descend from sarcopterygian ancestors, so in cladistic terms tetrapods are themselves bony fish.
