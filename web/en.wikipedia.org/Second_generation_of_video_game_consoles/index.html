Second generation of video game consoles

In the history of video games, the second generation of home consoles began in 1976 with the Fairchild Channel F and is generally considered to have ended in 1992. This generation distinguished itself from the first by using microprocessors and interchangeable ROM cartridges, allowing a single console to play an open-ended library of games sold separately, rather than a fixed set of built-in games.

History

The Fairchild Channel F (1976) was the first cartridge-based console, but the generation was defined by the Atari VCS (later Atari 2600), launched in 1977. Its licensed port of Space Invaders in 1980 — the first "killer app" for a home console — quadrupled sales. Mattel's Intellivision (1979) offered improved graphics and a famous comparative advertising campaign. The ColecoVision (1982) brought near-arcade-quality graphics and a port of Donkey Kong, while GCE's Vectrex (1982) used a built-in vector display. In Europe, the Philips Videopac (Odyssey² in North America) also found a market.

Games and developers

The era saw the birth of third-party game publishing: Activision, founded in 1979 by former Atari programmers, was the first independent developer for a console it did not manufacture. Hit titles included Adventure, Pitfall!, Asteroids, and Pac-Man — the latter's poor 2600 port becoming a symbol of the market glut.

The crash of 1983

A flood of low-quality titles, market saturation, and competition from home computers triggered the video game crash of 1983 in North America, which bankrupted several manufacturers and ended second-generation production there. The industry revived with the third generation, led by the Nintendo Entertainment System. See also Atari 2600 and video game crash of 1983.
