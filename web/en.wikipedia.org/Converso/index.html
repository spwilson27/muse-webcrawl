Converso

A converso (plural conversos) was a Jew in Spain or Portugal who converted to Catholicism, or one of their descendants. Mass conversions followed anti-Jewish violence in 1391 and the Alhambra Decree of 1492, which expelled practising Jews from Spain.

History

Conversos, also called "New Christians," rose to prominence in Spanish commerce, administration, and the Church, but faced deep suspicion from "Old Christians." Statutes of limpieza de sangre ("purity of blood") excluded those of Jewish ancestry from offices and institutions. The Spanish Inquisition, established in 1478, targeted conversos accused of secretly practising Judaism—so-called judaizantes or crypto-Jews; those found guilty could be burned at the stake in an auto-da-fé.

Legacy

Conversos and their descendants migrated across Europe, North Africa, and the Americas, and some communities in Portugal, such as Belmonte, preserved crypto-Jewish practices for centuries. The term differs from Marrano, a pejorative label applied to converts suspected of secret Judaism.
