The Twelve Imams

The names of the Twelve Imams are commonly rendered in calligraphy and displayed in Twelver Shia mosques and homes.

The Twelve Imams are the spiritual and political successors to the Islamic prophet Muhammad in the theology of Twelver Shi'ism, the largest branch of Shia Islam (and also revered in Alevism). Twelvers believe that the Imams — beginning with Ali ibn Abi Talib and continuing through his descendants — were divinely appointed, infallible (maʿṣūm) leaders who inherited Muhammad's authority to interpret Islam, and that the twelfth and final Imam, Muhammad al-Mahdi, entered occultation in the ninth century and will return at the end of time as the Mahdi.

Theology

According to Twelver doctrine, the Imamate is a divine office conveyed by explicit designation (naṣṣ) from one Imam to the next. The Imams are held to possess divinely granted knowledge and authority (walāya). The martyrdom of Husayn at Karbala in 680 is central to Shia identity and is commemorated at Ashura. The twelfth Imam is believed to have gone into a Lesser Occultation (874–941), communicating through four deputies, and then a Greater Occultation that continues until his return. During the occultation, religious authority is exercised by scholars (mujtahids and, in the dominant Usuli school, the marjaʿ system of emulation), a role given political form after 1979 in Iran's doctrine of velāyat-e faqīh.

Many of the Imams are buried at major shrines: Ali at Najaf, Husayn at Karbala, al-Kazim and al-Jawad at Baghdad (Kadhimiya), al-Rida at Mashhad, al-Hadi and al-Askari at Samarra, and (according to tradition) Hasan, Zayn al-Abidin, al-Baqir and al-Sadiq at al-Baqi cemetery in Medina.
