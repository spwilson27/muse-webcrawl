Equivalent variation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Equivalent variation (EV) is a measure of economic welfare change associated with a change in prices, developed in the tradition of Hicksian welfare economics. It answers the question: how much money, at the original prices, would have to be taken from (or given to) a consumer to make them exactly as well off as they would be after the price change?

Formally, if prices change from p0 to p1 while money income m is unchanged, the equivalent variation is:

EV = e(p0, u1) − e(p0, u0) = e(p0, u1) − m

where e(·) is the expenditure function and u1 is the utility level attained after the price change. For a price increase (a welfare loss), EV is negative: it is the amount of income that, taken away at the old prices, would reduce utility by as much as the price rise does.

Relationship to other welfare measures

Equivalent variation is closely related to compensating variation (CV), which instead asks how much money must be given or taken at the new prices to restore the consumer to their original utility. Both measures equal the change in consumer surplus when preferences are quasilinear (no income effects), but in general they differ, bracketing the surplus change when the good in question is normal. Along with CV, EV is used in cost–benefit analysis to value public projects and policy changes in money terms.

Properties

  • EV is measured relative to the new utility level and the old price vector.
  • For a beneficial price fall, EV is positive and equals the maximum the consumer would pay to secure the change.
  • Unlike changes in consumer surplus, EV and CV are exact welfare measures derived from preferences, independent of the path of price changes.
