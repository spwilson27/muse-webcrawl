Thorstein Veblen

Thorstein Bunde Veblen
Born: 30 July 1857, Cato, Wisconsin, U.S.
Died: 3 August 1929 (aged 72), Menlo Park, California, U.S.
Known for: Conspicuous consumption; the leisure class; institutional economics
Notable work: The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899)

Thorstein Bunde Veblen (30 July 1857 – 3 August 1929) was an American economist and sociologist of Norwegian descent. He is best known for his book The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), which introduced the concept of "conspicuous consumption" and subjected the manners and economics of America's wealthy elite to withering evolutionary analysis. Veblen was a founding figure of institutional economics and a sharp critic of neoclassical theory, capitalism, and the use of production for profit rather than for use.

Life

Veblen was born in Wisconsin to Norwegian immigrant farmers and raised in Minnesota, growing up speaking Norwegian. He studied at Carleton College and earned his PhD in philosophy from Yale in 1884, studying under William Graham Sumner. Unable to secure a steady academic post for years, he eventually joined the new University of Chicago in 1892, where he edited the Journal of Political Economy. His later academic career — at Stanford, the University of Missouri, and the New School in New York — was marked by controversy, restlessness, and personal scandal, but his intellectual influence grew steadily.

Ideas

In The Theory of the Leisure Class, Veblen argued that under modern conditions wealth is displayed through conspicuous leisure and conspicuous consumption: the wasteful spending of money on goods and services acquired primarily to signal status. He coined or popularised terms such as "conspicuous consumption", "pecuniary emulation", "invidious comparison", "trained incapacity", and occupational "vicarious leisure" performed by wives and servants on behalf of household heads.

In The Theory of Business Enterprise (1904) and later works, Veblen distinguished between "industry" — the machine process and the engineers and workers who run it, oriented toward serviceability — and "business", the pecuniary management concerned with profit, which he accused of deliberately "sabotaging" production to keep prices high. His 1921 essay collection The Engineers and the Price System proposed that engineers could run the industrial system more rationally, an idea that influenced the technocracy movement of the 1930s.

Legacy

Veblen's evolutionary, institution-focused approach founded a school of institutional economics carried on by figures such as John R. Commons and Wesley Clair Mitchell. His satirical prose style and analysis of status competition remain widely read in sociology and cultural criticism.
