Tibet Autonomous Region

The Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR), often shortened to Tibet, is an autonomous region of the People's Republic of China in southwest Asia. It covers most of the Tibetan Plateau, the highest region on Earth, with an average elevation of more than 4,000 metres. The region has an area of about 1.2 million square kilometres — the second-largest provincial-level division of China — but a population of only around 3.6 million, making it the least densely populated. Its capital and largest city is Lhasa.

Geography

The TAR spans the western and central Tibetan Plateau, bounded by the Himalayas to the south — including Mount Everest (Qomolangma), which it shares with Nepal — and the Kunlun Mountains to the north. Great Asian rivers including the Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra), the Yangtze, the Mekong and the Salween rise in or near the region. The northern Changtang is a high, cold desert of lakes and steppe inhabited by nomadic herders and wildlife such as the chiru (Tibetan antelope) and wild yak.

History

The area roughly corresponds to the historical Tibetan regions of Ü-Tsang and western Kham. Tibet was incorporated into the People's Republic of China after the Seventeen Point Agreement of 1951, and the Tibet Autonomous Region was formally established in 1965 following the 1959 uprising, after which the 14th Dalai Lama fled to India. The region's status and the treatment of Tibetan culture, religion and language remain internationally contentious, and a Tibetan government-in-exile is based in Dharamsala, India.

Economy and culture

The population is majority ethnic Tibetan, with Buddhism — especially the Gelug school, historically headed by the Dalai Lamas — central to cultural life; Lhasa's Jokhang Temple and Potala Palace are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Traditional livelihoods include barley farming, yak and sheep herding, and trade. Since the 2000s the Chinese government has invested heavily in infrastructure, most notably the Qinghai–Tibet railway (opened 2006), the world's highest railway, and tourism has grown rapidly. Han Chinese migration and modernisation have substantially changed Lhasa and other towns.
