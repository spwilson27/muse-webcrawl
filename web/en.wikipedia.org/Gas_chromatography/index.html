Gas chromatography

Gas chromatography (GC) is a common type of chromatography used in analytical chemistry for separating and analysing compounds that can be vaporised without decomposition. Typical uses include testing the purity of a substance and separating the components of a mixture; in preparative form it can isolate pure compounds.

Principles

A vapourised sample is injected into a column through which an inert carrier gas (the mobile phase — usually helium, hydrogen or nitrogen) flows. The column contains a stationary phase, either a packed solid or, in modern capillary columns, a thin liquid film on the inner wall of a long fused-silica tube. Components separate because they partition differently between the phases, eluting at characteristic retention times.

Instrumentation

A GC system comprises a heated injector, a temperature-programmable oven holding the column, and a detector — commonly a flame ionisation detector (FID) or, in GC–MS, a mass spectrometer, which identifies each separated compound. Autosamplers and two-dimensional GC (GC×GC) extend throughput and resolving power.

History

Gas–liquid chromatography was developed by Archer Martin and James Synge in the early 1950s; James and Martin's 1952 paper described the technique, building on Martin and Synge's Nobel-winning work on partition chromatography. Capillary columns were introduced by Marcel Golay later in the decade.

Applications

GC is used in forensics, environmental monitoring, food and flavour analysis, petrochemicals, clinical toxicology and pharmaceutical quality control.
