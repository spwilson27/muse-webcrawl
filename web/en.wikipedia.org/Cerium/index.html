Cerium is a chemical element with the symbol Ce and atomic number 58. It is a soft, ductile, silvery-white metallic element of the lanthanide series that tarnishes when exposed to air. Cerium is the most abundant of the rare-earth elements, making up about 0.0046% of the Earth's crust by weight — comparable in abundance to copper. It is found in several minerals, principally monazite and bastnäsite.

History

Cerium was discovered in 1803 independently by Jöns Jacob Berzelius and Wilhelm Hisinger in Sweden and by Martin Heinrich Klaproth in Germany. Berzelius named it after the dwarf planet Ceres, itself named for the Roman goddess of agriculture, which had been discovered two years earlier.

Applications

Cerium's main uses exploit the spark-producing properties of its alloy mischmetal in lighter flints (ferrocerium), the polishing action of cerium(IV) oxide ("optician's rouge") in glass manufacture, ceria's role in catalytic converters and self-cleaning ovens, and cerium compounds in phosphors and glass decolorizers. Cerium is unusual among lanthanides in having a stable +4 oxidation state in addition to the usual +3.
