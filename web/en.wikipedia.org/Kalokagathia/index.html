Kalokagathia (Greek: καλοκαγαθία) is an ancient Greek concept denoting the ideal of the complete gentleman: the harmonious union of physical beauty (kalos) and moral goodness or nobility (agathos). A person who embodied the ideal was called a kalos kagathos ("beautiful-and-good"), and the term carried strong connotations of aristocratic upbringing, civic virtue, courage, and cultivated excellence.

Usage in Greek thought

In classical Athens the phrase was commonly applied to the ideal citizen-soldier and gentleman of the aristocracy. Writers such as Xenophon and Aristotle used the term in ethical discussion: for Aristotle, kalokagathia represented the complete virtue of character — possessing all the moral excellences together, exercised for the sake of the noble. The concept linked appearance, conduct, and social standing, expressing a culture in which outward beauty was taken to reflect inward worth, an assumption that Greek literature also questioned, as in the contrast between handsome but base men and the ugly but wise Socrates.

Later reception

The ideal influenced Renaissance humanist notions of the well-rounded gentleman and, in the modern era, the Czech Sokol movement adopted kalokagathia as a guiding principle of balanced physical and moral education. The term is still used in philosophy and classical studies to discuss the Greek fusion of aesthetic and ethical value.
