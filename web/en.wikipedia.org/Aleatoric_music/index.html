Aleatoric music (also aleatory music or chance music; from the Latin word alea, meaning "dice") is music in which some element of the composition is left to chance, and/or some primary element of a composed work's realisation is left to the determination of its performer(s). The term is associated with the work of European composers in the 1950s, and with American composer John Cage, whose use of the I Ching to determine compositional choices made him a central figure of the movement.

History

Precursors include the Musikalisches Würfelspiel ("musical dice game") attributed to the era of Mozart and Haydn, in which precomposed measures were assembled in an order determined by dice rolls. In the twentieth century, Charles Ives experimented with passages that performers could arrange freely, and Henry Cowell introduced tone clusters and elastic forms. The term "aleatoric" was promoted in Europe after a 1957 lecture by acoustician Werner Meyer-Eppler and was taken up by Pierre Boulez in his essay "Aléa" (1957).

Types of indeterminacy

  • Chance composition — the score is produced by chance procedures (dice, coin tosses, the I Ching) but is fixed once written, as in Cage's Music of Changes (1951).
  • Mobile form — composed sections may be reordered by the performer, as in Stockhausen's Klavierstück XI (1956) or Boulez's Third Piano Sonata.
  • Indeterminate notation — graphic scores and verbal instructions leave pitch, rhythm or timbre partly to the performer, as in Earle Brown's December 1952.

Cage and 4′33″

Cage's most famous work, 4′33″ (1952), instructs performers not to play their instruments for the duration of the piece; the "music" consists of the ambient sounds of the environment. It grew out of Cage's conviction that any sound may constitute music and his interest in Zen Buddhism.
