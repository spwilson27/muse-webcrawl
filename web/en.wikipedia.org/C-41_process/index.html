C-41 process

The C-41 process is a chromogenic colour photographic film development process introduced by Kodak in 1972 as the standard method for processing colour negative film. Nearly all modern colour negative films are designed for C-41, and the process became the universal standard offered by photo laboratories worldwide.

Process steps

At a controlled temperature of 37.8 °C (100 °F), the film passes through a colour developer (about 3 minutes 15 seconds), a bleach, a fixer — often combined as a "blix" in minilab machines — washing stages and a final stabiliser or rinse bath. The developer reduces exposed silver halide and, through dye couplers in the emulsion layers, forms cyan, magenta and yellow dyes.

Characteristics

C-41 films have an orange mask to correct dye imperfections and produce negatives intended for printing on colour paper (RA-4 process) or scanning. The process's strict standardisation allowed one-hour minilabs to proliferate from the 1980s onward. Black-and-white chromogenic films such as Ilford XP2 are also processed in C-41 chemistry.
