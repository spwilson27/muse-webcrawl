Mule deer

The mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus) is a deer native to western North America, named for its large ears, which resemble those of a mule. It is the larger of the two species of Odocoileus, the other being the white-tailed deer, and is distinguished by its forked (dichotomously branching) antlers, black-tipped tail, and a distinctive bounding gait called stotting, in which all four feet leave the ground at once.

Distribution and habitat

Mule deer range from the western Great Plains through the Rocky Mountains to the Pacific coast, and from Saskatchewan and Alberta south into northern Mexico. They occupy deserts, shrublands, grasslands, forests and alpine meadows, migrating seasonally between high-elevation summer range and lower winter range. The black-tailed deer of the Pacific Northwest coast are considered subspecies of the mule deer.

Biology and behaviour

Mule deer are browsers, feeding on forbs, shrubs such as sagebrush and bitterbrush, grasses and acorns. Bucks shed and regrow their antlers annually; unlike white-tailed deer, the antlers branch in forks. The rut occurs in late autumn. Predators include cougars, coyotes, wolves and bears. Populations have declined in parts of the range owing to habitat loss, drought and chronic wasting disease, and mule deer are an important game species managed by state and provincial agencies.
