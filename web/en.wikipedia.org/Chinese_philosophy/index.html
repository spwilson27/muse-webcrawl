Chinese philosophy


The taijitu, symbol of complementary opposites

Chinese philosophy originated in the Spring and Autumn period and Warring States period, during a flowering of thought known as the Hundred Schools of Thought, which addressed questions of ethics, politics, metaphysics and the good life. It is characterised by its enduring traditions — principally Confucianism, Daoism, and Chinese Buddhism — along with influential schools such as Mohism, Legalism and the School of Names, and by concepts including the Dao, qi, yin and yang, li (ritual propriety or principle) and ren (humaneness).

Hundred Schools

Confucius (551–479 BC) emphasised ritual, family obligation, benevolence and rule by moral example; his followers Mencius and Xunzi debated whether human nature is good or innately in need of cultivation. Laozi and Zhuangzi, associated with Daoism, advocated alignment with the natural order (wu wei, effortless action) and scepticism toward social convention. Mozi preached impartial care and consequentialist ethics; Legalists such as Han Fei argued that order requires strict laws and administrative technique; logicians and rhetoricians explored language and paradox.

Imperial and later developments

Under the Han dynasty, Confucianism became state orthodoxy, synthesised with cosmology by thinkers such as Dong Zhongshu. Buddhism, arriving from India, was progressively sinicised, producing schools such as Chan (Zen) and Pure Land and stimulating metaphysical debate (the "Learning of the Dark"). In the Song and Ming dynasties, Neo-Confucianism — in the Cheng–Zhu school of principle (li) and the Lu–Wang school of mind (xin) — dominated intellectual life and spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

Modern period

The shocks of the 19th and 20th centuries brought Chinese thinkers into engagement with Western philosophy, science, liberalism and Marxism. The New Culture and May Fourth movements attacked tradition even as others sought creative syntheses; contemporary "New Confucians" have argued for the compatibility of Confucian values with modernity and democracy. Chinese philosophy today is studied and practised internationally, with renewed attention to its resources for ethics, environmental thought and political theory.
