A road surface (British English) or pavement (American English) is the durable surface material laid down on an area intended to sustain vehicular or foot traffic, such as a road or walkway. In the past, gravel road surfaces, hoggin, cobblestone and granite setts were extensively used, but these have mostly been replaced by asphalt concrete or Portland cement concrete — both of which are also called "pavements" in some usage — laid on a compacted base course.

Types

Asphalt concrete ("asphalt", "tarmac", "blacktop") is a composite of bitumen binder and mineral aggregate, laid hot and compacted by rollers. It is flexible, quick to repair and relatively quiet, but softens in extreme heat and typically needs resurfacing every 15–20 years. Concrete pavements are more rigid and durable, lasting 30 years or more, but cost more initially and are noisier. Surfaces of cobblestone, setts and brick survive in historic districts; chip seal and gravel are used on low-traffic rural roads.

Structure

A modern road is a layered structure: the surface course resists wear and provides skid resistance; beneath it the base and subbase courses spread wheel loads onto the subgrade (natural soil). Drainage is critical — water trapped in the layers accelerates freeze–thaw damage, potholes and stripping of the bitumen. Surface properties such as texture, friction and evenness are monitored with profilometers and skid-resistance testers, and distress is summarised by indices such as the International Roughness Index (IRI).

Environmental considerations

Impermeable surfaces increase stormwater runoff; permeable pavements allow infiltration. Studies of the urban heat island effect have prompted interest in lighter-coloured "cool pavements". Reclaimed asphalt pavement is among the most recycled materials in the world by tonnage.
