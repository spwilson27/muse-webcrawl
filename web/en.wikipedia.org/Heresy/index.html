Heresy

Heresy
[Image: Examination of a heretic]
EtymologyGreek hairesis, "choice"
DefinitionBelief or opinion contrary to orthodox religious doctrine
Contrasted withApostasy, blasphemy, schism

Heresy is any belief or theory that is strongly at variance with established beliefs or customs, particularly the accepted beliefs of an organised church. In Christian theology, heresy is the formal denial or doubt of a core doctrine defined by the church, distinguished from apostasy (total abandonment of the faith), schism (division over authority rather than doctrine), and blasphemy (speech against God). Similar concepts exist in Judaism (minut), Islam (bid'ah and zandaqa), and other traditions.

Etymology and usage

The word derives from the Greek hairesis, meaning a "choice" or a school of thought, a neutral term in antiquity. In the New Testament it came to denote divisive factions, and by the writings of Irenaeus of Lyons in the 2nd century it had acquired its pejorative sense of false teaching opposed to apostolic orthodoxy.

Christian history

The early church defined its doctrines against movements later labelled heresies — Gnosticism, Arianism, Nestorianism, Pelagianism — through the ecumenical councils. In medieval Europe, movements such as Catharism and Waldensianism provoked crusades and the establishment of the Inquisition. The last execution for heresy in Britain was in 1697 (Thomas Aikenhead, for blasphemy), while the Spanish Inquisition's final heresy execution was in 1826. Modern Catholic canon law retains excommunication as the penalty for obstinate heresy, but civil enforcement has disappeared in liberal democracies; some states still punish analogous offences under blasphemy or apostasy laws.
