The siege of Masada was one of the final events of the First Jewish–Roman War, occurring from 73 to 74 CE on and around a large hilltop in present-day Israel. The siege was chronicled by Flavius Josephus, the Roman-appointed Jewish historian and sole surviving source. According to Josephus, the long siege by the troops of the Roman Empire led to the mass suicide of the Sicarii rebels and resident Jewish families of the Masada fortress.

Masada has become a controversial event in Jewish history, one that some regard as a place of reverence, while others view it — and especially the mass suicide — as evidence of harmful extremism. The Roman siege works at Masada, including the great earthen ramp and the circumvallation wall with its camps, are among the best-preserved examples of Roman siege engineering anywhere.

Background

Masada is a natural plateau overlooking the Dead Sea, fortified by Herod the Great in the 1st century BC with palaces, storehouses, and cisterns. In 66 CE, at the start of the revolt, Jewish rebels of the Sicarii faction seized the fortress from its Roman garrison. After Jerusalem fell in 70 CE and the remaining strongholds of Herodium and Machaerus were taken, Masada was the last rebel position, holding some 960 people including women and children under Eleazar ben Ya'ir.

The siege

In the winter of 72/73 CE (or a year later), the governor Lucius Flavius Silva marched against Masada with Legio X Fretensis, auxiliary units, and thousands of Jewish prisoners as labourers. The Romans encircled the plateau with a circumvallation wall nearly 4 km long, studded with eight camps, preventing escape. Against the western face they built a massive siege ramp of earth and timber — still visible today — rising to the fortress walls; an iron-plated siege tower and battering ram were then pushed up the ramp to breach the wall. The defenders built an inner wall of timber and earth that absorbed the ram's blows, but Silva's men set it on fire.

The final night

According to Josephus, on the night before the Romans entered, Eleazar persuaded the defenders to kill themselves rather than be captured: the men killed their families, then drew lots to choose ten to kill the rest, and one of the ten to kill the others and himself. When the Romans stormed the fortress the next day, they found 960 dead and storehouses left intact — spared, Josephus says, to show that the defenders died by choice and not from want. Two women and five children survived by hiding in the water conduits and told the story to the Romans.

Historicity and legacy

Modern scholars question elements of Josephus's account, including the scale and manner of the suicide, and note parallels with other mass-suicide topoi in his writings (such as Jotapata). Excavations led by Yigael Yadin in 1963–65 uncovered the palaces, synagogue, ritual baths, scroll fragments, and skeletons of some 28 individuals — far fewer than Josephus's 960. In the 20th century Masada became a symbol of Jewish resistance — "Masada shall not fall again" — and a site of military ceremonies, while more recent scholarship and public debate treat the story with greater caution. Masada was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2001.
