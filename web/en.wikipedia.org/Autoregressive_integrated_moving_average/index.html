Autoregressive integrated moving average

FieldStatistics, econometrics
NotationARIMA(p, d, q)
Key figuresGeorge Box, Gwilym Jenkins
Used forTime series forecasting

In statistics and econometrics, and in particular in time series analysis, an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model is a generalization of an autoregressive moving average (ARMA) model. Both of these models are fitted to time series data either to better understand the data or to predict future points in the series (forecasting). ARIMA models are applied in some cases where data show evidence of non-stationarity, where an initial differencing step (corresponding to the "integrated" part of the model) can be applied one or more times to eliminate the non-stationarity of the mean function.

Definition

An ARIMA model is characterized by three terms: p, the order of the autoregressive part; d, the degree of first differencing involved; and q, the order of the moving average part. The model is usually denoted ARIMA(p, d, q). When d = 0 the model reduces to ARMA(p, q); when one of the other terms is zero, the model reduces to a pure autoregressive or pure moving-average model.

The Box–Jenkins approach

George Box and Gwilym Jenkins popularized the approach in their 1970 book Time Series Analysis: Forecasting and Control. The Box–Jenkins methodology involves three stages: model identification, parameter estimation, and diagnostic checking using residuals. Differencing is applied until the series appears stationary, often assessed using unit root tests such as the augmented Dickey–Fuller test.

Seasonal models

A seasonal extension, SARIMA, adds seasonal autoregressive, differencing, and moving-average terms at the seasonal lag, allowing the model to capture periodic patterns such as monthly or quarterly cycles.

Applications

ARIMA models are widely used in economics, finance, demand forecasting, and epidemiology, where they provide a flexible baseline against which more complex machine-learning methods are compared.
