Georgy Apollonovich Gapon (1870–1906) was a Russian Orthodox priest and popular working-class leader who organised the peaceful procession to petition Tsar Nicholas II on January 22, 1905 — Bloody Sunday — whose violent suppression ignited the Russian Revolution of 1905.

The Assembly of Factory Workers

With the knowledge of the police, Gapon founded the Assembly of Russian Factory and Mill Workers of St. Petersburg, a legal labour organisation that grew rapidly to some 8,000 members. When four members were dismissed from the Putilov Works in January 1905, a strike spread across the capital, and Gapon drafted a humble petition to the Tsar asking for reforms including an eight-hour day, fair wages, and a constituent assembly.

Bloody Sunday

On January 22 (January 9, Old Style), Gapon led a huge procession of workers, carrying icons and portraits of the Tsar, toward the Winter Palace. Soldiers fired on the crowds at several points, killing and wounding hundreds. Gapon escaped, famously crying that there was "no God any longer, no Tsar." The massacre destroyed the myth of the benevolent Tsar and set off strikes and mutinies across the empire.

Exile and death

Gapon fled abroad and associated with revolutionary parties, but after returning to Russia he entered into murky dealings with the police. In 1906 the Socialist Revolutionary Pinhas Rutenberg, who accused him of acting as a police agent, had him hanged at a cottage in Ozerki. Historians continue to debate Gapon's motives and sincerity.
