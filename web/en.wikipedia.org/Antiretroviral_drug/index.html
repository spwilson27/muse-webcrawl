Antiretroviral drug

Antiretroviral drugs are medications used to treat infections by retroviruses, primarily the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). When several such drugs are combined, the approach is known as antiretroviral therapy (ART), or highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART), which suppresses viral replication, allows immune recovery, and greatly reduces HIV-related illness and transmission.

Classes

Drug classes are distinguished by the stage of the viral life cycle they block: nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) such as tenofovir and emtricitabine; non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) such as efavirenz; protease inhibitors such as darunavir; integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs) such as dolutegravir; and entry inhibitors, including the CCR5 antagonist maraviroc and fusion inhibitors.

History

The first antiretroviral, zidovudine (AZT), was approved in 1987. Combination therapy introduced in the mid-1990s transformed HIV infection in wealthy countries from a fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition. The first single-tablet regimen was approved in 2006.

Use

Guidelines now recommend starting ART for all people living with HIV regardless of CD4 count. Antiretrovirals are also used for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), and prevention of mother-to-child transmission. Adherence is critical because missed doses can allow drug-resistant virus to emerge.
