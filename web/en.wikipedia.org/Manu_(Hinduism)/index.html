Manu (Hinduism)

Manu, the legendary first man and lawgiver.

Manu (Sanskrit: मनु) is a term of Hindu cosmology and tradition referring to the archetypal progenitor of humanity. In each cosmic cycle (kalpa), fourteen Manus are said to rule successively, each presiding over a period called a manvantara. The first Manu, Svayambhuva, is credited with authoring the Manusmriti (Laws of Manu), the most authoritative of the Dharma Shastra texts. The current age is said to be the reign of the seventh Manu, Vaivasvata, who is also the hero of the Hindu flood myth.

The fourteen Manus

Puranic literature lists fourteen Manus for each day of Brahma: Svayambhuva, Svarochisha, Uttama, Tamasa, Raivata, Chakshusha, Vaivasvata, Savarni, Daksha-savarni, Brahma-savarni, Dharma-savarni, Rudra-savarni, Raucya and Bhautya. Each manvantara lasts about 306.7 million human years, and each Manu is accompanied by his own Indra, gods and seven sages (saptarishi).

Vaivasvata Manu and the flood

Vaivasvata Manu, son of the sun god Vivasvan (Surya), is warned by Matsya, the fish avatar of Vishnu, of a coming deluge. Manu builds a great boat, saves his family, the seven sages, animals and the seeds of all plants, and after the waters recede repopulates the earth. The story, first told in the Shatapatha Brahmana and expanded in the Mahabharata and Puranas, is one of a family of flood myths found across cultures. His children include Ikshvaku, founder of the Solar dynasty of kings, and Ila.

Manu as lawgiver

The Manusmriti, composed in roughly the last centuries BCE and early centuries CE, presents itself as the discourse of Manu on dharma — law, caste duties, kingship, ritual and conduct. It became enormously influential in later Hindu jurisprudence and was one of the first Sanskrit texts translated into English, by Sir William Jones in 1794, profoundly shaping British colonial administration of Hindu law. Its provisions on caste and gender have also made it a focus of modern criticism and controversy.
