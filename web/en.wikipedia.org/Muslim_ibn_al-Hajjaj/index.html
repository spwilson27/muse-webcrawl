Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj

Abū al-Ḥusayn Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj ibn Muslim ibn Ward ibn Kawshādh al-Qushayrī an-Naysābūrī (c. 815 – 6 May 875), commonly known as Imam Muslim, was an Islamic scholar from Nishapur in Persia, particularly known as a muhaddith (scholar of hadith). His hadith collection, known as Sahih Muslim, is one of the six major hadith collections in Sunni Islam and is regarded by Sunni Muslims as the second most authentic collection after Sahih al-Bukhari.

Life

Muslim was born in Nishapur in northeastern Persia into a family of Arab descent belonging to the Qushayr tribe. He began studying hadith as a youth and travelled widely across the Islamic world — to the Hejaz, Egypt, Syria and Iraq — in pursuit of traditions, studying under leading teachers including Ahmad ibn Hanbal, and under Muhammad al-Bukhari, whose method he closely followed; the two remained on close terms. Tradition reports that he heard traditions from more than two hundred teachers. He supported himself through trade and was known for his wealth and generosity as well as his scholarship.

Sahih Muslim

His masterwork, al-Musnad al-Ṣaḥīḥ bi-Naql al-ʿAdl, universally known as Sahih Muslim, is said to have been selected from some 300,000 traditions he had collected, retaining only those he judged rigorously authentic — about 7,500 with repetitions, or some 4,000 without. Uniquely for its time, the work opens with a substantial introduction on the science of hadith criticism, and it arranges traditions topically with their chains of transmission (isnads) carefully collated so that variant wordings can be compared. Together with al-Bukhari's collection, the two books are known as the Sahihayn ("the two authentic collections").

Other works

Muslim wrote some two dozen works, several of which survive, including al-Kunā wa-l-Asmāʾ on narrators' names and patronymics, al-Munfaridāt wa-l-Wuḥdān on transmitters who narrate from a single teacher, and a book on the classes (tabaqat) of hadith transmitters.

Death and legacy

Muslim died at Nishapur in 875 and was buried there. His Sahih generated an extensive commentary literature, most famously the commentary of the 13th-century scholar al-Nawawi, and remains a foundational reference for Sunni law, theology and devotional practice.
