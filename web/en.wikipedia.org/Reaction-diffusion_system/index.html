Reaction–diffusion system

Reaction–diffusion systems are mathematical models describing how the concentration of one or more substances distributed in space changes under two processes: local chemical reactions, in which the substances are transformed into each other, and diffusion, which spreads them through space. They were introduced to biology by Alan Turing in his 1952 paper "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" and famously generate the patterns — spots, stripes, and spirals — found on animal coats and shells.

Equations

The canonical two-species system couples reaction kinetics f and g with diffusion terms for an activator and an inhibitor. When the inhibitor diffuses faster than the activator, homogeneous states can become unstable (a Turing instability), producing stationary periodic patterns.

Applications

Reaction–diffusion models are applied to animal pigmentation, seashell patterns, vegetation patterns in arid ecosystems, cardiac arrhythmias and spiral waves in excitable media, epidemic spread, and computer graphics, where they are used procedurally to generate organic-looking textures. Experimental chemical systems such as the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction exhibit related oscillation and wave behaviour.
