Difference engine

A difference engine is an automatic mechanical calculator designed to tabulate polynomial functions. Its name derives from the method of finite differences, which allows successive values of a polynomial to be computed by repeated addition alone, avoiding multiplication and division. The English mathematician Charles Babbage designed the most famous difference engines in the 1820s–1840s; although his full machines were never completed in his lifetime, working engines were built by others, and Babbage's design was finally constructed in 1991.

Difference Engine No. 2, built to Babbage's design in 1991.

History

Babbage proposed the engine in 1822 to eliminate errors in mathematical tables — crucial for navigation, astronomy and engineering. The British government funded Difference Engine No. 1, but escalating costs, disputes with his engineer Joseph Clement, and Babbage's shift to the more ambitious Analytical Engine led to its abandonment in 1842. Meanwhile the Swedish father-and-son team Georg and Edvard Scheutz completed a working difference engine in 1853, later used for producing tables.

Operation

The machine holds columns of decimal number wheels representing the successive differences of a polynomial. Each cycle adds each difference column to the next, mechanically and in parallel, so the tabulated values emerge automatically and can drive a printer to produce stereotype plates — eliminating transcription errors.

The 1991 construction

To mark the bicentenary of Babbage's birth, the Science Museum in London built Difference Engine No. 2 exactly to his drawings, using period-appropriate tolerances. It contains some 8,000 parts, weighs five tonnes, and works flawlessly — vindicating Babbage's design.
