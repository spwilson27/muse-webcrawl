Flintlock

The flintlock mechanism, showing the cock, flint, frizzen and pan.

Flintlock is a general term for any firearm that uses a flint-striking ignition mechanism, the first of which appeared in Western Europe in the early 16th century. The term may also apply to the mechanism itself, or to the complete weapon. Flintlock weapons were commonly used until the middle of the 19th century, when they were replaced by newer percussion lock systems. Although long superseded by modern firearms, flintlock weapons enjoy continuing popularity with black-powder shooting enthusiasts.

History

The flintlock developed from earlier spark-ignition systems such as the snaplock and snaphance. The true flintlock, in which a single steel plate (the frizzen) both covers the priming pan and provides the striking surface, is generally attributed to Marin le Bourgeoys, a French court gunmaker, around 1610–1615. Flintlocks offered substantial advantages over the matchlock: they needed no continuously burning slow match, were quicker to bring into action, could be kept loaded and ready, and were less vulnerable to rain being blown into the pan — though wet weather remained a serious enemy of all loose-powder ignition systems.

Flintlock muskets became the standard infantry arm of European armies through the 17th and 18th centuries, and were famously carried by both sides in the American Revolutionary War and the Napoleonic Wars. Rifled flintlock long arms, such as the American longrifle, were renowned for accuracy in skilled hands. The widespread adoption of the percussion cap in the 1820s–1840s, and later of self-contained metallic cartridges, ended the flintlock's military career.

Mechanism

The lock consists of a cock (hammer) holding a shaped piece of flint in its jaws; a hinged steel plate called the frizzen or battery, which covers the priming pan; and a trigger connected to a tumbler with half-cock (loading) and full-cock (firing) notches. When the trigger is pulled, the cock swings forward under mainspring pressure. The sharp edge of the flint scrapes down the face of the frizzen, shaving off white-hot particles of steel and pushing the frizzen open to expose the priming powder in the pan. The sparks ignite the priming charge, and flame passes through a touch-hole in the barrel to ignite the main propellant charge.

A well-made flintlock could fire reliably, but performance depended heavily on the quality and knapping of the flint, the temper of the frizzen, and dry powder. Misfires — a "flash in the pan" — were a familiar hazard of flintlock shooting.

Types

  • Snaphance — an early form with a separate steel and pan cover, popular in the late 16th century.
  • Miquelet lock — the Mediterranean pattern, used widely in Spain and Italy, with the mainspring external to the lock plate.
  • True flintlock — the French pattern that became standard across Europe and America.
  • Doglock — an English transitional form with an external safety catch or "dog".
