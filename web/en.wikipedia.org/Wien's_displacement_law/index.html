Wien's displacement law

Wien's displacement law states that the blackbody radiation curve for different temperatures will peak at different wavelengths that are inversely proportional to the temperature. The shift of that peak is a direct consequence of the Planck radiation law, which describes the spectral brightness or intensity of blackbody radiation as a function of wavelength at any given temperature. However, Wien's law was discovered by German physicist Wilhelm Wien a few years before Max Planck developed that more general equation, and describes the entire shift of the spectrum of blackbody radiation toward shorter wavelengths as temperature increases.

Formally, the wavelength of maximum emission is given by:

λpeak = b / T

where T is the absolute temperature in kelvins and b is a constant of proportionality called Wien's displacement constant, equal to 2.897771955 × 10−3 m·K, or more conveniently 2898 μm·K. If one is considering the peak of blackbody emission per unit frequency or per proportional bandwidth, one must use a different proportional constant.

Examples

The law explains everyday observations. A piece of iron heated in a fire first glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white as its temperature rises, because the peak of its emission moves from the infrared into the visible range. The Sun, with a surface temperature of about 5,778 K, peaks near 500 nm in the green part of the visible spectrum, though its broad emission appears yellow-white. Incandescent light bulbs, at roughly 2,700 K, emit mostly infrared and appear warm yellow. The cosmic microwave background, at 2.725 K, peaks in the microwave region near 1 mm wavelength.

History

Wilhelm Wien published the displacement law in 1893, building on his thermodynamic reasoning about an expanding cavity. His earlier, stronger claim about the full distribution (Wien's approximation) was superseded in 1900 by Planck's law, but the displacement relation for the peak survived unchanged as an exact consequence of the new theory. Wien received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1911 for his discoveries concerning the laws governing the radiation of heat.

Derivation

Setting the derivative of Planck's spectral radiance with respect to wavelength to zero yields a transcendental equation whose solution gives the constant b. In terms of frequency, the peak obeys a similar relation with a different constant, because of the nonlinear relationship between wavelength and frequency.
