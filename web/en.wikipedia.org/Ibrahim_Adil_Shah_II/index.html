Ibrahim Adil Shah II (1570 – 12 September 1627) was king of the Sultanate of Bijapur and a member of the Adil Shahi dynasty. Under him the sultanate reached its greatest extent, and he is credited with almost doubling the territory Bijapur held when he came to the throne. Ibrahim was a noted patron of the arts, music, and learning, and is remembered by the honorific Jagadguru ("teacher of the world").

Early life and accession

Ibrahim succeeded his uncle Ali Adil Shah I in 1580, while still a child, after Ali was assassinated. The early years of his reign were marked by factional struggles at court between the Deccani and Afaqi (foreigner) nobles, which he gradually brought under control as he came of age.

Reign

Ibrahim expanded Bijapur's frontiers, annexing territory from the neighbouring Ahmadnagar Sultanate and campaigning in the south. He maintained relatively tolerant religious policies and employed Hindus in high office. His court at Bijapur became a celebrated centre of painting, calligraphy, and music.

Patronage of the arts

Ibrahim was himself a musician and poet. His Kitab-e-Navras ("Book of the Nine Rasas"), a collection of songs in Dakhani Urdu, invokes Hindu deities and Muslim saints alike, reflecting the syncretic culture of his court. Bijapur painting flourished under his patronage, producing some of the finest Deccani miniatures.

Ibrahim Rauza

His tomb complex, the Ibrahim Rauza in Bijapur, built during his lifetime, is considered one of the masterpieces of Indo-Islamic architecture, its elegant proportions sometimes compared with those of the Taj Mahal, which it predates.

Death and succession

Ibrahim died in 1627 and was succeeded by his son Mohammed Adil Shah, under whom the great Gol Gumbaz was later raised.
