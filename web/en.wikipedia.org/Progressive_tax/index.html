Progressive tax

A progressive tax is a tax in which the tax rate increases as the taxable amount increases. The term applies to the way the tax rate progresses from low to high, with the result that a taxpayer's average tax rate is less than the person's marginal tax rate. The term can be applied to individual taxes or to a tax system as a whole. Progressive taxes are imposed in an attempt to reduce the tax incidence of people with a lower ability to pay, as such taxes shift the incidence increasingly to those with a higher ability-to-pay. The opposite of a progressive tax is a regressive tax, such as a sales tax, where the poor pay a larger proportion of their income compared to the rich.

Characteristics

The term is frequently applied in reference to personal income taxes, in which people with lower income pay a lower percentage of that income in tax than do those with higher income. It can also apply to adjustments of the tax base by using tax exemptions, tax credits, or selective taxation that creates progressive distribution effects. For example, a wealth tax, a sales tax on luxury goods, or the exemption of basic necessities may be described as having progressive effects, as it increases the tax burden of higher income families and reduces it on lower income families.

Marginal and effective rates

In a progressive income tax system, income is divided into brackets, each taxed at a different rate. A taxpayer in the highest bracket pays that top rate only on the portion of income falling within that bracket, not on their entire income. This distinction between the marginal tax rate (the rate on the last dollar earned) and the effective tax rate (total tax divided by total income) is central to understanding how progressive systems operate in practice.

History

Progressive taxation has roots in the work of economists such as Adam Smith, who wrote in The Wealth of Nations (1776) that subjects ought to contribute "in proportion to their respective abilities; that is, in proportion to the revenue which they respectively enjoy under the protection of the state." Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels advocated graduated income taxes in The Communist Manifesto (1848). In the United States, the first progressive income tax was imposed during the American Civil War, and the modern federal income tax was established following ratification of the Sixteenth Amendment in 1913.

Arguments for and against

Supporters argue that progressive taxation promotes fairness, reduces income inequality, and funds public services in line with citizens' ability to pay. Critics argue that high marginal rates can discourage work, saving, and investment, complicate the tax code, and encourage tax avoidance. Economists continue to debate the optimal degree of progressivity, with many tax systems combining progressive income taxes with broadly proportional or regressive consumption taxes.
