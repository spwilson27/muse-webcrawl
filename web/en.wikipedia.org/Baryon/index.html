In particle physics, a baryon is a composite subatomic particle made of an odd number of valence quarks — in the standard case, three. Baryons belong to the hadron family and are fermions, obeying Fermi–Dirac statistics. The most familiar baryons are the proton and the neutron, which together are called nucleons and make up almost all of the mass of ordinary matter. The name comes from the Greek barys, "heavy," reflecting that the particles first given the name were much heavier than the other class then known, the leptons ("light").

Properties and classification

Baryons carry baryon number +1 (antibaryons −1), a quantity conserved in all observed interactions of the Standard Model. They are classified by their quark content into families: nucleons (up and down quarks), delta baryons, lambda, sigma, xi (containing a strange quark), charmed and bottom baryons, and the omega baryons. Because quarks carry colour charge, baryons combine one quark of each colour — red, green, and blue — producing a colour-neutral whole, as required by quantum chromodynamics.

Stability and decay

The proton, the lightest baryon, is stable or has an extraordinarily long lifetime (experimental lower bounds exceed 10³⁴ years), a consequence of baryon number conservation. Free neutrons decay in about fifteen minutes into a proton, electron, and antineutrino via the weak interaction. All heavier baryons decay rapidly through chains of strong or weak decays ending in the lightest baryons.

Baryons in cosmology

Almost all of the visible matter in the universe is baryonic, and the cosmic excess of baryons over antibaryons — the baryon asymmetry — is one of the outstanding open problems in physics, addressed by the Sakharov conditions for baryogenesis. The cosmic ratio of photons to baryons, measured from the cosmic microwave background, fixes the baryon density of the universe at roughly five per cent of its total energy content.
