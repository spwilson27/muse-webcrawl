Bushido

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bushidō (武士道, "the way of the warrior") is a code of conduct and way of life associated with the samurai, the warrior class of feudal Japan. It emphasises virtues such as loyalty, honour, courage, frugality, martial skill and self-sacrifice, and was shaped by Zen Buddhism, Confucianism and Shinto.

Seven virtues

Popular accounts, especially Nitobe Inazō's 1899 book Bushido: The Soul of Japan, summarise the code in seven virtues: rectitude (義), courage (勇), benevolence (仁), respect (礼), honesty (誠), honour (名誉) and loyalty (忠義), with self-control sometimes added as an eighth.

Development

The values of the warrior class evolved over centuries, from the mounted archers of the Heian and Kamakura periods through the codified house laws of the Sengoku era to the more philosophical, Confucian-influenced writings of the peaceful Edo period, such as the Hagakure. Only in the Meiji era was "bushido" popularised as a unified national ethic, and its modern form owes much to that period's nation-building. Historians note that the tidy, uniform code is partly a modern construction.

Legacy

Bushido ideals influenced Japanese military conduct into the twentieth century and continue to be invoked in martial arts, popular culture and discussions of Japanese identity, while remaining a subject of scholarly debate and criticism.
