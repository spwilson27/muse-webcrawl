In mathematics and abstract algebra, group theory studies the algebraic structures known as groups. The concept of a group is central to abstract algebra: other well-known algebraic structures — rings, fields and vector spaces — can all be seen as groups endowed with additional operations and axioms. Groups recur throughout mathematics, and the methods of group theory have influenced many parts of algebra. Because groups encode symmetry, group theory is also a unifying language for geometry (through Klein's Erlangen program), number theory (through Galois theory and class field theory) and the physical sciences, where symmetry principles govern the laws of nature.

History

Group theory originated in three 19th-century sources: the theory of algebraic equations (Lagrange, Ruffini, Abel and Galois, who connected the solvability of a polynomial equation to the structure of the group of permutations of its roots), number theory (Euler and Gauss, working with modular arithmetic and abelian groups), and geometry (the transformation groups of Klein and Sophus Lie). Camille Jordan's Traité des substitutions (1870) systematised permutation groups; Cayley, Walther von Dyck and others developed the abstract definition; and the Burnside problem and the theory of group presentations shaped the early 20th century. The odd-order theorem of Feit and Thompson (1963) and the classification of finite simple groups, announced complete in the early 1980s, were landmarks of the modern era.

Main classes of groups

  • Permutation groups — groups of bijections of a set, including the symmetric and alternating groups.
  • Abelian groups — commutative groups, classified for the finitely generated case by a fundamental theorem relating them to free abelian groups and torsion.
  • Matrix groups and Lie groups — continuous groups of invertible matrices, central to geometry and physics.
  • Finite simple groups — the "atoms" of finite group theory: cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups, groups of Lie type, and 26 sporadic groups including the Monster.
  • Infinite discrete groups — studied by geometric group theory through presentations, Cayley graphs and actions on spaces.

Applications

In physics, Lie groups describe the symmetries of space-time and of fundamental forces; the Standard Model is built on the gauge group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1), and Noether's theorem connects symmetries to conservation laws. Chemists use point groups to classify molecular symmetry, spectra and crystal structures (the 230 space groups). Group theory underlies public-key cryptography and error-correcting codes, and appears in combinatorics via Burnside's lemma and Pólya enumeration. The possible moves of a Rubik's Cube form a group of some 4.3 × 1019 elements, a popular illustration of the theory.

Key theorems

  • Lagrange's theorem: the order of a subgroup divides the group order.
  • Cayley's theorem: every group is isomorphic to a permutation group.
  • The Sylow theorems on subgroups of prime-power order.
  • The isomorphism theorems and the Jordan–Hölder theorem.
  • The classification of finite simple groups.
