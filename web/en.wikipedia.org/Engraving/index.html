Engraving

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Engraving is the practice of incising a design onto a hard, usually flat surface by cutting grooves into it with a burin. The result may be a decorated object in itself, as when silver, gold, steel, or glass are engraved, or may provide an intaglio printing plate, of copper or another metal, for printing images on paper as prints or illustrations; these images are also called "engravings". Engraving was historically an important method of producing images on paper in artistic printmaking, in mapmaking, and for commercial reproductions and illustrations for books and magazines.

Technique

The engraver pushes a sharpened steel tool, the burin or graver, across the metal plate, throwing up a clean shaving and leaving a V-section groove. Lines swell and taper as pressure varies, giving engraving its characteristic crisp, controlled quality; tone is built from hatching and cross-hatching. The plate is inked, wiped so that ink remains only in the grooves, and printed under high pressure on dampened paper in an intaglio press.

History

Engraving on metal for printing developed in the Upper Rhine region around the 1430s–40s, growing out of goldsmiths' practice. Martin Schongauer raised it to an art, and Albrecht Dürer brought it to a peak of virtuosity around 1500. For three centuries engraving was Europe's principal means of reproducing images: reproductive engravers translated paintings into prints, and engraved plates illustrated books, maps and banknotes. From the 19th century, photography-based processes displaced commercial engraving, though steel engraving long survived for currency and security printing, where its fine lines resist forgery.

Modern practice

Today engraving continues in fine-art printmaking, in hand engraving of jewellery, firearms and trophies, and industrially through laser engraving and computer-controlled rotary engraving on plastics, metals and stone. The term is also loosely applied to inscriptions cut into monuments and memorials.
