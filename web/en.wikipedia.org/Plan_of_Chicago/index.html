Plan of Chicago

Published1909
AuthorsDaniel Burnham and Edward H. Bennett
SponsorCommercial Club of Chicago
Also known asThe Burnham Plan
Famous maxim"Make no little plans"

The Plan of Chicago, published in 1909 and often called the Burnham Plan, was a landmark comprehensive urban plan for the city of Chicago prepared by architect Daniel Burnham and his associate Edward H. Bennett, sponsored by the Commercial Club of Chicago. It is credited with founding modern city planning in the United States and shaping Chicago's lakefront, park system, and street layout.

Contents

The plan proposed six great improvements: reclaiming the lakefront as public parkland; a regional highway system; consolidation and relocation of railway terminals; a ring of forest preserves and parks around the city; systematic diagonal avenues cutting through the street grid; and a monumental civic center of government buildings west of the Loop. Jules Guerin's lavish watercolor renderings helped sell the vision to the public.

Implementation

Although the civic center was never built, much else was realized over the following decades: the lakefront parks and beaches (protected as public ground, in line with A. Montgomery Ward's litigation), Michigan Avenue Bridge, Wacker Drive, Navy Pier, the consolidation of Union Station, many diagonal streets, and the Cook County forest preserve district. Burnham's attributed exhortation — "Make no little plans; they have no magic to stir men's blood" — became a motto of the planning profession.

Legacy

The plan inaugurated the City Beautiful era of American planning and led to planning commissions across the country. Burnham and Bennett produced similar plans for San Francisco, Cleveland, and Manila and Baguio in the Philippines. Modern critics note the plan's neglect of housing and social issues, even as its physical framework continues to organize Chicago.
