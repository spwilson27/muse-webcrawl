Mole (espionage)


Also called: Penetration agent, deep-cover agent
Popularized by: John le Carré's novels

In espionage, a mole (also called a "penetration agent", "deep cover agent", or "sleeper agent") is a long-term spy who is recruited before having access to secret intelligence, and who then works their way into the target organization, often over years or decades, before beginning to pass information to their handlers.

Terminology

The term was popularized by the novelist John le Carré in Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy (1974), whose plot centres on the hunt for a Soviet mole at the top of British intelligence; le Carré said the KGB's own term was krot ("mole"). Intelligence agencies often avoid the word, preferring "penetration agent" or "agent in place". A mole differs from a defector or a walk-in, who volunteers information already in their possession.

Notable cases

Famous moles include Kim Philby and the other members of the Cambridge Five, who penetrated British intelligence on behalf of the Soviet Union; Aldrich Ames, a CIA officer who compromised numerous Soviet assets of the CIA; and Robert Hanssen, an FBI agent who spied for the Soviet Union and Russia for over twenty years. Within the Soviet Union, Oleg Penkovsky provided valuable intelligence to the West during the early 1960s, including during the Cuban Missile Crisis.

Counterintelligence

The search for moles — mole hunts — can be enormously damaging in itself: the suspicions of CIA counterintelligence chief James Jesus Angleton, fuelled by defector Anatoliy Golitsyn, paralysed parts of the agency's Soviet operations in the 1960s and 1970s, an episode later described as a "wilderness of mirrors".
