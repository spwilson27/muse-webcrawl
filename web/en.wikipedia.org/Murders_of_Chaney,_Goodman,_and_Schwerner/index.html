The murders of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner, also known as the Freedom Summer murders, took place in Mississippi in the United States during the Civil Rights Movement on the night of June 21–22, 1964. Three activists — James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner — were abducted and murdered in Neshoba County, Mississippi, by members of the Ku Klux Klan working with local law enforcement officers.

The three men had been working with the Council of Federated Organizations (COFO) and the Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) during Freedom Summer, a campaign to register African-American voters in Mississippi. Their disappearance drew national attention; their bodies were found buried in an earthen dam on August 4, 1964, after an extensive FBI investigation code-named MIBURN ("Mississippi Burning").

Investigation and trials

Because Mississippi authorities declined to prosecute for murder, the United States Department of Justice charged 18 individuals with conspiracy to violate the victims' civil rights. In 1967, seven defendants were convicted, including Deputy Sheriff Cecil Price; none served more than six years. In 2005, the state of Mississippi prosecuted Edgar Ray Killen, a Klansman who had helped organise the killings; he was convicted of three counts of manslaughter and sentenced to 60 years in prison, dying in custody in 2018.

Legacy

The murders galvanised public support for the civil rights cause and contributed to the passage of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. The case inspired the 1988 film Mississippi Burning.
