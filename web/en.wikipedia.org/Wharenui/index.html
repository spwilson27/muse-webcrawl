Wharenui

A wharenui, showing the tekoteko and koruru carvings at the front gable

A wharenui ([ˈfaɾɛnʉi]; literally "large house") is a communal meeting house of the Māori people of New Zealand, and the focal point of a marae. Generally situated at the centre of the marae complex and facing an open courtyard (marae ātea), the wharenui serves as a venue for meetings, funerals (tangihanga), weddings, celebrations and other important tribal occasions.

Architecture and symbolism

The wharenui is richly symbolic: the building is regarded as the body of an ancestor of the tribe. The carved figure at the apex of the front gable, the koruru, represents the ancestor's head; the sloping bargeboards (maihi) are his arms, ending in fingers (raparapa); the ridgepole (tāhuhu) is his backbone; the rafters (heke) are his ribs; and the interior is his belly (te puku). The central post supporting the ridge, the poutokomanawa, is the ancestor's heart.

Wharenui are typically decorated with intricate whakairo (carving), tukutuku (woven lattice panels of flax and other materials), and kōwhaiwhai (painted scroll patterns, typically in red, black and white) on the rafters. The interior carved wall panels (poupou) depict tribal ancestors and their stories, so that the house embodies the genealogy (whakapapa) and history of the iwi or hapū.

History

Wharenui developed from the chief's house (whare whakairo, carved house) of pre-European times. Large, elaborately carved meeting houses became widespread from the mid-nineteenth century, as iwi built them as statements of identity and prestige, sometimes as symbols of resistance during and after the New Zealand Wars. Famous examples include Te Hau-ki-Tūranga (1840s, now in the Museum of New Zealand Te Papa Tongarewa), Hinemihi at Clandon Park in England, and the meeting houses of the Arawa and Ngāti Porou carving schools led by masters such as Āpirana Ngata.

Most wharenui are named after an ancestor, and are entered under strict protocol: visitors remove their shoes and do not eat inside, as the house is sacred (tapu). The welcome ceremony (pōwhiri) on the marae ātea precedes entry into the house.
