Abelian group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an abelian group, also called a commutative group, is a group in which the result of applying the group operation to two group elements does not depend on the order in which they are written. That is, for all a and b in the group, a · b = b · a.

Definition and examples

The concept is named after Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel. Familiar examples include the integers under addition, the rational and real numbers under addition, and the cyclic groups. Every cyclic group is abelian, but the converse is not true. Non-examples include the symmetric group S3 and the group of rotations and reflections of a square.

Structure theory

The fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups states that every finite abelian group is the direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order. Finitely generated abelian groups decompose as a direct sum of a free abelian group (isomorphic to Zr) and a finite torsion part. Abelian groups can be identified with modules over the ring Z, a viewpoint central to homological algebra and algebraic topology.
