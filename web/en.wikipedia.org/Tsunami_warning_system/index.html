Tsunami warning system

A tsunami warning system is a system used to detect tsunamis in advance and issue warnings to prevent loss of life and property damage. It is made of two equally important components: a network of sensors to detect tsunamis, and a communications infrastructure to issue timely alarms permitting evacuation of coastal areas.

History

The first rudimentary system was established in Hawaii after the devastating 1946 Aleutian Islands tsunami, leading to the creation of the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC) in 1949. The catastrophic 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, in which no warning system existed for that ocean, spurred the creation of the Indian Ocean Tsunami Warning System and upgrades worldwide.

How it works

Warning centres monitor seismic stations to detect undersea earthquakes capable of generating tsunamis. Sea-level evidence comes from coastal tide gauges and from deep-ocean DART (Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis) stations — anchored buoys whose bottom-pressure recorders detect the minute pressure change of a passing tsunami and relay it by satellite. Forecast models then predict arrival times and wave heights for coastal locations. Alerts are disseminated through emergency broadcast systems, sirens, text messages and media.

Regional systems

Regional centres include the PTWC and the US National Tsunami Warning Center, Japan's Meteorological Agency system (the world's most rapid, issuing warnings within minutes), and centres in India, Australia and Indonesia under UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission coordination. Because a locally generated tsunami can arrive within minutes, authorities stress that strong or long shaking, or unusual sea behaviour, should itself be treated as a warning to move inland or uphill immediately.
