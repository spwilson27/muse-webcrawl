Pulp (tooth)

The dental pulp is the soft connective tissue at the centre of a tooth, containing nerves, blood vessels, and odontoblasts, enclosed by dentine within the pulp chamber and root canals.

The pulp, or dental pulp, is the part in the centre of a tooth made of living connective tissue and cells called odontoblasts. It occupies the pulp chamber in the crown and the root canals of the roots, communicating with the tissues around the tooth through the apical foramen at the root tip.

Structure and function

The pulp contains nerves, arterioles, venules, lymphatic tissue, and undifferentiated mesenchymal cells capable of forming new odontoblasts. Its functions include forming dentine (dentinogenesis), nourishing the tooth, sensing stimuli such as heat, cold, and pressure, and mounting a defensive response to injury by laying down reparative dentine. A peripheral layer of odontoblasts lines the pulp and extends processes into the dentinal tubules.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the pulp, called pulpitis, is most often caused by bacterial invasion through deep caries or trauma; reversible pulpitis produces transient sensitivity, while irreversible pulpitis causes spontaneous, lingering pain and may progress to pulp necrosis and periapical infection. Treatment options include pulp capping, pulpotomy, root canal therapy (which removes the pulp and fills the canal system), or extraction. With age, the pulp chamber shrinks as secondary dentine is deposited, and calcified "pulp stones" may form.
