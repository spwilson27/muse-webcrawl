The Malacca Sultanate (Malay: Kesultanan Melayu Melaka) was a Malay Muslim sultanate based in the modern-day state of Malacca, Malaysia. Founded around 1400 by Parameswara, a prince from Palembang, it grew into the dominant trading power of Southeast Asia, controlling the Strait of Malacca through which most trade between China and India passed.

Malacca at its height was among the busiest entrepôts in the world.

History

Parameswara converted to Islam and took the name Iskandar Shah. Under his successors, Malacca adopted Islam as the state religion, which helped attract Muslim Gujarati, Persian and Arab merchants. The sultanate maintained close tributary relations with Ming China—Admiral Zheng He's treasure fleets called repeatedly—and expanded its influence over the eastern coast of Sumatra and much of the Malay Peninsula. Its golden age came under Mansur Shah and Alauddin Riayat Shah, administered famously by the bendahara Tun Perak.

Trade and society

Malacca's port was governed by harbour-masters (shahbandars) assigned to different merchant communities; its legal codes, the Undang-Undang Melaka and maritime laws, regulated commerce. The Malay language, written in Jawi script, became the lingua franca of regional trade, and Malacca was the centre from which Islam spread across the archipelago.

Fall

In August 1511 a Portuguese fleet under Afonso de Albuquerque captured the city after fierce fighting. Sultan Mahmud Shah fled and continued resistance from Bintan and Kampar; his descendants founded the Johor Sultanate, which inherited Malacca's mantle.
