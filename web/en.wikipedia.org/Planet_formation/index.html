Planet formation

Planet formation
A protoplanetary disk, the birthplace of planets

Key stagesDisk formation, planetesimals, protoplanets, accretion

Planet formation is the process by which planets form from the gas and dust surrounding a young star. According to the widely accepted nebular hypothesis, stars form within collapsing clouds of interstellar gas and dust; the material that does not fall directly onto the star settles into a rotating protoplanetary disk, from which planets, moons, asteroids and comets subsequently assemble. The discovery of thousands of exoplanets since the 1990s, and direct imaging of disks with gaps and rings carved by young planets (such as the HL Tauri system observed by ALMA), has transformed planet formation from a purely theoretical subject into an observational science.

The protoplanetary disk

As a molecular cloud core collapses under gravity, conservation of angular momentum flattens the infalling material into a disk around the protostar. The disk is hot near the star and cold in its outer reaches; the location of the "snow line", beyond which water and other volatiles can condense as ice, increases the supply of solid material severalfold and is thought to explain why giant planets form preferentially beyond it. Over a few million years, viscous processes drive most of the gas inward onto the star or disperse it, setting a deadline for planet formation.

From dust to planetesimals

The first growth stage turns micrometre-sized dust grains into kilometre-sized planetesimals. Grains collide and stick at low relative speeds, but growth stalls at the so-called metre-size barrier, where bodies drift rapidly toward the star and fragment in collisions. Proposed solutions include the streaming instability, in which dense clumps of pebbles collapse gravitationally directly into planetesimals of tens to hundreds of kilometres across.

Building planets

Planetesimals then grow by accretion: a few large embryos enter a phase of runaway and then oligarchic growth, sweeping up their feeding zones. Terrestrial planets assemble over tens of millions of years through giant impacts between embryos — the Moon is thought to be the product of one such collision between the young Earth and a Mars-sized body. In the core accretion model for giant planets, a core of about ten Earth masses forms first and then rapidly captures a massive hydrogen-helium envelope from the disk before the gas disappears; the competing gravitational instability model instead forms giant planets by direct fragmentation of the disk. Many systems then undergo large-scale migration and dynamical rearrangement, which may explain "hot Jupiters" and the compact architectures found by the Kepler mission.
