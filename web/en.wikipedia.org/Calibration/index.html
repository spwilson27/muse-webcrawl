Calibration

Calibration is the set of operations that establish, under specified conditions, the relationship between values indicated by a measuring instrument or system and the corresponding values realised by standards. In measurement technology it means comparing an instrument against a traceable reference standard of known accuracy and documenting the deviation, so that the instrument's readings can be corrected and their uncertainty quantified.

Process

A typical calibration involves: selecting a reference standard with an unbroken chain of traceability to national or international standards (such as those maintained by NIST or other national metrology institutes); exposing both standard and test instrument to a series of known values across the instrument's range; recording the deviations; and adjusting the instrument, where possible, to minimise them. Results are recorded on a calibration certificate with the measurement uncertainty.

Why calibrate

Instruments drift over time through wear, environmental effects and use. Regular calibration at defined intervals maintains accuracy, ensures the quality and consistency of measurements, supports compliance with quality standards such as ISO 9001, and provides legal defensibility of measurements in trade, safety and science. Calibration applies to physical instruments (scales, thermometers, pressure gauges, torque wrenches), electronic equipment, and also — by extension — to probability forecasts, statistical models and monitor colour profiles.

Calibration vs adjustment

Strictly, calibration is the comparison and documentation; adjustment is the separate act of changing the instrument so that it reads correctly. A calibration may be performed before and after adjustment.
