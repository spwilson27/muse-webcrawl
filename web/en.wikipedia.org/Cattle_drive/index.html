Cattle drive

A cattle drive is the process of moving a herd of cattle from one place to another, usually by a group of cowboys on horses. The classic era of the American cattle drive, in the decades after the Civil War (roughly 1866 to the mid-1880s), saw millions of longhorn cattle driven north from Texas to railheads in Kansas and beyond, where they could be shipped to markets in the East.

History

After the Civil War, Texas held vast herds of semi-wild longhorn cattle that were worth little locally but could fetch high prices in northern cities. Trails such as the Chisholm Trail, the Goodnight–Loving Trail, the Western Trail, and the Shawnee Trail carried herds to rail towns including Abilene, Dodge City, and Ellsworth. Famous trail bosses and cattle barons, among them Charles Goodnight and Joseph McCoy, shaped the business. The drives declined as railroads reached Texas, barbed wire fenced the open range, and quarantine laws restricted the movement of Texas cattle.

The drive

A typical trail crew consisted of a trail boss, a cook, a horse wrangler, and about a dozen drovers for a herd of two to three thousand head. The chuck wagon carried food and bedrolls. Cattle moved 10 to 15 miles a day, grazing as they went. Hazards included river crossings, drought, lightning, and stampedes, which could be triggered by storms or sudden noises at night; night riders circled the herd, singing to calm the animals.

In popular culture

The cattle drive became a central image of the American West in dime novels, films such as Red River (1948), and the television series Rawhide. Modern recreational and commemorative drives are still held in parts of the United States and Australia, where droving over stock routes has a parallel tradition.
