Belizean–Guatemalan territorial dispute

The Belizean–Guatemalan territorial dispute is an unresolved territorial dispute between Belize and Guatemala, neighbours in Central America. Guatemala has long claimed sovereignty over part or all of the territory of Belize, basing its claim on the argument that it inherited Spain's colonial rights and that the 1859 Wyke–Aycinena Treaty, which recognised British Honduras's boundaries in exchange for a British-funded road, lapsed when the road was never built. Belize maintains that the treaty remains valid and that in any case its right to self-determination, affirmed when it became independent from the United Kingdom in 1981, settles the matter.

History

British logwood cutters settled the coast of the Bay of Honduras from the 17th century, and treaties between Britain and Spain permitted logging while recognising Spanish sovereignty. The 1859 treaty between Britain and Guatemala fixed the boundary of British Honduras; Article VII promised a cart road from Guatemala City to the Atlantic coast with shared financing. The road was never completed, and Guatemala later argued the treaty had been voided, reviving its claim. In the 20th century Guatemala periodically threatened invasion, and Britain maintained a military presence in Belize well after independence in 1981. Guatemala recognised Belize's independence in 1991 but without abandoning its claim.

Negotiations and referendums

A series of facilitated negotiations under the Organization of American States produced proposals, including a 2002 Ramphal–Reichler settlement that both governments failed to ratify. In 2008 the two countries agreed to submit the dispute to the International Court of Justice if approved by referendums. Guatemala voted in favour in April 2018, and Belize voted in favour in May 2019. Guatemala filed its application with the ICJ in June 2019, and the case — covering the land boundary, insular territories, and maritime areas — remains pending.

Incidents

The disputed western and southern borderlands, especially the "adjacency zone" monitored by the OAS, have seen periodic incidents involving Guatemalan settlers, loggers, and gold panners inside Belizean reserves, and occasional confrontations with Belize Defence Force patrols.
