Root canal treatment

Root canal treatment (also known as endodontic therapy, endodontic treatment, or root canal therapy) is a treatment sequence for the infected pulp of a tooth which is intended to result in the elimination of infection and the protection of the decontaminated tooth from future microbial invasion. Root canals, and their associated pulp chamber, are the physical hollows within a tooth that are naturally inhabited by nerve tissue, blood vessels and other cellular entities.

When it is needed

Treatment is indicated when the dental pulp becomes irreversibly inflamed or necrotic, usually from deep decay, repeated dental procedures, or trauma such as a cracked tooth. Symptoms may include prolonged sensitivity to heat or cold, spontaneous pain, pain on biting, or a swelling; sometimes a chronically infected tooth causes no pain at all. Without treatment, infection can spread to the surrounding bone as a dental abscess.

Procedure

Under local anaesthetic and usually a rubber dam, the dentist or endodontist opens the tooth, removes the pulp tissue, and cleans, shapes and disinfects the root canal system with fine files and irrigants such as sodium hypochlorite. The canals are then filled — obturated — most commonly with gutta-percha and sealer, and the tooth is sealed. Because a treated tooth becomes more brittle and has usually lost substantial structure, posterior teeth are typically restored with a crown. Modern treatment often uses rotary nickel-titanium instruments, apex locators, operating microscopes, and sometimes CBCT imaging.

Outcomes

Success rates are generally high — systematic reviews report survival or success of roughly 85–95% over several years depending on definitions and the tooth's initial condition. Failure, from persistent or re-introduced infection, may be managed by non-surgical retreatment or apical surgery (apicoectomy), or the tooth may be extracted. Despite its reputation, treatment with proper anaesthesia is usually no more painful than a filling.
