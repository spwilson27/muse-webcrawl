Typhoid fever

Typhoid fever, also known simply as typhoid, is a bacterial infection caused by Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica serovar Typhi. Symptoms vary from mild to severe and usually begin six to thirty days after exposure. Classically there is a gradual onset of a high fever over several days, accompanied by weakness, abdominal pain, constipation, and headaches; diarrhea is uncommon. Some people develop a skin rash of flat, rose-colored spots. Without treatment, symptoms may last weeks or months, and complications can include intestinal perforation and bleeding, which may be fatal.

Transmission and diagnosis

The bacterium is spread through food or water contaminated with the feces of an infected person. Humans are the only natural reservoir; some infected people become chronic carriers, continuing to shed bacteria while asymptomatic — the best-known example being Mary Mallon, "Typhoid Mary," a cook in New York in the early 1900s. Diagnosis is confirmed by culturing the bacterium from blood, stool, or bone marrow, though in many endemic settings it is diagnosed clinically or by the Widal test.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention rests on clean water, sanitation, and hand hygiene. Several typhoid vaccines exist, including an oral live vaccine and injectable conjugate vaccines recommended by the World Health Organization for endemic regions. Typhoid is treated with antibiotics such as azithromycin, fluoroquinolones, or ceftriaxone, but resistance — including extensively drug-resistant strains that emerged in Pakistan from 2016 — is an increasing problem. An estimated 9 million people contract typhoid annually, with around 110,000 deaths; the disease remains common in parts of Asia and Africa with poor sanitation.
