Rabbinic Judaism

Rabbinic Judaism
Mainstream form of Judaism since the 6th century CE
Based on the Written and Oral Torah

Rabbinic Judaism (Hebrew: יהדות רבנית), also called Rabbinism, has been a mainstream form of Judaism since the 6th century CE, after the codification of the Babylonian Talmud. It is characterized by the belief that the Written Torah ("Law", the first five books of the Hebrew Bible) cannot be correctly understood without reference to the Oral Torah, much of which was oral until written down in the Mishnah and Talmud.

Origins

Rabbinic Judaism developed after the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, when the Pharisaic school, under leaders such as Yohanan ben Zakkai at Yavneh, reshaped Jewish life around prayer, study and law rather than Temple sacrifice. The Mishnah was compiled under Judah the Prince around 200 CE; the Jerusalem and Babylonian Talmuds followed over the next several centuries.

Beliefs and practice

Rabbinic tradition holds that Moses received both the Written Torah and an oral tradition at Mount Sinai. Rabbinic literature — midrash, halakha and aggadah — interprets the Bible and applies it to daily life. Authority rests with ordained rabbis and a long chain of commentary, from the Geonim and medieval codifiers such as Maimonides to the Shulchan Aruch of Joseph Karo.

Branches

Nearly all modern Jewish denominations — Orthodox, Conservative, Reform and Reconstructionist — descend from the rabbinic tradition, though they differ in their view of halakha's binding authority.
