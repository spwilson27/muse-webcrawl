History of sanitation

The history of sanitation covers the development of systems for the disposal of human waste and the provision of clean water from the earliest civilisations to the present day. Sanitation has been one of the most important determinants of public health: improvements in water supply and sewerage are credited with much of the dramatic increase in life expectancy since the 19th century.

Ancient sanitation

The cities of the Indus Valley Civilization (c. 2600–1900 BC), such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, had sophisticated covered drains, bathing platforms and houses connected to street sewers — the earliest known urban sanitation systems. Mesopotamian and Minoan settlements also built drains and early flush latrines; the palace of Knossos had a flushing toilet in the second millennium BC. Ancient Egyptian homes used sand-filled boxes and limestone seats.

Roman sanitation

Rome built eleven aqueducts supplying hundreds of millions of litres of water daily, public baths, multi-seat public latrines flushed by running water, and the Cloaca Maxima, one of the world's earliest sewer systems, parts of which still function. Roman engineers used lead and terracotta pipes. However, standards collapsed in the Latin West after the fall of the empire.

Medieval and early modern period

Medieval towns relied on cesspits, chamber pots emptied into streets, and "night soil" collectors. Castles had garderobes discharging into moats or pits. Monastic houses maintained comparatively advanced water systems. Epidemics such as the Black Death (1347–51) were not curable by sanitation, but the perceived link between filth and disease slowly strengthened. In 1596 John Harington described a flush toilet for Elizabeth I, but the device did not spread for over two centuries.

The sanitary revolution

The 19th century brought the "sanitary revolution". In London, Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population argued that disease was caused by filth and miasma and demanded drainage and clean water. During a cholera outbreak in 1854, John Snow traced cases to a contaminated pump in Broad Street, Soho, founding epidemiology and demonstrating waterborne transmission. After the Great Stink of 1858, Parliament authorised Joseph Bazalgette's intercepting sewer network for London — 1,800 km of sewers that transformed the city. Germ theory, developed by Pasteur and Lister, gave the movement a scientific basis, and cities across Europe and North America built sewers, filtration plants and, later, chlorination systems.

20th century

The flush toilet, patented in improved forms by Alexander Cummings (1775) and popularised by makers such as Thomas Crapper, became universal in developed countries. Activated-sludge sewage treatment (1914) and later secondary and tertiary treatment improved effluent quality. Water chlorination, begun in the 1900s, nearly eliminated typhoid and cholera from treated supplies. Indoor plumbing became standard in new homes in the industrialised world by mid-century.

Global sanitation today

Despite progress, the World Health Organization and UNICEF estimate that billions of people still lack safely managed sanitation, and hundreds of millions practise open defecation, contributing to diarrhoeal disease that kills large numbers of children. International targets, including Sustainable Development Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation for all by 2030), drive investment, and new approaches such as container-based sanitation and reinvented non-sewered toilets are being developed for cities without conventional sewerage.
