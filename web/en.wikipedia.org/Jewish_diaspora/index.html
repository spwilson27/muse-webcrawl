Jewish diaspora

The Jewish diaspora (Hebrew: תְּפוּצָה, Tefutzah; Yiddish: גלות, Golus) is the worldwide dispersion of Jews outside the Land of Israel. Historically, the diaspora grew out of a series of exiles and migrations, beginning with the Assyrian captivity of the northern Kingdom of Israel in the 8th century BCE and the Babylonian captivity of Judah in the 6th century BCE.

Origins

In 722 BCE the Assyrians deported the population of the northern kingdom. In 586 BCE Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple, deporting much of Judah's population to Babylon. Many exiles returned under Persian rule after Cyrus the Great conquered Babylon, but substantial Jewish communities remained in Mesopotamia and spread around the Mediterranean, especially to Alexandria in Egypt.

Roman period

The First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE) ended with the Roman destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE; the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–136 CE) was followed by the banning of Jews from Jerusalem. Thereafter the centre of Jewish population shifted decisively outside the homeland, to Babylonia and the Mediterranean world.

Communities

Over the centuries, diaspora communities developed distinct traditions, broadly grouped as Ashkenazi (Central and Eastern Europe), Sephardi (Iberia and its exiles after the 1492 expulsion from Spain), and Mizrahi (the Middle East and North Africa), along with smaller groups such as the Ethiopian Beta Israel and the Jews of India and China.

Modern era

Mass emigration from Eastern Europe between 1881 and 1924 brought millions of Jews to the United States. The Holocaust destroyed most of Europe's Jewish population. The establishment of the State of Israel in 1948 was followed by large-scale aliyah from Europe, the Middle East, North Africa, Ethiopia and the former Soviet Union. Today the largest Jewish populations are in Israel and the United States.
