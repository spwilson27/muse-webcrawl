Dependent clause

A dependent clause (also called a subordinate clause) is a clause that provides an independent clause with additional information, but which cannot stand alone as a sentence. A dependent clause is a group of words that has both a subject and a verb but (unlike an independent clause) cannot stand on its own as a complete sentence.

Dependent clauses typically begin with a subordinating conjunction (such as because, although, when, if, since) or a relative pronoun (such as who, which, that). For example, in the sentence "I was happy because I passed the exam", the italicized words form a dependent clause.

Types

Dependent clauses are commonly classified by their function within the sentence. Adverbial clauses modify a verb, adjective, or adverb and express relationships such as time, cause, condition, or concession. Relative (adjective) clauses modify a noun or pronoun. Noun clauses function as a noun within a sentence, serving as a subject, object, or complement.

Recognition

A dependent clause lacks a complete thought. When a dependent clause stands alone it forms a sentence fragment, which is generally considered an error in formal writing unless used deliberately for stylistic effect.
