The Model Penal Code (MPC) is a model act designed to stimulate and assist U.S. state legislatures to update and standardize the penal law of the United States. It was prepared by the American Law Institute (ALI) and completed in 1962, under the direction of its chief reporter, Columbia Law School professor Herbert Wechsler. Although the MPC is not law in any jurisdiction by itself, it profoundly influenced the revision of criminal codes across the United States: by the 1980s, more than half of the states had enacted criminal codes drawing heavily on its structure and terminology.

Contents and approach

The Code is organized into four parts: general principles of liability, definitions of specific offenses, provisions governing treatment and corrections, and organization of correctional services. Its most influential contribution is its systematic treatment of culpability. Section 2.02 defines four levels of mental state — purposely, knowingly, recklessly, and negligently — and provides default rules for applying them to each material element of an offense, replacing the confusing variety of mens rea terms found in older statutes.

The MPC also rationalized the law of attempts, complicity, justification and excuse, and graded offenses by severity. Its treatment of homicide replaced the common-law felony murder complexities with a framework distinguishing murder, manslaughter, and negligent homicide.

Influence

No state adopted the MPC wholesale, but its vocabulary and structure became the standard template for American criminal law reform. Courts frequently consult the Code and its commentaries when interpreting statutes modeled on it, and it remains a staple of law school criminal law courses.

Revision projects

In the twenty-first century the ALI undertook projects to revise parts of the Code, most notably provisions on sexual assault and related offenses, as well as sentencing articles.
