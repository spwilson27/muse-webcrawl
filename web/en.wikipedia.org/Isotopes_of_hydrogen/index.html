Isotopes of hydrogen

The three naturally occurring isotopes of hydrogen.

Hydrogen (atomic number 1) has three naturally occurring isotopes, sometimes denoted 1H, 2H and 3H. They are the only isotopes with their own names: protium, deuterium and tritium. Heavier, highly unstable isotopes up to 7H have been synthesised in the laboratory.

Protium and deuterium

Protium (1H), with a single proton and no neutrons, makes up about 99.98% of natural hydrogen. Deuterium (2H or D), with one proton and one neutron, is stable and comprises about 0.016%; its oxide is "heavy water", used as a moderator in some nuclear reactors and as an NMR solvent. Because the mass ratio between the isotopes is unusually large, hydrogen shows the strongest isotope effects of any element.

Tritium

Tritium (3H or T) is radioactive, decaying by beta emission with a half-life of about 12.3 years into helium-3. It is produced in the atmosphere by cosmic rays and in nuclear reactors, and is used in self-powered lighting, as a tracer, and in fusion research.
