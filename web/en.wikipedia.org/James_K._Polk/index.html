James Knox Polk (2 November 1795 – 15 June 1849) was the 11th president of the United States, serving from 1845 to 1849. A protégé of Andrew Jackson — nicknamed "Young Hickory" — he had previously served as Speaker of the House of Representatives and governor of Tennessee. He is chiefly known for extending the territory of the United States through the Mexican–American War.

Early political career

Polk was born in North Carolina and moved with his family to Tennessee, where he practised law. He served fourteen years in the House of Representatives (1825–1839), the last four as Speaker, and was a loyal ally of Jackson's policies. He was governor of Tennessee from 1839 to 1841.

Presidency

The dark-horse Democratic nominee of 1844, Polk defeated Henry Clay on a platform of territorial expansion — the annexation of Texas and the occupation of Oregon. He pledged to serve one term and set himself four goals, all of which he substantially achieved: settling the Oregon boundary (the 49th-parallel compromise with Britain, 1846), lowering the tariff (the Walker Tariff), restoring an independent treasury, and acquiring California and the Southwest. The annexation of Texas helped precipitate war with Mexico (1846–1848); the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ceded California, New Mexico, and other territories — over 500,000 square miles — to the United States for $15 million. The expansion reignited the national argument over the extension of slavery, most famously in the failed Wilmot Proviso.

Death and reputation

Exhausted by his labours, Polk left office in March 1849 and died of cholera three months later, the youngest former president to die in retirement. Historians generally rank him among the more effective presidents for achieving his stated agenda, while criticising the war with Mexico and his expansion of slavery's territory.
