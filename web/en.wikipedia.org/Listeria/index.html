Listeria
Domain: Bacteria
Phylum: Bacillota
Class: Bacilli
Order: Bacillales
Family: Listeriaceae
Genus: Listeria

Listeria is a genus of bacteria that, as of 2024, contains more than two dozen recognised species. The genus is named after the British surgeon Joseph Lister, a pioneer of antiseptic surgery. Most species are harmless inhabitants of soil and decaying vegetation, but Listeria monocytogenes is a significant foodborne pathogen that causes the infection listeriosis.

Characteristics

Listeria are Gram-positive, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacteria. They are notable for their ability to grow at refrigeration temperatures (as low as 0 °C) and to tolerate high salt concentrations, traits that make them a persistent problem in food-processing environments. At body temperature they produce flagella and show a characteristic tumbling motility. L. monocytogenes can invade host cells and move directly from cell to cell using actin comet tails, evading the immune system.

Pathogenicity

Listeriosis primarily affects pregnant women, newborns, the elderly and people with weakened immune systems. It can cause septicaemia and meningitis, and infection during pregnancy may lead to miscarriage or stillbirth. Contaminated ready-to-eat foods such as deli meats, soft cheeses and smoked seafood are common sources of outbreaks. Unlike most foodborne illnesses, listeriosis has a high hospitalisation and fatality rate, around 20–30% in vulnerable groups.

Species

Besides L. monocytogenes, the genus includes L. innocua, L. ivanovii (which mainly infects ruminants), L. seeligeri, L. welshimeri and L. grayi, along with many species described since 2009 as the genus has expanded through environmental sampling.
