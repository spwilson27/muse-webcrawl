Prize (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A captured vessel brought into port for adjudication as a prize

In admiralty and international law, a prize is property, usually a vessel and its cargo, captured at sea during wartime under the authority of a belligerent state. The term prize of war refers to enemy property lawfully seized in naval warfare and condemned by a prize court, so that title passes to the captor's government and the proceeds may be distributed as prize money to the capturing crew.

Prize courts and prize money

A capture did not by itself transfer ownership: the vessel had to be taken before a prize court in the captor's country, which determined whether the ship and cargo were lawful prize — for example, enemy property, or neutral property carrying contraband or breaching a blockade. If condemned, the prize was sold and the proceeds distributed according to statute. In the Royal Navy, prize money was shared among the flag officers, captain, officers and crew of the capturing ship, and could amount to a fortune for a successful frigate captain during the Napoleonic Wars.

History

Prize law developed from medieval maritime custom and was elaborated by jurists such as Hugo Grotius. The Declaration of Paris of 1856 abolished privateering among its signatories and set rules protecting neutral goods. Prize courts sat in both world wars, but the advent of unrestricted submarine warfare and total war diminished the practice. The British Prize Act 1939 governed captures during the Second World War. Prize money was formally abolished in the Royal Navy after 1945, with residual funds distributed decades later.
