Cell cycle

The cell cycle, or cell-division cycle, is the series of events that take place in a cell that cause it to divide into two daughter cells. These events include the growth of the cell, the replication of its DNA, and cell division.

Phases

In eukaryotes, the cycle consists of interphase — subdivided into G1 (growth), S (DNA synthesis), and G2 (preparation for division) — and the mitotic (M) phase, comprising mitosis and cytokinesis. Cells that have temporarily or permanently stopped dividing are said to be in the quiescent G0 phase.

Regulation

Progression through the cycle is driven by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) activated by cyclins, and is monitored at checkpoints — notably the G1/S, G2/M, and spindle-assembly checkpoints — that ensure DNA is intact and fully replicated before division. Proteins such as p53 and the retinoblastoma protein act as tumour suppressors guarding these checkpoints.

Significance

Disruption of cell-cycle regulation is a hallmark of cancer: uncontrolled division results when checkpoints and growth controls fail. Many chemotherapy drugs target dividing cells by interfering with DNA replication or the mitotic spindle.
