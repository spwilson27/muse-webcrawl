Charcoal

Charcoal
TypeCarbon-rich solid fuel / material
ProductionSlow pyrolysis of wood or other biomass
Carbon contentTypically 65–95%

Charcoal is a lightweight black carbon residue produced by removing water and other volatile constituents from animal and vegetation substances. Charcoal is usually produced by slow pyrolysis, the heating of wood or other organic materials in the absence of oxygen. This process is called charcoal burning or carbonisation. The finished charcoal consists largely of carbon, with variable amounts of ash.

History

The production of wood charcoal dates back to ancient times. Charcoal was essential for early metallurgy because it burns hotter than raw wood and could smelt metals such as iron and copper. Charcoal kilns and earth mounds ("clamps") were used across Europe, Asia, and Africa. In the early modern period, charcoal consumption drove significant deforestation, one reason coke derived from coal gradually replaced it in iron smelting during the Industrial Revolution.

Production

Traditional production stacks wood in a mound covered with earth or turf, leaving limited air inlets; the wood is ignited and allowed to smoulder for days. Modern methods use steel kilns or retorts that recover by-products such as wood vinegar, tar, and methanol. Charcoal can also be made from coconut shells, bamboo, and agricultural residues. Activated carbon is a specially treated form with very high surface area used for filtration.

Properties and uses

Charcoal is porous, lightweight, and burns with little flame and smoke, reaching high temperatures. It is widely used as a cooking fuel (including briquettes), in water and air filtration, as a reducing agent in metallurgy, as a drawing medium in art (vine and willow charcoal), and in medicine, where activated charcoal is used to treat certain poisonings. Biochar, produced similarly, is applied to soils to improve fertility and sequester carbon.

Environmental impact

Inefficient traditional charcoal production releases methane and particulates, and unsustainable wood harvesting contributes to deforestation in parts of Africa and Asia. Improved kilns and sustainably managed woodlots can substantially reduce these impacts.
