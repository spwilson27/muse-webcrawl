German wine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

German wine is primarily produced in the west of Germany, along the river Rhine and its tributaries, with the oldest plantations going back to the Roman era. About 60 percent of German wine is produced in the state of Rhineland-Palatinate. Germany is the world's eighth-largest wine producer, with roughly 100,000 hectares of vineyards, and is best known internationally for its Riesling wines.

History

Viticulture in Germany dates to Roman times; the Romans planted vines along the Moselle and the Rhine. During the Middle Ages, monasteries expanded the vineyards, and Cistercian monks in the Rheingau created the Clos de Vougeot-like walled vineyard now known as Schloss Johannisberg's predecessors. The late 18th and early 19th centuries saw the codification of late harvesting; the famous story of the delayed harvest messenger at Schloss Johannisberg in 1775 is credited with the (accidental) invention of Spätlese wine. After the setbacks of phylloxera and the world wars, the 20th century saw mass production of sweet blended wines such as Liebfraumilch, which damaged Germany's reputation, followed from the 1990s by a quality renaissance led by dry Rieslings and Spätburgunder (Pinot noir).

Wine regions

Germany has 13 designated wine regions (Anbaugebiete) for quality wine: Ahr, Baden, Franconia, Hessische Bergstraße, Mittelrhein, Moselle, Nahe, Palatinate, Rheingau, Rheinhessen, Saale-Unstrut, Saxony, and Württemberg. Rheinhessen is the largest by area; the Moselle is famed for precipitously steep slate slopes, among the steepest vineyards in the world, and the Rheingau is regarded as the historic heart of Riesling.

Grape varieties

White varieties dominate, accounting for about two-thirds of plantings. Riesling is the flagship grape, covering roughly a quarter of the vineyard area, followed by Müller-Thurgau (Rivaner), Silvaner, Grauburgunder (Pinot gris) and Weißburgunder (Pinot blanc). Among red varieties, Spätburgunder is the most important and Germany is one of the world's largest producers of Pinot noir by area; Dornfelder is also widely planted.

Classification

German wine law classifies quality wines by the ripeness of the grapes at harvest (must weight). The Prädikat levels, in ascending order of ripeness, are Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Trockenbeerenauslese, and Eiswein (ice wine, made from grapes frozen on the vine). Dry wines are labelled trocken. The private VDP association classifies its members' vineyards on a Burgundian-style pyramid culminating in Grosse Lage and its dry wines labelled Grosses Gewächs (GG).
