François Mitterrand

François Maurice Adrien Marie Mitterrand (26 October 1916 – 8 January 1996) was a French politician who served as President of France from 1981 to 1995, the longest-serving president in French history and the first from the left under the Fifth Republic.

Early life and career

Mitterrand was born in Jarnac, Charente. Mobilized in 1939, he was wounded and captured in 1940, escaping from a German prisoner-of-war camp in 1941; his wartime record — work for the Vichy regime's prisoners' service followed by Resistance activity — remained controversial. After the war he rose quickly in the Fourth Republic, holding ministerial posts including Minister of the Interior and Minister of Justice. He narrowly lost the 1974 presidential election to Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, and in 1971 he took control of the refounded Socialist Party at the Épinay Congress.

Presidency

Mitterrand defeated Giscard in the 1981 election and implemented an initially ambitious left-wing programme — nationalizations, a wealth tax, retirement at sixty, and the abolition of the death penalty under Justice Minister Robert Badinter — before the 1983 "tournant de la rigueur" (austerity turn) committed France to the European Monetary System. He was re-elected in 1988 and navigated two periods of "cohabitation" with right-wing governments (1986–88 and 1993–95). A committed European integrationist, he worked with Helmut Kohl on the Maastricht Treaty. His presidency also saw the Grands Projets architectural programme in Paris, including the Louvre Pyramid and the Bibliothèque nationale de France.

Death and legacy

Mitterrand died of prostate cancer in January 1996, months after leaving office; the concealment of his illness during his presidency raised lasting questions about transparency in French public life.
