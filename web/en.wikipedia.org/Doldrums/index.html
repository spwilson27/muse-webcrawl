Doldrums

The doldrums, technically the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), is a belt of low pressure around the Earth near the equator where the northeast and southeast trade winds converge. It is characterised by calms and light, variable winds interspersed with violent squalls and thunderstorms, and by heavy rainfall.

Formation and movement

Solar heating near the equator warms the air, which rises in deep convection, creating a trough of low pressure and drawing in the trades from both hemispheres. The rising air produces towering cumulonimbus clouds and daily downpours. The zone migrates seasonally with the sun, moving north in the northern summer and south in winter, more markedly over land than ocean.

Effects on sailing

Sailing ships crossing the equator could be becalmed in the doldrums for days or weeks, exhausting water and provisions; combined with the horse latitudes, the zone shaped historic trade routes. The region remains significant for ocean racing, where skippers seek the narrowest crossing point, and for aviation, where its thunderstorms — a factor in the loss of Air France Flight 447 in 2009 — are a hazard.

Etymology

The word doldrums originally meant a state of dullness or sluggishness (from Old English dol, "dull") and was applied to the equatorial calms by sailors; by extension it now describes any period of stagnation or low spirits.
