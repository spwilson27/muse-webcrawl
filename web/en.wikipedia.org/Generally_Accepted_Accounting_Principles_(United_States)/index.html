Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP or U.S. GAAP) is the accounting standard adopted by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and is the default accounting standard used by companies based in the United States. The Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB), a non-profit organization, creates and updates these rules.

U.S. GAAP consists of a comprehensive set of accounting principles, standards and procedures that companies use to compile their financial statements, ensuring consistency, transparency and comparability across industries and reporting periods.

History

The modern system emerged after the stock market crash of 1929 and the Securities Acts of 1933 and 1934, which created the SEC and gave it authority over accounting standards. Standard-setting passed from the Committee on Accounting Procedure (1939–1959) to the Accounting Principles Board (1959–1973) and then to the FASB in 1973. In 2009 the FASB Accounting Standards Codification became the single authoritative source of U.S. GAAP for nongovernmental entities.

Basic principles

Core concepts include the accrual basis of accounting, the going-concern assumption, the matching principle (recognizing expenses in the same period as related revenues), revenue recognition, conservatism, materiality, consistency, full disclosure, and the separation of the business entity from its owners. GAAP requires the four basic financial statements: balance sheet, income statement, statement of cash flows, and statement of changes in equity.

Relationship to IFRS

U.S. GAAP is generally considered more rules-based, while the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), used in most other countries, are more principles-based. The FASB and the International Accounting Standards Board pursued a convergence program for many years, producing largely converged standards on revenue recognition and leases, though full adoption of IFRS in the United States has not occurred.
