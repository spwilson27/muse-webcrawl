The two-source hypothesis: Matthew and Luke independently used Mark and the hypothetical Q source.

Marcan priority (or Markan priority) is the hypothesis that the Gospel of Mark was the first of the three Synoptic Gospels to be written, and was used as a source by the authors of Matthew and Luke. It is a central element of the two-source hypothesis, the most widely held solution to the synoptic problem, which additionally posits a lost sayings collection, "Q", as the source of material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark.

Evidence

Several lines of argument support Marcan priority:

  • Shared content and order: About 90% of Mark's content appears in Matthew, and over half in Luke; where Matthew and Luke diverge in the ordering of episodes, one of them usually agrees with Mark's sequence, suggesting Mark is the middle term.
  • Editorial fatigue and improvements: Matthew and Luke regularly improve Mark's rough Greek style, clarify his harder or potentially embarrassing statements (for example, softening Mark's remarks about Jesus' family or about the disciples' failures), and expand his terse narrative — natural if they edited Mark, odd if Mark abbreviated them.
  • Harder readings: Mark's portrayal of Jesus is in places theologically more difficult (e.g., Mark 6:5, "he could do no deed of power there"; Mark 10:18, "Why do you call me good?"), and Matthew and Luke modify these passages, consistent with their dependence on Mark.
  • The "messianic secret" and Markan style: Distinctively Markan features, such as the historical present tense, Aramaic phrases with translation, and the "sandwich" (intercalation) technique, appear in diluted form in the others.

History of the hypothesis

Ancient tradition, reported by Papias and endorsed by Augustine, held that Matthew was written first (Matthean priority), and Mark was long treated as an abbreviation of Matthew — as its very name in some early sources implies. In the 1830s Karl Lachmann's argument from the order of pericopes, and the systematic work of Christian Hermann Weisse and later B. H. Streeter (1925), established Markan priority in mainstream critical scholarship, where it remains the majority view.

Alternatives

The Farrer hypothesis accepts Marcan priority but dispenses with Q, holding that Luke used Matthew directly. The Augustinian hypothesis and the Two-gospel hypothesis defend Matthean priority and continue to be argued by a minority of scholars. The question matters because it shapes how scholars reconstruct the historical Jesus, early Christian communities, and each evangelist's theology (redaction criticism).
