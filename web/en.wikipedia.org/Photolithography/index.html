Photolithography

Photolithography (also simply called lithography) is a process used in the manufacturing of integrated circuits and microelectromechanical systems. It involves using light to transfer a geometric pattern from a photomask to a light-sensitive chemical photoresist on a substrate, typically a silicon wafer. A series of chemical treatments then either etches the exposed pattern into the material or enables deposition of a new material in the desired pattern upon the material underneath the photoresist.

In modern semiconductor fabrication, photolithography is repeated many times — a modern microprocessor may require dozens of lithography steps — and it is the most expensive and critical step in the process, determining the minimum feature size that can be manufactured.

Process

The basic sequence of steps includes wafer cleaning and preparation, application of a photoresist by spin coating, soft baking, alignment of the mask, exposure to ultraviolet light (or extreme ultraviolet in EUV lithography), development of the pattern, hard baking, and etching or ion implantation. After processing, the resist is stripped and the cycle repeats for the next layer.

Light sources

Historically, mercury-vapor lamps provided ultraviolet light at wavelengths of 436 nm (g-line) and 365 nm (i-line). Deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography uses excimer lasers at 248 nm (KrF) and 193 nm (ArF). Extreme ultraviolet lithography, introduced into mass production in the late 2010s, uses plasma-generated light at 13.5 nm, reflected from multilayer mirrors in a vacuum.

Resolution

The minimum feature size is governed by the Rayleigh criterion, proportional to the wavelength of the light and inversely proportional to the numerical aperture of the lens. Techniques such as immersion lithography, multiple patterning, and computational lithography have extended the useful life of each wavelength generation.
