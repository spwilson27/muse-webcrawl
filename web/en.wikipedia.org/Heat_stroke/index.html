Heat stroke

Heat stroke is a life-threatening type of severe heat illness that results from the body overheating, usually in response to prolonged exposure to, or physical exertion in, high temperatures. It is defined by a core body temperature above 40 °C (104 °F) together with dysfunction of the central nervous system, such as confusion, seizures, or loss of consciousness. It can damage the brain and other internal organs and can be fatal if cooling is delayed.

Signs and types

In classic (non-exertional) heat stroke, which typically affects elderly or chronically ill people during heat waves, sweating often ceases and the skin is hot and dry. In exertional heat stroke, which affects athletes, soldiers, and labourers, sweating may persist. Other features include rapid pulse, nausea, headache, and, in severe cases, disseminated intravascular coagulation, rhabdomyolysis, and multi-organ failure. Heat stroke is the most severe condition on the spectrum of heat-related illness, beyond heat cramps and heat exhaustion.

Treatment

Immediate, rapid cooling is the cornerstone of treatment: the whole body is immersed in cold water where possible, or continuously doused and fanned to promote evaporative cooling. The principle "cool first, transport second" is applied for exertional cases. Intravenous fluids and monitoring in an intensive care unit may be required; antipyretic drugs such as paracetamol are ineffective because the temperature set-point is not raised.

Prevention

Prevention includes acclimatisation to heat, adequate hydration, rest breaks, light clothing, and rescheduling strenuous activity to cooler times of day. Heat stroke becomes more frequent and deadly during heat waves, which are increasing in intensity with climate change.
