Haredi Judaism

Haredi Judaism (Hebrew: יהדות חרדית, romanised: Yahadut Ḥaredit) is a stream of Orthodox Judaism characterised by strict adherence to traditional Jewish law and values and rejection of many aspects of modern secular culture. Its members are often called ultra-Orthodox in English, though many prefer the term Haredi (from ḥared, "one who trembles" at the word of God, cf. Isaiah 66:2). Haredi communities are concentrated in Israel, New York, London, Antwerp and other major Jewish population centres and number roughly two million worldwide.

Beliefs and practice

Haredim regard the Torah — both Written and Oral Law — as divinely revealed and binding, and structure daily life around mitzvot observance, prayer and intensive Torah study, which for men may continue full-time in yeshivas and kollels for years. Major sub-groups include Hasidic dynasties (such as Satmar, Belz, Ger and Chabad-Lubavitch), the non-Hasidic Lithuanian (Litvish/Misnagdim) yeshiva world, and Sephardi Haredim, represented politically in Israel by the Shas party. Common features include distinctive black dress, Yiddish (in many Ashkenazi communities), gender separation in many contexts, large families, restricted use of the internet and television, and deference to rabbinic authorities (da'as Torah).

History

Haredi Judaism crystallised in 19th-century Eastern and Central Europe as a reaction against the Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah), emancipation and Reform Judaism. After the near-destruction of its European heartland in the Holocaust, it was rebuilt in Israel and the United States, growing rapidly through high fertility. In Israel, Haredim constitute around 13–14% of the population and form a distinct political and social bloc, with long-running debates over military conscription, state funding and secular education requirements.
