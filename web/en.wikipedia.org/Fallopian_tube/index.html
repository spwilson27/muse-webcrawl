Fallopian tube

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The fallopian tubes, also known as uterine tubes, oviducts or salpinges (singular salpinx), are paired hollow organs in the human female reproductive system that stretch from the ovaries to the uterus. Each tube is roughly 10–13 cm long. The fallopian tubes provide the route by which an egg (ovum) travels from an ovary to the uterus, and fertilisation by sperm normally takes place within the tube.

Structure

Each fallopian tube is divided into four parts, from the ovary toward the uterus: the infundibulum, a funnel-shaped opening fringed with finger-like projections called fimbriae that sweep the egg into the tube; the ampulla, the widest and longest section, where fertilisation usually occurs; the isthmus, a narrower segment; and the intramural (interstitial) part, which passes through the uterine wall. The tube's inner lining is ciliated, and rhythmic muscular contractions together with ciliary motion move the egg or early embryo toward the uterus over several days.

Function and clinical significance

After ovulation, the fimbriae capture the released oocyte. If fertilisation occurs, the resulting zygote travels down the tube and implants in the uterine lining. Blockage or damage to the tubes — commonly from pelvic inflammatory disease, endometriosis or previous surgery — is a major cause of infertility. Implantation of an embryo within the tube produces an ectopic pregnancy, a potentially life-threatening condition. Surgical removal of a tube is a salpingectomy; inflammation of the tube is salpingitis. The tubes are named after the 16th-century Italian anatomist Gabriele Falloppio, who described them.
