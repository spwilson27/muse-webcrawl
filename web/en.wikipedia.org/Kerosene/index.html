Kerosene

Kerosene, or paraffin, is a combustible hydrocarbon liquid which is derived from petroleum. It is widely used as a fuel in aviation as well as households. Its name derives from the Greek kērós (κηρός) meaning "wax", registered as a trademark by Canadian geologist and inventor Abraham Gesner in 1854 before evolving into a generic trademark.

Properties

Kerosene is a light, oily liquid composed of hydrocarbons with roughly 10 to 16 carbon atoms per molecule, obtained by fractional distillation of petroleum between 150 °C and 275 °C. It is less volatile than gasoline and has a flash point between about 37 °C and 65 °C, making it comparatively safer to store and handle.

History

Distilled illuminating oils were produced from coal and oil shale in the 1850s by Gesner, James Young and Ignacy Łukasiewicz, among others. Kerosene rapidly displaced whale oil as the standard lamp fuel in the later 19th century, and lamp kerosene remained a major petroleum product until the spread of electric lighting.

Uses

Today the largest use of kerosene is as the basis of jet fuel (such as Jet A and Jet A-1). It is also used for heating and lighting in parts of the developing world, as a solvent and insecticide, and — highly refined grades such as RP-1 — as a rocket propellant. In Japan and some other countries, kerosene space heaters remain common in winter.
