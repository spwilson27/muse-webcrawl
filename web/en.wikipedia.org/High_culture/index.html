High culture

High culture refers to the set of cultural products and activities — such as fine art, classical music, serious literature, philosophy and theatre — that are regarded within a society as exemplifying the highest aesthetic and intellectual achievement, typically associated with educated elites and prestigious institutions. It is frequently defined in contrast to popular culture or "low culture", which is produced for mass consumption.

History of the concept

The distinction between high and low culture became prominent in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, articulated by critics such as Matthew Arnold, who in Culture and Anarchy (1869) defined culture as "the best that has been thought and said". In the twentieth century, Frankfurt School theorists Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer criticised mass culture as an industry, while Pierre Bourdieu analysed taste as a form of cultural capital that reproduces class distinctions.

Criticism

Cultural studies scholars have challenged the hierarchy implied by the term, arguing that it reflects the preferences of dominant classes and historically excluded women, working-class and non-Western creators. Many contemporary institutions now programme across the high/popular divide, and forms once dismissed as low culture — jazz, film, photography — have been absorbed into the canon.
