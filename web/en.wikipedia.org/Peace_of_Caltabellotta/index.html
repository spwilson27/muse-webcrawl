The Peace of Caltabellotta, signed on 31 August 1302, was the treaty that ended the War of the Sicilian Vespers, the twenty-year conflict between the Angevin kings of Naples and the Aragonese-backed rulers of Sicily that had begun with the uprising of 1282. It formally divided the former Norman-Hohenstaufen Kingdom of Sicily in two — a division that, in various forms, lasted for centuries.

Background

After the Sicilian Vespers revolt of Easter 1282 threw off the rule of Charles I of Anjou, the Sicilian barons offered the island's crown to Peter III of Aragon, husband of Constance, heiress of the Hohenstaufen kings. Decades of warfare followed, involving France, the papacy, and the Italian maritime republics. The Treaty of Anagni (1295) attempted a settlement but failed when the Sicilians rejected its terms and crowned Peter's younger son, Frederick, as their king. Angevin-Aragonese fighting continued until exhaustion, the accession of Frederick's brother James II of Aragon to the negotiations, and papal mediation pushed both sides to settle.

Terms

Under the peace, Frederick was recognised as ruler of the island of Sicily for his lifetime, but under the style "King of Trinacria" — using the island's ancient Greek name — rather than "King of Sicily," a title reserved to Charles II of Naples, who retained the mainland portion of the kingdom (the future Kingdom of Naples). On Frederick's death the island was, in theory, to revert to the Angevins, in exchange for compensation elsewhere. The settlement was sealed by Frederick's marriage to Charles's daughter Eleanor of Anjou in 1303. Pope Boniface VIII ratified the agreement, and the Sicilian parliament accepted it.

Consequences

The reversion clause was never implemented: Frederick's heirs continued to rule Sicily, and the island kingdom persisted as a separate Aragonese — later Spanish — possession. The treaty thus created two "Sicilies," reflected for centuries in the formula "Kingdom of the Two Sicilies," which survived until the two were reunited under the Bourbons in 1816 and finally absorbed into unified Italy in 1860–61. The long war and its settlement also reshaped the western Mediterranean: Catalan-Aragonese power extended into Sardinia and, later, Naples itself, which Alfonso V of Aragon conquered in 1442.
