Fifth-wheel coupling

The fifth-wheel coupling is the hitch mechanism that connects a semi-trailer to the towing truck (tractor unit). It consists of a large, flat, horseshoe-shaped coupling plate mounted on the tractor — the fifth wheel — into which a vertical steel pin on the trailer, the kingpin, locks. The arrangement provides the pivot point for articulation between tractor and trailer while transferring much of the trailer's weight onto the tractor's rear axles.

Design and operation

The fifth wheel plate is typically a heavy steel disc with a V-shaped notch opening to the rear. The kingpin (commonly 2 inches or 3.5 inches in diameter) projects downward from the trailer's bolster plate. To couple, the tractor backs under the trailer's front edge; the kingpin slides along the notch into the locking jaws at its center, which clamp shut around the pin, usually with a visible release handle confirming engagement. The plate is greased (or fitted with low-friction pads) so the trailer's bolster can pivot smoothly during turns. Most fifth wheels also tilt fore-and-aft on their mounting to accommodate uneven ground.

History

The name derives from the horizontal wheel that once supported the front of horse-drawn wagons and carriages, allowing the front axle to pivot. Steam traction engines adopted an iron "fifth wheel," and early motor trucks adapted the principle. In the 1910s–1930s inventors including Herman Farr and Charles Martin (of the Martin Rocking Fifth Wheel) developed the modern rocking, self-locking coupling used on semi-tractors; Fruehauf and other trailer builders popularized the semi-trailer system it enabled.

Recreational use

Scaled-down fifth-wheel hitches mounted in pickup truck beds are used to tow large "fifth-wheel" travel trailers, which are prized for stable towing and spacious interiors.
