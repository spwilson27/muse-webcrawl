Sarcoidosis is a systemic inflammatory disease of unknown cause characterized by the formation of granulomas — small clumps of immune cells — in affected organs. The lungs and the lymph nodes inside the chest are involved in more than 90 percent of cases, but the disease can affect virtually any organ, including the skin, eyes, heart and nervous system. Presentation ranges from an incidental finding on a chest X-ray to disabling organ damage; acute presentations with fever, joint pain and the tender red skin nodules of erythema nodosum are known as Löfgren syndrome and usually resolve spontaneously.

Signs and symptoms

Common symptoms include cough, breathlessness, fatigue, weight loss and enlarged lymph nodes. Skin manifestations include erythema nodosum and the chronic disfiguring plaques of lupus pernio. Ocular involvement can cause uveitis and blindness, while cardiac sarcoidosis may cause heart block and sudden death. The disease occurs worldwide, with notably high incidence in Scandinavia and among African Americans.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on compatible clinical and imaging findings, biopsy showing non-caseating granulomas, and exclusion of other causes such as tuberculosis and lymphoma. Many patients need no treatment because the disease remits on its own; when treatment is required, corticosteroids are first-line therapy, with methotrexate and other immunosuppressants, and anti-TNF agents such as infliximab, for refractory disease.

Cause

The cause is unknown but is thought to involve an abnormal immune response to an environmental trigger — possibly microbial or occupational antigens — in genetically susceptible people. Associations with certain HLA types and with exposures such as beryllium and mold have been reported.
