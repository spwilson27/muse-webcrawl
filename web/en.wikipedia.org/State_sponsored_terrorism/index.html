State-sponsored terrorism

State-sponsored terrorism involves government support for violent non-state actors.

State-sponsored terrorism is the support of violent non-state actors by a state's government in order to advance its foreign policy goals while maintaining plausible deniability. Support can include funding, weapons, training, intelligence, sanctuary, and logistical assistance. It is distinct from state terrorism, in which a state uses violence against civilians directly through its own agents.

Historical examples

During the Cold War, both superpowers supported armed proxies abroad. The United States has accused countries including Iran, Syria, Libya, and North Korea of sponsoring groups such as Hezbollah and Hamas; Libya accepted responsibility for the 1988 bombing of Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie. Pakistan has been accused of supporting militant groups in Kashmir and Afghanistan, and various states sponsored insurgent movements across Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East during the 20th century.

Responses

International responses to state sponsorship of terrorism include sanctions, diplomatic isolation, and, in some cases, military action. The United States maintains its State Sponsors of Terrorism list, and international law addresses state responsibility for aiding armed groups through bodies such as the International Court of Justice. Debates continue over the definition of terrorism itself and over which states qualify as sponsors.
