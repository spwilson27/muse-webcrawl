Barbary slave trade

The Barbary slave trade involved the capture and sale of European slaves in the Barbary states of North Africa between the 16th and 19th centuries. Barbary corsairs raided ships in the Mediterranean and Atlantic and launched coastal raids across southern and western Europe — reaching as far as Ireland, Iceland, and Newfoundland — seizing captives who were sold in the slave markets of Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Salé, or held for ransom.

Scale and conditions

Estimates of the number of Europeans enslaved vary; historian Robert Davis estimated that between 1 million and 1.25 million Europeans were captured by Barbary corsairs between 1530 and 1780. Captives were employed as galley slaves, labourers, domestic servants, or held in bagnios (slave prisons) while ransom was negotiated, often by religious redemption orders such as the Trinitarians and Mercedarians. Prominent captives included Miguel de Cervantes, held in Algiers from 1575 to 1580, whose experience influenced his writing.

Decline

The trade declined as European naval power grew and as diplomatic and military pressure mounted, including the Barbary Wars fought by the United States and the Anglo-Dutch bombardment of Algiers in 1816, after which large numbers of Christian slaves were freed. The French conquest of Algiers in 1830 ended the practice in the region.
