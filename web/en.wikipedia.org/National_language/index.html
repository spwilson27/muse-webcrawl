National language

A national language is a language (or languages) that has some connection — de facto or de jure — with a nation. There is little consistency in the use of the term. A national language may be the language of a nation-state, a language widely spoken within a country, or a language designated in law for purposes of national identity and official communication. The concept is distinct from, though often overlapping with, that of an official language: a language may be official without being national (as English is in many states), and a language may be recognized as national without being official.

De facto and de jure status

Some countries legally designate national languages. Ireland's constitution names Irish as "the national language," with English recognized as a second official language. India has no national language, though Hindi and English serve as official languages of the union government. Singapore recognizes Malay as its national language while having four official languages. In other countries, such as the United States and Australia, a widely used language (English) functions as the de facto national language without legal designation.

Functions and politics

National languages serve symbolic and practical roles: fostering national unity, standardizing education and administration, and expressing cultural heritage. The selection and promotion of a national language can be politically sensitive, particularly in multilingual states where the elevation of one language may disadvantage speakers of others. Movements for national revival have often centered on language, as in the revival of Hebrew in Israel or the promotion of Filipino in the Philippines.

Examples

Examples of designated national languages include Swahili in Kenya and Tanzania, Bengali in Bangladesh, Urdu in Pakistan (alongside English as official), Nepali in Nepal, Korean in both Koreas, and Quechua (co-official) in Peru and Bolivia.
