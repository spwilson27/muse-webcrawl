Remilitarization of the Rhineland

The remilitarization of the Rhineland occurred on 7 March 1936, when German military forces under Adolf Hitler entered the Rhineland, the demilitarised zone in western Germany established by the Treaty of Versailles and reaffirmed by the Locarno Treaties. The move was in direct violation of Articles 42 and 43 of the Treaty of Versailles and of the Locarno pact, under which Germany had voluntarily accepted the zone's demilitarised status.

Background

Hitler justified the occupation by pointing to the Franco-Soviet mutual assistance pact of 1935, which he claimed voided Locarno. In reality the march was a calculated gamble: the German force was small, with orders to retreat if the French army opposed it. Hitler later called the 48 hours after the march "the most nerve-racking" of his life.

Response

France, in political turmoil and weeks from elections, appealed to the League of Nations but would not act without Britain; Britain, where opinion held that Germany was merely walking "into her own back garden," declined to enforce the treaties. The League formally condemned the breach but imposed no sanctions. The episode fatally weakened the Versailles settlement, encouraged Hitler's later gambles in Austria and Czechoslovakia, and led Belgium to abandon its alliance with France and return to neutrality.
