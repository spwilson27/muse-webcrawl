The maritime republics (Italian: repubbliche marinare) were thalassocratic city-states in the Mediterranean, above all in Italy, which during the Middle Ages enjoyed political autonomy and economic prosperity thanks to their maritime activities. The best known are Venice, Genoa, Pisa and Amalfi; others included Ancona, Ragusa, Noli and Gaeta.

Characteristics

Unlike the inland communes, the maritime republics built their wealth on trade routes linking the western Mediterranean with the Byzantine Empire and the Islamic world. They maintained merchant colonies and fondachi in ports such as Constantinople, Acre and Alexandria, operated large war fleets that fought pirates and rival powers, and developed early forms of maritime law, insurance and double-entry bookkeeping.

The four great republics

Amalfi was the earliest to flourish, trading from the 9th century and producing the Tabula Amalfitana, a maritime code used across the Mediterranean. Pisa rose in the 11th century, sacking Palermo and Mahdia and helping to drive Muslim fleets from the western Mediterranean before being eclipsed by Genoa after the Battle of Meloria (1284). Genoa dominated trade in the western Mediterranean and the Black Sea, founding colonies from Corsica to Caffa in the Crimea. Venice, the most durable, built an overseas empire — the Stato da Màr — along the Adriatic and in Greece and Crete, and survived as an independent republic until 1797.

Rivalry and decline

The republics frequently fought one another: Pisa and Genoa contested Sardinia and Corsica, while Venice and Genoa fought four wars between 1256 and 1381, culminating in the War of Chioggia. The shift of trade to the Atlantic after 1492, the rise of the Ottoman Empire and the growing power of Spain and France gradually reduced their importance, though Ragusa survived as a tributary republic until 1808.

Legacy

The coats of arms of the four principal republics appear in the ensign of the modern Italian Navy. Their legal and commercial innovations — bills of exchange, commenda contracts and consular networks — helped shape the commercial revolution of medieval Europe.
