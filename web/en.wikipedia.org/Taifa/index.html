Taifa

The taifas (Arabic: طائفة, ṭāʾifa, plural طوائف, ṭawāʾif, from which Spanish taifa) were the independent Muslim-ruled principalities of the Iberian Peninsula (Al-Andalus) that emerged after the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba in the early 11th century. In the historiography of Spain they are known as the reinos de taifas ("taifa kingdoms").

History

During the civil wars known as the Fitna of al-Andalus (1009–1031), provincial governors, military leaders, and local dynasties seized power, and after the caliphate was abolished in 1031 some three dozen taifas existed at various times, including major states centred on Seville, Toledo, Granada, Zaragoza, Valencia, and Badajoz. Though often at war with one another and forced to pay tribute (parias) to the Christian kingdoms of the north, the taifa courts were celebrated centres of poetry, philosophy, science, and architecture.

Later periods

Weakened taifas called in the Almoravids from Morocco, who annexed them by the 1090s — a pattern called the "first taifas". Later collapses of Almoravid and then Almohad power produced "second" (12th century) and "third" (13th century) taifa periods, the last of which left Granada as the sole surviving Muslim state in Iberia until 1492.
