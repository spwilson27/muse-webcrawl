Quantum information theory

Quantum information theory is the study of the transmission, storage and manipulation of information using quantum systems, generalising classical information theory to the quantum domain. It grew out of the realisation, in the 1970s and 1980s, that quantum-mechanical phenomena such as superposition, entanglement and measurement disturbance allow information-processing tasks that are impossible or inefficient classically.

Key results

The fundamental unit is the qubit. Unlike classical bits, an unknown quantum state cannot be copied (the no-cloning theorem), and measurement generally disturbs the state — a fact exploited in quantum key distribution, pioneered by the BB84 protocol of Bennett and Brassard (1984). Entanglement acts as a resource: with a shared entangled pair and two classical bits, an unknown qubit state can be transferred exactly by quantum teleportation, while superdense coding uses entanglement to send two classical bits with one qubit. Quantum data compression is governed by the von Neumann entropy, the analogue of Shannon entropy, and the Holevo bound limits the classical information accessible from a quantum state.

Applications

Quantum information theory underlies quantum computing, quantum communication and quantum sensing. Its theorems on channel capacities (the Holevo–Schumacher–Westmoreland theorem and the quantum capacity theorem of Lloyd, Shor and Devetak) characterise how reliably classical and quantum information can be sent over noisy quantum channels, and it provides the framework for quantum error correction. The field sits at the intersection of physics, computer science and mathematics.
