False statements of fact

False statements of fact are a category of speech recognised in United States constitutional law as receiving reduced or no protection under the First Amendment. The principle derives from the Supreme Court's observation in Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc. (1974) that "there is no constitutional value in false statements of fact", although the Court has also cautioned that some false speech must be protected to give true speech the "breathing space" it needs.

Legal treatment

Historically, categories such as defamation, fraud, perjury and false statements to the government have been punishable. Defamation law permits recovery for knowingly or negligently false factual statements that harm a person's reputation, while public officials and public figures must prove "actual malice" under New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964). Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 1001) criminalises materially false statements to federal officials, and perjury statutes punish lies under oath.

United States v. Alvarez

In United States v. Alvarez (2012), the Supreme Court struck down the Stolen Valor Act of 2005, which had made it a crime to lie about receiving military honours. The plurality rejected the argument that false statements are categorically unprotected merely because they are false, holding instead that falsity alone does not remove speech from the First Amendment where no legally cognisable harm — as in fraud or defamation — is involved. The decision left open regulation of false speech tied to specific harms while protecting mere lies about oneself.
