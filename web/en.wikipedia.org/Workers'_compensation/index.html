Workers' compensation

Workers' compensation or workers' comp is a form of insurance providing wage replacement and medical benefits to employees injured in the course of employment, in exchange for mandatory relinquishment of the employee's right to sue the employer for negligence. This "compensation bargain" is the basis of no-fault workers' compensation systems around the world.

History

Before compensation statutes, injured workers had to sue their employers and overcome defences such as contributory negligence, assumption of risk, and the "fellow servant" rule — so most received nothing. Chancellor Otto von Bismarck's Germany introduced the first modern workers' compensation law in 1884 as part of his pioneering social-insurance system. Britain followed with the Workmen's Compensation Act 1897. In the United States, the Triangle Shirtwaist factory fire of 1911 galvanised reform; Wisconsin enacted the first effective state statute that year, and by 1949 all states had schemes. Australia, Canada and other industrial countries adopted similar systems, often compulsory and state-administered or insurer-delivered.

How it works

Employers must insure (or self-insure) against work-related injury and occupational disease. An injured employee files a claim; benefits typically include full medical treatment, temporary disability payments of roughly two-thirds of wages, permanent disability awards, vocational rehabilitation, and death benefits for dependants. Because benefits are paid regardless of fault, workers generally cannot also sue for pain and suffering, though third parties may still be liable.

Issues

Debates continue over benefit adequacy, fraud, claim suppression, classification of gig-economy workers as independent contractors (often outside coverage), and the recognition of occupational diseases and mental-health conditions.
