Islamic banking and finance

Islamic banking, Islamic finance, or Sharia-compliant finance is banking and financial activity consistent with the principles of Islamic law (Sharia) and its practical application through the development of Islamic economics. Sharia prohibits the acceptance of interest or fees for loans of money (riba), investment in businesses that provide goods or services considered contrary to Islamic principles (haram), and excessive uncertainty in contracts (gharar). Instead, Islamic finance emphasises profit-and-loss sharing, asset-backed transactions and trade-based structures.

Kuala Lumpur and the Gulf states are leading centres of the Islamic finance industry.

Principles

  • Prohibition of riba — interest on loans is forbidden; money is treated as a measure of value rather than a commodity that can itself be rented.
  • Risk sharing — provider and user of capital should share risks and returns, as in partnership contracts (mudarabah and musharakah).
  • Asset-backing — transactions must be linked to real assets or services, as in cost-plus sales (murabaha) and leasing (ijara).
  • Avoidance of gharar and maysir — excessive uncertainty and gambling-like speculation are prohibited.
  • Ethical investment screens — no financing of alcohol, pork, gambling, conventional interest-based finance or armaments, varying by board interpretation.

Products

Common instruments include sukuk — certificates representing ownership in assets, often described as Islamic bonds; takaful, cooperative Islamic insurance; and home-purchase plans structured as diminishing partnerships or leases rather than interest-bearing mortgages. Islamic banks maintain Sharia supervisory boards of scholars who certify products as compliant, while standard-setting bodies such as AAOIFI (Bahrain) and the IFSB (Kuala Lumpur) issue accounting and prudential standards.

History and scale

Modern Islamic banking began with experiments such as Egypt's Mit Ghamr savings bank (1963) and the founding of Dubai Islamic Bank (1975). Petrodollar wealth, the Islamic Development Bank (1975) and Malaysia's deliberate dual-system policy drove rapid growth from the 1990s. The industry's assets reached the order of several trillion US dollars by the 2020s, concentrated in Iran, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Malaysia, Kuwait and Qatar, with Islamic windows operated by many conventional banks and active markets in London and other Western centres.

Criticism

Critics — including some Islamic scholars — argue that many products replicate the cash flows of interest-based finance under different names ("Sharia arbitrage"), while economists debate whether profit-sharing ideals are realised in practice. Others question the consistency of rulings across jurisdictions and scholars.
