The Supreme Court of the United States (SCOTUS) is the highest court in the federal judiciary of the United States. It has ultimate appellate jurisdiction over all federal and state court cases involving federal law, and original jurisdiction over a small range of cases, notably suits between states. The Court holds the power of judicial review—the ability to invalidate statutes and executive actions that contravene the Constitution—a power established in Marbury v. Madison (1803).

Composition and appointment

Under the Judiciary Act of 1869 the Court consists of the Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices. Justices are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, and serve during "good behaviour"—in practice, for life, until death, retirement or removal by impeachment. The Court's size is set by Congress and has varied historically from six to ten members.

Jurisdiction and procedure

Nearly all cases reach the Court by petition for a writ of certiorari; the justices choose which to hear under the "rule of four". Of the several thousand petitions filed each term, the Court typically grants fewer than one hundred. Terms begin on the first Monday in October. Decisions are delivered as majority, concurring and dissenting opinions authored by individual justices.

Landmark decisions

Among its most consequential rulings are Marbury v. Madison (1803, judicial review), Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857, widely condemned and later repudiated), Brown v. Board of Education (1954, school desegregation), Miranda v. Arizona (1966, rights of the accused), Roe v. Wade (1973, abortion; overruled by Dobbs v. Jackson in 2022), and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015, same-sex marriage).

The building

For most of its history the Court met in the United States Capitol. Its permanent home, the Supreme Court Building—designed by Cass Gilbert in neoclassical style and bearing the motto "Equal Justice Under Law"—opened in 1935, championed by Chief Justice (and former President) William Howard Taft.
