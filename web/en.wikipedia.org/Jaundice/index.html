Jaundice, also known as icterus, is a yellowish or greenish pigmentation of the skin and sclera (the whites of the eyes) due to high bilirubin levels. It is a sign, not a disease in itself, and arises when bilirubin—produced by the breakdown of haem from old red blood cells—accumulates faster than the liver can conjugate and excrete it into bile. Jaundice becomes visible when serum bilirubin exceeds roughly 2–3 mg/dL and is often accompanied by dark urine, pale stools and itching (pruritus) when the cause is obstructive.

Causes

Causes are classically divided into three groups. Pre-hepatic jaundice results from excessive red-cell destruction (haemolysis), as in haemolytic anaemias, malaria or transfusion reactions, overwhelming the liver with unconjugated bilirubin. Hepatic (hepatocellular) jaundice reflects liver disease itself—viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, cirrhosis, drug injury, or inherited disorders such as Gilbert's syndrome and Crigler–Najjar syndrome. Post-hepatic (obstructive) jaundice follows blockage of the bile ducts by gallstones, strictures, or tumours of the pancreas or bile duct (cholangiocarcinoma).

Newborn jaundice

Most newborns develop mild physiological jaundice in the first week as the immature liver adapts; it is usually harmless and resolves spontaneously. Severe hyperbilirubinaemia, however, can cause kernicterus, a form of brain damage, and is treated with phototherapy or, rarely, exchange transfusion. Breast milk jaundice and haemolytic disease of the newborn (for example from Rh incompatibility) are recognised causes requiring monitoring.

Diagnosis and treatment

Evaluation combines history and examination with liver function tests, fractionated bilirubin, blood counts and imaging such as abdominal ultrasound to detect duct obstruction. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: antivirals or abstinence for hepatitis, endoscopic or surgical relief of obstruction, and supportive care while the liver recovers.
