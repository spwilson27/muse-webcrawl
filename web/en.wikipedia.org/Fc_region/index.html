Fc region

The fragment crystallizable region (Fc region) is the tail region of an antibody (immunoglobulin) that interacts with cell-surface receptors called Fc receptors and with some proteins of the complement system. It forms the base of the Y-shaped antibody molecule, below the hinge that connects it to the two antigen-binding (Fab) arms. The name derives from early experiments in which the protease papain cleaved the antibody molecule into two Fab fragments and a third fragment that readily crystallised.

Schematic of an antibody; the Fc region forms the stem of the Y.

Structure

The Fc region consists of the constant domains of the two heavy chains of the antibody — in immunoglobulin G (IgG), the CH2 and CH3 domains. It is a homodimer held together by non-covalent interactions and, in most classes, disulfide bonds in the hinge region. A conserved N-linked glycan is attached to each CH2 domain at asparagine 297 (in IgG), and the composition of this glycan strongly influences binding to Fc receptors and hence the effector activity of the antibody.

Function

Because the Fc region does not bind antigen, its role is to recruit effector mechanisms once the Fab arms have bound their target. Binding of the Fc region to Fcγ receptors on phagocytes triggers antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, while engagement of FcγRIIIa on natural killer cells triggers antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC). The Fc region also binds the first component of complement, C1q, initiating the classical complement pathway, and binds the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which rescues IgG from degradation and accounts for its long serum half-life of about three weeks.

Engineering

In therapeutic monoclonal antibodies, the Fc region is frequently engineered: mutations can silence effector function, extend half-life through enhanced FcRn binding, or enhance ADCC through afucosylation of the Fc glycan. Fc fusion proteins, such as etanercept, join the Fc region to another protein to confer antibody-like pharmacokinetics.
