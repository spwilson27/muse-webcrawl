Abolitionism in Brazil

Key lawLei Áurea (Golden Law), 13 May 1888
Earlier lawsLaw of the Free Womb (1871); Sexagenarian Law (1885)
Key figuresJoaquim Nabuco, José do Patrocínio, Luís Gama, Princess Isabel

Abolitionism in Brazil was the movement that campaigned for the end of slavery in the Empire of Brazil, culminating in the Lei Áurea ("Golden Law") of 13 May 1888, which abolished slavery entirely. Brazil was the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery and had received the largest number of enslaved Africans of any country in the transatlantic slave trade.

Gradual emancipation

Under British pressure Brazil formally ended the transatlantic trade in 1850. Emancipation then proceeded by stages: the Law of the Free Womb (Rio Branco Law, 1871) declared children born to enslaved women legally free, and the Sexagenarian Law (1885) freed enslaved people over sixty. In the 1880s abolitionism became a mass movement: figures such as the lawyer Luís Gama, the journalist José do Patrocínio and the statesman Joaquim Nabuco, author of O Abolicionismo (1883), campaigned in the press and parliament, while enslaved people themselves resisted and fled in growing numbers. Ceará and Amazonas abolished slavery provincially in 1884.

Abolition and aftermath

In May 1888, with slavery collapsing in practice, the parliament passed and the regent Princess Isabel signed the Lei Áurea, a two-article law declaring slavery extinct in Brazil. No compensation was paid to owners and no provision was made for the freed population, whose descendants faced lasting marginalisation. Abolition also weakened the monarchy, which fell the following year.
