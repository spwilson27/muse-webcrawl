Network packet

A network packet is a formatted unit of data carried by a packet-switched network. A packet consists of control information and user data: the control information, held in the packet's header (and sometimes a trailer), provides the data the network needs to deliver the payload — such as source and destination addresses, sequence numbers, and error-checking codes — while the user data is known as the payload. Packets are the fundamental unit of communication over the Internet and virtually all modern data networks.

Structure and encapsulation

As data passes down the protocol stack, each layer wraps it in its own header — a process called encapsulation. A segment from the transport layer (e.g. TCP or UDP) is encapsulated in an IP packet, which is in turn carried inside a link-layer frame such as an Ethernet frame. At each hop, routers read the IP header to forward the packet toward its destination, where the layers are peeled away in reverse.

Packet switching

In packet switching, messages are broken into packets that may travel over different routes and be reassembled at the destination; links are shared statistically among many flows, making far more efficient use of capacity than reserving a circuit. Packet size is limited by the path's maximum transmission unit (MTU) — 1500 bytes on standard Ethernet — with larger payloads fragmented or handled by path MTU discovery. Packets may be lost, delayed, duplicated, or reordered by the network; reliable protocols such as TCP detect and repair these problems using sequence numbers, acknowledgements, and retransmission.
