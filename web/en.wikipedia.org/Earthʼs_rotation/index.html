Earth's rotation

Earth's rotation is the rotation of planet Earth around its own axis, as well as changes in the orientation of the rotation axis in space. Earth rotates west to east. Viewed from the northern polar star Polaris, Earth turns counterclockwise. The North Pole is the point in the Northern Hemisphere where Earth's axis of rotation meets its surface; the South Pole is the other point where the axis meets the surface, in Antarctica.

Earth rotates west to east once per sidereal day.

Period

Earth rotates once relative to the distant stars in about 23 hours, 56 minutes, and 4 seconds — the sidereal day. Relative to the Sun, the period is on average 24 hours, the solar day, because Earth's orbital motion means it must turn a little further to bring the Sun back to the same meridian. The length of day is not constant: the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS) monitors rotation with techniques including very-long-baseline interferometry, satellite laser ranging, and GNSS, publishing the difference between astronomical time (UT1) and atomic time (UTC). Leap seconds were introduced from 1972 to keep UTC within 0.9 seconds of UT1, a practice international bodies have voted to abandon by or before 2035.

At the equator, the surface moves at about 1,670 kilometres per hour (about 465 m/s). The tangible effects of rotation include the alternation of day and night, the apparent daily motion of celestial bodies, the Coriolis effect on winds and ocean currents, the equatorial bulge that makes Earth an oblate spheroid, and the deflection demonstrated by the Foucault pendulum.

Variations

Earth's rotation speed changes on many timescales. Tidal friction from the Moon transfers angular momentum from Earth to the Moon's orbit: the day lengthens by roughly 2.3 milliseconds per century on average, and the Moon recedes about 3.8 cm per year; geological evidence from tidal rhythmites and ancient eclipse records shows days were far shorter in the remote past — roughly 18.7 hours some 620 million years ago and perhaps around 19 hours for a long stretch of the Proterozoic. Seasonal winds and ocean currents exchange angular momentum with the solid Earth, changing the length of day by fractions of a millisecond; the El Niño cycle has a measurable signature. Earthquakes redistribute mass — the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake shortened the day by about 1.8 microseconds — and melting ice and groundwater movement produce small secular trends; in the early 2020s rotation briefly sped up enough that some shortest-ever days were recorded. The rotation axis itself moves: polar motion includes the roughly 433-day Chandler wobble and an annual component, while the whole axis precesses over about 26,000 years and nutates with an 18.6-year period.

History of understanding

Daily rotation of the Earth was proposed in antiquity by figures such as Heraclides Ponticus and Aristarchus (in the heliocentric context), but the geocentric consensus held the heavens rotated instead. Copernicus assigned daily motion to Earth in 1543; Galileo defended it; Newton's mechanics explained it, and the conservation of angular momentum from the collapsing cloud that formed the solar system explains its origin. Foucault's pendulum (1851) provided a direct, popular demonstration.
