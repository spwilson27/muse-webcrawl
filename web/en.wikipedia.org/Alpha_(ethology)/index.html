Alpha (ethology)

Field: Ethology
Meaning: Highest-ranking individual in a social group
Popularised by: Wolf-pack research; Rudolf Schenkel (1947)
Related terms: Dominance hierarchy, beta, omega

In ethology, an alpha is the highest-ranking individual in a social group of animals. The term arose from studies of captive wolves, in which Rudolf Schenkel (1947) described an "alpha pair" leading the pack. It was later popularised by L. David Mech's 1970 book The Wolf. Subsequent research on wild wolves showed that packs are usually families, in which the "alphas" are simply the breeding parents, and Mech has spent decades trying to correct the misconception of packs led by fiercely competing alpha wolves.

Use across species

Dominance ranks are well documented in primates, where alpha males (and in some species alpha females) gain priority access to food and mates but may also maintain group order. Among chimpanzees, alpha status depends on alliances and social intelligence as much as strength. The concept has been extended, often loosely, to chickens (pecking order), elephants, and meerkats.

Cultural usage

"Alpha male" has entered popular culture as a label for a dominant, assertive man, a usage animal behaviour researchers criticise as a distortion of the underlying science. Ethologists generally prefer neutral terms such as "breeding male" or "dominant individual".
