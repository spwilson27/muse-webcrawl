Electoral Count Act

The Electoral Count Act of 1887 (ECA) is a United States federal law establishing procedures for the counting of electoral votes by Congress following a presidential election. It was enacted in response to the disputed election of 1876 and the ad hoc Electoral Commission that resolved it.

Congress counts the states' electoral votes in a joint session each January after a presidential election.

Provisions

The Act set a "safe harbor" deadline by which states should resolve disputes over their electors, provided that determinations made under laws enacted before election day would be treated as conclusive by Congress. It limited the grounds and procedure for objections during the joint session of Congress on 6 January, requiring any objection to be made in writing and signed by at least one member of each house—later raised to one-fifth of each chamber.

Later use and controversy

The Act's ambiguities were debated during the 2000 election recount in Florida and prominently after the 2020 election, when objections were raised to several states' electoral votes and the counting session was interrupted by the attack on the Capitol on 6 January 2021.

2022 reform

The Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition Improvement Act of 2022 substantially revised the ECA. It clarified that the Vice President's role in the counting is purely ministerial, raised the threshold for objections to one-fifth of each chamber, tightened the rules for state certification, and specified that only a state's governor (or other official identified in pre-election law) may submit a certificate of ascertainment.
