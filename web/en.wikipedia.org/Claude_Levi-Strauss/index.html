Claude Lévi-Strauss

Claude Lévi-Strauss (28 November 1908 – 30 October 2009) was a French anthropologist and ethnologist whose work was key in the development of the theories of structuralism and structural anthropology. He held the chair of Social Anthropology at the Collège de France between 1959 and 1982, was elected a member of the Académie française in 1973, and was a member of the French Academy. He is often called the "father of modern anthropology."

Life

Lévi-Strauss was born in Brussels to French Jewish parents and grew up in Paris, where he studied law and philosophy at the Sorbonne. In 1935 he took a post teaching sociology at the new University of São Paulo, and his expeditions among the Caduveo, Bororo, Nambikwara, and Tupi-Kawahib peoples of Brazil formed the basis of his later ethnographic work. After the fall of France he escaped to the United States, teaching at the New School for Social Research in New York, where his friendship with linguist Roman Jakobson helped shape structuralism. He returned to France in 1948 and published his doctoral thesis, The Elementary Structures of Kinship, in 1949.

Work

In Tristes Tropiques (1955), a memoir of his fieldwork written in a celebrated literary style, Lévi-Strauss reflected on the loss of indigenous cultures. His structural anthropology proposed that human minds everywhere organize experience through binary oppositions—raw and cooked, nature and culture, life and death—and that myths, kinship systems, and rituals are variations on universal structures. Major works include Structural Anthropology (1958), Totemism, The Savage Mind (1962), and the four-volume Mythologiques (1964–1971), beginning with The Raw and the Cooked, which analyzed hundreds of South American myths as transformations of a single system.

His analysis of kinship argued that the incest taboo is the universal rule that creates society by obliging groups to exchange spouses, making marriage a system of communication comparable to language. Structuralism, the method he pioneered, profoundly influenced literary theory, philosophy, and the human sciences in the 1960s, shaping thinkers such as Roland Barthes, Jacques Lacan, and Michel Foucault, before attracting criticism from post-structuralists and later anthropologists.

Legacy

Lévi-Strauss lived to 100 and remained a towering figure in French intellectual life. UNESCO marked his centenary in 2008. His insistence on the equal rationality of all human cultures and his warnings about the destruction of cultural diversity left a lasting mark on anthropology and public thought.
