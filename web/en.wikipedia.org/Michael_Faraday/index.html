Michael Faraday

Michael Faraday (1791–1867) was an English scientist who contributed to the study of electromagnetism and electrochemistry more than any other experimentalist of his century. Largely self-taught, he rose from bookbinder's apprentice to become the dominant figure of the Royal Institution in London. Albert Einstein kept a picture of Faraday on his wall alongside Newton and Maxwell.

Early life and Davy

Faraday was born into a poor family of the Sandemanian Christian sect and received only a basic education. Apprenticed to a bookbinder, he educated himself by reading the books he bound. After attending Humphry Davy's lectures in 1812, he sent Davy a bound volume of his notes and was hired as his assistant. He accompanied Davy on a European tour, married Sarah Barnard in 1821, and became Director of the Laboratory at the Royal Institution in 1825 and its first Fullerian Professor of Chemistry in 1833.

Discoveries

In 1821 Faraday demonstrated electromagnetic rotation — the principle of the electric motor. In 1831 he discovered electromagnetic induction, showing that a changing magnetic field induces an electric current, and built the first dynamo (the Faraday disc); this underlies every electric generator and transformer. His laws of electrolysis (1833–34) founded electrochemistry and gave science the terms electrode, anode, cathode, ion (coined with William Whewell). In chemistry he discovered benzene (1825), liquefied chlorine and other gases, and investigated alloys and optical glass. His 1845 discovery that magnetism rotates the plane of polarised light (the Faraday effect) and his work on diamagnetism led him to conceive of electric and magnetic fields filling space — an intuition James Clerk Maxwell later turned into the mathematical theory of the electromagnetic field.

Lectures and character

Faraday founded the Royal Institution's Christmas Lectures for young people in 1825 and delivered nineteen series himself; his Chemical History of a Candle remains a classic of popular science. A devout Sandemanian, he declined a knighthood and the presidency of the Royal Society, and turned down burial in Westminster Abbey; he is buried in Highgate Cemetery. The SI unit of capacitance, the farad, is named after him, as is the Faraday constant in electrochemistry.
