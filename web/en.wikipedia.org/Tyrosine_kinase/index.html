A tyrosine kinase is an enzyme that can transfer a phosphate group from ATP to the tyrosine residues of specific proteins inside a cell. It functions as an "on" or "off" switch in many cellular functions. Tyrosine kinases belong to a larger class of enzymes known as protein kinases, which also attach phosphates to other amino acids (serine and threonine).

Classes

In humans there are 90 tyrosine kinase genes. They are divided into two large families: receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs, 58 genes in 20 subfamilies), which span the cell membrane and include the insulin receptor and growth factor receptors such as EGFR, VEGFR, and PDGFR; and non-receptor (cytoplasmic) tyrosine kinases (32 genes in 10 families), including the Src, Abl, FAK, and Janus kinase (JAK) families.

Function

Phosphorylation of tyrosine residues by TKs creates binding sites for SH2 and PTB domain proteins, recruiting signaling complexes that control cell growth, division, differentiation, adhesion, and apoptosis. Regulation of TK activity is critical — dysregulation leads to cancer and developmental disorders.

Cancer and drugs

Many cancers are driven by constitutively active tyrosine kinases: BCR-ABL in chronic myeloid leukemia, HER2 amplification in breast cancer, mutant EGFR and ALK rearrangements in lung cancer. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) such as imatinib (Gleevec), erlotinib, gefitinib, and osimertinib are landmark targeted cancer therapies.
