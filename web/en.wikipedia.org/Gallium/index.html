Gallium is a chemical element with the symbol Ga and atomic number 31. It is a soft, silvery metal that melts at 29.76 °C — just above room temperature, so it will liquefy in a person's hand. Discovered spectroscopically by the French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran in 1875, gallium was one of the elements predicted by Dmitri Mendeleev ("eka-aluminium"), and its measured properties closely matched his predictions, strengthening acceptance of the periodic table.

Properties

Gallium expands on freezing (like water and bismuth) and wets glass and most metals. It has one of the largest liquid ranges of any metal, remaining liquid up to 2,203 °C, which makes the pure metal useful in high-temperature thermometers. Elemental gallium is not found in nature; it is obtained chiefly as a by-product of refining bauxite and zinc ores.

Compounds and uses

The principal uses of gallium are in semiconductor compounds. Gallium arsenide (GaAs) is used in integrated circuits, laser diodes, LEDs, and high-efficiency solar cells; gallium nitride (GaN) underpins blue and white LEDs and modern power electronics, and its invention enabled the Blu-ray disc. Gallium-based alloys such as Galinstan serve as non-toxic substitutes for mercury in thermometers. Radioactive gallium-67 and gallium-68 are used in nuclear medicine imaging, including PET scans.

Safety

Elemental gallium is considered of low toxicity, though it attacks aluminium and other structural metals by liquid-metal embrittlement — a classic chemistry demonstration in which a gallium-treated aluminium object crumbles.
