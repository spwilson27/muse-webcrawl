Roman Agora

LocationPlaka district, Athens, Greece
Period1st century BC – Roman Imperial period
PatronsJulius Caesar and Augustus (funding)
Notable structuresTower of the Winds, Gate of Athena Archegetis, Agoranomion, Fethiye Mosque

The Roman Agora (Greek: Ρωμαϊκή Αγορά) is an archaeological site in the Plaka district of Athens, Greece, located to the east of the ancient Agora and north of the Acropolis. Built in the late 1st century BC with funds donated by Julius Caesar and Augustus, it served as the commercial marketplace of Roman Athens, gradually taking over the market functions of the older Greek agora. Its best-preserved monument is the Tower of the Winds, considered the world's first meteorological station.

History

As Athens' ancient Agora became crowded with monuments, a dedicated commercial centre was planned to the east. Construction began with a grant from Julius Caesar around 51 BC and was completed under Augustus, with the complex dedicated about 11–9 BC. The Roman Agora housed the city's markets, including the oil and grain trades, under the supervision of market officials (agoranomoi). The site continued in use through Byzantine and Ottoman times, when the Fethiye Mosque was built on its edge in 1456, shortly after the Ottoman conquest.

Description

The complex is a large rectangular courtyard, about 111 by 104 metres, originally surrounded by Ionic colonnades with shops behind them. The main western entrance is the Gate of Athena Archegetis, a marble propylon of four Doric columns erected 11–9 BC with donations from Augustus; an eastern propylon in the Ionic order also survives. A dedicatory inscription records the role of the Athenian people and the priest and archon Nikias.

Within and around the court stood a market building sometimes identified as the Agoranomion (the market inspectors' office, decorated with reliefs of Augustus and Claudius), latrines, fountains, and in later centuries churches and the Fethiye Mosque, built for Mehmed the Conqueror.

Tower of the Winds

Just east of the courtyard stands the Tower of the Winds (Horologion of Andronikos Kyrrhestes), an octagonal marble tower built about 50 BC by the astronomer Andronicus of Cyrrhus. Each face bears a relief of one of the eight wind gods, below which were sundials; inside was a sophisticated water clock (clepsydra) fed from the Acropolis, and a rotating bronze Triton on top acted as a weathervane. In early Christian times the tower served as a church bell tower; under the Ottomans, dervishes used it as a tekke. It is the best-preserved building of the Roman Agora and one of the most complete surviving structures of classical antiquity.

Excavations

Systematic excavation by the Greek Archaeological Society began in 1837 and continued through the 20th century as the dense Ottoman-era housing of Plaka was cleared. The site remains an active archaeological park adjacent to the Ancient Agora and the Library of Hadrian.
