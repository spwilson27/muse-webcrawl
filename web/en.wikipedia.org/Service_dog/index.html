Service dog


A guide dog guiding its blind handler.

A service dog is a dog individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. Service dogs are a type of service animal and are also known in many countries as assistance dogs. Common categories include guide dogs for blind and visually impaired people, hearing dogs for deaf people, mobility assistance dogs, medical alert and response dogs, and psychiatric service dogs.

History

Organised guide dog training began in Germany during and after the First World War to assist blinded veterans; the first school, founded in Oldenburg in 1916, was followed by schools in other countries. In the United States, Dorothy Harrison Eustis founded The Seeing Eye in 1929 after writing about the German programme. From the 1970s onward, training expanded beyond guide work to hearing, mobility and other forms of assistance, and organisations such as Assistance Dogs International developed standards and accreditation.

Training

Service dogs undergo extensive socialisation and obedience training, followed by task training specific to their handler's disability, often totalling 18–24 months before placement. Common breeds include Labrador Retrievers, Golden Retrievers and Standard Poodles, selected for temperament, health and size. Dogs that are unsuitable ("career-change" dogs) are typically rehomed as pets. Handlers may also train their own dogs, a right recognised under US law.

Access rights

In the United States, the Americans with Disabilities Act grants people with disabilities the right to be accompanied by a service dog in places of public accommodation, housing law provides related protections, and the Air Carrier Access Act framework now limits airline recognition to trained service dogs. Many other countries grant similar access to accredited assistance dogs; etiquette guidance stresses that service dogs should not be petted, fed or distracted while working.
