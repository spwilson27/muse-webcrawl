Tailless aircraft

Definition: Fixed-wing aircraft with no separate horizontal tailplane
Pitch control: Elevons on the wing
Examples: Concorde, Vulcan, Mirage III, Me 163
Related: Flying wing, delta wing, canard

A tailless aircraft is a fixed-wing aircraft with no separate horizontal stabilising surface — neither a conventional tailplane nor a forward canard. Pitch and roll control is combined in surfaces on the wing trailing edge called elevons.

Design

Without a tail to provide a balancing moment, a tailless design must achieve longitudinal stability from the wing itself, using sweep combined with washout (twist) or reflexed aerofoil sections. Tailless aircraft include pure flying wings, in which the fuselage is integrated into the wing, and tailless deltas, which retain a distinct fuselage. Removing the tail reduces drag and weight and can lower radar cross-section, at the cost of reduced pitch authority and a limited centre-of-gravity range.

History

Early tailless designs include J. W. Dunne's swept, twisted biplanes (from 1910), among the first inherently stable aeroplanes, and Alexander Lippisch's deltas. Wartime examples include the rocket-powered Messerschmitt Me 163 Komet and the Horten Ho 229 flying wing jet. After the war, tailless deltas became prominent in military aviation — the Avro Vulcan bomber, Dassault Mirage fighters — and in the supersonic airliner Concorde. Modern fly-by-wire controls have eased the stability penalties, and many stealth aircraft and UAVs adopt tailless configurations.
