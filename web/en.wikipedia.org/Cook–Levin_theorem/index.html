Cook–Levin theorem

In computational complexity theory, the Cook–Levin theorem, also known as Cook's theorem, states that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete. That is, any problem in the complexity class NP can be reduced in polynomial time to deciding the satisfiability of a Boolean formula. The theorem was proved by Stephen Cook in his 1971 paper "The complexity of theorem proving procedures" and, independently, by Leonid Levin in the Soviet Union in 1973.

Significance

The Cook–Levin theorem was the first demonstration that an NP-complete problem exists, establishing the template for all later completeness results: to show a problem is NP-complete, it suffices to reduce SAT (or any known NP-complete problem) to it in polynomial time. Richard Karp's 1972 list of 21 NP-complete problems built directly on this foundation. The theorem also sharpens the P versus NP problem: a polynomial-time algorithm for SAT alone would yield polynomial algorithms for every problem in NP.

Proof idea

The proof encodes the entire computation of a nondeterministic Turing machine running in polynomial time as a Boolean formula. Variables describe the contents of each tape cell, the head position and the machine's state at each time step; clauses enforce that the initial configuration is correct, that each step follows the machine's transition rules, and that some step reaches an accepting state. The formula is satisfiable exactly when the machine has an accepting computation, and its size is polynomial in the running time, giving the required reduction.

History

Cook presented the result at the 1971 ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing. Levin, working in the USSR on universal search problems, arrived at a similar result independently; Western researchers learned of it later, and the theorem now bears both names.
