Greedy algorithm

A greedy algorithm builds a solution step by step, always taking the locally optimal choice.

A greedy algorithm is an algorithmic paradigm that builds up a solution piece by piece, always choosing the next piece to be the one that offers the most obvious and immediate benefit — the locally optimal choice — in the hope that these choices will lead to a globally optimal solution. Greedy algorithms are simple and efficient, but they are not guaranteed to find an optimum for every problem.

When greedy works

A problem can be solved optimally by a greedy algorithm when it has two properties: the greedy choice property, meaning a global optimum can be reached by making a locally optimal choice, and optimal substructure, meaning an optimal solution contains optimal solutions to its subproblems. Problems defined by matroids and certain related structures (such as greedoids) are guaranteed to admit greedy solutions. Classic examples include activity selection, Huffman coding, fractional knapsack, and minimum spanning tree algorithms such as Kruskal's and Prim's.

Failures and approximation

For many problems the greedy approach fails: making change with arbitrary coin denominations, the 0/1 knapsack problem, and the travelling salesman problem are standard counterexamples. Nevertheless, greedy heuristics often give good approximations — for example, the greedy set-cover algorithm achieves a logarithmic approximation ratio — and greedy methods are widely used in scheduling, graph algorithms, and compression.
