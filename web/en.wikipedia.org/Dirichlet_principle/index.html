Dirichlet principle

In mathematics, the Dirichlet principle most commonly refers to the pigeonhole principle, a counting argument also attributed to Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet. In its simplest form it states that if more than n objects are distributed into n boxes, at least one box must contain more than one object. More generally, if m objects go into n boxes with m > n, some box holds at least ⌈m/n⌉ objects.

Applications

Despite its simplicity, the principle has far-reaching consequences. Dirichlet used it to prove his approximation theorem on Diophantine approximation and his theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions. It underlies arguments showing that hashing schemes must have collisions, that any set of points in a bounded region contains two close together, and numerous results in combinatorics, geometry, and computer science.

Generalizations

The infinite form states that if infinitely many objects are placed into finitely many boxes, some box contains infinitely many objects. Measure-theoretic and probabilistic (averaging) versions appear throughout analysis. The term is also used, in a different sense, for Dirichlet's principle in the calculus of variations concerning minimizers of the Dirichlet energy.
