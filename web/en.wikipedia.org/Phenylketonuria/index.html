Phenylketonuria

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an inborn error of metabolism that results in decreased metabolism of the amino acid phenylalanine. It is caused, in most cases, by mutations in the PAH gene encoding the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, which converts phenylalanine to tyrosine; the disorder is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Untreated, accumulating phenylalanine can lead to intellectual disability, seizures, behavioural problems, eczema, and a characteristic mousy odour of the skin and urine, where the excess is excreted as phenylketones — hence the name.

Screening and treatment

PKU was identified by the Norwegian physician Asbjørn Følling in 1934. Because dietary treatment is highly effective when begun early, PKU became the prototype of newborn screening: the Guthrie bacterial inhibition test, introduced in the 1960s, allowed mass testing of newborns from a dried blood spot. Treatment centres on a lifelong diet low in phenylalanine — restricting protein-rich foods and the sweetener aspartame (which contains phenylalanine) and supplementing with special amino-acid formulas. Newer therapies include sapropterin (a form of the cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin) and pegvaliase, an enzyme-substitution therapy for adults.

Incidence

PKU occurs in roughly 1 in 10,000 to 15,000 newborns in populations of European descent, with higher rates in Ireland and Turkey and lower rates in African and Asian populations. Maternal PKU — elevated phenylalanine in pregnancy — can damage a fetus that does not itself have PKU.
