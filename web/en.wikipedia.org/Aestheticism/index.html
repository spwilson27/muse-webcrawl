Aestheticism

Aestheticism (also the Aesthetic movement) was an art movement in the late 19th century that valued the appearance of literature, music, and the arts over their functions. According to Aestheticism, art should be produced to be beautiful, rather than to teach, moralize, or perform any other purpose, a stance exemplified by the slogan "art for art's sake." The movement flourished in Britain in the 1880s and had followers across Europe and North America.

Origins

The philosophy is generally traced to Théophile Gautier in France, who suggested art is inherently amoral and need serve no purpose. The Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, Walter Pater's criticism, and James McNeill Whistler's paintings helped shape the English movement. Oscar Wilde became its most famous public figure, promoting aesthetic dress and decor in lectures across Britain and America.

Characteristics

Aesthetic artists emphasized intense mood, refinement of sensation, and elaborate craftsmanship and symbolism. In literature the movement is associated with the Decadents; in the visual arts with Whistler, Dante Gabriel Rossetti, and Aubrey Beardsley's illustrations. Japonisme and blue-and-white porcelain became fashionable emblems of aesthetic taste.

Decline

The movement faded in the 1890s, hastened by Wilde's trials in 1895, but it influenced modernism and later movements such as Art Nouveau and symbolism.
