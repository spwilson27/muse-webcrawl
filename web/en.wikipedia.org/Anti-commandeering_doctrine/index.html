Anti-commandeering doctrine

In American constitutional law, the anti-commandeering doctrine holds that the federal government of the United States may not compel the states, or their officials, to implement or enforce federal regulatory programs. The doctrine derives from the Tenth Amendment and from the structure of federalism, and traces its roots to Prigg v. Pennsylvania (1842).

Key cases

In New York v. United States (1992), the Supreme Court held that Congress could not force a state legislature to enact a law requiring states to take title to low-level radioactive waste. In Printz v. United States (1997), the Court invalidated provisions of the Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act that required state and local officers to conduct background checks. In Murphy v. NCAA (2018), the Court extended the doctrine, holding that Congress may not dictate what a state legislature may and may not enact, and struck down the federal ban on state-authorized sports gambling.

Rationale and limits

The Court reasons that commandeering undermines political accountability — voters cannot tell which level of government to blame — and intrudes on state sovereignty. The doctrine does not prevent Congress from regulating individuals directly, preempting state law under the Supremacy Clause, or conditioning federal funds on state cooperation.
