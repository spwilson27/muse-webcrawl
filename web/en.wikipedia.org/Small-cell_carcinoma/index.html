Small-cell carcinoma

Micrograph of small-cell carcinoma of the lung

Small-cell carcinoma is a type of highly malignant cancer that most commonly arises within the lung, in which case it is called small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). It can also arise in other sites, including the prostate, cervix, oesophagus and gastrointestinal tract, where it is termed extrapulmonary small-cell carcinoma. The name derives from the appearance of the tumour cells under the microscope: they are small, round to spindle-shaped cells with scant cytoplasm, finely granular chromatin and inconspicuous nucleoli.

Small-cell lung cancer

Small-cell lung cancer accounts for roughly 13–15% of all lung cancers. It is strongly associated with cigarette smoking and typically arises centrally in the lung, near the bronchi. SCLC grows rapidly, metastasises early and is prone to paraneoplastic syndromes such as the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) and ectopic Cushing syndrome. It is distinguished from non-small-cell lung carcinoma both biologically and in its management.

Staging and treatment

SCLC is conventionally divided into limited-stage disease, confined to one hemithorax and treatable within a single radiation field, and extensive-stage disease, which has spread beyond it. The tumour is initially very sensitive to chemotherapy and radiation therapy; standard treatment combines platinum-based chemotherapy (cisplatin or carboplatin with etoposide) with thoracic radiotherapy in limited-stage disease. Immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors such as atezolizumab or durvalumab has been added to extensive-stage regimens. Despite high initial response rates, relapse is common and long-term prognosis remains poor.

Genetics

Near-universal features of SCLC include inactivation of the tumour suppressor genes TP53 and RB1. The cancer is classified among the neuroendocrine tumours because its cells express neuroendocrine markers and may secrete peptide hormones.
