Drama

The masks of tragedy and comedy, symbols of drama

Drama is the specific mode of fiction represented in performance: a play, opera, mime, ballet, etc., performed in a theatre, or on radio or television. Considered as a genre of poetry in general, the dramatic mode has been contrasted with the epic and the lyrical modes since Aristotle's Poetics (c. 335 BC) — the earliest work of dramatic theory. The term comes from a Greek word meaning "deed" or "act" (Classical Greek: δρᾶμα, drâma), derived from "I do" (Classical Greek: δράω, dráō).

History

Classical drama

Western drama originated in Athens in the 6th century BC, in religious festivals honouring Dionysus, where tragedies by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides and comedies by Aristophanes defined the classical canon. Roman drama adapted Greek forms in the tragedies of Seneca and comedies of Plautus and Terence. In Asia, Sanskrit theatre (Nāṭya Śāstra), Chinese opera and Japanese Noh developed independent dramatic traditions.

Medieval and early modern

European medieval theatre grew from church liturgy into mystery and morality plays. The Renaissance revived classical models: in England, Christopher Marlowe and William Shakespeare raised drama to new heights; Spain had Lope de Vega and Calderón; France codified neoclassical tragedy under Racine and comedy under Molière.

Modern drama

The 19th century brought realism and naturalism in Ibsen, Strindberg, Chekhov and Shaw. Twentieth-century movements included expressionism, Brecht's epic theatre, the Theatre of the Absurd (Beckett, Ionesco), and postwar playwrights from Arthur Miller and Tennessee Williams to Caryl Churchill and August Wilson.

Forms and media

Beyond the stage, "drama" denotes serious works for film and television, radio plays, and a genre of literature and film characterised by emotional conflict. Major subgenres include tragedy, comedy, melodrama, tragicomedy and historical drama.

Elements

Aristotle identified six elements of drama: plot, character, thought (theme), diction, music and spectacle. Modern theory continues to analyse dramatic structure — exposition, rising action, climax and resolution — and conventions such as dialogue, soliloquy, aside and the fourth wall.
