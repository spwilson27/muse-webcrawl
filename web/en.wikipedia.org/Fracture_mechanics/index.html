Fracture mechanics

Fracture mechanics quantifies how cracks grow in materials and when they cause failure.

Fracture mechanics is the field of mechanics concerned with the study of the propagation of cracks in materials. It uses methods of analytical solid mechanics to calculate the driving force on a crack and those of experimental solid mechanics to characterise a material's resistance to fracture, its fracture toughness.

Foundations

The theoretical foundations were laid by A. A. Griffith in 1921, who explained the weakness of brittle materials by an energy balance: a crack extends when the elastic strain energy released exceeds the energy needed to create new surfaces. In the 1950s George Irwin recast Griffith's ideas in terms of stresses near a crack tip, introducing the stress intensity factor K, and showed that a crack grows unstably when K reaches a critical material property, the fracture toughness KIc.

Branches

Linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM) applies when plastic deformation near the crack tip is small, as in brittle fracture. Where plasticity is significant, elastic–plastic fracture mechanics uses parameters such as the J-integral (developed by James R. Rice) and crack-tip opening displacement. Time-dependent crack growth — fatigue under cyclic loading, creep, and stress-corrosion cracking — is described by relations such as Paris's law, which links crack growth rate per cycle to the range of the stress intensity factor.

Applications

Fracture mechanics underpins the safe design and inspection of aircraft, ships, bridges, pressure vessels and pipelines. Catastrophic failures such as those of the Liberty ships in the Second World War and the de Havilland Comet airliners in the 1950s drove its development and the routine use of damage-tolerance analysis and non-destructive testing.
