Pantograph (transport)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pantograph is an apparatus mounted on the roof of an electric train, tram or electric bus to collect power through contact with an overhead catenary wire. It consists of a sprung, jointed frame — traditionally in a diamond shape, though single-arm (Z-shaped) designs are now common — topped by a wide contact head fitted with carbon strips that slide along the wire as the vehicle moves.

Operation

Springs or pneumatic actuators press the pantograph upward so that the contact strips maintain steady pressure against the wire despite variations in wire height, while its low mass allows it to follow the wire's undulations at speed. The carbon strips are sacrificial: cheaper and easier to replace than the copper contact wire, they also help lubricate the contact. High-speed trains may use specially designed pantographs, sometimes with small aerodynamic flaps, and careful matching of pantograph and overhead-line dynamics is essential above roughly 200 km/h to avoid loss of contact and arcing.

History

Pantographs were developed in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as an improvement on the trolley pole and bow collector, which were unsuitable for higher speeds and currents. The flat "paddle" pantograph appeared on American interurban lines around 1900, and the diamond frame design became standard on main-line electric railways worldwide. Alternatives such as the third rail are used on many metro systems, but pantograph-and-catenary supply remains dominant for main-line and high-speed electrification.
