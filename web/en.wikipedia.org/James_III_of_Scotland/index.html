James III (c. 1451/1452 – 11 June 1488) was King of Scots from 1460 until his death in battle in 1488. His reign saw Scotland acquire Orkney and Shetland but was plagued by conflict with his nobility and his own brothers, ending in rebellion and the king's killing after the Battle of Sauchieburn.

Early reign

James succeeded his father James II, killed by an exploding cannon at Roxburgh, at about nine years old. His minority was dominated by the Boyd family, who seized the young king in 1466 and ruled until their overthrow in 1469. That year James married Margaret of Denmark; her dowry, partly unpaid, was redeemed by pledging the Norwegian territories of Orkney (1468) and Shetland (1469), which were annexed to the Scottish crown — Scotland's last major territorial expansion.

Conflict and unpopularity

As an adult James pursued ambitious, expensive policies: dreams of recovering Brittany or conquering territory abroad, a grandiose choir at Stirling, and cultivation of low-born favourites — musicians, architects and craftsmen such as the mason Robert Cochrane — whom the magnates resented. His brothers Alexander, Duke of Albany, and John, Earl of Mar, quarrelled with him; Mar died in custody in 1479 and Albany allied with Edward IV of England. In 1482 an English army under Richard, Duke of Gloucester, advanced to Edinburgh while Scottish nobles at Lauder Bridge hanged Cochrane and other favourites and detained the king in Edinburgh Castle. James recovered authority but never trust.

Death

In 1488 discontented southern lords rebelled, using the king's 15-year-old heir, the future James IV, as their figurehead. James III was defeated at the Battle of Sauchieburn near Stirling on 11 June 1488 and died during or shortly after the battle — tradition says he was thrown from his horse and murdered at a nearby mill. He was buried at Cambuskenneth Abbey. His son, who had not authorised the attack, reportedly wore an iron penitential belt for the rest of his life.
