The Aswan Dam refers to two dams across the Nile River at Aswan in southern Egypt: the older Aswan Low Dam, built by the British (1898–1902, later twice heightened), and the Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, one of the world's largest embankment dams.

The High Dam

After the 1952 Egyptian revolution, President Gamal Abdel Nasser made the High Dam the centrepiece of national development. When the United States and Britain withdrew financing in 1956, Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal — precipitating the Suez Crisis — and the Soviet Union stepped in with loans and engineers. Construction ran from 1960 to 1970; the dam was officially inaugurated in January 1971.

Effects

The dam ended the Nile's annual flood cycle, enabled perennial irrigation and a major expansion of farmland, and generated large amounts of hydroelectricity. Its reservoir, Lake Nasser, is one of the world's largest artificial lakes. Costs included the displacement of over 100,000 people, mostly Nubians; the silting of the reservoir and loss of fertile flood silt; increased salinisation and coastal erosion in the delta; and threats to ancient monuments, prompting UNESCO's famous rescue campaign that moved the temples of Abu Simbel and Philae.
