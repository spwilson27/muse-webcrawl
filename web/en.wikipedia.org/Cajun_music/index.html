Cajun music

Cajun music
Stylistic originsFrench folk music, Acadian ballads, country, blues
Cultural origins18th–19th century, Acadiana, Louisiana
Typical instrumentsDiatonic accordion, fiddle, triangle, guitar, vocals
Related genresZydeco, swamp pop, Cajun country

Cajun music is an emblematic form of American roots music, rooted in the ballads of the French-speaking Acadians who settled in Louisiana after their expulsion from Canada in the 18th century. It is traditionally sung in Louisiana French and built around the diatonic button accordion and the fiddle.

History

Early Cajun music was a fiddle-driven tradition of ballads and dance tunes played at fais do-do house dances. The German-style diatonic accordion arrived in the late 19th century and came to dominate the sound. The first commercial Cajun recording, "Allons à Lafayette" by Joe Falcon and Cléoma Breaux, was made in 1928. Accordionist Amédé Ardoin, a Black Creole musician, laid foundations shared by both Cajun music and zydeco before his early death.

During the 1930s the influence of western swing and country music brought string bands and French-language covers of country songs. After the Second World War, musicians such as Iry LeJeune revived the accordion-centred sound. D. L. Menard's "La Porte En Arrière" (The Back Door) became the best-known Cajun song. In the 1960s and 1970s bands such as the Balfa Brothers carried the music to folk festivals, and a broader Cajun cultural renaissance followed.

Characteristics

Cajun dance music is built for two-steps and waltzes. A typical band pairs an accordion lead with fiddle, rhythm guitar, bass, drums and the triangle ('tit fer) keeping time. Lyrics are usually in French and deal with love, loss, dancing and bayou life. Cajun music differs from the related zydeco tradition, which developed among Black Creoles and leans more heavily on rhythm and blues, washboard percussion and piano accordion.
