Autosomal recessive

In autosomal recessive inheritance, two carrier parents have a 25% chance of an affected child in each pregnancy.

Autosomal recessive is one of several patterns by which a genetic trait or disorder is inherited. A condition is autosomal recessive when two copies of a disease-associated variant — one inherited from each parent — in a gene on an autosome (a non-sex chromosome) are required for the condition to appear. People with one copy are carriers: usually unaffected themselves, they can pass the variant to their children.

Characteristics

Recessive disorders often appear to "skip generations": affected children are commonly born to two unaffected carrier parents, and each pregnancy of such a couple carries a 25% chance of an affected child, a 50% chance of a carrier child and a 25% chance of a child with neither copy. Males and females are affected equally. Recessive conditions are more common in children of consanguineous parents, who are more likely to carry the same rare variant inherited from a shared ancestor.

Examples

Classic autosomal recessive disorders include cystic fibrosis, phenylketonuria (PKU), Tay–Sachs disease, sickle cell disease, spinal muscular atrophy, hemochromatosis and many inborn errors of metabolism. Most enzyme deficiencies are recessive, because one functioning copy of an enzyme gene usually produces enough enzyme activity. Carrier screening programmes — for example for Tay–Sachs in Ashkenazi Jewish populations and for thalassemias in Mediterranean and Asian populations — have substantially reduced the incidence of several severe recessive diseases.

Contrast with dominant inheritance

In autosomal dominant conditions a single variant copy suffices, so such conditions usually appear in every generation and each child of an affected person has a 50% risk. Recessive inheritance instead hides in carriers, which is why family history may show no previous cases.
