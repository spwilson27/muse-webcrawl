Persian Constitutional Revolution

Members of the first Majles (National Assembly), opened in October 1906.

The Persian Constitutional Revolution (Persian: مشروطیت, Mashrūtiyyat), also known as the Constitutional Revolution of Iran, took place between 1905 and 1911 during the Qajar dynasty. The revolution led to the establishment of a parliament in Persia (Iran) and the granting of the first Iranian constitution, making Persia one of the earliest constitutional states in Asia.

Background

By the turn of the 20th century, Persia was weakened by Qajar misrule, foreign concessions to Britain and Russia, rising taxation, and inflation. Merchants, clerics (ulama), and a growing class of intellectuals influenced by Enlightenment and constitutional ideas began demanding limits on the shah's absolute power, a house of justice, and an end to arbitrary government.

Course of events

Protests escalated in 1905 after officials bastinadoed merchants in Tehran accused of raising prices. Protesters took sanctuary (bast) at shrines and, in the summer of 1906, some 14,000 people encamped in the grounds of the British legation. Mozaffar ad-Din Shah capitulated on 5 August 1906, signing a decree for a National Consultative Assembly (Majles). The first Majles opened on 6 October 1906, and the shah signed the constitution on 30 December 1906, days before his death.

His successor, Mohammad Ali Shah, opposed constitutional limits and, with the help of the Russian-officered Cossack Brigade under Colonel Vladimir Liakhov, bombarded the Majles in June 1908 and suspended the constitution. Constitutionalists resisted: Sattar Khan and Bagher Khan defended Tabriz, while forces from Rasht and Isfahan marched on Tehran, deposing Mohammad Ali Shah in July 1909 and restoring the constitution. The second Majles convened, but instability, foreign intervention under the 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention, and tribal and fiscal crises undermined the movement; Russian pressure forced the Majles' dissolution in December 1911, closing the revolutionary period, though the constitution, repeatedly amended, formally survived until 1979.

Legacy

The revolution introduced enduring concepts into Iranian political life — the rule of law, representative government, a free press, and anjumans (popular societies) — and remains a touchstone for later Iranian democratic movements.
