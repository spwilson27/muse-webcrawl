Spindle checkpoint

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Kinetochore attachment monitored by the spindle checkpoint

The spindle checkpoint, also known as the spindle assembly checkpoint, is a cell-cycle control mechanism that prevents separation of duplicated chromosomes (anaphase) until each chromosome is properly attached to the mitotic spindle. It thereby ensures that daughter cells each receive a complete set of chromosomes.

Mechanism

Unattached kinetochores catalyse formation of the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC), composed of proteins including Mad2, BubR1, Bub3 and Cdc20. The MCC inhibits the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), preventing it from targeting securin and cyclin B for degradation. When all kinetochores achieve amphitelic (bipolar) attachment, checkpoint signalling ceases, the APC/C is activated, sister chromatids separate, and the cell exits mitosis.

Significance

Defects in the spindle checkpoint cause chromosome mis-segregation and aneuploidy, which are associated with cancer and developmental disorders. Checkpoint proteins such as Mad1, Mad2, Bub1 and BubR1 were first identified in yeast screens for mutants insensitive to spindle poisons such as benomyl.
