Great Glen Fault

TypeStrike-slip fault (sinistral, with later dextral reactivation)
LengthAbout 100 km onshore (over 300 km including offshore continuations)
TrendNE–SW across the Scottish Highlands
FormedCaledonian orogeny, Silurian–Devonian
FeaturesLoch Ness, Loch Lochy, Loch Linnhe; Caledonian Canal

The Great Glen Fault is a major geological fault that runs the length of the Great Glen (Glen Albyn) in the Scottish Highlands, from Loch Linnhe in the south-west to the Moray Firth near Inverness in the north-east. The glen's string of lochs — Loch Lochy, Loch Oich and the famous Loch Ness — occupy the fault's shattered, easily eroded zone, and the Caledonian Canal (opened 1822) exploits the same line across Scotland.

Geological history

The fault originated during the Caledonian orogeny, the mountain-building episode caused by the closure of the Iapetus Ocean and collision of Laurentia with Baltica and Avalonia in the Silurian and Devonian (roughly 430–390 million years ago). In its main phase it moved as a left-lateral (sinistral) strike-slip fault, displacing the Northern Highlands terrane relative to the Grampian terrane by perhaps 100 km or more; debate continues over the exact displacement, with estimates ranging from tens to several hundred kilometres. The fault was reactivated several times, including right-lateral (dextral) movement in the Carboniferous and minor movements in the Mesozoic and Cenozoic as the North Atlantic opened.

Seismicity

The fault remains weakly active. Small earthquakes occur along its length, notably near Inverness, which experienced a significant tremor in 1816, and around Comrie at the related Highland Boundary Fault. The Great Glen Fault continues offshore to the north-east as the Walls Boundary Fault in Shetland and has been linked by some geologists with the Sorgenfrei–Tornquist Zone across the North Sea, and to the south-west with faults in Ireland.
