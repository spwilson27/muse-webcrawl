An appendectomy (American English) or appendicectomy (British English) is a surgical operation in which the vermiform appendix is removed. Appendectomy is normally performed as an urgent or emergency procedure to treat complicated acute appendicitis. It may be performed either with an open incision (laparotomy) or through several small incisions with cameras and surgical instruments (laparoscopic surgery).

Laparoscopic removal of the appendix

History

The first successful appendectomy dates to 1735, when French surgeon Claudius Amyand performed the operation at St George's Hospital in London — remarkably, the patient was an 11-year-old boy whose appendix, perforated by a pin, Amyand removed. In 1886, American surgeon Reginald Heber Fitz published the landmark paper establishing appendicitis as a distinct diagnosis and advocating early removal. Charles McBurney described his eponymous point and gridiron incision in 1889–1894. Kurt Semm performed the first laparoscopic appendectomy in 1981.

Procedure

The patient is placed under general anesthesia. In open surgery, an incision is made in the lower right abdomen (often at McBurney's point); the appendix is located, its blood supply (the appendicular artery in the mesoappendix) is ligated, and the appendix is divided at its base and removed. In laparoscopic surgery, three small ports are used and the abdomen is inspected with a camera; this approach generally allows faster recovery and is preferred when the diagnosis is uncertain. Antibiotics are given before and after surgery.

Recovery and risks

Most patients leave the hospital within one to three days and return to normal activities within a few weeks. Risks include wound infection, intra-abdominal abscess, bleeding, and adhesions. Removing the appendix early, before perforation, greatly reduces mortality; a ruptured appendix can cause peritonitis, which remains dangerous.

Alternatives

Recent trials have shown that many cases of uncomplicated appendicitis can be managed with antibiotics alone, though a substantial proportion of patients eventually require appendectomy within a few years.
