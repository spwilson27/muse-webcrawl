Aztec writing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aztec writing is the pictorial and logographic writing system used by the Mexica and other Nahua peoples of central Mexico before and shortly after the Spanish conquest. It combined pictograms, logograms and, increasingly in the contact period, phonetic elements used to spell out Nahuatl words, especially names of persons and places.

TypePictographic / logographic
LanguageClassical Nahuatl
Periodc. 15th–16th centuries (and post-conquest use)

Characteristics

Aztec writing was not a full phonetic script capable of recording any sentence; rather, it functioned as a sophisticated mnemonic and notation system read aloud by trained scribes (tlacuiloque). Glyphs represented objects, concepts, calendar signs and sounds. Place-name glyphs often used rebus-like combinations — for example, combining pictures whose Nahuatl names produced the sounds of the toponym.

Codices

Scribes painted books (codices) on deerhide or bark paper (amatl) in folding-screen format, recording tribute lists, genealogies, calendars, maps and histories. Most pre-conquest books were destroyed during the conquest; surviving examples include the Codex Borbonicus and the Codex Borgia group, while famous post-conquest manuscripts such as the Codex Mendoza (c. 1541) blend traditional glyphs with Spanish glosses commissioned for the colonial authorities.

Decipherment

Modern scholars, notably Alfonso Lacadena and Marc Zender, have demonstrated that Nahua writing contained more systematic phoneticism than once believed, particularly in place and personal names, where glyphs could spell syllables. The script continued in use in some communities into the 17th century before being displaced by the Latin alphabet.
