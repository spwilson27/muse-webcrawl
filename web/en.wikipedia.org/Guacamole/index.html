Guacamole

Guacamole (Spanish: [wakaˈmole]) is an avocado-based dip, spread, or salad first developed in Mexico. In addition to its use in modern Mexican cuisine, it has become part of international cuisine and American cuisine as a dip, condiment and salad ingredient.

Etymology and history

The name comes from Classical Nahuatl āhuacamōlli [aːwakaˈmoːlːi], literally "avocado sauce", from āhuacatl "avocado" + mōlli "sauce". Avocados were first cultivated in South Central Mexico some 10,000 years ago, and the Aztecs made an avocado sauce by at least the 16th century. A Spanish description from the early colonial period described a preparation made by grinding avocados with salt.

Ingredients

The traditional base is ripe avocados mashed with sea salt. Common additions include lime or lemon juice (which also slows browning), chopped onion, cilantro, tomato, garlic, and jalapeño or other chiles. Recipes vary regionally; some include cumin, peas, or pomegranate seeds. The dish is often prepared in a molcajete, a volcanic stone mortar.

Commercial success

Guacamole consumption in the United States has grown enormously, closely tied to Super Bowl Sunday, when tens of millions of pounds of avocados are consumed. The Hass avocado, the most common variety used, is prized for its creamy texture and high oil content. Browning caused by oxidation is a perennial problem for commercial producers, who use high-pressure processing to extend shelf life.
