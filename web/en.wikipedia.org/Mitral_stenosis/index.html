Mitral stenosis

Mitral stenosis is a valvular heart disease characterized by narrowing of the mitral valve opening, which obstructs blood flow from the left atrium to the left ventricle during diastole. The normal mitral valve area is about 4–6 cm²; symptoms usually develop as it falls below about 2 cm².

Causes

By far the most common cause worldwide is rheumatic heart disease, in which repeated inflammation fuses the valve commissures and thickens and calcifies the leaflets, producing a characteristic "fish-mouth" or "button-hole" valve. Rarer causes include severe mitral annular calcification in the elderly, congenital abnormalities, and inflammatory or infiltrative diseases.

Signs and symptoms

As left atrial pressure rises, patients develop progressive breathlessness on exertion, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, and pink frothy or blood-streaked sputum. Long-standing disease can cause pulmonary hypertension, right-sided heart failure, and atrial fibrillation with risk of systemic embolism. Classic examination findings include a loud first heart sound, an opening snap, and a low-pitched rumbling mid-diastolic murmur at the apex, along with malar flush in advanced cases.

Diagnosis and treatment

Echocardiography establishes the valve area, pressure gradient, and suitability for intervention. Treatment includes diuretics, rate control, and anticoagulation when atrial fibrillation or other indications are present. Definitive treatment of severe symptomatic mitral stenosis is usually percutaneous mitral balloon commissurotomy (valvuloplasty) in suitable anatomy, or surgical valve repair or replacement when it is not.
