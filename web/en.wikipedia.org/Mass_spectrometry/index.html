Mass spectrometry

Mass spectrometry (MS) is an analytical technique that measures the mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) of ions. A mass spectrometer ionises chemical species, sorts the resulting ions by their mass-to-charge ratio in electric or magnetic fields, and detects them to produce a mass spectrum — a record of ion abundance against m/z. The results identify unknown compounds, determine isotopic composition, quantify known substances, and reveal chemical structure. Mass spectrometry is used throughout chemistry, biology, medicine, forensics, environmental science, geology and planetary exploration.

Principle and components

A mass spectrometer has three essential parts:

  • An ion source, which converts sample molecules into gas-phase ions. Methods include electron ionisation (EI), chemical ionisation, electrospray ionisation (ESI) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation (MALDI) — the soft ionisation methods that, from the 1980s, opened large biomolecules to MS.
  • A mass analyser, which separates ions by m/z. Common analysers include magnetic sectors, quadrupoles, ion traps, time-of-flight (TOF) tubes, Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance cells and the Orbitrap.
  • A detector, such as an electron multiplier or Faraday cup, which counts arriving ions.

The whole analyser path is kept under high vacuum. Samples are often separated first by gas or liquid chromatography (GC-MS, LC-MS). In tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS), a selected ion is fragmented and its products analysed, giving structural information crucial to proteomics and drug metabolism studies.

History

The technique grew out of J. J. Thomson's parabola spectrograph experiments with cathode rays in the early 1900s and Francis Aston's mass spectrograph (1919), which demonstrated the existence of isotopes and earned Aston the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Arthur Dempster developed electron ionisation and the spark source. Mass spectrometers separated uranium isotopes in the Manhattan Project's calutrons, and post-war instruments became routine in petroleum and organic chemistry. The development of ESI by John Fenn and MALDI by Koichi Tanaka was recognised by the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Applications

Mass spectrometry identifies proteins and metabolites, sequences peptides, measures drug concentrations in blood, dates geological samples through isotope ratios, detects explosives and chemical agents, authenticates food, and studies extraterrestrial matter — spectrometers have flown on probes to Mars and Titan.
