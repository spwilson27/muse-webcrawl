Interbreeding between archaic and modern humans


Archaic ancestry in modern populations is concentrated outside Africa for Neanderthal DNA.

When anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) dispersed out of Africa, they encountered and interbred with archaic human populations, including Neanderthals and Denisovans. Analysis of ancient and modern genomes since 2010 has shown that this admixture left a measurable legacy: most non-African genomes carry roughly 1–4 per cent Neanderthal ancestry, and some Oceanian and Asian populations carry additional Denisovan ancestry — up to about 5 per cent in Melanesians. Evidence also suggests gene flow from an unidentified "ghost" archaic population within Africa.

Neanderthal admixture

The draft Neanderthal genome published in 2010 by Svante Pääbo's group at the Max Planck Institute demonstrated that non-Africans share more derived alleles with Neanderthals than Africans do, implying interbreeding, probably in the Middle East some 50,000–60,000 years ago after the main out-of-Africa migration. Later studies identified further pulses of gene flow, including early gene flow from modern humans into Neanderthals. Natural selection purged much Neanderthal DNA from functionally important regions, but retained segments include genes affecting immunity, skin and hair, and high-altitude adaptation via Denisovan EPAS1 in Tibetans.

Denisovan and other admixture

The Denisovans, identified from a finger bone in Siberia's Denisova Cave, contributed ancestry chiefly to Papuan, Aboriginal Australian and other Oceanian genomes, with smaller amounts across East and South Asia, indicating more than one admixture event. Genomic evidence also points to interbreeding among archaic groups themselves: the Denisova 11 fossil ("Denny") was a first-generation hybrid of a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father.
