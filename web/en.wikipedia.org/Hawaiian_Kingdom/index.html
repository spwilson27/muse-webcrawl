Hawaiian Kingdom

The Hawaiian Kingdom, also known as the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi (Hawaiian: Ke Aupuni Hawaiʻi), was a sovereign state located in the Hawaiian Islands. The country was formed in 1795, when the warrior chief Kamehameha the Great of the island of Hawaiʻi conquered the islands of Oʻahu, Maui, Molokaʻi, and Lānaʻi, unifying them under one government. In 1810, the whole Hawaiian archipelago became unified when Kauaʻi and Niʻihau joined the kingdom voluntarily.

History

The kingdom was ruled by the House of Kamehameha until 1872, and then by the House of Kalākaua until 1893. During the nineteenth century Hawaiʻi achieved broad international recognition, concluding treaties with the United States, Britain, France, and other powers; Britain and France formally recognised its independence in 1843. The kingdom adopted constitutions in 1840, 1852, 1864, and 1887 — the last, the so-called "Bayonet Constitution", having been forced on King Kalākaua and sharply reducing royal authority.

The economy shifted from sandalwood exports to whaling provisioning and then to sugar plantations, which brought large-scale immigration of labourers from China, Japan, Portugal, and elsewhere. The Reciprocity Treaty of 1875 and its renewal, granting the United States rights at Pearl Harbor, deepened American influence.

Overthrow and annexation

On January 17, 1893, Queen Liliʻuokalani was overthrown by a committee of American and European businessmen backed by the United States minister and the landing of U.S. troops. A Republic of Hawaii followed in 1894, and the islands were annexed by the United States in 1898 during the Spanish–American War. In 1993 the U.S. Congress passed the Apology Resolution acknowledging the illegality of the overthrow. The Hawaiian sovereignty movement continues to debate the kingdom's legal status.
