Indigenous peoples of California

The Indigenous peoples of California are the diverse Native American groups who have lived in the region now called California for at least 12,000 years. Before European contact, California was one of the most culturally and linguistically diverse regions in North America, home to an estimated 300,000 people speaking as many as 100 distinct languages. Unlike many other regions, most California groups were not farmers; they developed sophisticated management of wild resources, particularly acorns, salmon, and game, sustained by practices such as controlled burning.

Major groups

Notable peoples include the Chumash of the Santa Barbara coast, famed for plank canoes (tomols); the Pomo of the north coast, known for fine basketry; the Miwok around San Francisco Bay and the Sierra foothills; the Yokuts of the San Joaquin Valley; the Maidu and Wintun of the Sacramento Valley; the Hupa, Karuk, and Yurok of the Klamath River region; the Kumeyaay of the San Diego area; and the Cahuilla and Mojave of the southern deserts. These groups are part of the broader Indigenous peoples of the Americas.

Spanish, Mexican, and American periods

Spanish colonization beginning in 1769 established 21 missions along the coast, into which thousands of Native people were coerced; disease and harsh conditions caused catastrophic mortality. Under Mexican rule the missions were secularized in the 1830s. The California Gold Rush after 1848 brought state-sanctioned violence and massacres — the California genocide — that reduced the Native population from about 150,000 in 1848 to roughly 30,000 by 1870. Eighteen treaties negotiated in 1851–52 were never ratified, leaving most California tribes landless.

Present day

California today has more residents of Native American heritage than any other state and over 100 federally recognized tribes, along with many unrecognized communities seeking acknowledgement. Tribal governments operate enterprises including casinos authorized under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, and movements for land return, cultural revival, and language revitalization are active across the state. In 2019 the governor of California formally apologized for the state's historic violence against Native peoples.
