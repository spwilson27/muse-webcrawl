Law of noncontradiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bust of Aristotle]

In logic, the law of noncontradiction (also law of contradiction, or principle of non-contradiction) states that contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time: formally, ¬(p ∧ ¬p) — "not both p and not-p". It is one of the three classic laws of thought, together with the law of identity and the law of excluded middle.

Aristotle's formulation

The law was first systematically stated by Aristotle in Book Gamma of the Metaphysics: "the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject in the same respect". Aristotle called it the most certain of all principles and argued that it cannot be directly demonstrated — since all demonstration rests on it — but can be defended elenctically, by showing that anyone who says anything meaningful implicitly relies on it. He distinguished ontological, logical, and psychological versions of the principle.

Consequences

In classical logic, a contradiction entails everything (the principle of explosion, ex falso quodlibet), which is why consistency is essential to classical reasoning. Symbolic logic expresses the law as a tautology, and it is presupposed in proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum).

Challenges

Some philosophers and logicians have questioned the law. Dialetheists such as Graham Priest argue that certain contradictions — notably the liar paradox — may be true, and paraconsistent logics are designed to reason without explosion even in the presence of contradictions. Heraclitus and Hegel have sometimes been read as qualifying the principle, and some Eastern philosophical traditions have been interpreted as transcending it, though such readings are contested.
