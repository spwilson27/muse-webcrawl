Islamic economics

Islamic economics
BasisQuran, Sunnah, and Sharia jurisprudence (fiqh)
Key prohibitionsRiba (interest/usury); gharar (excessive uncertainty); maysir (gambling)

Islamic economics refers to the knowledge of economics — or economic activities and processes — in terms of Islamic principles and teachings. Islam has a set of special moral norms and values about individual and social economic behaviour. Proponents seek an economic system grounded in the Quran and the Sunnah, centred on justice, equitable distribution of wealth, and the prohibition of exploitation, in contrast to both unfettered capitalism and state socialism.

Core principles

Prohibition of riba

The best-known rule is the ban on riba, generally interpreted as interest on loans. Instead of lending at interest, Islamic finance favours risk-sharing contracts: mudarabah (profit-sharing partnerships), musharakah (joint ventures), and asset-backed sales such as murabaha (cost-plus financing) and ijara (leasing).

Gharar and maysir

Transactions involving excessive uncertainty (gharar) or speculation akin to gambling (maysir) are prohibited, which restricts conventional insurance and many derivatives; cooperative insurance (takaful) developed as an alternative.

Zakat and waqf

Zakat, the obligatory alms levy of 2.5 percent on certain forms of wealth, institutionalises redistribution to the poor, while the waqf (charitable endowment) historically funded schools, hospitals, and public works across the Muslim world.

History

Classical Muslim jurists and thinkers — including Abu Yusuf, al-Ghazali, and Ibn Khaldun — wrote on taxation, prices, sound money, and the division of labour, while medieval Islamic markets developed instruments such as the sakk (origin of the word "cheque") and hawala transfer networks. The modern discipline, however, dates to the mid-20th century, as writers in British India and the Arab world — notably Abul A'la Maududi and later Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr — formulated "Islamic economics" as a distinct system amid decolonisation. The first modern Islamic banks appeared in Egypt and the Gulf in the 1960s–70s (Mit Ghamr Savings Bank, 1963; Dubai Islamic Bank, 1975), boosted by oil revenues.

Islamic finance today

Islamic financial assets grew to several trillion US dollars worldwide by the 2020s, concentrated in Malaysia, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iran, with sukuk (Islamic bonds) issued by governments and corporations. Standard-setting bodies such as AAOIFI and the IFSB harmonise practice, though scholars debate whether many products merely replicate interest-based finance in form while differing little in substance.

Criticism

Critics range from economists who doubt there is a coherent, uniquely "Islamic" economics distinct from welfare-oriented capitalism, to devout scholars who charge the industry with legal formalism. Defenders answer that the system's ethical constraints and asset-backing impart stability, noting Islamic banks' relative resilience during the 2008 financial crisis.
