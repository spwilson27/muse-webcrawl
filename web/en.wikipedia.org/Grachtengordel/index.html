The Grachtengordel (Dutch for "canal belt") is the concentric ring of canals in the centre of Amsterdam, Netherlands. Dug during the Dutch Golden Age in the 17th century, the canal ring is one of the most famous and best-preserved examples of early modern city planning, and its inner core was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2010.

Layout

The belt consists of four main canals running in concentric semicircles around the old medieval centre: the Singel, Herengracht ("Gentlemen's Canal"), Keizersgracht ("Emperor's Canal"), and Prinsengracht ("Prince's Canal"). They are connected by radial canals and streets and end at both sides in the Amstel river and the IJ bay. The total length of the canals in the ring is about 6.3 kilometres, crossed by numerous bridges.

History

Amsterdam's expansion plans were drawn up in the early 1600s as the city grew explosively rich from trade. Construction of the western canal ring began in 1613; the section south of the Leidsegracht followed later in the century. Plots along the Herengracht's "Golden Bend" were reserved for the city's wealthiest merchants, whose tall, narrow gabled houses — constrained by taxes on canal-front width — became the defining image of Amsterdam.

Heritage

The canal houses now serve as homes, offices, hotels, and museums, including the Anne Frank House on the Prinsengracht. The Grachtengordel remains the social and cultural heart of Amsterdam and a model of hydraulic and urban planning studied worldwide.
