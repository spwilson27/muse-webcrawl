Pope Paul VI (Latin: Paulus VI; born Giovanni Battista Montini; 26 September 1897 – 6 August 1978) was head of the Catholic Church and sovereign of Vatican City from 21 June 1963 until his death. A career Vatican diplomat who served in the Secretariat of State and as Archbishop of Milan, he was elected to succeed John XXIII and is best known for bringing the Second Vatican Council to its conclusion and overseeing the first phase of its implementation.

Vatican II and reform

Paul VI reconvened the Council in 1963 and steered it to completion in 1965, promulgating its constitutions, decrees and declarations, including the liturgy constitution that led to the reformed Mass of Paul VI (the Novus Ordo) promulgated in 1969. His implementation of the Council also included reform of the Roman Curia, revision of canon law (completed after his death), and the creation of the Synod of Bishops. These changes provoked both progressive dissatisfaction and traditionalist opposition — Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre's movement being the most consequential.

Teaching and diplomacy

His encyclicals include Ecclesiam Suam (1964) on dialogue, Populorum Progressio (1967), which called development "the new name for peace" and criticised unbridled capitalism, and Humanae Vitae (1968), which reaffirmed the Church's ban on artificial contraception and was widely dissented from in the West. Paul VI was the first modern pope to travel extensively: he visited the Holy Land (1964), India, the United Nations in New York (1965, pleading "No more war, war never again"), Colombia, Uganda, Asia and Australia. His meeting with Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras in Jerusalem, and their joint lifting of the mutual excommunications of 1054 in 1965, marked a turning point in Catholic–Orthodox relations. Paul VI was canonised by Pope Francis on 14 October 2018.
