Dispersal

Dispersal may refer to several concepts in biology, geography and other fields. In biology, dispersal is the movement of individuals or propagules (such as seeds, spores or larvae) away from their parent or population of origin, with consequences for gene flow, population dynamics and the geographic distribution of species.

Biological dispersal

Organisms disperse both actively, by their own locomotion, and passively, carried by wind, water or other animals. Plants rely heavily on seed dispersal mechanisms: wind (anemochory), water (hydrochory), animals (zoochory) and ballistic ejection. Dispersal reduces competition and inbreeding, allows colonization of new habitats, and helps populations escape predators and pathogens. Barriers to dispersal shape biogeographic patterns and can lead to speciation in isolated populations. Humans have greatly altered natural dispersal, both by transporting species around the world — sometimes with invasive consequences — and by fragmenting habitats.

Other uses

The term is also used for the scattering of light, the breakdown of a crowd or fleet, the distribution of assets, and in demography for population dispersal away from urban centers.
