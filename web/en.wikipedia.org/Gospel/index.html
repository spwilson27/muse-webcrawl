Gospel

For other uses, see Gospel (disambiguation). For the genre of accounts of Jesus's life, see Canonical gospels.


A medieval illuminated gospel manuscript

Gospel originally meant the Christian message ("the gospel"), but in the 2nd century it came to be used also for the books in which the message was reported. In this sense a gospel can be defined as a loose-knit, episodic narrative of the words and deeds of Jesus, culminating in his trial and death and concluding with various reports of his post-resurrection appearances.

The word derives from the Old English gōdspel ("good news" or "glad tidings"), a translation of the Greek euangelion. In the New Testament the term is used first for the proclaimed message of salvation; the apostle Paul uses it dozens of times for his preaching of Christ's death and resurrection.

Canonical gospels

The four canonical gospels—Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John—form the opening of the New Testament. Matthew, Mark, and Luke are known as the Synoptic Gospels because they share much content and structure, giving rise to the Synoptic problem concerning their literary relationship. Most scholars date Mark to around 70 CE and regard it as the earliest of the four, with Matthew and Luke drawing on Mark and a hypothetical sayings source known as Q. John, markedly different in style and theology, is usually dated to the end of the first century.

Non-canonical gospels

Many other gospels were written in antiquity, including the Gospel of Thomas (a collection of sayings), the Gospel of Peter, the Gospel of Mary, and various infancy gospels narrating Jesus's childhood. These texts were not accepted into the canon recognised by the mainstream church, though several are valued by historians for the light they shed on early Christian diversity.

Other uses

By extension, "gospel" denotes any authoritative account regarded as unquestionably true, and it gives its name to gospel music, the African-American religious music tradition that emerged from spirituals, hymns, and blues in the early twentieth century. The expression "gospel truth" preserves the sense of the absolutely reliable.
