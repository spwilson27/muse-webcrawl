The Call of Cthulhu


The February 1928 issue of Weird Tales, in which the story appeared.

"The Call of Cthulhu" is a short story by the American writer H. P. Lovecraft. Written in the summer of 1926, it was published in the pulp magazine Weird Tales in February 1928. It is the only story by Lovecraft in which the extraterrestrial entity Cthulhu himself makes a formal appearance, and it became the central narrative of the shared fictional universe later called the Cthulhu Mythos.

Plot

The narrator, Francis Wayland Thurston, pieces together the story from the notes of his deceased grand-uncle, a Brown University professor. Three interlocking investigations — a sculptor's nightmares of a monstrous clay bas-relief, a New Orleans police raid on a bayou cult, and a Norwegian sailor's account of his ship's encounter with an island that rose from the Pacific — converge on the conclusion that the Great Old One Cthulhu lies dreaming in the sunken city of R'lyeh, worshipped by cults around the world, awaiting the time when the stars are right and he will rise again. The story's most famous line is the cult's chant: "Ph'nglui mglw'nafh Cthulhu R'lyeh wgah'nagl fhtagn" — "In his house at R'lyeh, dead Cthulhu waits dreaming."

Themes

The story is a founding text of Lovecraft's philosophy of cosmicism: the idea that humanity is insignificant in a vast, indifferent universe, and that forbidden knowledge of humanity's true place in the cosmos drives investigators mad. Its famous opening declares that "the most merciful thing in the world... is the inability of the human mind to correlate all its contents."

Legacy

"The Call of Cthulhu" has inspired numerous adaptations, including the 1981 role-playing game Call of Cthulhu and a 2005 silent-film adaptation by the H. P. Lovecraft Historical Society made in the style of a 1920s movie.
