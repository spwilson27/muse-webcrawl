Geothermal area

A geothermal area is a region where heat from the Earth's interior reaches the surface at an unusually high rate, producing features such as hot springs, geysers, fumaroles, mud pots and steaming ground. Geothermal areas occur mainly along tectonic plate boundaries and in volcanic regions where magma lies close to the surface and groundwater can circulate through hot rock.

Surface features

The character of a geothermal area depends on the temperature of the system and the availability of water. Hot springs form where heated water rises and pools; where water is heated under pressure and erupts intermittently, a geyser results. Fumaroles vent steam and volcanic gases such as hydrogen sulfide, which may oxidise to deposit bright yellow sulfur. Acidic gases dissolving in shallow water produce bubbling mud pots, and hydrothermal alteration bleaches and weakens the surrounding rock, giving many geothermal fields their vivid colours. Terraces of silica sinter or travertine may be deposited by mineral-rich waters.

Notable geothermal areas

The world's largest concentration of geysers is in Yellowstone National Park in the United States. Other major areas include the volcanic zones of Iceland, the Taupō Volcanic Zone in New Zealand (notably Rotorua and Wairakei), the Phlegraean Fields near Naples, the Valley of Geysers in Kamchatka, and the geothermal fields of El Tatio in Chile and Dallol in Ethiopia.

Uses

Geothermal areas have long been used for bathing and cooking — in Iceland, Japan (onsen), New Zealand and elsewhere — and for space heating. High-temperature fields support geothermal power generation, while lower-temperature resources are used directly for greenhouse heating, aquaculture and district heating. Many geothermal areas are protected as national parks or reserves both for their scientific value and because their fragile mineral terraces and microbial ecosystems are easily damaged.

Ecology

Hot springs host distinctive communities of heat-loving microorganisms, including cyanobacteria and archaea that form colourful microbial mats. Enzymes from organisms such as Thermus aquaticus, isolated from Yellowstone's springs, became essential tools in molecular biology, most famously Taq polymerase used in the polymerase chain reaction.
