The United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA or US FDA) is a federal agency of the Department of Health and Human Services. The FDA is responsible for protecting and promoting public health through the control and supervision of food safety, tobacco products, caffeine products, dietary supplements, prescription and over-the-counter pharmaceutical drugs (medications), vaccines, biopharmaceuticals, blood transfusions, medical devices, electromagnetic radiation emitting devices (ERED), cosmetics, animal foods and feed, and veterinary products.

History

The modern FDA traces its origins to the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906, championed by chief chemist Harvey Washington Wiley, which prohibited adulterated and misbranded food and drugs in interstate commerce. The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, passed after the sulfanilamide elixir disaster killed more than 100 people, greatly expanded the agency's authority. The Kefauver–Harris Amendments of 1962, prompted by the thalidomide tragedy, required proof of effectiveness as well as safety before marketing.

Scope of regulation

The FDA regulates products accounting for roughly a fifth of consumer spending in the United States. Its responsibilities include approving new drugs and biologics, inspecting manufacturing facilities, setting labelling requirements, overseeing food additives, and coordinating recalls of unsafe products. The agency's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN) oversees most food matters, while the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) handles pharmaceuticals.

Organisation

The agency is led by the Commissioner of Food and Drugs, appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate. Its headquarters campus is at White Oak in Silver Spring, Maryland. Field offices and laboratories across the country conduct inspections and product testing.

Criticism

The FDA has faced criticism both for excessive slowness in approving beneficial drugs and for approving products later found unsafe, such as the painkiller Vioxx (withdrawn in 2004). Its oversight of dietary supplements, which under the 1994 DSHEA statute requires no premarket approval, is frequently debated.
