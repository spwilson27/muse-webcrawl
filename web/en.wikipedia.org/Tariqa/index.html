Tariqa

A tariqa is a Sufi order organized around a master and a chain of transmission.

A tariqa (Arabic: طريقة, romanized ṭarīqa, plural ṭuruq) is an Islamic Sufi order or school of mystical practice. The term originally meant a "path" or "way" — the spiritual path toward God — and came to denote the organized brotherhoods that formed around Sufi masters (shaykhs or pirs) beginning in the 12th and 13th centuries.

Organization

A tariqa is headed by a shaykh, who guides disciples (murids) through stages of spiritual discipline. The authority of the shaykh derives from a chain of transmission (silsila) tracing back through earlier masters to the Prophet Muhammad. Members typically take an oath of allegiance (bay'a) and receive instruction in the order's litanies and practices of remembrance (dhikr).

Major orders

Prominent tariqas include the Qadiriyya, founded in the tradition of Abdul Qadir Gilani; the Naqshbandi order, known for its silent dhikr; the Shadhiliyya; the Chishti order of South Asia; the Mevlevi order associated with Rumi; and the Tijaniyya of West Africa. Orders spread along trade and pilgrimage routes and played major roles in the Islamization of regions of Africa and Asia.

Practices

Practices vary by order but commonly include collective and individual dhikr, recitation of litanies (wird), retreat (khalwa), visitation of saints' tombs (ziyara), and in some orders music and movement, such as the whirling of the Mevlevis or the devotional singing of qawwali in the Chishti tradition.
