Choline is an essential nutrient for humans and many other animals. It is a water-soluble cation that forms various salts, such as choline chloride and choline bitartrate. Choline is a component of the phospholipids phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin, which are abundant in cell membranes, and it is the precursor of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine. Humans synthesize small amounts of choline in the liver, but not enough to meet requirements, so it must also be obtained from the diet.

Metabolism

Choline is absorbed in the small intestine and transported to tissues, where it is phosphorylated to phosphocholine, oxidized to betaine, or acetylated to acetylcholine. As betaine, choline participates in one-carbon metabolism as a methyl donor, supporting the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine and thereby influencing DNA methylation and epigenetic regulation.

Dietary sources and requirements

Rich sources of choline include eggs, liver, meat, fish, dairy products, soybeans, and cruciferous vegetables. Health authorities set adequate intake levels at roughly 425 mg per day for adult women and 550 mg per day for adult men, with higher needs in pregnancy and lactation. Choline was recognized as an essential nutrient by the Institute of Medicine in 1998.

Deficiency and excess

Choline deficiency can cause fatty liver (hepatic steatosis), liver damage, and muscle damage, because phosphatidylcholine is required to export triglycerides from the liver in very-low-density lipoproteins. Deficiency during pregnancy is associated with impaired neural tube closure and altered brain development. Very high intakes of choline can cause a fishy body odor, sweating, salivation, and low blood pressure, effects related to its conversion to trimethylamine by gut bacteria. The tolerable upper intake level for adults is 3.5 grams per day.

History

Choline was discovered by Adolph Strecker in 1862 in pig and ox bile (from the Greek cholē, bile) and was chemically synthesized in 1866. Its nutritional essentiality was demonstrated in the 20th century through animal feeding experiments and human parenteral nutrition studies.
