User-generated content

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

User-generated content includes text, images, audio and video published by the users of a platform rather than by its owner.

User-generated content (UGC), alternatively known as user-created content (UCC), is any form of content, such as text, posts, images, videos, reviews and audio, published by users on online platforms such as social media, blogs, wikis and video-sharing sites. UGC is in contrast to traditional content published by professional writers, producers and media organizations.

The term emerged in the mid-2000s with the rise of "Web 2.0" platforms — YouTube, Wikipedia, Flickr, Facebook and later Instagram and TikTok — that made publishing tools available to anyone with an internet connection. Time magazine named "You" its Person of the Year for 2006 in recognition of the phenomenon.

Characteristics

The OECD's influential 2007 definition identified three requirements for UGC:

  • Publication: the content is published on a publicly accessible website or a page accessible to a selected group;
  • Creative effort: a certain amount of creative effort is put into creating the work or adapting existing works;
  • Outside professional routines: it is created outside of professional routines and practices, typically without an institutional or commercial market in mind.

In practice the last criterion has blurred, as professional creators, influencers and brands now produce much of the content on UGC platforms, and successful amateurs monetize their work.

Uses

Entertainment and social media

Most UGC serves entertainment, self-expression and social connection: short videos, memes, livestreams, fan fiction and game modifications ("mods").

Marketing

Brands solicit UGC — reviews, unboxing videos, hashtag campaigns — because consumers generally trust peer content more than advertising. Legal questions arise around ownership, licensing and disclosure of paid promotion.

News and civic life

Bystander photos and video have become major sources for news reporting of disasters, protests and conflicts, raising verification challenges for journalists. Governments and campaigns also monitor and mobilize user content.

Legal and policy issues

  • Copyright: uploaders frequently post material they do not own; platforms in the United States rely on the DMCA safe harbour, conditional on removing infringing content on notice, while the EU's 2019 Copyright Directive imposed additional filtering obligations.
  • Moderation: platforms must police enormous volumes of UGC for illegality, harassment and misinformation, work that is partly automated and partly done by human moderators.
  • Ownership and terms: posting usually grants the platform a broad licence to the content while the creator retains copyright.

Criticism

Critics argue that UGC platforms extract value from unpaid labour, that recommendation systems amplify sensational or harmful material, and that the flood of amateur content devalues professional creative work. Defenders answer that UGC democratizes media production and has enabled new art forms, communities and careers.
