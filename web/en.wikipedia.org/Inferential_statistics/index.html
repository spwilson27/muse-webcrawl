Inferential statistics

Statistical inference is the process of using data analysis to infer properties of an underlying probability distribution. Inferential statistics contrasts with descriptive statistics, which merely summarizes the data observed. Inference makes propositions about a population using data drawn from the population with some form of sampling.

Inferential statistics generalizes from a sample to the population from which it was drawn.

Main tasks

The principal goals of statistical inference include estimation — giving point estimates and interval estimates (confidence intervals) for unknown population parameters; hypothesis testing — assessing whether the data support a claim about the population, often summarized by a p-value; and prediction of future observations. Related tasks include model selection, regression analysis, and the design of experiments and surveys.

Approaches

The dominant schools are frequentist inference, based on the sampling distribution of statistics under repeated sampling, and Bayesian inference, which combines prior beliefs with the likelihood of the observed data to produce a posterior distribution. Likelihood-based and resampling methods (such as the bootstrap) also play important roles. Each approach relies on assumptions about how the data were generated; violating assumptions such as random sampling can invalidate conclusions.

Sampling distributions

A key idea is that statistics computed from random samples vary from sample to sample according to a sampling distribution. The central limit theorem states that the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size grows, which underlies many classical tests and intervals. The standard error quantifies the expected variability of an estimate.
