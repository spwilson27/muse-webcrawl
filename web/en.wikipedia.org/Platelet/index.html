Platelet

Platelets are small anucleate cell fragments essential for clotting

Produced byMegakaryocytes in bone marrow
LifespanAbout 8–9 days
Normal count150,000–450,000 per microlitre

Platelets, also called thrombocytes, are a component of blood whose function (along with the coagulation factors) is to react to bleeding from blood vessel injury by clumping, thereby initiating a blood clot. Platelets have no cell nucleus; they are fragments of cytoplasm shed from megakaryocytes of the bone marrow and released into the circulation. In a healthy adult, between 150,000 and 450,000 platelets are present in each microlitre of blood, and each circulates for about eight to nine days before being removed, mainly by the spleen.

Function in haemostasis

When a vessel wall is damaged, exposed collagen and von Willebrand factor capture passing platelets, which adhere, change shape, and release granule contents including ADP and thromboxane A2. These signals recruit and activate more platelets, which aggregate through fibrinogen bridges between their GPIIb/IIIa receptors, forming a primary platelet plug. The platelet surface also supports the coagulation cascade, which stabilises the plug with fibrin. Beyond clotting, platelets participate in inflammation, wound healing and immune defence.

Disorders and medicine

Low platelet counts (thrombocytopenia) increase bleeding risk, whereas abnormally high counts (thrombocytosis) or overactive platelets promote thrombosis. Antiplatelet drugs such as aspirin and clopidogrel inhibit platelet activation and are mainstays of treatment for heart attack and stroke prevention. Platelet concentrates are transfused to bleeding patients and to those with marrow failure; donated platelets can be collected by apheresis-style separation. The Italian physician Giulio Bizzozero is credited with identifying platelets as a distinct blood element in 1882.
