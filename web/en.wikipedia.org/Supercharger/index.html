Supercharger

A supercharger is an air compressor that increases the pressure or density of air supplied to an internal combustion engine, giving each intake cycle more oxygen and allowing more fuel to be burned, thus increasing power output. Unlike a turbocharger, which is driven by exhaust gases, a supercharger is mechanically driven, usually by a belt from the engine's crankshaft.

Types

Roots superchargers use meshing lobed rotors to move air from inlet to outlet and sit prominently atop many drag-racing and hot-rod engines. Twin-screw superchargers compress air internally between intermeshing screws and are more efficient. Centrifugal superchargers use an impeller like that of a turbocharger and build boost with engine speed. All mechanical types consume some engine power to drive them, a parasitic loss partly offset by the additional charge density.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because it is directly coupled to the crankshaft, a supercharger responds almost instantly to throttle input, without the "turbo lag" of exhaust-driven systems, and provides strong low-speed torque. Its disadvantages are the mechanical power draw, added weight and noise, and typically lower peak efficiency than a modern turbocharger. Intercoolers are commonly fitted to cool the compressed charge air and reduce knock.

Applications

Superchargers were crucial to high-altitude aircraft engines in the Second World War (often with two-stage, intercooled designs), and remain common in drag racing, muscle cars such as supercharged versions of the Ford Mustang and Dodge Challenger, and some production and marine engines. The term "supercharger" is also used by Tesla for its Supercharger fast-charging network for electric vehicles, an unrelated usage.
