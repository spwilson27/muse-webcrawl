The departments of France (French: départements) are the principal administrative divisions of the French Republic, ranking below the regions and above the arrondissements and communes. There are 101 departments: 96 in metropolitan France and five overseas departments (Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana, Réunion, and Mayotte) that are simultaneously regions.

History

The departments were created by decree of the National Assembly during the French Revolution, on 4 March 1790, replacing the provinces of the Ancien Régime with rational, roughly equal units named mostly after rivers, mountains, and other geographical features — deliberate symbols of national unity. The number and boundaries have changed many times through annexations, losses of territory, and subdivisions; the Paris region was reorganised in 1968, Corsica was split into two departments (2A Corse-du-Sud and 2B Haute-Corse) in 1976, and Mayotte became the 101st department in 2011.

Administration

Each department is governed by an elected departmental council (conseil départemental), which manages social welfare, departmental roads, collèges (lower secondary schools), and local services, and is represented by a prefect appointed by the national government. The departmental capital is the prefecture.

Numbering

Departments are numbered roughly alphabetically from 01 (Ain) to 95 (Val-d'Oise). The numbers appear in postal codes, vehicle registration plates, and INSEE codes, and remain part of everyday French life.

Characteristics

The most populous department is Nord (about 2.6 million inhabitants) and the least populous is Lozère (about 76,000). The largest by area is French Guiana overseas and Gironde in the metropole. Since the regional mergers of 2016, the role of departments has been debated, but proposals to abolish them have not succeeded.
