Glass ceiling

The glass ceiling is a metaphor describing an invisible barrier that prevents certain demographic groups — most commonly women and ethnic minorities — from advancing beyond a particular level in a hierarchy, despite being qualified for higher positions. The term was popularised in the 1980s and is now widely used in discussions of workplace inequality.

Origin and usage

The phrase is often traced to a 1984 remark by magazine editor Gay Bryant, and gained wider currency through a 1986 Wall Street Journal report on barriers facing women in corporate America. In 1991 the United States Congress established the Glass Ceiling Commission to study the barriers to advancement of women and minorities in business.

Evidence and related concepts

Studies of executive representation show women and minorities are consistently under-represented at the highest corporate levels relative to their share of the workforce. Related concepts include the "glass cliff", in which women are more likely to be appointed to leadership roles in times of crisis, and the "sticky floor", describing barriers at the lowest levels of an organisation. Explanations range from structural bias and exclusionary networks to differing career-interruption patterns, and remain a subject of research and debate.
