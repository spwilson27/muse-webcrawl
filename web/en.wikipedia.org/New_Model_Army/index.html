The New Model Army was a standing army formed in 1645 by the Parliamentarians during the English Civil War, and disbanded in 1660 after the Restoration. It was created through the Self-denying Ordinance and the reorganisation of Parliament's regional forces into a single national army, in which promotion was based on merit rather than social standing or aristocratic rank — an unusually modern principle for its time.

Commanded first by Sir Thomas Fairfax, with Oliver Cromwell as his second-in-command and lieutenant general of the horse, the army's soldiers were noted for discipline, regular pay, and a strong streak of religious (Independent and Puritan) conviction; they were known as "Ironsides" and later popularly as "Roundheads".

Formation and the Civil War

The New Model was established by ordinance in February 1645: about 22,000 men — 11 regiments of foot, 11 of horse, and one of dragoons — wearing red coats (the origin of the British Army's famous redcoat). Its first great victory was the decisive Battle of Naseby on 14 June 1645, followed by Langport, which destroyed the Royalist field armies and effectively ended the First Civil War.

Political role

After the war, disputes over arrears of pay and religious toleration politicised the army. In 1647 its Agitators and the Levellers debated the future constitution at the Putney Debates. The army purged Parliament in Pride's Purge (December 1648), tried and executed Charles I, and campaigned to establish the Commonwealth. Cromwell led it to victory over the Scots at Dunbar (1650) and over Charles II at Worcester (1651). The army underpinned the Protectorate until the Restoration of Charles II in 1660, when it was paid off and disbanded — though its Coldstream Guards regiment survived into the modern British Army.

Legacy

The New Model Army set precedents for a professional, centrally funded standing army in England, for meritocratic promotion, and for the army's intervention in politics — a model both emulated and feared in later centuries.
