Landscape with the Fall of Icarus is a painting in oil on canvas long thought to be by the Flemish Renaissance artist Pieter Bruegel the Elder, though it is now generally regarded as a good early copy, executed in the 1560s, of a lost Bruegel original from about 1558. It is held by the Royal Museums of Fine Arts of Belgium in Brussels. The work is famous for its radically de-centred treatment of its mythological subject: the fatal fall of Icarus is reduced to a tiny, almost unnoticed detail in an expansive landscape of everyday labour.

Description

In the foreground a ploughman works his field, a shepherd gazes skyward at nothing in particular, and an angler fishes, while a great merchant ship sails toward a sunlit port city. Only on close inspection, at the lower right near the ship, can one see two bare legs thrashing in the water — Icarus, who flew too close to the sun on wings of feathers and wax, plunging to his death unnoticed by everyone. The composition embodies a Flemish proverb: "No plough stands still because a man dies."

In literature

The painting inspired two celebrated 20th-century poems: W. H. Auden's "Musée des Beaux Arts" (1938), which meditates on how suffering occurs "while someone else is just walking dully along", and William Carlos Williams's "Landscape with the Fall of Icarus" (1960). A second version of the composition, on panel, is in the Van Buuren Museum in Brussels.
