Tepui

A tepui (also spelled tepuy) is a flat-topped mountain or mesa found in the Guiana Highlands of South America, especially in Venezuela and western Guyana. The word tepui comes from the language of the indigenous Pemon peoples, in which it means "house of the gods". Around 115 tepuis rise above the Gran Sabana in Venezuela's Canaima National Park and neighbouring regions; the most famous are Mount Roraima, Kukenan, and Auyán-tepui.

Geology

Tepuis are remnants of a vast sandstone plateau of Precambrian age, part of the Roraima Supergroup deposited roughly 1.7 billion years ago. Erosion over hundreds of millions of years removed the surrounding rock, leaving isolated table mountains of hard quartzite and sandstone with sheer cliffs up to 1,000 metres high. The summits are typically barren, rocky landscapes of weathered pinnacles and labyrinthine valleys, with thin, nutrient-poor soils.

Ecology

Isolated from the surrounding lowlands by their cliffs, tepui summits function as "islands in the sky", hosting highly endemic flora and fauna that evolved in isolation. The biota includes many carnivorous plants such as Heliamphora pitcher plants, numerous endemic frogs including the tiny pebble toads (Oreophrynella), and thousands of plant species found nowhere else; the region is recognised as the Pantepui biogeographic province. Angel Falls, the world's tallest uninterrupted waterfall at 979 metres, plunges from the edge of Auyán-tepui.

Exploration and culture

The tepuis are sacred in Pemon cosmology. Mount Roraima, the highest tepui at 2,810 metres, was first climbed in 1884 by Everard im Thurn's expedition, and its otherworldly summit helped inspire Arthur Conan Doyle's 1912 novel The Lost World. Roraima also stands at the tripoint of Venezuela, Brazil, and Guyana. Tourism to Roraima and Auyán-tepui is now a significant activity in Canaima National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
