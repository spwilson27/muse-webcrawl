Ibadi Islam

Ibadi Islam or Ibadism (Arabic: الإباضية, al-ʾIbāḍiyya) is a branch of Islam that is neither Sunni nor Shia. It is the predominant school of Islam in Oman, and Ibadi communities also survive in the M'zab valley of Algeria, on the island of Djerba in Tunisia, in the Nafusa mountains of Libya, and historically in Zanzibar and parts of East Africa.

History

The movement arose in Basra, Iraq, in the late 7th century CE, in the aftermath of the first Muslim civil wars. It traces its name to ʿAbd Allāh ibn Ibāḍ al-Tamīmī, one of its early spokesmen, while its first organiser is usually held to be Jābir ibn Zayd, a scholar from Oman. Ibadis developed from the moderate wing of the early Khawārij (Kharijites) — though Ibadis themselves reject the Kharijite label — opposing both Umayyad rule and the extremism of groups such as the Azāriqa.

Persecuted in Iraq, Ibadi missionaries carried the teaching to the margins of the Islamic world. Ibadi imamates were established in Oman, in Yemen and the Hadhramaut, and in North Africa, where the Rustamid imamate at Tahert (777–909 CE) governed much of the central Maghreb. In Oman the elected imamate persisted in various forms for over a millennium, and an Ibadi sultanate ruled Zanzibar until the revolution of 1964.

Beliefs and practices

Ibadis hold that the leader of the Muslim community (imam) should be elected by qualified Muslims on merit, regardless of lineage, and that a sinful ruler may legitimately be deposed. They emphasise righteous conduct: grave sinners who do not repent are regarded as guilty of kufr niʿma ("ingratitude for God's blessing") rather than outright unbelief. Distinctive doctrines include the view that the Qur'an is created (shared with the Muʿtazila), denial that believers will see God in the afterlife, and a gradation of relations with other Muslims ranging from association (wilāya) to dissociation (barāʾa). Ibadi law (fiqh) is among the earliest developed in Islam and differs in details from Sunni and Shia schools — for example, in the posture of the hands during prayer and in rules of inheritance.

Ibadism is often described by its adherents and outside observers alike as a moderate and tolerant tradition; in Oman it coexists with Sunni and Shia minorities under a sultanate that promotes interfaith tolerance.
