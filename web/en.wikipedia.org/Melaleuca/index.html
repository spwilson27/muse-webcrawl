Melaleuca

Melaleuca is a genus of nearly 300 species of plants in the myrtle family, Myrtaceae, commonly known as paperbarks, tea-trees or honey-myrtles. They range in size from small shrubs that rarely grow to more than 16 m (52 ft) high, to trees up to 35 m (115 ft). Their flowers generally occur in groups, forming a "head" or "spike" resembling a bottlebrush. Most melaleucas are endemic to Australia, with a few also occurring in New Guinea and Southeast Asia.

Description

Melaleucas are shrubs or trees, often with flaky, papery bark. The leaves are evergreen, arranged alternately or sometimes opposite, and contain oil glands that give crushed leaves a characteristic scent. The flowers are produced in dense clusters along the stems; each flower has five petals, often small and soon falling, and numerous showy stamens that give the inflorescence its brush-like appearance. The fruit is a woody capsule containing many tiny seeds.

Uses and ecology

Some species, notably Melaleuca alternifolia, are the source of tea tree oil, used in cosmetics and alternative medicine. Others are planted as ornamentals and street trees. In Florida, Melaleuca quinquenervia, introduced for swamp drainage and timber, has become a serious invasive weed in the Everglades. Melaleucas are important habitat and food plants for many Australian birds and insects, and many species are adapted to fire, resprouting from lignotubers or regenerating from canopy-stored seed.

Taxonomy

The genus was first formally described by Carl Linnaeus in 1767. The boundaries between Melaleuca and the related genus Callistemon (bottlebrushes) have long been debated; some taxonomists merged Callistemon into Melaleuca in 2006, though this is not universally accepted.
