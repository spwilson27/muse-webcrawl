Henry Clay (1777–1852)

Henry Clay (April 12, 1777 – June 29, 1852) was an American lawyer and statesman who represented Kentucky in both the U.S. Senate and House of Representatives. He was the seventh Speaker of the House and the ninth Secretary of State. Known as "The Great Compromiser", Clay brokered the Missouri Compromise of 1820, the compromise that ended the Nullification Crisis in 1833, and the Compromise of 1850, repeatedly defusing sectional crises over slavery. He ran for president five times and never won—a record that made him one of the most significant American politicians never to hold the office.

Career

Born in Hanover County, Virginia, Clay studied law under George Wythe and moved to Lexington, Kentucky, in 1797, becoming a leading trial lawyer. Rising with the Democratic-Republican Party, he became Speaker of the House almost immediately upon his election in 1811 and, as a leading "War Hawk", pushed the United States toward the War of 1812; he then negotiated the Treaty of Ghent ending it.

Clay championed the "American System"—protective tariffs, a national bank, and federal funding for internal improvements such as roads and canals—to bind the national economy together. He founded the Whig Party with other opponents of Andrew Jackson, whose vetoes and bank war he fought through the 1830s and 1840s. He served as Secretary of State under John Quincy Adams after the "corrupt bargain" controversy of the 1824 election, in which the House chose Adams and Clay's support was decisive.

Legacy

Clay's final triumph, the Compromise of 1850, admitted California as a free state and organised the Mexican Cession territories, postponing civil war for a decade. Abraham Lincoln called Clay his "beau ideal of a statesman" and modelled his own economic programme on the American System. A Senate committee chaired by John F. Kennedy in 1957 named Clay one of the five greatest senators in U.S. history.
