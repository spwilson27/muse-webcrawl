House of Burgesses

House of Burgesses
Type: Lower house (from 1643)
Established: 1619
Disbanded: 1776
Location: Jamestown, later Williamsburg, Virginia

The House of Burgesses was the elected representative element of the Virginia General Assembly, the legislative body of the Colony of Virginia. Convened first in 1619, it was the first representative legislative assembly in the European colonies in North America, and its creation marked the beginning of representative government in what would become the United States.

History

The Virginia Company of London, seeking to make the colony more attractive to settlers, instructed Governor Sir George Yeardley to establish a "General Assembly" including elected representatives — called burgesses — from each of Virginia's settlements. The first session met on July 30, 1619, in the church at Jamestown, with 22 burgesses representing 11 boroughs. Early sessions combined legislative, executive and judicial functions, and from 1643 the burgesses sat as a separate house alongside the Governor's Council.

Only white male landowners could vote for burgesses. Over the 17th and 18th centuries the House grew in assertiveness against royal governors, passing laws on taxation, appropriations and internal governance. Its members included many figures who later led the American Revolution: George Washington, Thomas Jefferson, Richard Henry Lee and Patrick Henry, whose 1765 Virginia Resolves against the Stamp Act were debated — and partly adopted — in the House.

Dissolution and legacy

After Governor Lord Dunmore dissolved the Assembly in 1774, the burgesses reconvened as part of a series of revolutionary conventions. In 1776 the House of Burgesses was formally replaced by the Virginia House of Delegates under the Commonwealth of Virginia's new constitution. The House's 157-year history is regarded as the seedbed of American legislative practice and self-government.
