Military science fiction

Military science fiction is a subgenre of science fiction that depicts the use of science fictional technology, including spaceships and weapons, for military purposes, and features principal characters who are members of a military organisation engaged in combat, sometimes in outer space or on other planets. It pays detailed attention to military organisation, tactics, hierarchy, training and traditions, and often explores the ethics of war and the relationship between the military and civilian society.

Powered armour, space navies and future infantry are staples of the subgenre.

Characteristics

Works in the subgenre typically feature plausible military structures extrapolated from existing armed forces, internally consistent weapons and logistics, and combat described with tactical realism. Unlike space opera, which may use military settings as backdrop for adventure, military science fiction centres the profession of arms: recruitment, training, chain of command, camaraderie, sacrifice and the political context of conflict. Authors with personal military experience, such as David Drake (an Vietnam veteran) and Robert A. Heinlein, strongly shaped the genre.

History

Early examples include H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds (1898) and his story "The Land Ironclads" (1903), which anticipated tank warfare. The pulp magazines of the 1930s–50s carried tales of future war, and John W. Campbell's Astounding encouraged technically detailed treatments. Robert A. Heinlein's Starship Troopers (1959), centred on the Mobile Infantry and its powered armour, is widely considered the defining work of the subgenre, though its politics of earned citizenship remain debated. Joe Haldeman's The Forever War (1974), a Hugo and Nebula Award winner, answered from the experience of the Vietnam War. Jerry Pournelle's Falkenberg's Legion stories and David Drake's Hammer's Slammers series established the mercenary-company format, while Lois McMaster Bujold's Vorkosigan Saga, David Weber's Honor Harrington series and the tie-in fiction of game universes such as BattleTech and Warhammer 40,000 broadened the field.

Common themes

  • First contact gone wrong and interstellar war
  • Powered armour, mecha and artificial intelligence in combat
  • The citizen-soldier and military service as a path to citizenship
  • Mercenaries and private military companies
  • The effects of relativistic time dilation on soldiers and societies
  • Alien enemies designed as foils for human military virtues or flaws

Notable works

  • Starship Troopers (1959) by Robert A. Heinlein
  • The Forever War (1974) by Joe Haldeman
  • Ender's Game (1985) by Orson Scott Card
  • Armor (1984) by John Steakley
  • Old Man's War (2005) by John Scalzi
  • The Honor Harrington series by David Weber

In other media

The subgenre thrives in film and television (Aliens, Starship Troopers, Battlestar Galactica), video games (Halo, Mass Effect), anime (Mobile Suit Gundam) and tabletop wargames. Several real armed forces include military science fiction on professional reading lists.
