Sápmi is the cultural region traditionally inhabited by the Sámi people. It stretches across the northern parts of Norway, Sweden and Finland, and the Kola Peninsula of Russia, covering roughly 390,000 square kilometres. The region is sometimes referred to in English as "Lapland", though many Sámi regard that term as outdated or pejorative.

Geography

Sápmi spans tundra, boreal forest and mountainous terrain, including part of the Scandinavian Mountains. Much of the region lies north of the Arctic Circle and experiences polar night in winter and the midnight sun in summer. Major rivers include the Tana, the Torne and the Kemi.

People and culture

The Sámi are an Indigenous Finno-Ugric people whose traditional livelihoods include reindeer herding, fishing, hunting and handicrafts known as duodji. Sámi languages, of which Northern Sámi is the most widely spoken, belong to the Uralic language family. Cultural symbols include the gákti (traditional clothing), the joik (a form of song), and the Sámi flag, adopted in 1986.

Politics

Sámi parliaments exist in Norway, Sweden and Finland, providing a measure of self-governance and a voice in matters affecting Sámi land and culture. The Nordic Sámi Convention seeks to strengthen cross-border cooperation. Reindeer herding rights and land-use disputes, including mining and wind-power development, remain central political issues.

History

Archaeological evidence suggests Sámi ancestors have lived in the region for thousands of years. From the Middle Ages the Sámi were increasingly taxed by neighbouring states, and from the 17th century onwards they faced missionary activity and assimilation policies, including the Norwegianization era in Norway. Since the late 20th century there has been a strong cultural and political revival.
