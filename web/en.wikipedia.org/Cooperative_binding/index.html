Cooperative binding

Cooperative binding occurs when a macromolecule with multiple binding sites for a ligand shows increased or decreased affinity at its remaining sites after one ligand has bound. Positive cooperativity makes binding of subsequent ligands easier; negative cooperativity makes it harder.

Haemoglobin

The classic example is haemoglobin, whose four subunits bind oxygen cooperatively, producing a sigmoidal oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve. Binding of the first oxygen molecule shifts haemoglobin from the tense (T) state to the relaxed (R) state, raising the affinity of the other subunits. This allows haemoglobin to load oxygen efficiently in the lungs and release it in tissues where it is needed. The degree of cooperativity is measured by the Hill coefficient; haemoglobin's value is about 2.8–3.0, below the maximum of 4 but far above the non-cooperative value of 1.

Models

Two classic models describe cooperativity: the concerted (MWC) model of Monod, Wyman, and Changeux, in which all subunits switch state together, and the sequential (KNF) model of Koshland, Némethy, and Filmer, in which subunits change conformation individually upon binding.
