Cafe racer

A cafe racer is a genre of sport motorcycle that originated among British riders in the early 1960s. Cafe racers were standard production bikes heavily modified by their owners for speed and handling, optimized for quick rides over short distances between transport cafés. The term grew out of the rocker subculture centred on venues such as London's Ace Cafe, where riders raced between cafés—or, in one popular account, raced back before a jukebox record finished playing.

Configuration

The classic cafe racer layout emphasizes a lightweight, tuned engine in a stiff frame, with low, narrow clip-on handlebars or "ace bars", a long fuel tank often with knee indents, rear-set footpegs and controls, a swept-back exhaust, and a single seat with a distinctive rear cowl or "bum stop". Riders removed every non-essential part to cut weight and tuned engines to reach "the ton"—100 mph. Famous period hybrids include the Triton (Triumph engine in a Norton featherbed frame) and the Norvin.

Revival

The style revived worldwide from the 2000s onward, influencing factory models such as Triumph's Thruxton and Moto Guzzi's V7 Racer, and a large custom-building scene, though critics distinguish show-built "cafe-style" bikes from the stripped functional machines of the originals.
