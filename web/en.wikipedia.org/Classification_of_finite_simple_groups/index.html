Classification of finite simple groups

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the classification of finite simple groups is a theorem stating that every finite simple group is either cyclic of prime order, an alternating group, a group of Lie type, or one of 26 sporadic groups. It is one of the largest theorems in the history of mathematics: its proof, completed in the early 2000s, spans tens of thousands of pages written by hundreds of authors between roughly 1955 and 2004.

Sporadic groupsExceptional cases fitting no family26

The cyclic groups of prime order and the alternating groups An (n ≥ 5) are straightforward. The groups of Lie type are analogues over finite fields of the simple Lie groups, constructed by Chevalley, Steinberg, Suzuki and Ree. The 26 sporadic groups include the five Mathieu groups, the Janko groups, and the largest, the Monster group, with about 8×1053 elements.

History of the proof

The Feit–Thompson theorem (1963), which states that every group of odd order is solvable, launched the modern classification programme. Daniel Gorenstein coordinated the effort from the 1970s, announcing in 1981 that the proof was essentially complete. However, gaps remained — notably the classification of quasithin groups, settled by Aschbacher and Smith in 2004, and refinements of the even case published over the following years. A second-generation proof, reorganising and simplifying the argument, is being produced under the coordination of Gorenstein, Lyons and Solomon; six of its projected twelve volumes have appeared.

Significance

The classification yields complete descriptions of all finite groups up to extension: any finite group is built from simple groups as composition factors. It has applications in representation theory, combinatorics, number theory and, through the Monster, in string theory via monstrous moonshine.
