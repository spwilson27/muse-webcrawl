Comparative advantage

Comparative advantage is an economic principle describing how individuals, firms, or nations can gain from specialisation and trade. A producer has a comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce that good at a lower opportunity cost than other producers. Even if one country is more efficient at producing everything (an absolute advantage), both parties can benefit from trade if each specialises where its comparative advantage lies.

History

The concept is most associated with David Ricardo, who set it out in his Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817), using the example of England and Portugal trading cloth and wine: Portugal could produce both more cheaply, yet both countries gained if Portugal concentrated on wine and England on cloth. Robert Torrens had described a similar idea slightly earlier, in 1815. John Stuart Mill further developed the theory, analysing how the gains from trade are divided.

The Ricardian model

In the simplest model, two countries produce two goods using labour alone. Each country exports the good in which it has a comparative advantage and imports the other; total world output rises, allowing both to consume beyond their individual production possibility frontiers. Later models, such as the Heckscher–Ohlin model, explained comparative advantage by differences in factor endowments (capital, labour, land) rather than technology alone.

Criticism and extensions

Critics note that the classical model assumes full employment, immobile capital, and no transport costs or adjustment burdens for displaced workers. Economists have also developed concepts such as revealed comparative advantage for empirical measurement, and dynamic comparative advantage, in which government policy or learning-by-doing creates future advantage.
