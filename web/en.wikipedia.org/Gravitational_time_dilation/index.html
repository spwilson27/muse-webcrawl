Gravitational Time Dilation

Gravitational time dilation is a form of time dilation, an actual difference of elapsed time between two events as measured by observers situated at varying distances from a gravitating mass. The lower the gravitational potential (the closer the clock is to the source of gravitation), the slower time passes, speeding up as the gravitational potential increases. Albert Einstein originally predicted this in his theory of relativity.

Description

Clocks that are far from massive bodies (or at higher gravitational potentials) run more quickly, and clocks close to massive bodies run more slowly. For a non-rotating sphere, the rate of a clock at radius r relative to a distant observer is reduced by the factor √(1 − rs/r), where rs is the Schwarzschild radius. Near Earth's surface the effect is tiny — a clock at sea level accumulates about two fewer seconds... in practice, precision atomic-clock experiments have measured rate differences for height differences of less than one metre.

Experimental confirmation

The effect was confirmed by the Pound–Rebka experiment (1959), which measured the gravitational redshift of gamma rays in a 22.5-metre tower at Harvard, and by the Hafele–Keating experiment with flown atomic clocks. The Gravity Probe A rocket experiment (1976) verified the prediction to high precision.

Applications

Gravitational time dilation, combined with velocity time dilation, must be corrected for in the Global Positioning System: satellite clocks would otherwise drift by about 38 microseconds per day, producing navigational errors of kilometres. The effect becomes extreme near black holes, where time for an infalling observer appears frozen to a distant watcher at the event horizon.
