Llywelyn ab Iorwerth (c. 1173 – 11 April 1240), known as Llywelyn the Great (Welsh: Llywelyn Fawr), was a Welsh prince and ruler of the kingdom of Gwynedd in north Wales. By the end of his life he exercised overlordship over much of Wales, becoming the most dominant Welsh ruler of the medieval period.

Rise to power

Llywelyn, a grandson of Owain Gwynedd, seized control of Gwynedd in the 1190s, overcoming his uncles and cousins. He captured Mold in 1199 and negotiated with King John of England, marrying John's illegitimate daughter Joan in 1205. Relations with John later broke down; the king invaded Gwynedd in 1211, but Llywelyn recovered his lands by allying with the English barons against John.

Dominance in Wales

After 1215 Llywelyn extended his authority over the Welsh princes of the south, recognised at the Council of Aberdyfi in 1216, and concluded the Treaty of Worcester with Henry III in 1218. His later years were spent securing the succession of his son Dafydd, while his other son Gruffudd contested the inheritance. He retired to the Cistercian abbey of Aberconwy, where he died in 1240.

Legacy

Llywelyn is remembered as a statesman and lawgiver who maintained Welsh autonomy for four decades and laid foundations later built upon by his grandson, Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native Prince of Wales.
