The First Council of Lyon (Latin: Concilium Lugdunense I) was the thirteenth ecumenical council recognised by the Roman Catholic Church, meeting at Lyon in 1245. Convoked by Pope Innocent IV, who had fled Rome for the safety of Lyon in his struggle with the Holy Roman Emperor, the council is chiefly remembered for formally deposing Emperor Frederick II.

Proceedings

The council met in three sessions on 28 June, 5 July and 17 July 1245, attended by about 250 bishops, the Latin patriarchs of Constantinople, Antioch and Aquileia, and envoys from many rulers. Innocent IV set out five "wounds" of the Church: clerical corruption, the loss of the Holy Land (Jerusalem had fallen to the Khwarezmians in 1244), the threat of the Mongols, the schism with the Greek Church, and the persecution of the Church by Frederick II.

Deposition of Frederick II

The emperor's advocate Thaddeus of Suessa defended his master vigorously, but on 17 July Innocent pronounced the sentence of deposition: Frederick was declared guilty of sacrilege, heresy and felony, his subjects were released from their oaths of allegiance, and his imperial and royal titles were revoked. The sentence did not end Frederick's power — he ruled until his death in 1250 — but it shattered the prestige of the Hohenstaufen dynasty.

Other measures

The council promulgated 38 constitutions dealing with clerical discipline, crusade taxation (a twentieth of revenues for the Holy Land), aid for the Latin Empire of Constantinople, and measures against the Mongol threat. It also ordered the wearing of a red hat by cardinals, a custom that later became emblematic of their rank. The council's crusade plans contributed to the decision of King Louis IX of France to launch the Seventh Crusade in 1248.
