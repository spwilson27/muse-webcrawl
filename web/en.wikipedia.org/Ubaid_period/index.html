Ubaid period

The Ubaid period (c. 6500–3800 BCE) is a prehistoric period in Mesopotamia named after Tell al-'Ubaid, near Ur in southern Iraq, where its characteristic pottery was first identified. It follows the earlier Samarra and Halaf cultures and precedes the Uruk period, during which the first cities and writing appeared.

Ubaid culture is marked by large village settlements practising irrigation agriculture in the south and dry farming in the north, by fine painted pottery with geometric designs (later increasingly plain wares as the potter's wheel spread), and by distinctive tripartite houses and large public buildings that are seen as precursors of Sumerian temples, such as the sequence at Eridu.

The Ubaid horizon spread widely: Ubaid-style material culture is found across northern Mesopotamia, Syria, and down the Arabian shore of the Persian Gulf, suggesting trade networks reaching toward Dilmun and Magan. Scholars debate whether its spread reflects migration, exchange, or emulation.

Social organization appears to have grown more complex and hierarchical over the period, laying foundations for the urban, temple-centred society of the following Uruk period.
