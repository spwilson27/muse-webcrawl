National Bank Act

The National Bank Acts of 1863 and 1864 were United States federal laws that established a system of national banks and created the United States National Banking System. They encouraged development of a national currency backed by Treasury securities held by the banks, and established the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency as part of the Department of the Treasury.

Enacted1863, amended 1864
PresidentAbraham Lincoln
Key figureSalmon P. Chase, Secretary of the Treasury

Background

Before the Civil War, American banking was dominated by state-chartered banks issuing their own notes, which circulated at varying discounts. The war created an urgent need for federal finance and a stable currency. Secretary of the Treasury Salmon P. Chase proposed national banks that would buy government bonds and issue uniform notes.

Provisions

The 1863 act created national bank charters, set capital and reserve requirements, and provided for note issuance secured by bonds deposited with the Treasury. The 1864 act strengthened supervision, taxation, and receivership. A 10 percent tax on state bank notes in 1865 effectively drove them from circulation.

Legacy

The system unified the currency and helped finance the Union war effort, though it produced an inelastic currency and periodic panics. It lasted until the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 created a central bank, but national bank charters remain in use today.
