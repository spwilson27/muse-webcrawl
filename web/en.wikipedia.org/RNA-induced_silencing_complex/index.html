The RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) is a multiprotein complex that mediates gene silencing in the RNA interference (RNAi) pathway. A single-stranded guide RNA — either a small interfering RNA (siRNA) or a microRNA (miRNA) — is loaded into RISC, whose catalytic heart is an Argonaute protein. The guide strand base-pairs with complementary messenger RNA targets, directing Argonaute to cleave them or repress their translation, thereby silencing the corresponding gene.

Mechanism

Double-stranded RNA precursors are first cut by the enzyme Dicer into short duplexes of about 21 nucleotides. One strand — the guide — is selected and loaded into Argonaute while the passenger strand is discarded or destroyed; strand choice follows thermodynamic asymmetry of the duplex ends. In the mature, activated complex (sometimes called holo-RISC), Argonaute's PIWI domain, an RNase H-like nuclease, cleaves target mRNAs opposite the middle of the guide strand. Perfect complementarity, typical of siRNAs, leads to cleavage, whereas the partial complementarity typical of miRNAs mainly causes translational repression and accelerated mRNA decay, assisted in animals by the scaffold protein GW182/TNRC6.

Components and discovery

Besides Argonaute and the guide RNA, reported RISC components in various organisms include Dicer, the RNA-binding protein TRBP in humans, fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP) and the helicase MOV10. The complex's activity was characterised in Drosophila cell lysates around 2000–2001 following the discovery of RNAi by Fire and Mello (1998), and the identification of Argonaute2 as the sole human "slicer" was a landmark in understanding the pathway. RISC underlies natural antiviral defence and gene regulation as well as experimental RNAi and therapeutic siRNA drugs such as patisiran.
