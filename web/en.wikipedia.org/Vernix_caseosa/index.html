Vernix caseosa

Vernix caseosa (Latin for "cheesy varnish") is the waxy, white, cheese-like substance that coats the skin of newborn human infants. It is produced during the third trimester of pregnancy by the fetus's sebaceous glands together with shed skin cells, and it is unique to humans among primates.

Composition and development

Vernix consists of roughly 80% water held in corneocytes, with lipids (including squalene, wax esters, and ceramides) and proteins, including antimicrobial peptides and enzymes. It begins to form around the 20th week of gestation as part of the development of the fetal skin barrier, initially covering the back and skin folds, and is progressively shed into the amniotic fluid near term — one reason post-term babies may be born with little vernix remaining.

Functions

Within the womb, vernix protects the fetal skin from prolonged exposure to amniotic fluid, aids in the formation of the acid mantle, and may contribute to temperature regulation. During delivery it lubricates the birth canal. After birth it moisturises the newborn's skin, helps maintain body temperature, and provides innate antimicrobial protection; the World Health Organization recommends delaying the first bath for at least six hours (preferably 24) so that vernix is absorbed naturally rather than washed away.
