Flight recorder

A flight recorder is an electronic recording device placed in an aircraft for the purpose of facilitating the investigation of aviation accidents and incidents. The device may be referred to colloquially as a "black box", although flight recorders are in fact painted bright international orange to aid recovery. Flight recorders are usually of two types: the flight data recorder (FDR), which preserves the history of flight parameters, and the cockpit voice recorder (CVR), which preserves the recent history of sounds in the cockpit, including crew conversation.

History

One of the earliest flight recording devices was developed in France in the 1930s by François Hussenot. The modern flight recorder is generally credited to Australian scientist David Warren, who in 1953, while investigating crashes of the de Havilland Comet, conceived a unit that recorded cockpit conversation and instrument readings. Despite initial indifference, his design was taken up commercially in the 1960s, and recorders became mandatory on airliners worldwide.

Design and survivability

Recorders are typically installed in the tail section of the aircraft, where survival is most likely. Modern units must withstand impacts of 3,400 g, fires of 1,100 °C, and deep-sea pressure, and are fitted with an underwater locator beacon that transmits a pulse for at least 30 days. Solid-state recorders store FDR data for at least 25 hours of flight and CVR audio for at least 2 hours.

Regulation

The International Civil Aviation Organization issues standards for flight recorders, implemented by national regulators such as the FAA and EASA. Following accidents in which recorders were difficult to locate, proposals have advanced for deployable recorders and for streaming flight data to the ground.
