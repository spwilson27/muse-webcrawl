Cardinal Mazarin

Cardinal Jules Mazarin (born Giulio Mazzarino; 14 July 1602 – 9 March 1661) was an Italian cardinal, diplomat, and politician who served as chief minister to the kings of France from 1642 until his death. As the successor to Cardinal Richelieu, and as mentor to the young Louis XIV, Mazarin consolidated royal absolutism and made France the dominant power in Europe.

Early life and rise

Born at Pescina in the Abruzzo, Mazarin studied in Rome and Spain, entered papal diplomatic service, and impressed Richelieu while negotiating during the War of the Mantuan Succession. Naturalised French, he entered Louis XIII's service, became a cardinal in 1641 (though never ordained a priest), and on Richelieu's death in 1642 became chief minister and godfather to the future Louis XIV.

Chief minister

Mazarin guided France through the final phase of the Thirty Years' War, concluded by the Peace of Westphalia (1648), and negotiated the Treaty of the Pyrenees (1659) with Spain, which included the marriage of Louis XIV to Maria Theresa. Domestically he survived the Fronde (1648–1653), a series of revolts by nobles and parlements provoked by war taxation, twice fleeing into exile before returning in triumph. His flexible, patient diplomacy contrasted with Richelieu's severity.

Legacy

Mazarin amassed one of the largest private fortunes of the century and a celebrated art collection and library — the Bibliothèque Mazarine, France's first public library. His foundation, the Collège des Quatre-Nations, now houses the Institut de France. On his death in 1661, Louis XIV declared he would be his own chief minister, ruling according to principles Mazarin had taught him.
