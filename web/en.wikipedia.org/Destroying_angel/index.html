Destroying angel

Destroying angel is the common name for several deadly poisonous species of white mushrooms in the genus Amanita. The name is applied principally to Amanita bisporigera and Amanita ocreata in North America and Amanita virosa in Europe.

Description

Destroying angels are entirely white, with a smooth cap, white gills, a ring (annulus) on the stem, and a cup-like volva at the base. They are mycorrhizal, growing near the roots of trees, particularly oaks, in forests during summer and autumn.

Toxicity

These species contain amatoxins, the same class of toxins found in the death cap (Amanita phalloides). Amatoxins inhibit RNA polymerase, halting protein synthesis and destroying liver and kidney cells. A single cap can be lethal. Symptoms typically begin with severe gastrointestinal distress six to twelve hours after eating, followed by a deceptive apparent recovery and then liver failure.

Treatment

Treatment is supportive and urgent, potentially including activated charcoal, silibinin, and, in severe cases, liver transplantation. Because the early symptoms are delayed, victims may not connect the illness to the meal, making these mushrooms especially dangerous to foragers who mistake them for edible white species.
