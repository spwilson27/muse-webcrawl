Common descent is a concept in evolutionary biology applicable when one species is the ancestor of two or more species later in time. According to modern evolutionary biology, all living beings on Earth are descended from a common ancestor — the last universal common ancestor (LUCA) — that lived approximately 3.5 to 3.8 billion years ago.

History

The idea that species share ancestry was proposed by naturalists including Pierre Louis Maupertuis and Erasmus Darwin, and was developed by Charles Darwin in On the Origin of Species (1859), which presented descent with modification by natural selection. Darwin famously concluded that "probably all the organic beings which have ever lived on this earth have descended from some one primordial form". Alfred Russel Wallace independently arrived at similar conclusions.

Evidence

Evidence for common descent includes homologous structures (such as the pentadactyl limb of vertebrates), vestigial organs, the fossil record's succession of forms, the geographic distribution of species, and — most decisively — molecular evidence: the near-universal genetic code, shared genes and proteins, endogenous retroviruses inserted at identical genomic locations, and nested hierarchies reconstructed independently from anatomy and DNA sequences that agree with each other.

Phylogenetics

Scientists reconstruct evolutionary relationships as phylogenetic trees, in which branching points represent common ancestors. The pattern of similarities among species, arranged in a nested hierarchy, is the signature prediction of common descent. Formal statistical tests comparing universal common ancestry with competing hypotheses have strongly favored a single origin for extant life, though horizontal gene transfer among early microbes complicates the picture of the earliest branches.
