High Renaissance

The culminating phase of the Italian Renaissance, c. 1495–1527, centred on Rome.

In art history, the High Renaissance was a short period of the most exceptional artistic production in the Italian states, particularly Rome, capital of the Papal States, and in Florence, during the Italian Renaissance. Most art historians state that the High Renaissance started around 1495 or 1500 and ended in 1520 with the death of Raphael, although some say it ended in 1527 with the Sack of Rome by the troops of Charles V, or about 1530.

The period is defined by the careers of three towering figures—Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519), Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564) and Raphael Sanzio (1483–1520)—and by the patronage of Popes Julius II and Leo X, who made Rome the artistic capital of Europe. In architecture its central figure is Donato Bramante, whose Tempietto (1502) and new design for St Peter's Basilica set the classical language of the age; in Venice, Giorgione and the young Titian developed a parallel High Renaissance style based on colour and atmosphere.

Characteristics

High Renaissance art is characterised by harmony, balance and compositional unity (often pyramidal), mastery of linear perspective, chiaroscuro and sfumato, idealised naturalism grounded in anatomical study, and a confident synthesis of classical antiquity with Christian subjects. Canonical works include Leonardo's Last Supper (1495–98) and Mona Lisa, Michelangelo's David (1501–04) and the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–12), and Raphael's School of Athens (1509–11) and Madonnas.

End and aftermath

The Sack of Rome in 1527 scattered artists across Italy and Europe and is conventionally taken to close the period; the more restless, elongated style that succeeded it is known as Mannerism. The High Renaissance nonetheless remained, for Vasari and for later centuries, the standard against which Western painting and sculpture were measured.
