The cactus wren (Campylorhynchus brunneicapillus) is a species of wren endemic to the deserts of the southwestern United States and northern and central Mexico. It is the state bird of Arizona.

Description

The cactus wren is the largest wren in North America, measuring roughly 18 to 22 cm (7 to 9 inches) in length. It has a brown head with a distinctive broad white stripe over the eye (supercilium), a long slightly downcurved bill, barred wings and tail, and a throat and upper breast marked with heavy dark spots that form a characteristic dark patch. The belly is pale with scattered dark spots. Its song is a loud, harsh, mechanical chattering often likened to the sound of a car engine that will not start.

Distribution and habitat

The species inhabits arid and semi-arid country throughout the Sonoran, Mojave, and Chihuahuan deserts, including coastal sage scrub, desert grassland, and urban desert landscaping. Its range extends from southern California, Nevada, Utah, and Arizona through New Mexico and Texas, and south into central Mexico.

Behavior

Cactus wrens are bold, inquisitive birds that forage on the ground and in low vegetation for insects, spiders, seeds, and fruit. They are poor fliers over long distances and mostly move in short, low flights. Pairs maintain territories year-round and often travel in family groups after the breeding season.

Nesting

The cactus wren builds a football-shaped nest with a side entrance tunnel, typically placed in a cholla cactus, prickly pear, yucca, or other spiny plant that deters predators. Both sexes build; nests are also constructed for roosting throughout the year. The female lays three to seven eggs, which she incubates for about 16 days.

Status

The cactus wren is listed as a species of Least Concern by the IUCN, though the coastal population in southern California has declined substantially due to habitat loss and fragmentation.
