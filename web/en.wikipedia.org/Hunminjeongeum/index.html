Hunminjeongeum

The Hunminjeongeum is the 1446 document that promulgated Hangul, the Korean alphabet.

Hunminjeongeum (Korean: 훈민정음; "The Proper Sounds for the Instruction of the People") is a document describing an entirely new writing system for the Korean language, promulgated in 1446 by King Sejong the Great of Joseon and the scholars of the Hall of Worthies. The script it introduced is now known as Hangul.

Creation

Before Hangul, Korean was written with adapted Chinese characters (hanja), which were difficult for commoners to learn. Sejong's preface famously states that because the speech of Korea differs from that of China, the people's sounds could not be adequately expressed, and so he created twenty-eight new letters "that all people may easily learn and use daily." The companion commentary volume, the Hunminjeongeum Haerye, explained the design of the letters.

Design of the letters

Hangul is a featural alphabet: the basic shapes of the consonant letters depict the position of the speech organs, and strokes are added systematically for related sounds. Vowel letters combine symbols for heaven, earth, and man. Letters are grouped into syllabic blocks. The original system had 28 letters; modern Korean uses 24 basic letters.

Reception and legacy

The new script was opposed by Confucian officials such as Choe Malli, who saw abandonment of Chinese characters as a betrayal of civilization, and it was later suppressed by King Yeonsangun. It survived among women and common people, was revived in the late 19th century, and after 1945 became the official script of both Koreas. The Haerye edition is inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register, and South Korea celebrates Hangul Day on 9 October.
