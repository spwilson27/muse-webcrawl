Hexachord

In music theory, a hexachord (from Greek: ἑξάχορδος, "six-stringed") is a series of six successive notes. The term is used in two principal senses: for the medieval and Renaissance system of hexachords associated with Guido of Arezzo, which organised the musical gamut for sight-singing, and in modern set theory for any six-note pitch collection (hexad).

The Guidonian hexachord

The hexachord system, described in the 11th century by the Benedictine monk Guido of Arezzo, assigned the solmization syllables ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la (taken from the hymn Ut queant laxis) to six-note segments of the scale, each containing a single semitone, between mi and fa. Three types of hexachord were recognised: the natural hexachord (C–A, with B natural), the hard hexachord (durum, G–E, with B natural as mi), and the soft hexachord (molle, F–D, with B♭ as fa). Overlapping hexachords covered the whole gamut of about twenty notes, and singers moved from one hexachord to another by mutation, changing syllables at a shared pitch. The system, taught with the aid of the "Guidonian hand," remained the foundation of elementary music education into the 17th century and underlies the modern solfège syllables (ut later becoming "do").

Hexachords in modern theory

In twentieth-century pitch-class set theory, a hexachord is any unordered collection of six pitch classes. Hexachords are prominent in twelve-tone theory: in combinatoriality, a tone row can be built from two complementary hexachords, a property explored by Milton Babbitt and others. The diatonic scale's first six notes, whole-tone subsets, and the "Petrushka chord" are frequently cited examples of hexachordal sets.
