Light therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Light therapy, or phototherapy, consists of exposure to daylight or to specific wavelengths of light using light-emitting diodes, fluorescent lamps, or very bright full-spectrum light boxes, administered for a prescribed amount of time. In medicine it is an established treatment for seasonal affective disorder (SAD) and certain circadian-rhythm sleep disorders, and specific forms of phototherapy are used in dermatology and neonatology for other purposes.

Seasonal affective disorder and sleep

The best-known application is treatment of seasonal affective disorder, a pattern of depression recurring with the winter months at higher latitudes, described formally by Norman Rosenthal and colleagues at the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health in 1984. A typical regimen involves sitting near a light box producing 10,000 lux of diffuse white light (filtered to remove ultraviolet) for about 30 minutes each morning. Meta-analyses find bright light therapy comparable in effect size to antidepressant trials for SAD, and there is evidence — first shown in studies in the late 1990s and 2000s — that it can also benefit non-seasonal major depression, including as an adjunct to medication. Light timed to the morning or evening is also used to shift circadian phase in delayed or advanced sleep phase disorders and jet lag, exploiting the sensitivity of retinal ganglion cells (via the photopigment melanopsin) that signal the brain's clock.

Other medical uses

  • Neonatal jaundice: blue light converts bilirubin in the infant's skin into water-soluble forms that can be excreted.
  • Skin conditions: narrowband UVB and PUVA (psoralen plus UVA) are used for psoriasis, eczema, and vitiligo; blue light is used for acne.
  • Wound and pain applications: low-level laser/red-light therapies are marketed for various indications with more variable evidence.

Risks and side effects

Bright light therapy is generally well tolerated; reported side effects include headache, eye strain, nausea, and agitation, and it can precipitate hypomania or mania in people with bipolar disorder. UV-based treatments carry risks of sunburn and increased long-term skin cancer exposure and are medically supervised. Light boxes for SAD are regulated as medical devices in some countries and sold widely for home use.
