Entomology

Entomology (from Greek ἔντομον, entomon, "insect", and -λογία, "study") is the scientific study of insects, a branch of zoology. With over a million described species — more than half of all known living organisms — insects dominate terrestrial biodiversity, and entomology encompasses their classification, physiology, behaviour, ecology and interactions with humans.

History

Insect study dates to antiquity, but entomology became a distinct science in the 16th and 17th centuries with the works of Ulisse Aldrovandi and Jan Swammerdam. William Kirby is regarded as the father of entomology in England; his Introduction to Entomology (1815–1826), written with William Spence, popularised the field. Jean-Henri Fabre's vivid studies of insect behaviour later brought entomology to a broad readership.

Subdisciplines

Entomology is subdivided by taxon — coleopterology (beetles), lepidopterology (butterflies and moths), hymenopterology (ants, bees and wasps), dipterology (flies) — and by application. Medical entomology studies insects that transmit diseases such as malaria and dengue; forensic entomology uses insect succession on corpses to estimate time of death; agricultural entomology addresses crop pests and pollinators; and industrial entomology covers beneficial insects such as honey bees and silkworms.

Importance

Insects pollinate a large share of the world's food crops, recycle nutrients, and serve as food for countless other animals, while a minority are pests or disease vectors that cause enormous economic losses and human suffering. Entomological research underpins integrated pest management, conservation biology and much of ecology.
