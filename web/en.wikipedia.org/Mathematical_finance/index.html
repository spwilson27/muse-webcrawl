Mathematical finance

Mathematical finance, also known as quantitative finance and financial mathematics, is a field of applied mathematics concerned with mathematical modelling of financial markets. It draws on probability theory, stochastic calculus, statistics and numerical methods to price derivatives, manage risk and construct portfolios.

History

The field is often traced to Louis Bachelier's 1900 thesis modelling share prices as a random walk, and to the Black–Scholes–Merton model of 1973, which gave a closed-form price for European options using the idea of dynamic hedging and no-arbitrage. The fundamental theorems of asset pricing link absence of arbitrage to the existence of equivalent martingale measures, the basis of risk-neutral valuation.

Topics

Key topics include derivative pricing (options, futures, swaps, credit derivatives), interest-rate models such as Vasicek, CIR and Heath–Jarrow–Morton, stochastic volatility, risk measures such as value at risk, portfolio theory descending from Markowitz, and computational methods including Monte Carlo simulation and finite-difference solution of partial differential equations. Practitioners, known as quants, work in banks, hedge funds and trading firms; the field has been criticised for model risk, notably after the 2008 financial crisis.
