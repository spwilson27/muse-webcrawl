Poisson point process

In probability theory, statistics and corresponding fields, a Poisson point process is a type of random mathematical object that consists of points randomly located on a mathematical space with the essential feature that the points occur independently of one another. The Poisson point process is often called simply the Poisson process, but it is also called a Poisson random measure, Poisson random point field or Poisson point field. This point process has convenient mathematical properties, which has made it a frequently used model in disciplines such as queueing theory, telecommunications, and astronomy.

The process is named after French mathematician Siméon Denis Poisson, although Poisson never studied the process himself. Its name derives from the fact that the number of points of the process falling into a region of the underlying space follows a Poisson distribution.

Definitions

A Poisson point process can be defined by two properties: the number of points in any bounded region follows a Poisson distribution whose mean is proportional to the measure of the region, and the numbers of points in disjoint regions are independent random variables. A homogeneous Poisson process has a constant rate (or intensity), while an inhomogeneous or nonhomogeneous Poisson process has a rate that varies with position or time.

Homogeneous Poisson process

Viewed as a counting process on the real line, the homogeneous Poisson process with rate λ is characterised by stationary, independent increments: the waiting times between successive points (interarrival times) are independent exponentially distributed random variables with mean 1/λ. Such processes are widely used to model events occurring randomly in time, such as the arrival of customers, radioactive decays, or telephone calls.

Applications

Poisson point processes are used to model spatial patterns including the positions of stars, the distribution of trees in a forest, the locations of base stations in a wireless network, and events in spacetime in particle physics.
