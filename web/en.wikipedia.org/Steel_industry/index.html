Steel industry

The steel industry is the business of producing steel—an alloy of iron and carbon—and fabricating it into products for construction, transport, machinery, packaging, and energy. World crude steel production is roughly 1.9 billion tonnes per year, with China accounting for more than half of output, followed by India, Japan, and the United States.

Production methods

Most steel is made by two routes: the integrated blast furnace–basic oxygen furnace route, which smelts iron ore with coke, and the electric arc furnace route, which melts scrap or direct-reduced iron and accounts for a growing share thanks to lower emissions. Major producers include China Baowu, ArcelorMittal, Nippon Steel, and HBIS Group.

History

Modern steelmaking began with the Bessemer process in the 1850s and expanded through the open-hearth and basic oxygen processes. Steel powered railways, shipbuilding, and skyscrapers in the industrial age. After the Second World War production shifted from the United States and Europe toward East Asia, and in the twenty-first century to China and India.

Economics and environment

Steelmaking is energy-intensive and responsible for roughly 7–9 percent of global carbon dioxide emissions. The industry faces chronic overcapacity debates and is investing in hydrogen-based direct reduction and carbon capture under "green steel" programs. Steel is also the world's most recycled material, with high recovery rates from end-of-life products.
