The Indian Rivers Inter-link project is a proposed large-scale civil engineering programme to connect India's rivers through a network of reservoirs, canals and channels, transferring water from water-surplus basins to water-deficit regions. It is one of the largest proposed water infrastructure projects in the world.

Background

Ideas for inter-basin transfer date to the 19th century, notably proposals by Sir Arthur Cotton, and were revived in the 1970s by K. L. Rao's Ganga–Cauvery link proposal and Captain Dastur's Garland Canal scheme. The National Water Development Agency (NWDA) was established in 1982 to study feasibility, and a National Perspective Plan framed the Himalayan and Peninsular components.

Components

The Himalayan component envisages links among the Brahmaputra and Ganges tributaries, while the Peninsular component would link rivers including the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Cauvery, and west-flowing rivers south of the Tapi. Roughly 30 links and thousands of kilometres of canals have been studied, with claimed benefits in irrigation, drinking water, hydropower and flood moderation.

Progress and debate

The Ken–Betwa link in Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh became the first link to move toward implementation in the 2020s. Supporters cite drought and flood mitigation; critics raise ecological damage, displacement, interstate and international water disputes (including with Nepal and Bangladesh), cost, and doubts about surplus-water assumptions under climate change.
