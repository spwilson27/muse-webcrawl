Treaty of Amiens

Treaty of Amiens
Signed25 March 1802, Amiens, France
PartiesUnited Kingdom; French Republic; Spain; Batavian Republic
ContextEnd of the War of the Second Coalition (French Revolutionary Wars)
EffectPeace between Britain and France until May 1803

The Treaty of Amiens, signed on 25 March 1802 in the French city of Amiens, temporarily ended hostilities between the United Kingdom and the French Republic during the French Revolutionary Wars. Negotiated for Britain by Lord Cornwallis and for France by Joseph Bonaparte, it produced the only period of general peace in Europe between 1793 and 1814 — a truce that lasted barely fourteen months before the Napoleonic Wars began.

Terms

Britain agreed to return most of the French, Spanish and Dutch colonies it had captured, keeping only Trinidad (from Spain) and Ceylon (from the Dutch). France was to evacuate Naples and the Papal States; Egypt was restored to the Ottoman Empire; Malta was to be returned to the Knights of St John and guaranteed neutral; and Britain recognised the French-sponsored republics in Italy and elsewhere. In Britain the peace was initially popular — taxes could fall and tourists flocked to Paris — but it quickly soured as Napoleon continued to intervene in Switzerland, Italy and Germany.

Breakdown

Each side accused the other of bad faith: Britain refused to evacuate Malta on schedule, citing French expansion, while France protested British press attacks and the harbouring of émigré plotters. In May 1803 Britain declared war, beginning the Napoleonic Wars proper. Historians debate whether Napoleon ever intended a lasting peace or merely a breathing space; in any case, Amiens remains a byword for a fragile, short-lived settlement.
