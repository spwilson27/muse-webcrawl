Synthetic language

Synthetic language
FieldLinguistic typology, morphology
ContrastAnalytic language
SubtypesFusional, agglutinative, polysynthetic

A synthetic language is a language that uses inflections and other bound morphemes to express syntactic relationships within a sentence. Synthetic languages combine (synthesise) multiple concepts into single words — for example, encoding tense, person, number, and case within a verb or noun form rather than with separate particles and word order, as analytic languages do. The contrast, introduced into typology by the Schlegel brothers and Wilhelm von Humboldt, is a matter of degree: most languages fall on a spectrum.

Types

  • Fusional (inflecting) languages fuse several grammatical categories into single, irregular affixes: Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Russian, and Arabic. A single ending may simultaneously encode case, number, and gender.
  • Agglutinative languages string together distinct affixes, each carrying one function: Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, Japanese, and Sumerian.
  • Polysynthetic languages build very long words incorporating noun objects into verbs, as in Inuktitut, Mohawk, and many languages of the Americas and Siberia; a single word may translate an entire English sentence.

Measures

Typologists quantify synthesis by the morpheme-per-word ratio (index of synthesis) and the fusion of categories (index of fusion). At the analytic extreme sit languages such as Vietnamese and Mandarin Chinese, and — in many respects — modern English, which historically shed most of Old English's inflections.
