Halley's Comet

Halley's Comet (1P/Halley) is a short-period comet visible from Earth every 75–76 years.

Halley's Comet, officially designated 1P/Halley, is a short-period comet visible from Earth every 75–76 years. Halley is the only known short-period comet that is regularly visible to the naked eye from Earth, and the only naked-eye comet that can appear twice in one human lifetime. It last appeared in the inner parts of the Solar System in 1986 and will next appear in mid-2061.

Halley's returns to the inner Solar System have been observed and recorded by astronomers since at least 240 BC. Clear records of the comet's appearances were made by Chinese, Babylonian, and medieval European chroniclers, but it was not until 1705 that English astronomer Edmond Halley recognised these appearances as reappearances of the same comet. The comet is named after him.

Orbit and structure

Halley has a highly elliptical, retrograde orbit with a period of about 75–76 years, a perihelion of 0.586 AU (inside the orbit of Venus), and an aphelion of about 35 AU, beyond the orbit of Neptune. In 1986 the European Space Agency's Giotto probe and the Soviet Vega probes flew by the comet — the first close-up observations of a cometary nucleus. They showed a dark, peanut-shaped nucleus roughly 15 km long and 8 km wide, covered in a crust of dark material with jets of gas and dust escaping from vents on the sunlit side. The nucleus has an albedo of about 0.04, making it one of the darkest objects in the Solar System; its surface consists largely of water ice mixed with carbonaceous material.

Historical apparitions

The 1066 apparition was depicted in the Bayeux Tapestry and interpreted as an omen of the death of Harold II at the Battle of Hastings. The 1301 apparition may have inspired the Star of Bethlehem in Giotto's fresco Adoration of the Magi — one reason the probe of 1986 bore the artist's name. The 1910 apparition caused public alarm when spectroscopy revealed cyanogen gas in the tail through which Earth was predicted to pass; the event coincided with the death of Mark Twain, who was born with the comet in 1835 and died the day after its 1910 perihelion, as he had predicted. The 1986 apparition was unfavourably placed for Northern Hemisphere observers but was extensively studied by spacecraft and ground-based campaigns.

Meteor showers

Debris shed by Halley produces two annual meteor showers: the Eta Aquariids in May and the Orionids in October, when Earth passes through the stream of particles along the comet's orbit.
