Human sacrifice in Aztec culture
Culture: Mexica (Aztec), Central Mexico
Period: c. 1300s–1521
Principal sites: Templo Mayor, Tenochtitlan
Methods: Heart extraction, decapitation, gladiatorial sacrifice, child sacrifice to Tlaloc
Evidence: Codices, Spanish accounts, tzompantli excavations

Human sacrifice was a central religious practice in Aztec (Mexica) culture, performed on a scale unmatched elsewhere in Mesoamerica. Victims — most often war captives, but also slaves and, for the rain god Tlaloc, children — were killed in temple rituals understood by the Mexica as necessary repayment of a debt to the gods, nourishing the sun so that the world would not end.

Cosmology and ritual

In Mexica cosmology the gods had sacrificed themselves to set the fifth and present sun in motion; human blood and hearts (the precious chalchiuatl) sustained it in turn. The most common rite, performed atop pyramids such as the Templo Mayor, was heart extraction: priests stretched the victim over a sacrificial stone, opened the chest with an obsidian or flint knife, and offered the heart to the sun. Other rites included gladiatorial sacrifice, in which a captive fought tethered against fully armed warriors; sacrifice by arrows; and the flaying rites of Xipe Totec. Skulls of victims were displayed on a rack, the tzompantli.

Scale and debate

Spanish chroniclers reported enormous numbers — notably the claim of 80,400 victims at the 1487 rededication of the Templo Mayor — which many modern scholars consider exaggerated, though excavations by Mexico's Templo Mayor Project and the discovery of the Huey Tzompantli tower of hundreds of skulls since 2015 have confirmed the practice's substantial scale. Historians debate the relative weight of religious, political (terror and state display) and other explanations. Aztec "flower wars" (xochiyaoyotl) were arranged battles partly intended to supply captives.
