HIV prevention

HIV prevention refers to the set of biomedical, behavioural, and structural practices used to reduce the risk of transmission of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), the virus that causes AIDS. Because no cure or licensed vaccine exists, prevention remains the central tool for controlling the HIV epidemic, which has caused tens of millions of deaths since the early 1980s.

Sexual transmission

Consistent and correct use of condoms is highly effective at preventing sexual transmission. Other key strategies include:

  • Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP): antiretroviral medication (e.g. tenofovir/emtricitabine, or long-acting injectable cabotegravir) taken by HIV-negative people at substantial risk, reducing the risk of acquiring HIV through sex by about 99% when taken as prescribed.
  • Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP): a 28-day course of antiretrovirals begun within 72 hours after a possible exposure.
  • Treatment as prevention (TasP) / U=U: people living with HIV who take antiretroviral therapy and maintain an undetectable viral load do not sexually transmit the virus ("Undetectable = Untransmittable").
  • Voluntary medical male circumcision, which reduces female-to-male transmission risk by roughly 60%, and regular testing and treatment of other sexually transmitted infections.

Blood-borne and injecting transmission

Needle and syringe programmes, opioid substitution therapy, never sharing injection equipment, screening of blood and organ donations, and universal precautions in health care have dramatically reduced transmission through blood. Harm-reduction services are a central part of national HIV strategies.

Mother-to-child transmission

Without intervention, HIV can pass from mother to child during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding in 15–45% of cases. Antiretroviral therapy for the mother and prophylaxis for the infant reduce this risk to below 1–2%.

Public health context

Global programmes led by UNAIDS, the WHO, PEPFAR, and the Global Fund combine these tools with HIV testing, education, condom distribution, and efforts to reduce stigma. New HIV infections worldwide have fallen substantially since their peak in the mid-1990s, though roughly 1.3 million new infections still occurred annually in the early 2020s. Research into an HIV vaccine and broadly neutralising antibodies continues.
