The Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, also known as the 1951 Refugee Convention or the Geneva Convention of 28 July 1951, is a United Nations multilateral treaty that defines who a refugee is and sets out the rights of individuals who are granted asylum and the responsibilities of nations that grant asylum.

The Convention was adopted by a United Nations conference in Geneva in July 1951 and entered into force on 22 April 1954. Initially limited to protecting refugees from events occurring before 1 January 1951, chiefly in Europe, its scope was extended worldwide and without temporal limit by the 1967 Protocol.

Definition of refugee

Article 1 defines a refugee as a person who, "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion, is outside the country of his nationality and is unable or, owing to such fear, is unwilling to avail himself of the protection of that country." Exclusion clauses deny protection to those responsible for serious crimes, including war crimes.

Rights and obligations

The Convention guarantees refugees treatment at least as favourable as nationals in matters such as religion, access to courts, and elementary education, and provides for identity papers, travel documents, and protection against penalties for illegal entry. Its cornerstone is Article 33's principle of non-refoulement: no state shall expel or return a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on a Convention ground. The principle is widely regarded as customary international law, binding even non-parties.

Supervision and criticism

The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) supervises the Convention's application. Critics note that the treaty was designed for individual persecution rather than mass displacement by war or climate change, that procedures vary widely between states, and that burden-sharing remains uneven — debates central to contemporary asylum politics.
