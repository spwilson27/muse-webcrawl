Canadian Red Ensign

The Canadian Red Ensign (French: Red Ensign canadien) is a defaced British Red Ensign that served as the unofficial flag of Canada from the late 19th century and as its civil ensign and de facto national flag until 1965, when it was replaced by the current maple leaf flag.

Design

The flag consists of a red field with the Union Jack in the canton and the shield from the coat of arms of Canada in the fly. The shield combined the arms of the founding provinces; its composition changed as new provinces joined and as the arms of Canada were redesigned in 1921.

History

Unofficial versions of a Canadian red ensign appeared as early as 1868, bearing the arms of Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick. In 1892 the British Admiralty authorized the flag for use at sea by Canadian merchant vessels. Although Canada never formally adopted it as the national flag, it flew over Parliament and was carried by Canadian troops in both world wars. Between 1945 and 1965 it was authorized for official use on federal buildings.

Replacement

Prime Minister Lester B. Pearson's government sought a distinct national flag, provoking the Great Canadian Flag Debate of 1964. The red-and-white maple leaf design by George Stanley was adopted, and the Red Ensign was lowered on Parliament Hill on February 15, 1965.

Later use

The Red Ensign remains a symbol for some Canadians attached to the country's British heritage and military history. Modified versions survive as the provincial flags of Ontario and Manitoba, both adopted in 1965–66. The flag is occasionally displayed by veterans' groups, and occasionally also by controversial political movements, a usage criticized by historians and veterans' organizations.
