Intravenous immunoglobulin

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is a therapy consisting of pooled immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies extracted from the plasma of thousands of blood donors, administered intravenously. It was first used in 1952 for immune deficiency and remains standard replacement therapy for primary immunodeficiencies such as common variable immunodeficiency and X-linked agammaglobulinemia.

Uses

Beyond replacement, high-dose IVIG is used as an immunomodulatory treatment in Kawasaki disease, immune thrombocytopenic purpura, Guillain–Barré syndrome, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, and various other autoimmune conditions. Its mechanisms are multiple and incompletely understood, involving Fc receptor blockade, anti-idiotypic antibodies, and cytokine modulation. Adverse effects are usually mild infusion reactions, though rare renal failure, thrombosis, and anaphylaxis occur. IVIG is on the WHO List of Essential Medicines.
