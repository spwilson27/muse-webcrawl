Information asymmetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Information asymmetry deals with the study of decisions in transactions where one party has more or better information than the other. This asymmetry creates an imbalance of power in transactions, which can sometimes cause markets to fail. The problem was formalised in George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons'", and the field earned Akerlof, Michael Spence, and Joseph Stiglitz the 2001 Nobel Prize in Economics.

Adverse selection

Adverse selection occurs when hidden information before a transaction drives good risks or products out of the market. In Akerlof's model, sellers of used cars know their quality while buyers do not; buyers offer an average price, owners of good cars withdraw, and the market can collapse toward only "lemons" being traded. In insurance, people who know they are high-risk are most eager to buy cover.

Moral hazard

Moral hazard arises after a transaction, when one party changes behaviour because the other bears the cost — for example, insured drivers taking more risks, or employees shirking when effort is unobservable. It is the mirror problem of the principal–agent relationship.

Solutions

Markets and governments respond with signalling (Spence's model of education as a signal of ability), screening (menus of contracts that induce self-selection), warranties, reputation, brand names, certification, disclosure rules, and mandatory insurance. These mechanisms reduce but rarely eliminate the asymmetry.
