Shareholder rights plan

How a poison pill dilutes a hostile bidder's stake

A shareholder rights plan, known colloquially as a poison pill, is a type of defensive tactic used by a corporation's board of directors against a hostile takeover. It was invented by the corporate lawyer Martin Lipton of Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz in 1982, at the height of the era of hostile tender offers and corporate raiders. In the typical plan, the board issues rights (options) to existing shareholders that become exercisable — at a deep discount — if an acquirer crosses a threshold ownership stake (often 10–20%). The resulting mass dilution makes the target prohibitively expensive for the bidder, unless the board redeems the rights, typically for a nominal sum.

Mechanism

Most modern plans use a "flip-in" structure: if any shareholder acquires more than the trigger percentage without board approval, all other shareholders may buy additional shares at half price, flooding the market and diluting the acquirer's position (the acquirer's own rights become void). A "flip-over" provision allows target shareholders, after a merger, to buy the acquirer's shares at a discount. Because the board can redeem the pill at any time, the pill does not make a takeover impossible; it forces the bidder to negotiate with the board or win a proxy fight to replace it.

Legal history

Delaware courts upheld the poison pill in Moran v. Household International, Inc. (1985), and the related Unocal standard requires defensive measures to be reasonable in relation to the threat posed. In Unitrin v. American General the court added that defences must not be "draconian" — neither coercive nor preclusive. The 2011 Airgas decision confirmed that a board acting in good faith may keep a pill in place even against a fully financed, all-cash offer, effectively endorsing the "just say no" defence. Related doctrines include the Revlon duties that arise once a sale of the company becomes inevitable.

Variants and modern use

Variants include dead-hand pills (redeemable only by incumbent directors — struck down in Delaware), "chewable" pills that fall away for qualifying all-cash offers, and tax-benefit preservation plans that protect net operating loss carryforwards with low (under 5%) triggers. Shareholder activists and proxy advisers such as ISS generally oppose long-lived pills adopted without a shareholder vote, so most modern plans are adopted for a limited term or put to ratification. High-profile uses include Netflix's 2012 pill against Carl Icahn, the pill adopted by Twitter's board in April 2022 following Elon Musk's unsolicited bid (it was redeemed as part of the eventual merger agreement), and numerous pills adopted during the market volatility of 2020.

Criticism

Critics argue that pills entrench management and deprive shareholders of takeover premiums; empirical studies have associated pill adoption with lower firm value in some samples. Defenders respond that pills give boards bargaining power to extract higher prices or pursue long-term strategies, and point to evidence that targets with pills obtain higher premiums when they are eventually sold.
