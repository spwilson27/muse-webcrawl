Rigor mortis

Rigor mortis (from Latin rigor "stiffness" and mortis "of death"), or postmortem rigidity, is the fourth stage of death. It is one of the recognisable signs of death, characterised by stiffening of the limbs of the corpse caused by chemical changes in the muscles postmortem (specifically, a loss of adenosine triphosphate, ATP). In humans, rigor mortis can occur as soon as four hours after death.

Biochemistry

During life, ATP is required to detach myosin cross-bridges from actin filaments so muscles can relax. After death, respiration and ATP production cease. As the remaining ATP is depleted, the actin and myosin filaments become permanently linked, and the muscles remain contracted and stiff until the muscle proteins themselves begin to decompose through autolysis and putrefaction.

Timeline

Under normal conditions, rigor begins a few hours after death, is complete within roughly 6–12 hours, persists for a period commonly around 18–36 hours, and then passes as decomposition proceeds. The timing is highly variable and depends on ambient temperature, the deceased's physical condition and activity before death, and other factors. Because of this variability, rigor mortis is only a rough guide when estimating the time of death in forensic investigations.

Other uses

In the meat industry, rigor mortis is an important stage in the conversion of muscle to meat; chilling carcasses too rapidly during rigor can cause "cold shortening" and tougher meat.
