Microbiome

A microbiome is the community of microorganisms — bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and other microscopic life — that inhabit a particular environment, together with their genomes and the surrounding conditions. Microbiomes are found in soil, oceans, plants, and the bodies of animals, including the human body, where the gut microbiome in particular has profound effects on health.

Human microbiome

The human body hosts trillions of microbes, concentrated in the gut but also present on the skin, in the mouth, and elsewhere. The gut microbiome aids digestion, synthesizes vitamins, trains the immune system and produces metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids. Its composition is shaped by diet, birth mode, antibiotics and environment. The NIH Human Microbiome Project (2007–2016) mapped the microbial communities of healthy adults.

Dysbiosis and disease

Disturbances of the microbiome, called dysbiosis, have been associated with inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, diabetes, allergies, and possibly neurological conditions. Fecal microbiota transplantation is an established treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection, and probiotics, prebiotics and live biotherapeutics are active areas of research.

Environmental microbiomes

Soil microbiomes drive nutrient cycling and plant growth, while ocean microbiomes — including the photosynthetic cyanobacterium Prochlorococcus — produce much of Earth's oxygen and underpin marine food webs. The plant rhizosphere microbiome influences crop health and is a target of sustainable agriculture research.

Study methods

Microbiomes are studied with marker-gene sequencing (such as 16S rRNA), shotgun metagenomics, metatranscriptomics and culturomics, which together reveal who is present, what genes they carry, and what they are doing.
