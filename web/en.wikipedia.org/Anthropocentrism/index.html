Anthropocentrism

Anthropocentrism is the belief that human beings are the central or most important entity on the planet. The term can be used interchangeably with humanocentrism, and some refer to the concept as human supremacy or human exceptionalism. From an anthropocentric perspective, humankind is seen as separate from nature and superior to it, and other entities such as animals, plants, and mineral resources are perceived as resources for humans to use.

Background

Anthropocentrism has roots in the Judeo-Christian tradition, notably the account in the Book of Genesis in which humans are given dominion over the Earth, and in classical philosophy, where Aristotle placed humans at the top of the scala naturae, or great chain of being. The idea was later reinforced by thinkers such as René Descartes, who regarded animals as automata lacking minds.

Environmental philosophy

In environmental philosophy, anthropocentrism is often contrasted with ecocentrism and biocentrism, which hold that nature has intrinsic value independent of human interests. Critics such as Lynn White Jr., in his 1967 essay "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis", argued that anthropocentric attitudes contributed to environmental degradation. Defenders distinguish between strong anthropocentrism and weaker, enlightened forms that support conservation because a healthy environment serves human well-being.

Animal rights

Philosophers including Peter Singer and Tom Regan have criticized anthropocentrism as a form of speciesism, arguing that the interests of non-human animals deserve moral consideration. The concept is also debated in discussions of artificial intelligence, astrobiology, and the ethics of space exploration.
