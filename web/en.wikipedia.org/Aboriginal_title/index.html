Aboriginal title

Also called: Indigenous title, native title

Jurisdictions: Australia, Canada, New Zealand, United States and other common-law countries

Aboriginal title is a common law doctrine that recognises the land rights of Indigenous peoples to their traditional territories. Under the doctrine, the acquisition of sovereignty by a colonial power did not by itself extinguish the pre-existing rights of Indigenous inhabitants, whose occupation of the land is treated as a burden on the Crown's radical (underlying) title.

Principles

Aboriginal title arises from Indigenous peoples' own laws and customs and their occupation of land from before colonisation, rather than from any grant by the state. It is generally inalienable except to the Crown, and it may be extinguished only by a clear and plain intention of the sovereign — historically through treaties, purchase or legislation. The content of the title ranges from rights to use land for traditional practices to, in the strongest cases such as Canada's Tsilhqot'in Nation decision (2014), a right to decide how the land is used and to enjoy its economic benefits.

By jurisdiction

In the United States, the Marshall Court decisions of the 1820s–1830s grounded federal Indian law in the doctrine of discovery. In Australia the doctrine took the name "native title" and was recognised in the Mabo decision of 1992. Canada recognises Aboriginal title under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982, and New Zealand law addresses Māori customary title through the courts and treaty settlements.
