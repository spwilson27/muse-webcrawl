Cosmic horror

Cosmic horror (also called Lovecraftian horror) is a subgenre of horror fiction and weird fiction that emphasises the horror of the unknown and incomprehensible rather than gore or shock. Its central theme — termed "cosmicism" — is that human beings are insignificant in the wider universe, that human interests mean nothing to the cosmos, and that contact with the true nature of reality would be psychologically devastating. It is most closely associated with the American writer H. P. Lovecraft (1890–1937).

Themes and motifs

Common features include ancient, godlike entities (Lovecraft's "Old Ones," such as Cthulhu); forbidden knowledge that drives investigators mad; the fragility of sanity when confronted with truths beyond human comprehension; non-Euclidean geometry and alien landscapes; and humanity's helplessness — protagonists rarely triumph, and survival usually comes through ignorance or flight. Lovecraft summarised his philosophy in the opening of "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928): "The most merciful thing in the world, I think, is the inability of the human mind to correlate all its contents."

Antecedents and successors

Precursors include Algernon Blackwood, Arthur Machen, Lord Dunsany, and Robert W. Chambers's The King in Yellow (1895). Lovecraft's contemporaries and correspondents — Clark Ashton Smith, Robert E. Howard, August Derleth — expanded the shared "Cthulhu Mythos," and later writers including Ramsey Campbell, Thomas Ligotti, Caitlín R. Kiernan and Victor LaValle have reworked the mode, sometimes explicitly critiquing Lovecraft's racism. Cosmic horror has strongly influenced film (Alien, The Thing, Annihilation), video games (Bloodborne, Dead Space) and tabletop role-playing (Call of Cthulhu).
