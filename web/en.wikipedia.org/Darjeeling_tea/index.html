Darjeeling tea is a tea made from the leaves of Camellia sinensis grown and processed in the Darjeeling district of West Bengal, India. Traditionally manufactured as a black tea, it is prized for its light colour and distinctive muscatel aroma, and is often called "the Champagne of teas".

History

Tea planting in Darjeeling began in the 1840s, when Dr Arthur Campbell, a civil surgeon of the British East India Company, experimented with seeds obtained from China. Commercial gardens followed from the 1850s, and by the early twentieth century Darjeeling teas commanded premium prices in London auctions.

Flushes

Production is divided into seasonal harvests called flushes. The first flush (spring) is light, floral, and highly sought after; the second flush (early summer) develops the famous muscatel character; monsoon and autumnal flushes produce fuller, less delicate teas. Gardens at high altitude, cool nights, and mist contribute to the flavour.

Protection and counterfeiting

Far more tea is sold worldwide as "Darjeeling" than the district produces — around 10 million kg annually — so the Tea Board of India administers a certification scheme and logo. In 2004 Darjeeling tea became the first Indian product to receive a Geographical Indication (GI) tag, and it is a protected geographical indication in the European Union.

Industry

Some 87 tea gardens operate in the district, employing tens of thousands of workers, many of them Nepali-speaking Gorkhas. Labour conditions, wages, and land rights have been recurring issues, and the industry is affected by climate change, ageing bushes, and competition from Nepal-grown teas of similar character.
