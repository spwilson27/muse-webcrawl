Parkinsonism

Parkinsonism is a clinical syndrome characterised by a combination of movement abnormalities: tremor at rest, rigidity, bradykinesia (slowness of movement), and postural instability. It is named after Parkinson's disease, which is by far its most common cause, but the identical syndrome can result from a number of other conditions and from various drugs and toxins.

Causes

Besides Parkinson's disease itself, parkinsonism occurs in the "atypical parkinsonian" or "Parkinson-plus" disorders: multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, and dementia with Lewy bodies. These tend to respond poorly to levodopa and have a worse prognosis. Secondary parkinsonism can be drug-induced — most notably by antipsychotic dopamine-blocking drugs — or caused by vascular disease (vascular parkinsonism), head injury, encephalitis, hydrocephalus, or toxins such as MPTP, whose accidental use by drug users in 1982 famously produced sudden, severe parkinsonism and advanced understanding of the disease.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by brain imaging when an atypical or secondary cause is suspected. A good and sustained response to levodopa supports a diagnosis of Parkinson's disease. Drug-induced parkinsonism is managed by withdrawing or changing the offending drug. Distinguishing the causes matters because treatment, course, and prognosis differ markedly between Parkinson's disease and the other parkinsonian syndromes.
