Waqf

A mosque complex sustained for centuries by waqf endowment revenues.

A waqf (Arabic: وَقْف; plural أَوْقَاف awqāf), also known in North Africa as a habous, is an inalienable charitable endowment under Islamic law. It consists of property — typically land or buildings, but also cash — permanently dedicated by a founder (waqif) for religious or charitable purposes, with the revenue supporting the designated beneficiaries in perpetuity.

Mechanics

Once a waqf is declared, the property is considered to belong to God and cannot be sold, inherited, or given away. A trustee (mutawalli or nazir) administers the endowment according to the founder's deed (waqfiyya). Jurists distinguish the public or charitable waqf (waqf khayri), benefiting mosques, schools, hospitals, fountains, roads, and the poor, from the family waqf (waqf ahli), whose income supports the founder's descendants, with any ultimate remainder passing to charity.

Historical role

From the early centuries of Islam, waqfs financed much of the public infrastructure of Muslim societies: the great mosque-madrasa complexes of Cairo, Damascus, and Istanbul, caravanserais, soup kitchens, libraries, and water systems. By the Ottoman period, waqf revenues underpinned a large share of urban economic life, and women were prominent founders. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, many states brought awqaf under ministries of religious endowments, and colonial and nationalising regimes confiscated or restricted them; a modern revival has made waqf funds a growing instrument of Islamic finance and philanthropy.
