In computer science, a reduced instruction set computer (RISC) is a computer architecture designed to simplify the individual instructions given to the computer to accomplish tasks. Compared to a complex instruction set computer (CISC), a RISC computer might require more instructions to accomplish a task because the individual instructions perform simpler operations, but the simpler instructions can be executed faster, often in a single clock cycle, and lend themselves to pipelining.

History

The philosophy emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s from projects such as John Cocke's IBM 801 minicomputer, the Berkeley RISC project led by David Patterson, and the Stanford MIPS project led by John Hennessy. Studies showed that most programs used only a small subset of the instructions provided by then-current processors, and that complex instructions were often slower than sequences of simple ones. Patterson coined the term RISC, in contrast to CISC.

Design principles

Typical characteristics include a uniform instruction format, a large number of general-purpose registers, a load–store architecture in which only load and store instructions access memory, simple addressing modes, and an emphasis on optimizing the compiler-visible instruction set. Later RISC designs added superscalar execution, out-of-order execution, and other performance features while keeping the instruction set streamlined.

Impact

RISC architectures achieved early success in workstations (SPARC, MIPS, PA-RISC, Power) and later dominated embedded and mobile computing through the ARM architecture, which powers nearly all smartphones. Apple transitioned its Mac computers to ARM-based Apple silicon in 2020. The open RISC-V instruction set, originating at UC Berkeley in 2010, has seen rapidly growing adoption in academia and industry.
