Neuroendocrine tumor
SpecialtyOncology, endocrinology
Common sitesGastrointestinal tract, pancreas, lungs
SymptomsOften none; flushing, diarrhoea, wheezing in functional tumours
MarkersChromogranin A, synaptophysin

Neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) are neoplasms that arise from cells of the endocrine and nervous systems. They occur most commonly in the intestine (where they were historically called carcinoid tumors), the pancreas, and the lungs, but can develop throughout the body. Many NETs produce hormones or vasoactive substances, causing distinctive clinical syndromes.

Classification

The World Health Organization classifies neuroendocrine neoplasms by differentiation and grade. Well-differentiated NETs are graded 1 to 3 according to mitotic count and Ki-67 proliferation index; poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs), including small-cell types, are high grade by definition. Diagnosis relies on morphology plus immunohistochemical staining for chromogranin A and synaptophysin.

Symptoms

Non-functioning tumors may grow silently or cause pain, obstruction, or bleeding, and are often found incidentally or after metastasis to the liver. Functioning tumors secrete substances such as serotonin, causing carcinoid syndrome — episodic flushing, diarrhoea, wheezing, and eventually right-sided heart valve disease. Pancreatic NETs include insulinomas (causing hypoglycaemia), gastrinomas (Zollinger–Ellison syndrome), and glucagonomas.

Diagnosis and treatment

Imaging includes CT, MRI, and somatostatin-receptor PET scanning (such as Ga-68 DOTATATE), since most well-differentiated NETs express somatostatin receptors. Treatment centres on surgery for localised disease; advanced disease may be managed with somatostatin analogues, targeted agents such as everolimus and sunitinib, peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT), chemotherapy, and liver-directed therapies. Prognosis varies widely with site, grade, and stage; many low-grade NETs progress slowly over years.
