Erythropoiesis (from Greek erythro-, "red", and -poiesis, "making") is the process by which red blood cells (erythrocytes) are produced. In the adult human it takes place in the red bone marrow and replaces the roughly 1% of circulating red cells lost each day — about 200 billion new cells daily. Erythropoiesis is the erythroid branch of haematopoiesis, the formation of all blood cells from haematopoietic stem cells.

Stages

A committed erythroid progenitor (passing through burst-forming and colony-forming unit stages, BFU-E and CFU-E) gives rise to a recognisable sequence of precursors: proerythroblast, early (basophilic), intermediate (polychromatic), and late (orthochromatic) erythroblasts. As development proceeds the cell shrinks, accumulates haemoglobin, condenses its nucleus, and finally expels it to become a reticulocyte. Reticulocytes leave the marrow, circulate for a day or two while losing residual RNA and organelles, and mature into biconcave erythrocytes that live about 120 days.

Regulation

The dominant regulator is the hormone erythropoietin (EPO), produced mainly by the kidneys in response to low tissue oxygen, sensed through the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway. EPO drives progenitor survival and proliferation; this is why kidney failure causes anaemia and why recombinant EPO is used to treat it — and illicitly as a blood-doping agent in sport. Effective erythropoiesis also requires iron, vitamin B12, and folate; deficiency of any of these produces characteristic anaemias. In the fetus, red cells are made first in the yolk sac, then the liver and spleen, before the marrow takes over.

Clinical significance

Disorders of erythropoiesis include the anaemias (underproduction, or production of defective cells, as in thalassaemia and sideroblastic anaemia) and polycythaemia (overproduction, as in polycythaemia vera or in response to chronic hypoxia at altitude). Reticulocyte counts in the blood are a standard clinical measure of how actively the marrow is producing red cells.
