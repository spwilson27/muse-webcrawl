Uvea

Uvea
DetailsMiddle layer of the eye
PartsIris, ciliary body, choroid
SystemVisual system

The uvea, also called the uveal layer or uveal tract, is the pigmented middle layer of the three concentric layers that make up the eye. It consists of the iris, the ciliary body and the choroid, and lies between the outer sclera and the inner retina.

Structure

The iris is the coloured diaphragm that regulates the diameter of the pupil and hence the amount of light reaching the retina. The ciliary body produces aqueous humour and, through the zonules of Zinn, adjusts the shape of the lens for accommodation. The choroid is a vascular, heavily pigmented layer that supplies the outer retina and absorbs stray light.

Function

Together the uveal structures control light entry, focus, nutrition of the outer retina and the optical quality of the image. The dark melanin pigmentation of the uvea prevents internal reflections within the eye.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the uvea, called uveitis, may be anterior (iritis), intermediate, posterior or panuveitis, and is associated with infections and autoimmune diseases. Uveal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy in adults, arising most often in the choroid.
