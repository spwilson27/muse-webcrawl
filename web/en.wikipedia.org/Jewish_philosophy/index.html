Jewish philosophy

Jewish philosophy refers to the conjunction of serious study of philosophy and Jewish theology. It includes the philosophical reflection of Jewish thinkers from antiquity to the present, often engaging with Greek, Islamic, Christian, and modern European philosophy while interpreting the Hebrew Bible and rabbinic tradition.

Ancient and medieval philosophy

Philo of Alexandria (1st century CE) first systematically combined Jewish scripture with Greek philosophy. In the medieval Islamic world, Saadia Gaon's Book of Beliefs and Opinions applied rationalist kalam to Judaism. Solomon ibn Gabirol, Bahya ibn Paquda, and Judah Halevi — whose Kuzari defended revealed religion against philosophy — were major figures. Moses Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed (1190) sought to reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with Judaism and became the most influential work of medieval Jewish philosophy.

Modern philosophy

Baruch Spinoza, though excommunicated, profoundly shaped modern thought. Moses Mendelssohn led the Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah). In the 20th century, Martin Buber (I and Thou), Franz Rosenzweig (The Star of Redemption), Emmanuel Levinas, and Abraham Joshua Heschel developed dialogical, existential, and ethical philosophies. Hermann Cohen's neo-Kantian system was also influential.

Themes

Recurring themes include the nature of God, creation, revelation, free will and providence, the problem of evil, the meaning of the commandments (mitzvot), messianism, and the relationship between Athens and Jerusalem — reason and faith.
