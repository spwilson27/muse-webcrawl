Stellar evolution

Stellar evolution is the sequence of changes a star undergoes during its lifetime, from its birth in a molecular cloud to its final state as a compact remnant. A star's mass at birth is the dominant factor in its evolution: it sets the star's luminosity, lifetime, internal structure and the manner of its death.

Main sequence

After formation, a star settles onto the main sequence, fusing hydrogen into helium in its core. Low-mass stars burn their fuel slowly — red dwarfs may shine for trillions of years — while massive stars, despite having more fuel, consume it so rapidly that they live only a few million years.

Post-main-sequence: low and intermediate mass

When core hydrogen runs out, the core contracts and heats, igniting hydrogen in a surrounding shell; the envelope expands and cools, producing a red giant. Helium fusion then begins — explosively, in a helium flash, for Sun-like stars. After further stages on the asymptotic giant branch, the star sheds its envelope as a planetary nebula, leaving a carbon–oxygen white dwarf that cools indefinitely.

Massive stars

Stars above about eight solar masses fuse successively heavier elements — carbon, neon, oxygen, silicon — building an onion-like interior ending in an inert iron core. Because iron fusion consumes rather than releases energy, the core collapses catastrophically, triggering a core-collapse supernova that disperses newly forged elements into space and leaves a neutron star or, for the most massive stars, a black hole.

Observational basis

Because individual stars evolve too slowly to watch directly, the theory is tested against populations: star clusters, whose members share an age but span a range of masses, trace out the predicted sequences on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram.
