Regenerative cooling (rocket)

Regenerative cooling is a method of cooling liquid-propellant rocket engines in which some or all of the propellant is passed through channels milled into the walls of the combustion chamber and nozzle before being injected into the chamber and burned. The propellant absorbs heat from the walls, keeping them below their melting point, and the heat is not wasted because the warmed propellant carries the energy back into combustion — hence "regenerative".

Principle

Combustion gases in a rocket chamber can exceed 3,000 °C, far above the melting point of the chamber materials. In a regeneratively cooled engine, the fuel (and less commonly the oxidiser) is pumped at high pressure through hundreds of small channels surrounding the chamber and nozzle throat, where heat flux is highest. The coolant typically flows from the nozzle exit upward (counter-flow) so the coldest propellant meets the hottest region at the throat.

History and use

The concept was proposed by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and first used practically in the 1930s–40s. It is used in most large liquid rocket engines, including the RS-25 (Space Shuttle main engine), the Vulcain of Ariane, and SpaceX's Merlin and Raptor engines, the latter using both methane fuel and oxygen in cooling circuits. Challenges include coking of hydrocarbon fuels (such as RP-1 kerosene) in the channels, which limits how much heat they can absorb, and the very high pump pressures required.

Related techniques

Regenerative cooling is often combined with film cooling, ablative liners in smaller engines, or dump cooling in expander-cycle engines.
