Chronic myeloid leukemia

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), also called chronic myelogenous leukemia, is a myeloproliferative neoplasm in which the bone marrow produces too many granulocytic white blood cells. It accounts for roughly 15 percent of adult leukemias in the United States, with about 9,000 new cases a year and a median age at diagnosis in the mid-sixties. CML is uniquely associated with the Philadelphia chromosome and its BCR::ABL1 fusion gene, the first consistent genetic abnormality identified in any cancer.

History

Cases were described in the 1840s by John Hughes Bennett and Rudolf Virchow, who coined the term leukemia ("white blood"). In 1960 Peter Nowell and David Hungerford in Philadelphia found the shortened chromosome 22 that bears the disease's signature; in 1973 Janet Rowley showed it arises from a reciprocal translocation with chromosome 9. Identification of the BCR::ABL1 fusion in the 1980s and of its kinase activity led directly to the targeted drug imatinib, developed by Brian Druker and colleagues and approved by the FDA in 2001 — a landmark in precision medicine.

Clinical course

Untreated, CML evolves from a chronic phase, in which patients may feel well for years, through an accelerated phase to blast crisis, an acute leukemia that is usually fatal. Treatment response is measured hematologically (normalization of blood counts), cytogenetically (disappearance of Philadelphia-positive cells), and molecularly (falling BCR::ABL1 transcript levels on the International Scale). Milestones at 3, 6, and 12 months guide whether to continue or switch a tyrosine kinase inhibitor.

Epidemiology

CML occurs worldwide at an incidence of about 1–2 cases per 100,000 people per year, slightly more often in men. Ionizing radiation is the only established environmental risk factor; there is no strong hereditary component. Because patients now live for decades, the prevalence of CML has risen steadily since the introduction of TKIs.
