Petri dish

A Petri dish with bacterial colonies growing on agar.

A Petri dish (alternatively known as a Petri plate or cell-culture dish) is a shallow transparent lidded dish that biologists use to hold growth medium in which cells can be cultured, originally, cells of bacteria, fungi and small mosses. The container is named after its inventor, German bacteriologist Julius Richard Petri, who invented it in 1887 while working as an assistant to Robert Koch.

Petri dishes are usually cylindrical, mostly with diameters ranging from 30 to 200 millimetres (1.2 to 7.9 in), and a height ratio of roughly 1:10. Glass versions can be reused after sterilization (via an autoclave or one hour of dry heating in a hot air oven at 160 °C, for example); plastic versions are often used for sterile disposable use.

History

Petri published his invention in 1887 in a paper titled "Eine kleine Modification des Koch'schen Plattenverfahrens" ("A small modification of Koch's plate method"). Before Petri's dish, Koch's laboratory cultured bacteria on flat glass plates covered by bell jars, which were prone to contamination. Petri's design — a slightly larger inverted dish serving as a lid — dramatically reduced contamination while allowing gas exchange, and became standard laboratory equipment within a few years.

Microbiology

Petri dishes are often used to make agar plates, which are used in microbiology studies. The dish is partially filled with a nutrient medium, usually agar-based, that supports the growth of microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi. After inoculation and incubation, colonies form on the surface of the medium; because each colony generally arises from a single cell or spore, plates are essential for isolating pure cultures and for counting viable organisms (colony-forming units). Agar plates are also used in antibiotic sensitivity testing, such as the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion test.

Other uses

Petri dishes are used for cell culture of animal and plant cells, for observing small organisms, for evaporation and drying, for storing small samples, and in entomology and chemistry. Large glass Petri dishes are sometimes used as simple observation containers in schools and laboratories.
