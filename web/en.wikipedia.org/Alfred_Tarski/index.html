Alfred Tarski (1901–1983)

Alfred Tarski (born Alfred Teitelbaum; 14 January 1901 – 26 October 1983) was a Polish-American logician and mathematician. A prolific author best known for his work on model theory, metamathematics, and algebraic logic, he also contributed to abstract algebra, topology, geometry, measure theory, mathematical logic, set theory, and analytic philosophy.

Educated at the University of Warsaw, Tarski was a member of the Lwów–Warsaw school of logic. Emigrating to the United States in 1939, he taught and carried out research in mathematics at the University of California, Berkeley, from 1942 until his death.

Work on truth

Tarski's most celebrated achievement is his semantic theory of truth, set out in The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages (1933, expanded German edition 1935). He gave a rigorous definition of truth for formal languages using the notion of satisfaction in a model, and stated Convention T: a satisfactory definition must imply, for every sentence p, the equivalence "'p' is true if and only if p."

His related undefinability theorem shows that a sufficiently rich consistent formal language cannot contain its own truth predicate.

Other contributions

  • The Banach–Tarski paradox (with Stefan Banach, 1924), decomposing a ball into finitely many pieces that can be reassembled into two balls of the same size.
  • Foundations of model theory, including the Tarski–Vaught test and quantifier elimination results.
  • The decision method for elementary algebra and geometry, showing the first-order theory of the real numbers is decidable.
  • Work on relation algebras, cylindric algebras, and the Lindenbaum–Tarski algebra of sentential logic.

Legacy

Tarski is widely considered, alongside Aristotle, Gottlob Frege and Kurt Gödel, one of the greatest logicians of all time. His students at Berkeley included many leading logicians, among them Julia Robinson, Robert Vaught and C. C. Chang.
