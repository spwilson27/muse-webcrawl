Electric vehicle battery

An electric vehicle battery (EVB) is a rechargeable battery used to power the electric motors of a battery electric vehicle (BEV) or hybrid electric vehicle (HEV). Most modern EV batteries use lithium-ion chemistry, offering high energy density and long cycle life.

A typical EV battery pack contains thousands of individual cells and is the most expensive component of the vehicle.

Construction

Battery packs are assembled from cells grouped into modules. Common cell formats include cylindrical (such as 18650, 2170, and 4680), prismatic, and pouch cells. Cathode chemistries include nickel manganese cobalt (NMC), nickel cobalt aluminium (NCA), and lithium iron phosphate (LFP), the latter favoured for lower cost and greater thermal stability.

Characteristics

Pack capacities in passenger cars commonly range from about 40 kWh to over 100 kWh, giving ranges of roughly 250–600 km depending on the vehicle. Battery management systems monitor cell voltage, temperature, and state of charge to ensure safety and longevity. Degradation over time reduces capacity; modern packs typically retain 70–80% of capacity after many years of use.

Charging

EV batteries can be charged slowly from household AC power or rapidly at DC fast-charging stations, some of which can add hundreds of kilometres of range in 15–30 minutes. Charging speed is limited by cell chemistry and thermal management.

Recycling and second life

Spent EV batteries retain materials of value — lithium, nickel, cobalt, and manganese — and recycling capacity is growing. Packs no longer suitable for vehicles may be repurposed for stationary energy storage.
