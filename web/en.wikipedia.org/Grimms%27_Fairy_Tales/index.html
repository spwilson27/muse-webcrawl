Grimms' Fairy Tales, originally known as Children's and Household Tales (Kinder- und Hausmärchen), is a German collection of fairy tales by Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm, first published on 20 December 1812. The first edition contained 86 stories; by the seventh edition of 1857, the collection had grown to 200 tales and 10 children's legends. It is one of the most famous and influential collections of folklore in the world.

Contents

Famous tales in the collection include "Cinderella" (Aschenputtel), "Snow White", "Rapunzel", "Hansel and Gretel", "Rumpelstiltskin", "Sleeping Beauty" (Little Briar Rose), "The Frog Prince", "Little Red Riding Hood" and "The Bremen Town Musicians". The Grimms drew on oral informants such as Dorothea Viehmann and the Hassenpflug family, as well as older literary sources.

Method and revisions

The brothers, scholars of German language and law, initially aimed to preserve German folk tradition as part of a national cultural project. Across successive editions Wilhelm increasingly edited the tales — toning down some violence against children while retaining cruelty toward villains, removing sexual references, and adding moral and Christian elements — reshaping them for a middle-class child audience.

Legacy

The tales have been translated into more than 160 languages and adapted countless times in theatre, opera, film and animation, including Walt Disney's Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937). Scholars regard the collection as foundational to the academic study of folklore.
