Born rule

The Born rule is a key postulate of quantum mechanics that gives the probability that a measurement of a quantum system will yield a given result. In its simplest form, it states that the probability density of finding a system in a given state is proportional to the square of the magnitude of the system's wave function at that state. It is named after the German physicist Max Born, who proposed it in a paper of 1926.

Statement

If a wavefunction ψ describes a system, the probability density of measuring the particle at position x is given by

ρ(x) = |ψ(x)|2

More generally, if a system in state |ψ⟩ is measured with respect to an observable with eigenstates |φi⟩, the probability of obtaining the corresponding eigenvalue is |⟨φi|ψ⟩|2. The sum of the probabilities over all possible outcomes is one, provided the wavefunction is normalized.

History

Born introduced the rule in his 1926 paper on the collision processes of quantum mechanics, initially in a footnote as a probability interpretation of the scattering amplitude. Albert Einstein's famous remark in a 1926 letter to Born, that the Old One does not play dice, was a response to this probabilistic character of the new theory. The rule was later incorporated as one of the standard postulates of quantum mechanics in the formulations of John von Neumann.

Interpretations and derivations

Different interpretations of quantum mechanics treat the Born rule differently: in the Copenhagen interpretation it is an irreducible postulate; in the many-worlds interpretation, decision-theoretic arguments by David Deutsch and David Wallace attempt to derive it; Hugh Everett's relative-state formulation and later work by Wojciech Zurek on envariance have also aimed to derive the rule from more basic principles. Gleason's theorem shows that, for Hilbert spaces of dimension at least three, any non-contextual probability assignment to measurement outcomes must take the Born form.
