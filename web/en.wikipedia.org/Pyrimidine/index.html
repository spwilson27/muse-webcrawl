Pyrimidine

Pyrimidine is an aromatic heterocyclic organic compound similar to pyridine: a six-membered ring containing two nitrogen atoms at the 1 and 3 positions. It is one of the three diazines (six-membered rings with two nitrogen atoms), the others being pyrazine (1,4) and pyridazine (1,2). Pyrimidine rings form the core of three of the canonical nucleobases of nucleic acids: cytosine, thymine, and uracil.

Pyrimidine
Formula: C4H4N2
Class: Diazine, aromatic heterocycle
Melting point: 20–22 °C
Derived bases: Cytosine, thymine, uracil

Occurrence and derivatives

The pyrimidine nucleobases pair with purines in DNA and RNA: cytosine pairs with guanine, and thymine (or uracil in RNA) pairs with adenine. Beyond the nucleobases, the pyrimidine scaffold appears in many biologically and pharmaceutically important molecules, including the vitamin thiamine, the barbiturate drugs, and the anticancer agent 5-fluorouracil.

Synthesis

The ring can be assembled by condensation reactions, classically from a three-carbon fragment such as malondialdehyde or malonate derivatives and an amidine, which supplies the N–C–N unit. See pyrimidine metabolism for how living cells build and break down these rings.
