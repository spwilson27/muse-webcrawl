The Taiping Rebellion, also known as the Taiping Civil War, was a massive rebellion and civil war in China between the Manchu-led Qing dynasty and the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom, a theocratic state ruled by Hong Xiuquan. Lasting from 1850 to 1864, it was one of the deadliest wars in history, with total deaths commonly estimated at 20 to 30 million, including civilians killed by famine and disease.

Origins

Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka schoolteacher who had repeatedly failed the imperial examinations, came to believe — through visions and his reading of Protestant Christian tracts — that he was the younger brother of Jesus Christ, charged with ridding China of "demons", including the Qing rulers and the Confucian order. His God Worshipping Society grew rapidly in impoverished Guangxi province, and open revolt began with the Jintian Uprising in January 1851.

Course of the war

Taiping forces swept north, taking Nanjing in 1853, which Hong renamed Tianjing ("Heavenly Capital") and made his capital. The Taiping proclaimed radical reforms: common ownership of land, equality of the sexes, bans on opium, foot-binding, and prostitution, and a new examination curriculum based on Hong's scriptures. Internal purges — above all the Tianjing incident of 1856, in which the East King Yang Xiuqing was murdered — badly weakened the movement. A failed expedition to take Beijing and growing Qing military organization under regional armies, especially Zeng Guofan's Xiang Army and foreign-officered units such as the Ever Victorious Army, turned the tide. Tianjing fell in July 1864; Hong had died weeks earlier, and organised resistance soon collapsed.

Consequences

The war devastated much of central and eastern China, accelerated the Qing dynasty's decline, and shifted military power toward provincial Han Chinese officials — a devolution with lasting consequences for modern Chinese history. The Taiping were later celebrated by Chinese nationalists and Communists as heroic forerunners of revolution.
