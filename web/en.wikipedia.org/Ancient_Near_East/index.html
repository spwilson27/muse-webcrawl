Ancient Near East

The ancient Near East is the home of early civilisations within a region roughly corresponding to the modern Middle East: Mesopotamia (modern Iraq), ancient Egypt, ancient Iran (Elam and Persia), Anatolia (modern Turkey), the Levant (modern Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Israel and Palestine), and Arabia. It is one of the cradles of civilisation, where agriculture, cities, writing, law codes, and empires first developed.

Overview

The region's history is conventionally studied from the Neolithic (c. 10,000 BC), when farming began in the Fertile Crescent, through the rise of the Sumerian city-states of Mesopotamia in the 4th millennium BC, and onward to the conquests of Alexander the Great (330s BC) or the Arab conquests of the 7th century AD, depending on scholarly usage. Major cultures and powers included Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, Assyria, the Hittites, Pharaonic Egypt, Israel and Judah, Phoenicia, Urartu, Elam, and the Achaemenid Persian Empire — the largest empire the world had yet seen.

Contributions

The ancient Near East produced the earliest known writing systems (cuneiform in Mesopotamia and hieroglyphs in Egypt, both c. 3400–3300 BC), the Code of Hammurabi and other foundational legal collections, the wheel's widespread adoption, monumental architecture such as ziggurats and pyramids, sophisticated mathematics and astronomy (including the sexagesimal system whose divisions of hours and circles survive today), and the first alphabets, from which most modern writing systems descend.

Study

The field is investigated through archaeology and the study of ancient texts in disciplines such as Assyriology and Egyptology. The decipherment of cuneiform and hieroglyphs in the 19th century, and excavations at sites including Ur, Nineveh, Babylon, and Hattusa, recovered a documentary record spanning three thousand years.
