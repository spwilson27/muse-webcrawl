Baltic Shield

The Baltic Shield (or Fennoscandian Shield) is a segment of the Earth's crust belonging to the East European Craton, representing a large part of Fennoscandia, northwestern Russia, and the northern Baltic Sea. It is composed predominantly of Archaean and Proterozoic gneisses and greenstone belts which have undergone numerous phases of deformation, metamorphism, and magmatism. The shield is notable for its great age — its oldest rocks are over three billion years old — and for its long-term tectonic stability.

Extent and subdivisions

The shield underlies Norway, Sweden, Finland, the Karelia and Kola regions of Russia, and the floor of the northern Baltic. Geologists divide it into provinces based on the age and character of crust formation: the Archaean domains of Kola, Karelia, and the Belomorian belt in the northeast; the Palaeoproterozoic Svecofennian domain of Finland and Sweden, formed around 1.9 billion years ago; and the younger Transscandinavian igneous belt and Sveconorwegian domain in the southwest.

Geological history

The shield's crust was assembled through a series of ancient mountain-building episodes — the Saamian, Lopian, Svecofennian, Gothian, and Sveconorwegian orogenies — as volcanic arcs and microcontinents collided and fused. Since the Cambrian the region has been largely stable, forming the basement beneath the sedimentary platform cover that extends across the rest of the East European Plain.

Landscape and resources

Repeated Pleistocene glaciations scraped the shield nearly bare of sediment, leaving the lake-dotted, low-relief terrain characteristic of Finland and much of Sweden; the region is still rebounding isostatically after the melting of the ice sheet, rising by up to about a centimetre per year around the Gulf of Bothnia. The shield hosts important mineral deposits, including the great iron ores of Kiruna in Sweden, gold and base metals in Finland, and the nickel and apatite deposits of the Kola Peninsula.
