Maxwell's equations are a set of four partial differential equations that form the foundation of classical electromagnetism. Published by Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell between 1861 and 1865, they describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated by charges and currents, and how they propagate through space.

The equations

The four equations are: Gauss's law, which states that electric charge produces an electric field; Gauss's law for magnetism, which states that there are no magnetic monopoles; Faraday's law, which states that a changing magnetic field induces an electric field; and the Ampère–Maxwell law, which states that magnetic fields are generated by electric currents and by changing electric fields—Maxwell's own addition of the displacement current term.

Electromagnetic waves

Combining the equations in empty space yields a wave equation whose speed matches the measured speed of light, leading Maxwell to conclude that light itself is an electromagnetic wave. The equations were later recast into their compact modern vector form by Oliver Heaviside. Together with the Lorentz force law, they underpin electrical engineering, optics, and much of modern technology, and they set the stage for Einstein's theory of special relativity.
