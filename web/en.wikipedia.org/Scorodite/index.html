Scorodite is a common hydrated iron arsenate mineral, with the chemical formula FeAsO4·2H2O. It is found in the oxidised zones of arsenic-bearing ore deposits and as a product of the weathering of arsenopyrite and other arsenic minerals. Scorodite was described in 1818 from the Lobenstein district of Saxony, Germany; its name derives from the Greek skorodion, "garlic-like", for the garlic odour it gives off when heated — a sign of its arsenic content.

Properties and occurrence

Scorodite crystallises in the orthorhombic system, forming dipyramidal crystals, drusy crusts or earthy masses in shades of green to blue-green and brown. It is dimorphous with the monoclinic mineral parascorodite. Important localities include Tsumeb in Namibia, the Ouro Preto district of Brazil, and numerous mines in Cornwall, Saxony and the western United States.

Environmental significance

Because scorodite is one of the least soluble arsenate phases under oxidising, acidic conditions, it is studied and deliberately precipitated in hydrometallurgy as a stable form for fixing arsenic waste from gold and copper processing. Its long-term stability in mine waste and soils is an active subject of geochemical research, since reductive dissolution can remobilise toxic arsenic.
