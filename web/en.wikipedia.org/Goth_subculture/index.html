The goth subculture is a music-based subculture that began in the United Kingdom during the early 1980s, developing out of the post-punk scene. It is associated with the gothic rock genre and with an aesthetic drawn from gothic fiction, horror films, and 19th-century mourning dress: black clothing, black hair, pale makeup, and dark eyeliner worn by all genders. The subculture survived well beyond its founding music scene and spread internationally, spawning fashion, clubs, festivals, and literature.

Origins

Gothic rock emerged from British post-punk around 1979–1981, with Bauhaus's debut single "Bela Lugosi's Dead" (1979) widely treated as the genre's founding record. Early fans of bands such as Siouxsie and the Banshees, The Cure, Joy Division, and The Sisters of Mercy adopted a dark visual style. The Batcave club in London, opened in 1982, became a focal point, and the press label "goth" — earlier applied loosely by critics — attached itself to the scene.

Music and scenes

The core goth genres include gothic rock, deathrock, dark wave, ethereal wave, and cold wave; adjacent styles such as industrial, EBM, and neofolk are often embraced within broader "dark alternative" club nights. Major gatherings include Germany's Wave-Gotik-Treffen, which draws some 20,000 attendees annually, and the Whitby Goth Weekend in England.

Fashion and ideas

Goth fashion blends Victorian and Edwardian mourning attire, punk's DIY ethos, fetish elements, and androgyny. The subculture is frequently linked to an appreciation of dark, romantic, and morbid themes, though members' politics, spirituality, and beliefs vary widely. Researchers have noted the scene's longevity and the tendency of many participants to remain involved for life.
