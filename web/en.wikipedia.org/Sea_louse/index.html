Sea louse

Sea lice are marine ectoparasitic copepods of the family Caligidae, comprising some 559 species in 37 genera, which feed on the mucus, epidermal tissue and blood of host fish. The name is especially applied to species affecting salmon: the salmon louse (Lepeophtheirus salmonis) and species of Caligus. Sea lice are a major pest of farmed salmon worldwide.

Life cycle

Sea lice pass through free-swimming nauplius stages, an infective copepodid stage that settles on a host, attached chalimus stages anchored by a frontal filament, and mobile pre-adult and adult stages that can move over the host's surface and mate. Females carry long paired strings of eggs. Development is temperature-dependent, taking a few weeks in warm water.

Effects on fish

Feeding lice erode skin and fins, causing stress, osmotic imbalance and open lesions that admit secondary infections; heavy infestations can kill juvenile fish. In salmon farming, lice are controlled with medicines (such as emamectin benzoate and hydrogen peroxide baths), cleaner fish (lumpfish and wrasse), lasers, thermolicers and freshwater treatments, but resistance to several chemical treatments has evolved. Farm-origin lice spilling onto wild juvenile salmon migrations have been the subject of scientific and public controversy in Norway, Scotland, Ireland and British Columbia.
