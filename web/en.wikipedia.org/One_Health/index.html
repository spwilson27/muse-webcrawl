One Health

One Health is an integrated, unifying approach that aims to sustainably balance and optimise the health of people, animals and ecosystems. It recognises that the health of humans, domestic and wild animals, plants and the wider environment are closely linked and interdependent, and it mobilises multiple sectors, disciplines and communities to work together on threats at the human–animal–environment interface.

Origins

The idea has roots in 19th-century comparative medicine — Rudolf Virchow coined the term "zoonosis" and observed that between animal and human medicine there are no dividing lines — and in Calvin Schwabe's "One Medicine" concept of the 1960s. The Wildlife Conservation Society's "Manhattan Principles" of 2004 popularised the modern "One World, One Health" framing amid outbreaks of SARS, H5N1 avian influenza and Ebola. In 2022 the FAO, UNEP, WHO and the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) launched a joint One Health plan of action; the formal quadripartite definition dates from 2021.

Scope

One Health is applied above all to zoonotic diseases — roughly 60 percent of known human infectious diseases and about 75 percent of emerging ones originate in animals — and to pandemic prevention, antimicrobial resistance, food safety, and the health effects of climate change, biodiversity loss and intensive livestock production. Practical examples include joint surveillance of avian influenza in poultry and people, rabies control through mass dog vaccination, and monitoring of wildlife trade and wet markets.

Criticism and challenges

Supporters argue the approach prevents costly spillovers at source; critics note that funding and institutional practice still lag behind the rhetoric, and that environmental health remains the weakest partner compared with human and veterinary medicine.
