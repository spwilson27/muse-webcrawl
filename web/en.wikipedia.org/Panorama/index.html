Panorama

Panorama
Term coined byRobert Barker (patent, 1787)
MeaningA wide, unbroken view of a whole region or scene
Historical mediumCylindrical paintings in purpose-built rotundas

A panorama (formed from the Greek pan, "all", and horama, "view") is any wide-angle view or representation of a physical space, whether in painting, drawing, photography, film or a three-dimensional model. The word was coined by the Irish painter Robert Barker for the giant cylindrical paintings he patented in 1787 and exhibited in London, and it soon passed into general use for any broad, comprehensive prospect.

The panorama craze

Barker's invention presented a 360-degree painting viewed from a central platform in a purpose-built rotunda, lit from above so that no frame or edge was visible; visitors felt transported into the scene itself. Panoramas became a mass medium of the nineteenth century: cities, landscapes and above all battles and current events were displayed across Europe and North America, often with three-dimensional foreground objects (the faux terrain) blending into the painted distance. Moving panoramas — long canvases scrolled between rollers with narration and music — toured widely, a precursor of cinema.

Surviving examples and modern usage

Famous surviving rotunda panoramas include the Panorama Mesdag (1881) in The Hague, the Bourbaki Panorama (1881) in Lucerne, and the Racławice Panorama (1894) in Wrocław. In the twentieth century the term broadened: wide-format and stitched panoramic photography, cinematic widescreen formats, 360-degree digital imagery in mapping and virtual-reality services, and even figurative uses — a "panorama" of a subject meaning a comprehensive survey — all descend from Barker's spectacle.
