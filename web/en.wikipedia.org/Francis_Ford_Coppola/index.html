Francis Ford Coppola

Francis Ford Coppola (born 1939), a central figure of the New Hollywood era.

Francis Ford Coppola (born 7 April 1939) is an American film director, producer and screenwriter. A leading figure of the New Hollywood movement of the 1970s, he is widely regarded as one of the greatest filmmakers of all time, with five Academy Awards among many honours.

Career

Born in Detroit to a family of Italian immigrants — his father Carmine was a composer and musician — Coppola studied theatre at Hofstra University and film at UCLA, where he worked under Roger Corman, directing the low-budget horror film Dementia 13 (1963). He won his first Oscar for the screenplay of Patton (1970).

His breakthrough came with The Godfather (1972), adapted from Mario Puzo's novel, which won the Academy Award for Best Picture and redefined the gangster genre. The Godfather Part II (1974), The Conversation (1974) and Apocalypse Now (1979) — whose production in the Philippines became legendary for its difficulties — cemented his reputation; the latter two both won the Palme d'Or at Cannes, making him one of few directors to win it twice.

Later work includes The Outsiders and Rumble Fish (both 1983), The Cotton Club (1984), Bram Stoker's Dracula (1992), and the self-financed epic Megalopolis (2024). Through his company American Zoetrope, co-founded with George Lucas in 1969, Coppola championed directors such as his daughter Sofia Coppola. He is also a noted vintner, owning the Inglenook winery in Napa Valley.
