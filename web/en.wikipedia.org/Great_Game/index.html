The Great Game

The Great Game was a political and diplomatic confrontation that existed for most of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century between the British Empire and the Russian Empire over Afghanistan and neighbouring territories in Central and South Asia. The term was popularised in Rudyard Kipling's 1901 novel Kim.

After the British gained control of India, they became concerned that Russian expansion southward through the Central Asian khanates would threaten the jewel of the British Empire. Russia, for its part, sought access to warm-water ports and influence on its southern frontier. The rivalry involved espionage, exploration, diplomacy, and proxy conflicts, with Afghanistan serving as a buffer state between the two empires.

Origins

The rivalry is usually dated from the 1813 Treaty of Gulistan between Russia and Persia, or from the Russo-Persian War of 1804–1813. British fears grew as Russia annexed the khanates of Kokand, Bukhara, and Khiva in the 1860s and 1870s, steadily advancing toward the frontiers of India.

The Anglo-Afghan Wars

Britain intervened in Afghanistan in the First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842), which ended in disaster with the destruction of a British army retreating from Kabul in 1842. The Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880) followed a Russian mission to Kabul; it ended with the Treaty of Gandamak and the installation of Abdur Rahman Khan, with Britain controlling Afghan foreign policy while avoiding direct occupation.

Exploration and espionage

Agents and explorers—often called "players" of the Game—such as William Moorcroft, Alexander Burnes, and the Indian surveyors known as pundits mapped the passes of the Hindu Kush and Pamirs. The Panjdeh incident of 1885, when Russian forces clashed with Afghan troops, nearly brought the two empires to war.

End of the Game

The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 settled the rivalry: Persia was divided into spheres of influence, Afghanistan was recognised as a British sphere, and both powers agreed to respect the integrity of Tibet. The two empires then cooperated as allies against Germany in the First World War.
