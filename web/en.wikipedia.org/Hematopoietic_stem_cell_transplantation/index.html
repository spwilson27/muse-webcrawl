Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the transplantation of multipotent hematopoietic stem cells, usually derived from bone marrow, peripheral blood, or umbilical cord blood, in order to re-establish blood cell production (haematopoiesis) in a patient whose bone marrow or immune system is damaged or defective. It was formerly commonly called bone marrow transplantation, a term still widely used for the procedure when the cells are taken from marrow.

Indications

HSCT is most often performed for cancers of the blood or bone marrow, such as acute and chronic leukaemias, lymphomas, and multiple myeloma. It is also used for non-malignant conditions including aplastic anaemia, inherited immune deficiencies such as severe combined immunodeficiency, haemoglobin disorders such as thalassaemia and sickle cell disease, and certain inborn errors of metabolism.

Types

In autologous transplantation, the patient's own stem cells are collected in advance, stored, and returned after the patient receives high-dose chemotherapy or total body irradiation ("conditioning") that destroys the diseased marrow. In allogeneic transplantation, the cells come from a donor — a matched sibling, a volunteer from a donor registry, or a partially matched (haploidentical) family member. Donor cells can attack residual cancer (the graft-versus-leukaemia effect) but can also attack the patient's tissues.

Procedure

Stem cells are now most often collected from peripheral blood after the donor is given growth factors such as G-CSF that mobilise stem cells out of the marrow. The recipient's conditioning regimen may be myeloablative (destroying the marrow completely) or reduced-intensity. The harvested cells are infused intravenously and home to the marrow, with recovery of blood counts — engraftment — typically taking two to four weeks, during which the patient is severely immunocompromised.

Complications

The principal risks are infection during the period of low blood counts, organ toxicity from conditioning, infertility, and, in allogeneic transplantation, graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), in which donor immune cells attack the recipient's skin, gut, and liver. GVHD is managed with immunosuppressive drugs such as ciclosporin and methotrexate used as prophylaxis. Long-term survivors face risks of secondary cancers and chronic GVHD but many are cured of otherwise fatal diseases.

History

Following early attempts in the 1950s, E. Donnall Thomas performed the first successful allogeneic transplants in the late 1960s and 1970s and received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1990. Registries of volunteer donors, beginning with the Anthony Nolan register in 1974, have grown to tens of millions of potential donors worldwide, and more than a million transplants had been performed cumulatively by the 2010s.
