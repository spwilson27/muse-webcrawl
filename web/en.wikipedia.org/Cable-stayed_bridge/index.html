Cable-stayed bridge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cable-stayed bridge is a bridge in which the deck is supported directly by cables (stays) running from one or more towers (pylons). Unlike a suspension bridge, whose deck hangs from vertical suspenders attached to main cables draped between towers, a cable-stayed bridge's stays are anchored straight from the tower to the deck, forming a fan-like or harp-like pattern.

Design variants

In the harp design the stays run roughly parallel, attached at intervals up the tower; in the fan design they converge near the tower top; most modern bridges use a modified fan. Towers may be single or paired, and decks are usually of steel or prestressed concrete. The stays put the deck into compression and the tower carries most of the load to the foundations.

History and examples

Early examples appeared in the 1950s in Germany, notably the Strömsund Bridge in Sweden (1956) and Düsseldorf's Theodor Heuss Bridge (1957). Cable-stayed designs became the standard choice for medium-to-long spans of roughly 200 to 1,000 metres. Famous examples include the Millau Viaduct in France, the Øresund Bridge between Denmark and Sweden, Japan's Tatara Bridge, and the Russky Bridge in Russia, whose 1,104-metre main span is the longest of any cable-stayed bridge.

Advantages

Compared with suspension bridges, cable-stayed bridges need no massive anchorages, can be cantilevered out from the towers during construction, and are often more economical for their span range; compared with other types they offer an elegant, distinctive silhouette.
