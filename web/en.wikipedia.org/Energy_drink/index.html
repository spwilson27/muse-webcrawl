Energy drink

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An energy drink is a type of functional beverage containing stimulant compounds, usually caffeine, which is marketed as providing mental and physical stimulation (marketed as "energy", but distinct from food energy). They may or may not be carbonated and may also contain sugar, other sweeteners, herbal extracts, taurine, and amino acids. They are a subset of the larger group of energy products, which includes sports drinks and energy gels, and are distinct from sports drinks, which are advertised to enhance sports performance.

History and market

Early energy beverages appeared in Japan in the 1960s, notably Lipovitan D, sold as a medicinal tonic. In Europe, Dietrich Mateschitz adapted the Thai drink Krating Daeng to launch Red Bull in Austria in 1987, creating the modern category; major brands such as Monster and Rockstar followed in the United States in the 2000s. The global market is worth tens of billions of dollars annually, with heavy marketing aimed at young consumers and associations with extreme sports, gaming and music.

Effects and health concerns

The stimulant effect is essentially that of caffeine — typically 80 mg per 250 ml serving, comparable to a cup of coffee — and claims for other ingredients such as taurine and B-vitamins have limited scientific support. Excessive consumption, especially combined with alcohol or exercise, has been linked with palpitations, sleep disturbance, anxiety, raised blood pressure and, in rare cases, serious adverse events. Health bodies advise moderation, and the effects on children and adolescents are of particular concern.

Regulation

Regulation varies widely. The European Union requires labelling of high-caffeine drinks; some countries restrict sales to minors — England, for example, has legislated to ban sales of high-caffeine energy drinks to under-16s, and several retailers adopted voluntary bans. Lithuania and Poland were among the first countries to prohibit sales to minors by law.
