Postmodernism

Postmodernism is a term used to describe a wide range of developments in critical theory, philosophy, architecture, art, literature, and culture that emerged in the mid-to-late 20th century as a departure from, or reaction against, modernism. Broadly characterised by skepticism toward grand narratives and universal truths, postmodern thought emphasises the contingent, constructed nature of knowledge and meaning.

Origins and definition

The term appeared in various fields from the 1870s onward, but gained its current currency after Jean-François Lyotard's The Postmodern Condition (1979), which defined the postmodern as "incredulity toward metanarratives". Thinkers associated with postmodernism — Derrida's deconstruction, Foucault's analyses of power and knowledge, Baudrillard's simulacra — questioned Enlightenment ideals of objective truth, progress, and stable identity, while in literature authors such as Thomas Pynchon, Jorge Luis Borges, and Italo Calvino employed metafiction, fragmentation, and intertextuality.

In architecture and art

Postmodern architecture, exemplified by Robert Venturi's call for "complexity and contradiction" and buildings such as the Vanna Venturi House, reacted against modernist austerity by reintroducing ornament, historical reference, and playful eclecticism. In art, postmodernism blurred boundaries between high and low culture, embracing appropriation and pastiche.

Criticism

Critics, including Noam Chomsky, Richard Dawkins, and Alan Sokal (of the Sokal affair), have accused postmodern writing of obscurantism and relativism; defenders argue it exposes hidden assumptions and power structures in claims to universal truth.
