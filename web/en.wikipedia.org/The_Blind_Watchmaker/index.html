The Blind Watchmaker: Why the Evidence of Evolution Reveals a Universe without Design is a 1986 book by the British evolutionary biologist Richard Dawkins. It explains and argues for the theory of evolution by natural selection.

Overview

The title refers to the watchmaker analogy used by William Paley in his 1802 book Natural Theology, which argued that the complexity of living things implies a designer. Dawkins counters that natural selection — a blind, unconscious process — can produce the appearance of design without any designer. He introduces the concept of "cumulative selection" to show how small, successive changes can build up complex adaptations, and presents his "biomorph" computer program, which evolves simple shapes under artificial selection, to illustrate the power of the process.

Reception

The book was widely praised for its clarity and won the Royal Society of Literature's W. H. Heinemann Award and the Los Angeles Times Book Prize for science in 1987. It remains one of the best-known popular accounts of evolution.
