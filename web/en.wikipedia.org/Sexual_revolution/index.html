Sexual revolution

Protesters of the 1960s, when attitudes to sexuality changed rapidly in the West.

The sexual revolution was a social movement that challenged traditional codes of behaviour related to sexuality and interpersonal relationships throughout the Western world from the 1960s into the 1970s and beyond. It involved increased acceptance of premarital sex, contraception, homosexuality, alternative forms of sexuality and women's sexual autonomy.

Causes

A decisive technological factor was the introduction of the combined oral contraceptive pill, approved in the United States in 1960, which separated contraception from the act of intercourse and placed control largely in women's hands. Earlier groundwork included the Kinsey reports of 1948 and 1953, which documented that behaviour diverged widely from stated norms, and the work of sex researchers Masters and Johnson. The broader counterculture of the 1960s, second-wave feminism, gay liberation after the Stonewall riots of 1969, and legal changes — such as liberalised divorce and abortion laws — reinforced the shift.

Effects and debates

Practices and attitudes changed measurably: premarital sex became widely accepted, cohabitation and out-of-wedlock births rose, and obscenity laws were relaxed. Critics at the time and since have blamed the revolution for family breakdown and the spread of sexually transmitted infections, concerns sharpened by the AIDS epidemic of the 1980s, while supporters emphasise gains in personal freedom, gender equality and honesty about sexuality. Historians often note earlier "first" sexual revolutions in the 1920s and distinguish the later period by the scale of contraceptive use and the mainstreaming of once-taboo topics.
