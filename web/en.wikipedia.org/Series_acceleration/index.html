Series acceleration

Partial sums of a series and an accelerated estimate of its limit.

Series acceleration is a collection of techniques in numerical analysis for improving the rate of convergence of a sequence or series. A slowly converging series may require an impractical number of terms to reach a desired accuracy, so acceleration methods transform the partial sums into a new sequence that approaches the same limit more rapidly.

Motivation

Many important constants and functions are defined by infinite series whose terms decrease too slowly for direct summation. The alternating harmonic series, for example, converges to ln 2 but its error after n terms is roughly 1/(2n), so millions of terms would be needed for high precision. Acceleration extracts more information from the terms already computed, exploiting patterns in the remainders.

Methods

Classical methods include Kummer's transformation, which compares a series with a known series of similar behaviour, and the Euler transform for alternating series. Aitken's delta-squared process and its generalisation, Wynn's epsilon algorithm, build extrapolations from consecutive partial sums and are closely related to Padé approximants and continued fractions. Richardson extrapolation, originally devised for finite-difference calculations, applies whenever the error admits an expansion in powers of a step parameter. For divergent series, summation methods such as Borel summation and Levin's transformations assign generalised sums.

Applications

Series acceleration is used in computing mathematical constants, evaluating special functions, summing perturbation expansions in physics, and speeding up iterative algorithms. Its effectiveness depends on the structure of the series: methods tuned to alternating, monotone, or logarithmically convergent sequences can fail or even diverge when applied outside their intended class, so error estimates and stability checks remain essential.
