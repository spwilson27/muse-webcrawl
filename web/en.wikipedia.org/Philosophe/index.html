Philosophe

The philosophes (French for "philosophers") were the intellectuals of the 18th-century Enlightenment in France and, by extension, elsewhere in Europe. They applied reason to the study of many areas of learning, including philosophy, history, science, politics, economics, and social issues, and were prominent critics of religious intolerance, feudal privilege, and censorship.

Characteristics

Unlike academic philosophers in the modern sense, the philosophes were men (and some women, such as Émilie du Châtelet) of letters who wrote for a broad educated public. They gathered in salons, coffeehouses, and academies, and corresponded across Europe in a "Republic of Letters." Their characteristic genres included the essay, the pamphlet, the philosophical tale, and the encyclopedia article.

Major figures

Leading philosophes included Voltaire, Montesquieu (whose The Spirit of the Laws developed the separation of powers), Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d'Alembert (editors of the Encyclopédie), the Baron d'Holbach, Condillac, Helvétius, and Condorcet. Though they disagreed on much — Rousseau famously broke with the group — they shared commitments to reason, toleration, and reform.

Influence

The philosophes' critiques of the Ancien Régime profoundly shaped the political thought of the American and French Revolutions and continue to define the self-image of the modern Enlightenment tradition.
