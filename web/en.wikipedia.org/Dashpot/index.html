A dashpot, also known as a damper, is a mechanical device that resists motion via viscous friction. The resulting force is proportional to the velocity of the moving part, but opposite in direction, slowing the motion and absorbing energy. Dashpots are commonly constructed as a piston moving through an oil-filled cylinder.

Types

Linear dashpots resist straight-line motion and are characterised by a damping coefficient relating force to velocity. Rotary dashpots resist rotation, typically with vanes turning in a viscous fluid, and are used in small mechanisms such as glove-box lids and toilet seats for soft closing. Air dashpots use a piston in an air cylinder with a small vent, avoiding the leakage problems of oil.

Applications

Dashpots appear in vehicle shock absorbers (usually combined with springs), door closers, anti-slam mechanisms, and as snubbers protecting structures and piping. They are also used to damp oscillations in sensitive instruments.

In modelling

In physics and materials science, the dashpot is the standard symbol for a purely viscous element. Combined with a spring (the Hookean element), it forms the Maxwell and Kelvin–Voigt models of viscoelasticity, used to describe creep and stress relaxation in polymers and biological tissues.
