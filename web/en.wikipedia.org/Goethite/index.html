Goethite is a mineral of the diaspore group, consisting of iron(III) oxide-hydroxide, specifically the α-polymorph, α-FeO(OH). It is found in soil and other low-temperature environments such as sediment. Goethite has been well known since ancient times for its use as a pigment.

Brown ochre consisting mainly of goethite was among the earliest paints used by humans, found in cave art dating back tens of thousands of years. The mineral was named in 1806 by Johann Georg Lenz in honour of the German polymath and poet Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, who collected minerals.

Formation and occurrence

Goethite often forms through the weathering of other iron-rich minerals, and thus is a common component of soils, concentrated in laterite soils. It is also a primary precipitate in marine and lake sediments and a major constituent of bog iron ores. The fine-grained material historically mined as iron ore and called limonite is largely composed of goethite.

Properties

Goethite crystallises in the orthorhombic system, commonly as acicular or prismatic crystals, stalactitic and botryoidal masses, and earthy aggregates. Its colour ranges from yellow-brown to reddish brown and near black. It is antiferromagnetic at room temperature, and upon heating above about 300 °C it dehydrates to hematite.

Uses

Goethite is an important iron ore and the principal component of yellow ochre pigment. It has also been found as a biomineral: the radular teeth of limpets contain goethite fibres that give them exceptional tensile strength.
