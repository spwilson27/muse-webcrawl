The GNU General Public License (GNU GPL or simply GPL) is a series of widely used free software licenses, or copyleft licenses, that guarantee end users the freedoms to run, study, share, and modify the software. The license was the first copyleft license for general use, and was originally written by Richard Stallman, the founder of the Free Software Foundation (FSF), for the GNU Project.

The GPL gives the recipients of a computer program the rights of the Free Software Definition. These rights are: the freedom to use the work for any purpose, to study how it works and change it, to distribute copies, and to distribute modified versions. To protect these freedoms, the license requires that derivative works be distributed under the same license terms — the copyleft mechanism — and that source code be made available to recipients of binaries.

History

GPLv1 was released in February 1989. GPLv2 followed in June 1991 and became one of the most widely used licenses in free software, adopted notably by the Linux kernel. GPLv3, published on 29 June 2007 after a long public consultation, addressed issues that had arisen since 1991, including software patents, digital rights management ("tivoization"), and compatibility with other free licenses.

Terms and conditions

Distributors of GPL-covered software must preserve copyright notices, provide or offer the complete corresponding source code, and license modifications under the GPL. The license provides no warranty. Additional variants and related licenses include the Lesser GPL (LGPL), which permits linking with non-free code in certain ways, and the Affero GPL (AGPL), which extends copyleft to software run over a network.

Use and criticism

GPL software includes the Linux kernel, the GNU Compiler Collection, WordPress, and many core utilities of free operating systems. The license has been tested in courts in several countries and generally upheld. Critics, particularly from industry, have described copyleft as "viral", while proponents argue it preserves user freedom; permissive licenses such as MIT and Apache have nonetheless grown more popular for new projects in recent years.
