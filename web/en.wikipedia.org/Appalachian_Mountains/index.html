The Appalachian Mountains, often called the Appalachians, are a mountain system in eastern to northeastern North America. The Appalachians first formed roughly 480 million years ago during the Ordovician Period. They once reached elevations similar to those of the Alps and the Rocky Mountains before experiencing natural erosion over hundreds of millions of years. Today the chain runs for about 3,300 kilometres from the island of Newfoundland in Canada southwestward to central Alabama in the United States.

Geography

The range is divided into northern, central and southern sections and into physiographic provinces including the Blue Ridge, the Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians, the Great Appalachian Valley, the Allegheny Plateau and the Piedmont. The highest peak is Mount Mitchell in North Carolina, at 2,037 metres the highest point in North America east of the Mississippi River. The Appalachian Trail, a hiking path of some 3,500 kilometres, follows the range from Mount Katahdin in Maine to Springer Mountain in Georgia.

Geology

The Appalachians are the product of a series of continental collisions — the Taconic, Acadian and Alleghanian orogenies — culminating in the assembly of the supercontinent Pangaea about 300 million years ago, when North America collided with Africa. Rocks of the Scottish Highlands and the Atlas Mountains of Morocco were once part of the same mountain system, separated when the Atlantic Ocean opened. Erosion has since worn the range down and exposed ancient metamorphic rocks at its core.

Ecology

The Appalachians are renowned for biodiversity, particularly the temperate deciduous forests of the southern mountains, which are among the richest in the world for salamanders, freshwater mussels and tree species. The region hosts cove hardwood forests, spruce–fir forests at high elevations, and vast coal deposits laid down in Carboniferous swamps, which drove the industrial history — and the environmental controversies, including mountaintop removal mining — of central Appalachia.

History and culture

The mountains formed a formidable barrier to early European westward expansion from the British colonies, a frontier formalised by the Royal Proclamation of 1763. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the region became synonymous with coal mining, timber and distinctive cultural traditions in music, crafts and dialect. The term Appalachia now denotes a cultural and economic region whose rural poverty has been a focus of U.S. policy since the 1960s.
