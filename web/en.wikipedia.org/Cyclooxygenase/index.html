Cyclooxygenase

Cyclooxygenase (COX), officially known as prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase (PTGS), is an enzyme (EC 1.14.99.1) that is responsible for the formation of prostanoids, including thromboxane and prostaglandins such as prostacyclin, from arachidonic acid. A member of the animal-type heme peroxidase family, it is also known as prostaglandin G/H synthase.

Isoforms

Humans express two main cyclooxygenase isoforms. COX-1 (PTGS1) is constitutively expressed in most tissues and maintains the normal lining of the stomach, kidney function and platelet aggregation. COX-2 (PTGS2) is induced by inflammatory stimuli and produces prostaglandins that mediate pain, fever and inflammation. A third variant, COX-3, is a splice variant of COX-1 identified in 2002, though its functional significance remains debated.

Function and mechanism

The enzyme catalyses two reactions at separate active sites: a cyclooxygenase reaction that converts arachidonic acid to prostaglandin G2 (PGG2), and a peroxidase reaction that reduces PGG2 to prostaglandin H2 (PGH2). PGH2 is then converted by tissue-specific synthases into the various prostaglandins, prostacyclin and thromboxane A2.

Pharmacology

Pharmaceutical inhibition of COX can provide relief from the symptoms of inflammation and pain. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen exert their effects through inhibition of COX. Aspirin irreversibly acetylates the enzyme, whereas most other NSAIDs inhibit it competitively and reversibly. Selective COX-2 inhibitors, such as celecoxib, were developed to reduce gastrointestinal side effects, though some were withdrawn over cardiovascular concerns.

History

The cyclooxygenase enzyme was first isolated and characterised in the 1970s through the work of John Vane and others, which explained the mechanism of action of aspirin-like drugs and contributed to Vane's share of the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.
