Oriental studies

Oriental studies is the academic field that historically examined the languages, history, religions, literatures and cultures of Asia and North Africa — the regions Europeans called "the Orient". Emerging in European universities in the 17th and 18th centuries, it encompassed philology of languages such as Arabic, Persian, Sanskrit, Chinese and Hebrew, and produced the dictionaries, grammars and translations on which later scholarship built.

History

Early centres included the School of Oriental and African Studies (SOAS) in London, founded 1916, and older chairs of Arabic and Hebrew at Oxford, Cambridge, Leiden and Paris. Figures such as William Jones, who in 1786 noted the kinship of Sanskrit with Greek and Latin, helped found comparative philology. The field was closely entangled with European colonial administration, which funded language training for officials.

Critique and transformation

Edward Said's Orientalism (1978) argued that Western scholarship had constructed a stereotyped, essentialised "Orient" that served imperial power. The critique reshaped the humanities and contributed to the replacement of "Oriental studies" by area-specific fields — Middle Eastern studies, South Asian studies, East Asian studies, Sinology, Indology and Egyptology among them — and to today's broader Asian and Middle Eastern studies programmes.
