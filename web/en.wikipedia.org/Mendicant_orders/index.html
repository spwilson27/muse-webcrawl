Mendicant orders are, primarily, certain Roman Catholic religious orders that have adopted for their male members a lifestyle of poverty and travelling itinerant preaching. Members, known as friars (from Latin frater, "brother"), renounced property both individually and communally, depending for their survival on the charity of other people. The key orders founded in the thirteenth century were the Franciscans and the Dominicans.

Origins

The mendicant movement arose in response to the growth of towns, universities, and a money economy in medieval Europe. Unlike monks, who withdrew to rural monasteries and lived from landed endowments, friars settled in cities, where they preached, heard confessions, taught in the universities, and ministered to the urban poor. The movement was embraced by the papacy as an instrument against heresy — particularly the Cathars and Waldensians — whose own apostolic poverty had attracted popular support.

The great orders

  • Franciscans (Order of Friars Minor) — founded by Saint Francis of Assisi, approved orally by Pope Innocent III in 1209/1210 and definitively in 1223.
  • Dominicans (Order of Preachers) — founded by Saint Dominic, approved by Pope Honorius III in 1216; distinguished by intellectual training and preaching against heresy.
  • Carmelites (Order of the Brothers of the Blessed Virgin Mary of Mount Carmel) — originating with hermits on Mount Carmel, adapted to mendicant life in the 1240s.
  • Augustinians (Order of Saint Augustine) — formed in 1256 by the union of several hermit groups under the Rule of St Augustine.

These four are known as the "great mendicant orders". Later additions included the Servites and various smaller congregations.

Controversies

The friars' success provoked conflict with the secular clergy and university masters, who resented their privileges of preaching and hearing confessions. Within the Franciscan order, disputes over the strictness of poverty — between the Spirituals and the Conventuals — led to persecution of the rigorists and lasting divisions. The Second Council of Lyons (1274) suppressed many smaller mendicant congregations.

Influence

Mendicant friars staffed the medieval universities (Thomas Aquinas and Duns Scotus were friars), served as papal inquisitors and missionaries from Asia to the Americas, and shaped late medieval devotion, art, and urban life.
