Beta Lyrae

Beta Lyrae (β Lyrae, abbreviated Beta Lyr or β Lyr), also named Sheliak, is a multiple star system in the constellation of Lyra, approximately 960 light-years from the Sun. Its primary is a famous eclipsing binary whose continuous light variation, discovered by John Goodricke in 1784, made it the prototype of the Beta Lyrae class of eclipsing variables — semi-detached binaries in which the components are so close that they are distorted into ellipsoidal shapes by their mutual gravity.

Properties

The eclipsing pair, Beta Lyrae Aa1 and Aa2, orbit each other in about 12.94 days, and the period is gradually lengthening as mass is transferred from the less massive donor star to its companion at a rate of roughly two hundred-thousandths of a solar mass per year (the "Beta Lyrae paradox," in which the less massive star is the more evolved). The accreting star is largely hidden inside a thick accretion disc, so the system appears as a single blue-white star of apparent magnitude varying between about 3.25 and 4.34. Wider components make the system at least quintuple.

Nomenclature

The traditional name Sheliak derives from the Arabic al-shiliyāq, associated with the constellation Lyra. In 2016 the International Astronomical Union's Working Group on Star Names approved the name Sheliak specifically for the primary component Beta Lyrae Aa1.

Significance

Beta Lyrae has been intensively studied for over two centuries as a laboratory for interacting binary evolution, mass transfer, and accretion discs. Its light curve — with eclipses of unequal depth and no flat intervals between them — contrasts with the box-like eclipses of detached systems such as Algol, illustrating the effects of tidal distortion and gas streams between the stars.
