Murrine

Murrine slices revealing patterned cross-sections

Murrine (singular: murrina) are decorative patterns or images made in a glass cane that are revealed when the cane is cut into thin cross-sections. They are produced by layering molten glass of different colours around a core and drawing it out into a long rod; when sliced, each piece shows the same pattern. The finished slices are often arranged and fused into vessels, plates, and other glass objects, a technique closely associated with the glassmakers of Murano, Venice.

History

Mosaic glass techniques date to ancient Egypt and Rome, where vessels of fused patterned slices were prized luxury goods. The craft was revived in the 19th century, notably by Venetian glassmakers such as Vincenzo Moretti, and the term murrine became standard. In the 20th and 21st centuries, artists have created highly elaborate pictorial murrine — portraits, animals, and landscapes composed from tens of thousands of individual glass threads.

Related technique

Murrine are the basis of millefiori ("a thousand flowers"), in which many flower-like murrine are packed together and fused.
