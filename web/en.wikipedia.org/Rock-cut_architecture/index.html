Rock-cut architecture

Rock-cut architecture is the practice of creating a structure by carving it out of solid natural rock. Rock that is not part of the structure is removed until the only rock left is the architectural elements of the excavated interior. Rock-cut architecture is especially associated with the cave temples of India and China and the rock tombs of the ancient Near East and Mediterranean.

One kind can be distinguished as monolithic rock-cut architecture, in which a structure is excavated from a free-standing rock by cutting from the outside inward, as with the Kailasa temple at Ellora. The other main type is excavated architecture, in which chambers are hollowed out from a cliff face or underground, leaving the exterior rock largely untouched—examples include the Treasury at Petra and the churches of Lalibela, Ethiopia.

India

India preserves more than 1,500 rock-cut structures, including the caves of Ajanta and Ellora in Maharashtra, the Elephanta Caves near Mumbai, and the cave temples of Badami and Mahabalipuram in the south. Buddhist chaitya halls and viharas were among the earliest examples, later joined by Hindu and Jain temples.

Elsewhere

Rock-cut tombs are found in ancient Egypt (such as the tombs of Beni Hasan and Abu Simbel), in Lycia and Phrygia in Anatolia, in Nabataean Petra, and in Etruscan Italy. In China, Buddhist cave grottoes at Yungang, Longmen, and Mogao contain vast numbers of carved shrines and statues. Cappadocia in Turkey is famous for rock-cut churches and entire underground cities such as Derinkuyu.
