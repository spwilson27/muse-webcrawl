Interest rate

Interest rate

DefinitionAmount charged by a lender to a borrower for the use of assets, expressed as a percentage of the principal
UnitPercent per annum (% p.a.)
Related conceptsAPR, APY, discount rate, real interest rate, nominal interest rate
Key institutionsCentral banks (e.g. the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank)

An interest rate is the amount of interest due per period, as a proportion of the amount lent, deposited, or borrowed (called the principal sum). The total interest on an amount lent or borrowed depends on the principal sum, the interest rate, the compounding frequency, and the length of time over which it is lent, deposited, or borrowed.

Interest compensates the lender for the time value of money and the risk of default.

Interest rates are one of the most important prices in a modern economy. They influence decisions about saving, borrowing, and investment, and they are the principal tool through which central banks conduct monetary policy.

History

The charging of interest dates back to antiquity. Mesopotamian records from around 3000 BC show loans of grain and silver bearing interest, and the Code of Hammurabi regulated maximum rates. Religious traditions variously restricted or prohibited interest: medieval Christian doctrine condemned usury, and Islamic finance continues to prohibit riba. Modern interest-rate theory developed with the work of economists such as Irving Fisher, who distinguished nominal from real rates, and John Maynard Keynes, who placed the interest rate at the centre of his theory of liquidity preference.

Nominal and real rates

The nominal interest rate is the rate quoted on a loan or deposit, without adjustment for inflation. The real interest rate is the nominal rate minus the rate of inflation, and measures the growth in purchasing power of the funds lent. The relationship is summarised by the Fisher equation: real rate ≈ nominal rate − expected inflation.

Determinants

  • Monetary policy — central banks set a short-term policy rate that anchors other rates.
  • Inflation expectations — lenders demand compensation for expected erosion of purchasing power.
  • Credit risk — riskier borrowers pay a premium over the risk-free rate.
  • Term — longer maturities usually carry higher rates, producing the yield curve.
  • Liquidity — less liquid instruments tend to pay more.

Compounding

Interest may be simple, calculated only on the original principal, or compound, calculated also on previously accumulated interest. With compounding frequency m times per year at nominal rate r, the amount grows as A = P(1 + r/m)mt. The effective annual rate rises with compounding frequency.

Negative interest rates

During the 2010s several central banks, including the European Central Bank, the Swiss National Bank, and the Bank of Japan, set policy rates below zero, and trillions of dollars of bonds traded at negative yields — an historically unusual situation in which lenders effectively paid for the privilege of lending.
