Vaucluse is a department in the southeastern French region of Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur. It takes its name from the village of Fontaine-de-Vaucluse, famous for its great karst spring, and its prefecture is Avignon, the medieval seat of the Avignon Papacy. The department was created in 1793, largely from former papal territories (the Comtat Venaissin and Avignon) annexed by revolutionary France.

Geography

Vaucluse is bounded by the Rhône to the west and the Durance to the south. Its landscape rises from the fertile Rhône plain — orchards, market gardens and the vineyards of Châteauneuf-du-Pape and the Côtes du Rhône — to the limestone plateaus of the Monts de Vaucluse, the Luberon range (a regional natural park), and Mont Ventoux (1,909 m), the "Giant of Provence", a legendary climb of the Tour de France. The department also includes a small exclave, the canton of Valréas, entirely surrounded by Drôme.

History

The area was inhabited in antiquity by Celtic-Ligurian tribes and was thoroughly Romanised; Orange preserves a famous Roman theatre and triumphal arch. From 1274 the Comtat Venaissin belonged to the papacy, and from 1309 Avignon hosted the popes during the Avignon Papacy and the Western Schism. Papal rule — tolerant of a notable Jewish community, the Juifs du pape — lasted until the French Revolution annexed the territories in 1791. During the Second World War the Luberon maquis was active in the Resistance; the poet René Char led a maquis section in the region.

Culture and economy

Vaucluse is famed for lavender fields, truffles (the "black diamond" of the Comtat), melons from Cavaillon, and wines including Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Gigondas and Vacqueyras. The Avignon Festival, founded in 1947, is one of the world's great theatre festivals. The village of Fontaine-de-Vaucluse was the beloved retreat of the poet Petrarch, who lived there in the 14th century and wrote of its spring and the Sorgue river.
