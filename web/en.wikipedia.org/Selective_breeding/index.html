Selective breeding (also called artificial selection) is the process by which humans use animal breeding and plant breeding to selectively develop particular phenotypic traits by choosing which typically animal or plant males and females will sexually reproduce and have offspring together. Domesticated animals are known as breeds, normally bred by a professional breeder, while domesticated plants are known as varieties, cultigens, cultivars, or breeds.

History

Selective breeding began with the domestication of plants and animals more than 10,000 years ago: farmers kept seed from the best-yielding plants and bred the most docile or productive animals. Maize was transformed from the wild grass teosinte, wheat and rice were selected for non-shattering ears and larger grains, and the domestic dog was diversified from wolves into hundreds of breeds differing enormously in size and behaviour. Systematic, recorded breeding advanced in the 18th century with figures such as Robert Bakewell, who improved sheep and cattle by careful selection and inbreeding.

Methods and effects

Breeders select parents exhibiting desired traits whose inheritance they wish to strengthen, cull individuals with undesirable traits, and may use inbreeding to fix traits or outcrossing and hybridisation to combine them. Response to selection depends on the heritability of the trait. Long-term experiments, such as the Illinois corn selection experiment begun in 1896, show selection can continue to change traits for a hundred generations. The Russian farm-fox experiment, begun by Dmitry Belyaev in 1959, selected silver foxes purely for tameness and produced correlated changes in coat, ears, and behaviour — the "domestication syndrome."

Selective breeding also carries risks: reduced genetic diversity, inbreeding depression, and harmful correlated traits, as in the health problems of many purebred dogs or the extreme muscling of Belgian Blue cattle. Charles Darwin opened On the Origin of Species (1859) with variation under domestication, using artificial selection as the intuitive model for natural selection.
