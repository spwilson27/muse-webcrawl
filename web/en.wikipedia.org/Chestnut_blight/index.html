Chestnut blight
Causal agentCryphonectria parasitica (fungus)
HostsAmerican chestnut, European chestnut, oaks
First detected (N. America)1904, New York City

Chestnut blight is a fungal disease caused by Cryphonectria parasitica that devastated the American chestnut (Castanea dentata) in the first half of the 20th century. The fungus forms sunken cankers on bark that girdle and kill the tree above the canker, releasing spores spread by wind, rain, birds, and insects.

History

The blight was first noticed in 1904 on chestnuts at the New York Zoological Garden (Bronx Zoo), almost certainly introduced on imported Asian chestnut stock. Within about fifty years it had killed an estimated four billion American chestnuts — roughly a quarter of the hardwood trees of the Appalachian forests — functionally eliminating a dominant species, though roots often survive and resprout without reaching maturity.

Effects and response

The loss transformed eastern North American forest ecology and rural economies dependent on chestnut timber and nuts. Asian chestnuts carry natural resistance, and Chinese chestnut genes have been bred into American trees through backcross programmes led by The American Chestnut Foundation; separately, researchers have developed transgenic blight-tolerant trees expressing a wheat detoxifying enzyme. In Europe, naturally spreading hypoviruses that weaken the fungus have limited blight damage.
