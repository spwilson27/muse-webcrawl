Ship stability


A vessel at an angle of heel, showing the centre of gravity (G), centre of buoyancy (B), and metacentre (M).

Ship stability is an area of naval architecture and ship design that deals with how a ship behaves at sea, both in still water and in waves, intact and damaged. A stable ship tends to return to an upright position after being heeled by wind, waves, or turning forces; an unstable ship may develop a permanent list or capsize. Stability calculations are a fundamental part of ship design and operation, and are governed by regulations of the International Maritime Organization (IMO).

Basic principles

A floating ship is acted on by its weight, applied at the centre of gravity (G), and by buoyancy, applied at the centre of buoyancy (B), the centroid of the displaced volume. When the ship heels, the shape of the submerged hull changes and B moves sideways, so that the buoyant force creates a righting lever (GZ) with the weight, tending to restore the ship to upright. The metacentric height (GM) — the distance between G and the metacentre M, the point where the line of buoyant force crosses the ship's centreline — is the standard measure of initial stability: a positive GM means the ship is stable, though an excessively large GM makes a stiff ship that rolls violently, while a small GM makes a tender ship.

Large-angle and dynamic stability

Beyond small angles, stability is described by the curve of righting levers (GZ curve), whose maximum value, the angle at which it vanishes, and the area beneath it (representing the energy needed to capsize the ship) all matter. Dynamic effects — rolling in waves, cargo shifting, water trapped on deck, and the free surface effect of liquids sloshing in partly filled tanks — can drastically reduce effective stability.

Damage stability

Ships are also required to survive flooding of one or more compartments after collision or grounding, remaining afloat with adequate residual stability. Famous stability disasters include the capsizing of the ferry Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) and the Estonia (1994), both involving water on vehicle decks, and the loss of the container ship Cougar Ace's near-capsize during ballast exchange.

Inclining experiment

A ship's actual GM is verified by an inclining experiment, in which known weights are moved across the deck and the resulting small angle of heel is measured, allowing the position of the centre of gravity to be calculated.
