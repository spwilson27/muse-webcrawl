Increasing methane emissions are a major contributor to the rising concentration of greenhouse gases in Earth's atmosphere, and are responsible for up to one-third of near-term global heating. During 2019, about 60% (360 million tons) of methane released globally was from human activities, while natural sources contributed about 40% (230 million tons). Reducing methane emissions by capturing landfill gas, reducing methane leaks from fossil fuel production, and changing agricultural practices — particularly in livestock and rice production — is a key strategy for mitigating climate change, because methane is a short-lived but potent greenhouse gas.

Global budget

Methane (CH₄) has more than doubled in atmospheric concentration since the pre-industrial era, reaching over 1,900 parts per billion in the early 2020s. Its perturbation lifetime in the atmosphere is around twelve years, after which it is destroyed mainly by reaction with hydroxyl radicals, eventually oxidising to carbon dioxide and water. Because of this short lifetime, cutting methane emissions produces relatively rapid climate benefits, a point emphasised in the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report and in the Global Methane Pledge launched at COP26 in 2021, under which more than 150 countries committed to reduce global methane emissions by at least 30% from 2020 levels by 2030.

Anthropogenic sources

The largest human-caused source is agriculture, dominated by enteric fermentation in ruminant livestock (cattle, sheep, goats) and by manure management, together with methane released from flooded rice paddies. The fossil fuel sector is the second major source: coal mining releases trapped gas, and oil and gas systems leak methane during extraction, processing, transmission ("fugitive emissions"), and through venting and incomplete flaring. Waste contributes through anaerobic decomposition of organic matter in landfills and wastewater. Satellite instruments such as TROPOMI aboard Sentinel-5P have revealed large "super-emitter" events from oil, gas and coal infrastructure that are often underreported in national inventories.

Natural sources

Natural wetlands are the single largest natural source, as anaerobic microbes in waterlogged soils produce methane. Other natural sources include termites, wild animals, oceans and lakes, geological seeps, and emissions from thawing permafrost and subsea clathrates in the Arctic — the latter a subject of active research because of the risk of positive climate feedbacks.

Mitigation

Technical measures include leak detection and repair in oil and gas systems, capturing coal-mine methane, covering landfills and capturing their gas, alternate wetting and drying in rice cultivation, feed additives such as 3-NOP and Asparagopsis seaweed that suppress rumen methanogenesis, and anaerobic digestion of manure and food waste. The International Energy Agency estimates that a large fraction of fossil-fuel methane abatement can be achieved at no net cost because the captured gas can be sold.
