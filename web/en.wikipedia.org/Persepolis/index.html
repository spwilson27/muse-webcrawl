Persepolis

Persepolis was the ceremonial capital of the Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BC). It is situated in the plains of Marvdasht, about 60 kilometres northeast of Shiraz in present-day Iran. Founded by Darius I in 518 BC, the city was built on an immense half-artificial, half-natural terrace and served as the stage for royal receptions and the celebration of the New Year festival, Nowruz. In Persian it is known as Takht-e Jamshid, "Throne of Jamshid".

Construction

Darius ordered the construction of the great terrace and the Apadana palace, whose audience hall held some 10,000 guests and was roofed on 72 columns. His successors Xerxes I and Artaxerxes I added the Gate of All Nations — guarded by colossal human-headed bulls — the Hall of a Hundred Columns, and further palaces and treasuries. The famous Apadana stairway reliefs depict delegations from 23 subject nations bringing tribute to the king.

Clay tablets found in the fortifications and treasury, written in Elamite, record the administration of the site and the rations paid to its workers, providing a rare documentary window on the empire's economy.

Destruction

In 330 BC Alexander the Great captured Persepolis and looted its treasury, reputedly the greatest concentration of wealth in the ancient world. The palace complex was subsequently burned; ancient accounts differ on whether the fire was revenge for the Persian burning of Athens or a drunken accident during a banquet, egged on, in one story, by the Athenian courtesan Thaïs.

Archaeology

Excavations by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago in the 1930s uncovered much of the terrace. Persepolis was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.
