Olympic mascot

An Olympic mascot is a character, usually an animal native to the host country or a figure drawn from its culture, created to embody the spirit of an edition of the Olympic Games and to promote the event, especially to children. Mascots have been a standard feature of the Games since the 1968 Winter Olympics.

History

The first mascot is generally considered to be Schuss, an unofficial stylised skier figure used at the 1968 Winter Olympics in Grenoble. The first official Summer Olympic mascot was Waldi, a dachshund, at Munich 1972; Waldi's multicoloured design echoed the Games' visual identity. From then on nearly every Games, Winter and Summer, has had one or more mascots, and the Paralympic Games have followed with their own mascots since the 1980s.

Notable mascots

  • Misha (Moscow 1980) — a bear, among the most beloved mascots; its tearful image at the closing ceremony became iconic.
  • Sam the Olympic Eagle (Los Angeles 1984), designed by Disney's C. Robert Moore.
  • Hodori the tiger (Seoul 1988) and Cobi the dog (Barcelona 1992), the latter designed by Javier Mariscal.
  • The Fuwa (Beijing 2008) — a set of five characters.
  • Wenlock and Mandeville (London 2012), and Miraitowa (Tokyo 2020).
  • Bing Dwen Dwen, a panda, for the Beijing 2022 Winter Games.

Role

Mascots appear in merchandising, licensed products, animated films, stamps and stadium entertainment. Host organising committees choose them through design competitions, and the characters are meant to communicate the host's culture and the Olympic values of excellence, friendship and respect — though commercial considerations and design controversies, such as the mixed reception of some abstract designs, are recurring themes.
