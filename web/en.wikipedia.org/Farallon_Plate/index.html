Farallon Plate

The Farallon Plate was an ancient oceanic tectonic plate that began subducting under the west coast of North America — then part of the supercontinent Pangaea — during the Jurassic period. Once one of the largest plates on Earth, covering much of the eastern Pacific, it has been almost entirely subducted into the mantle beneath North America; its surviving remnants include the Juan de Fuca, Explorer, Gorda, Rivera, Cocos and Nazca plates.

History

Named after the Farallon Islands off San Francisco, the plate formed when Pangaea broke up and the young Atlantic opened. For more than 150 million years it converged with North America, generating coastal mountain building, the Laramide orogeny in the Rocky Mountains (attributed to a period of shallow "flat-slab" subduction), and extensive volcanic arcs. About 30 million years ago the Pacific–Farallon spreading ridge reached the subduction zone off California; the plate split, the San Andreas Fault system began to form, and the remaining fragments retreated north and south.

Remnants and the mantle slab

Seismic tomography has imaged the subducted Farallon slab deep in the mantle beneath North America, extending as far east as the Atlantic margin — evidence that helps explain past episodes of volcanism far inland, such as the ignimbrite flare-up of the Great Basin. The slab's presence beneath the continent continues to influence mantle flow and the geology of western North America, including the Basin and Range Province and the Yellowstone hotspot debate.
