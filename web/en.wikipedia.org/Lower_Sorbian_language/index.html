Lower Sorbian (dolnoserbšćina) is a West Slavic minority language spoken by the Sorbs in Lower Lusatia, in the German state of Brandenburg, centred on the city of Cottbus (Chośebuz). It is one of two literary Sorbian languages, the other being Upper Sorbian, spoken further south in Saxony. Lower Sorbian is severely endangered: most native speakers are elderly, and intergenerational transmission has largely ceased outside a small number of villages and revival programmes.

History

Sorbian-speaking Slavs settled Lusatia in the 6th century. Lower Sorbian developed as a distinct written language during the Reformation: the first printed Lower Sorbian book was a hymnal of 1574, and Mikławš Jakubica had completed a (then-unprinted) New Testament translation in 1548. Through the 19th and 20th centuries, Germanisation pressures — school policies, industrialisation around Cottbus, and Nazi-era suppression of Sorbian institutions — steadily eroded the language area. The GDR formally recognised the Sorbs and supported Sorbian institutions, but lignite mining destroyed many Sorbian villages in the Cottbus district.

Features

Lower Sorbian is closely related to Polish and Upper Sorbian, with distinctive features including retention of the dual number (alongside singular and plural), seven cases, and characteristic sound changes: it has preserved g where Upper Sorbian shifted it to h, and developed the fricative ś and ź sounds. The standard language is based on the Cottbus dialect. Its alphabet includes the letters ś, ź, ć, č, ž, š, ł, ň and ŕ (the last unique to Lower Sorbian orthography in older usage).

Revitalisation

Revival efforts include the Witaj immersion kindergarten and school programme, the weekly newspaper Nowy Casnik (founded 1848), Lower Sorbian radio programming from RBB, and the School for Lower Sorbian Language and Culture in Cottbus, which teaches many adult learners. Despite official recognition under German law and the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, UNESCO classifies Lower Sorbian as seriously endangered.
