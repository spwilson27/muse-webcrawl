The Russian-American Company (Russian: Российско-американская компания) was a state-sponsored chartered company formed in 1799 under Emperor Paul I. Granted a monopoly over trade in Russian America — principally the fur trade in Alaska and the Aleutian Islands — it was Russia's first joint-stock company and effectively governed the colony for nearly seven decades.

History

The company grew out of the earlier fur-trading ventures of Grigory Shelikhov and Alexander Baranov, who became its first chief manager in Alaska. From its colonial capital at New Archangel (present-day Sitka), the company organized sea-otter hunting, built forts and settlements as far south as Fort Ross in California (1812), and traded with Spanish, Mexican, British, and American merchants.

Decline and dissolution

Depletion of sea-otter and fur-seal populations, competition, and the costs of administering a distant colony steadily undermined the company's profitability. Its charter was renewed in 1824 and 1844 but not again; in 1867 the United States purchased Alaska, and the company's assets were sold off, with operations winding up by 1881. Many of its records remain an essential source for the history of the North Pacific.
