Coefficient of relationship

The coefficient of relationship is a measure of the degree of consanguinity (or biological relationship) between two individuals. It was defined by Sewall Wright in 1922 and derived from his definition of the coefficient of inbreeding. The measure is most commonly used in genetics and genealogy, and gives the probability that a gene selected randomly from an individual and a gene selected at the same locus from a related individual are identical by descent.

Calculation

The coefficient of relationship between two individuals is computed by summing, over all paths connecting them through common ancestors, the product of (1/2) raised to the number of steps in each path. Parent and offspring share a coefficient of 1/2; full siblings also 1/2; half-siblings 1/4; first cousins 1/8; and unrelated individuals approach 0. An individual is related to itself with a coefficient of 1 (neglecting inbreeding).

Applications

The coefficient underlies the concept of inclusive fitness in evolutionary biology: according to Hamilton's rule, an altruistic behaviour can be favoured by natural selection when rB > C, where r is the coefficient of relationship, B the reproductive benefit to the recipient, and C the cost to the actor. The measure is also used in animal breeding, in the calculation of inbreeding coefficients in pedigrees, and in some legal and medical contexts concerning close-kin relationships.
