Kurt Tucholsky

Kurt Tucholsky (9 January 1890 – 21 December 1935) was a German journalist, satirist, and writer, one of the most prominent publicists of the Weimar Republic. Writing under his own name and four famous pseudonyms — Peter Panter, Theobald Tiger, Ignaz Wrobel, and Kaspar Hauser — he produced essays, satirical poems, chansons, reviews, and aphorisms that attacked militarism, the judiciary, and the rising Nazi movement.

Life and career

Tucholsky studied law, served as a soldier on the Eastern Front in the First World War, and emerged a committed pacifist. From 1913 he wrote for Siegfried Jacobsohn's journal Die Schaubühne, later Die Weltbühne, becoming its defining voice and briefly its editor after Jacobsohn's death in 1926. He also wrote for cabaret and for the satirical magazine Ulk. His early novella Rheinsberg: A Storybook for Lovers (1912) was a bestseller; the wistful novel Schloss Gripsholm (1931), written in Swedish exile, remains his best-known book.

Exile and death

Disillusioned with the Weimar Republic's failure to confront militarism and Nazism, Tucholsky lived mainly in Paris and Sweden from 1924. The Nazis burned his books in May 1933 and stripped him of citizenship. Increasingly ill and silent, he died in Gothenburg in December 1935 from an overdose of sleeping medication, generally regarded as suicide.

Legacy

Tucholsky's aphorisms — such as "Soldiers are murderers" (the slogan at the centre of a famous 1931 trial of his editor Carl von Ossietzky) — remain fixtures of German political culture. The Kurt Tucholsky Prize for literary journalism is awarded in his honour, and his complete works fill many volumes.
