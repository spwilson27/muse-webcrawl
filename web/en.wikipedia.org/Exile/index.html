Exile

Exile is the state of being barred from one's native country, typically for political or punitive reasons. A person may be expelled by a government (external exile), confined to a remote region within their own country (internal exile), or leave voluntarily under threat — a self-imposed exile that blurs into emigration. The exiled person is distinct from a refugee, though the conditions often overlap.

In history

Exile is among the oldest political punishments. Athens practiced ostracism, banishing a citizen for ten years by popular vote. Rome exiled figures such as Cicero and Ovid. The Babylonian captivity of the Jews became a central motif of exile in religious literature. Famous modern exiles include Napoleon (to Elba, then St Helena), Dante (from Florence), Victor Hugo, Leon Trotsky, and numerous deposed monarchs and governments-in-exile during the Second World War.

Governments in exile

A government-in-exile claims legitimacy while residing abroad, usually after occupation of its territory. Polish, Czechoslovak, and other governments operated from London during the Second World War; the Central Tibetan Administration in India is a modern example.

Exile in literature

Exile — its grief, alienation, and creative stimulus — is a major theme of world literature, from Ovid's Tristia and Dante's Divine Comedy to twentieth-century writers such as James Joyce, Joseph Brodsky, and Edward Said, whose essay "Reflections on Exile" is a classic treatment.
