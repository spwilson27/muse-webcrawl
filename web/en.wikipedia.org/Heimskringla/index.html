Heimskringla is a collection of sagas about Swedish and Norwegian kings, written in Old Norse in Iceland around 1230 and attributed to the poet and historian Snorri Sturluson. It is the best known of the kings' sagas and a foundational text for the history of early Scandinavia.

Contents

The work begins with the legendary Ynglinga saga, which euhemerises Odin and the Norse gods as migrant kings from the east, and proceeds through sixteen sagas: the lives of Halfdan the Black, Harald Fairhair (the first king of a united Norway), Haakon the Good, Olaf Tryggvason, and the longest and most celebrated, Óláfs saga helga on Saint Olaf, whose fall at the Battle of Stiklestad (1030) occupies the work's centre. It continues through Harald Hardrada—killed at Stamford Bridge in 1066—and his successors, ending with Magnus Erlingsson in 1177.

Sources and method

Snorri drew on earlier written sagas, genealogies, and above all skaldic poetry, whose intricate verse he treated as a near-contemporary witness and quoted as evidence. In his prologue he articulated an early source-critical method, distinguishing the skaldic poems composed in kings' lifetimes from later tales. Modern historians use Heimskringla with caution—its dates and speeches are literary constructions—but its narrative artistry and its preservation of skaldic stanzas make it indispensable. The principal medieval manuscripts were destroyed in the Copenhagen fire of 1728, though copies and transcripts survive.

Translations and influence

Heimskringla has been translated into many languages and shaped the popular image of the Viking Age kings; it deeply influenced Norwegian national historiography in the 19th century and remains a classic of world literature.
