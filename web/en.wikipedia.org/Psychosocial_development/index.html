Psychosocial development

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psychosocial development is the development of personality and social-emotional capacities across the lifespan, most famously described by the German-American psychologist Erik Erikson (1902–1994). In his theory of psychosocial development, first published in Childhood and Society (1950), Erikson proposed that personality develops through eight stages from infancy to late adulthood. At each stage, the individual confronts a psychosocial crisis — a conflict between two opposing tendencies — whose resolution shapes the ego strengths (or "virtues") carried into later life.

Erikson's eight stages

StageApproximate ageCrisisVirtue
1InfancyTrust vs. mistrustHope
2Early childhood (1–3)Autonomy vs. shame and doubtWill
3Preschool (3–5)Initiative vs. guiltPurpose
4School age (6–11)Industry vs. inferiorityCompetence
5Adolescence (12–18)Identity vs. role confusionFidelity
6Young adulthoodIntimacy vs. isolationLove
7Middle adulthoodGenerativity vs. stagnationCare
8Late adulthoodEgo integrity vs. despairWisdom

Erikson called the underlying mechanism the epigenetic principle: each component of personality arises at its appointed time, and successful (though never total) resolution of each crisis builds on previous stages. Unlike Freud's psychosexual stages, which end in adolescence and emphasize instinctual drives, Erikson's stages span the entire lifespan and emphasize social relationships and cultural context.

Key stages

The fifth stage, identity vs. role confusion, is the most widely studied: adolescents explore roles, values, and beliefs to form a coherent identity, a process later researchers such as James Marcia operationalized into identity statuses (diffusion, foreclosure, moratorium, achievement). In middle adulthood, generativity — concern for guiding the next generation through parenting, mentoring, or productive work — contrasts with stagnation and self-absorption. In old age, reflecting on one's life with acceptance yields ego integrity and wisdom; unresolved regret yields despair.

Criticism and influence

Erikson's theory has been criticized for vague, difficult-to-test concepts, a reliance on clinical observation, and cultural and gender biases — the stage sequence may reflect mid-20th-century Western life patterns, and Carol Gilligan and others noted its framing around male development. Nonetheless, the theory remains foundational in developmental psychology, education, and social work, and its concepts of identity crisis (a term Erikson coined) and lifespan development have entered common usage. Joan Erikson later proposed a ninth stage addressing the challenges of very old age.
