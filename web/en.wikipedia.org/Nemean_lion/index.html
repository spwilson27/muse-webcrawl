Nemean lion

The Nemean lion (Greek: Νεμέος λέων) was a monster in Greek mythology that inhabited the region of Nemea in the Peloponnese. It was regarded as the offspring of Typhon and Echidna, or in some accounts of the moon goddess Selene. The lion ravaged the countryside around Nemea, seizing women and luring rescuers into its cave, and could not be killed by mortal weapons because its golden fur was impervious to attack.

The first labour of Heracles

Slaying the Nemean lion was the first of the Twelve Labours imposed on Heracles by King Eurystheus. Heracles attempted to shoot the lion with arrows, but they bounced off its hide; he tried his club and his sword to no better effect. At last he blocked one entrance to the lion's two-mouthed cave, entered by the other, and wrestled the beast, strangling it with his bare hands.

Heracles then faced the problem of skinning the lion, since no blade would cut its hide. He eventually succeeded by using one of the lion's own claws to slice the pelt. Thereafter he wore the skin as an impenetrable cloak and the lion's head as a helmet — the costume in which he is most often depicted in classical art. According to the myth, when Heracles returned wearing the skin, Eurystheus was so frightened that he hid in a large bronze jar and ordered that future labours be reported from outside the city walls.

Legacy

Zeus is said to have placed the lion among the stars as the constellation Leo, in commemoration of Heracles' victory. The skinning of the Nemean lion became one of the most frequently depicted episodes of the Heracles cycle in ancient sculpture and vase painting.
