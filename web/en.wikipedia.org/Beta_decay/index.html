Beta decay

In nuclear physics, beta decay (β-decay) is a type of radioactive decay in which an atomic nucleus emits a beta particle (a fast electron or positron) and a (anti)neutrino, transforming the nucleus into an isobar of the same mass number. Beta decay is mediated by the weak interaction.

Types

In β− decay, a neutron is converted to a proton, and the process creates an electron and an electron antineutrino: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄e. In β+ decay, a proton is converted to a neutron, creating a positron and an electron neutrino. A third process, electron capture, competes with β+ decay: the nucleus absorbs an inner atomic electron and emits a neutrino. A free neutron decays by β− with a mean lifetime of about 15 minutes; a free proton cannot undergo β+ decay, since the neutron is heavier, but bound protons in proton-rich nuclei can.

History

Beta rays were identified by Ernest Rutherford in 1899 as a component of radioactivity. The continuous energy spectrum of beta electrons puzzled physicists and led Wolfgang Pauli in 1930 to postulate the neutrino to preserve energy conservation. Enrico Fermi's 1934 theory of beta decay gave the first satisfactory account of the weak force. The discovery in 1956 that beta decay violates parity, by Chien-Shiung Wu and collaborators, reshaped particle physics.

Applications

Beta emitters are used in medicine (for example phosphorus-32 and yttrium-90 therapy), in thickness gauging in industry, and as tracers. The beta decay of carbon-14 underpins radiocarbon dating. Double beta decay, including the hypothetical neutrinoless mode, is a major probe of whether the neutrino is its own antiparticle.
