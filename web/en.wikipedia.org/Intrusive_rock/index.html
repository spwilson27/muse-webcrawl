Intrusive rock

Intrusive rock is formed when magma penetrates existing rock, crystallizes, and solidifies underground to form intrusions, such as batholiths, dikes, sills, laccoliths, and volcanic necks. Intrusive rock is one of the two classes of igneous rock, the other being extrusive rock (volcanic rock), which erupts onto the surface.

Because magma cools slowly underground, intrusive rocks are typically coarse-grained (phaneritic), with crystals large enough to see with the naked eye. Common examples are granite, diorite, and gabbro.

Intrusive bodies

Intrusions are classified by shape and relationship to the surrounding country rock. Concordant bodies such as sills and laccoliths lie parallel to bedding, while discordant bodies — dikes, stocks, and batholiths — cut across it. Batholiths are the largest intrusions, exceeding 100 square kilometres in outcrop area, and form the cores of many mountain ranges, such as the Sierra Nevada Batholith.

Formation

When magma rises through the crust it may stall in magma chambers, where it cools and crystallises over thousands to millions of years. Fractional crystallisation and assimilation of country rock change its composition. Subsequent uplift and erosion expose the solidified intrusions at the surface, as at Devil's Tower in Wyoming, a volcanic neck.
