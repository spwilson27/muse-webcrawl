Barcode

A barcode is a method of representing data in a visual, machine-readable form. Initially, barcodes represented data by varying the widths, spacings and sizes of parallel lines; these are referred to as linear or one-dimensional (1D) codes. Later, two-dimensional (2D) variants using rectangles, dots, hexagons and other patterns, called matrix codes, were developed.

History

The barcode was invented by Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver, who received a U.S. patent in 1952. Widespread commercial use came in the 1970s with the adoption of the Universal Product Code (UPC) in North American retail; the first UPC-scanned item was a pack of Wrigley's chewing gum in Troy, Ohio, on 26 June 1974. The related EAN system is used internationally, administered by the GS1 organization.

Types and uses

Common 1D symbologies include UPC, EAN, Code 39, and Code 128, used in retail, logistics, libraries, and healthcare. Common 2D codes include the QR code, Data Matrix, and PDF417, which store far more data and can be read by smartphone cameras. Barcodes are read by optical scanners (barcode readers) that measure reflected light from the contrasting bars and spaces.

Benefits

Barcoding speeds up checkout and inventory processes and greatly reduces data-entry errors compared with manual keying, making it foundational to modern supply chain management.
