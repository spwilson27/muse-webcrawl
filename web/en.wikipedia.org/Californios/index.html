Californios

Californios are Hispanic Californians, especially the Spanish-speaking, Catholic people of Spanish, Mexican, and Indigenous descent who resided in Alta California (present-day California) before the Mexican–American War and the American conquest of the territory in 1846–1848. They formed a distinct regional culture centred on missions, presidios, pueblos, and cattle ranchos.

History

Spanish colonization of Alta California began in 1769 with the Portolá expedition and the founding of missions by Junípero Serra. After Mexican independence in 1821, the Californios became Mexican citizens; the secularization of the missions in the 1830s redistributed vast lands to rancho families, and a hide-and-tallow trade with foreign merchants developed. Prominent Californio families included the Vallejos, Picos, Carrillos, de la Guerras, and Bandinis.

American conquest

During the Mexican–American War, Californio forces fought U.S. troops at battles including San Pasqual (1846), where Californio lancers under Andrés Pico defeated Stephen Kearny's column. The Capitulation of Cahuenga (1847) ended fighting in Alta California; the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) promised to respect Californio property rights, but the California Land Act of 1851 forced landowners through lengthy legal proceedings that stripped many families of their ranchos.

Culture and legacy

Californio culture is remembered for skilled horsemanship (the vaquero tradition), ranchos, fandango dances, and Californio Spanish. Their era was romanticized, sometimes inaccurately, in works such as Richard Henry Dana's Two Years Before the Mast and Helen Hunt Jackson's novel Ramona. Descendant communities and heritage organizations continue to preserve Californio history.
