Nitroglycerin (NG), also known as glyceryl trinitrate (GTN), is a dense, colourless, oily, explosive liquid most commonly produced by nitrating glycerol with white fuming nitric acid under conditions appropriate to the formation of the nitric acid ester. It is one of the most powerful and shock-sensitive explosives known, and in medicine it is used as a vasodilator for treating heart conditions such as angina and chronic heart failure.

History

Nitroglycerin was first synthesised by the Italian chemist Ascanio Sobrero in 1847. Alfred Nobel, whose family manufactured it, invented dynamite in 1867 by absorbing nitroglycerin into kieselguhr (diatomaceous earth), making it far safer to handle; he later developed ballistite and blasting gelatin. Dynamite revolutionised mining, quarrying, tunneling, and warfare, and the fortune it earned Nobel funded the Nobel Prizes.

Properties

Pure nitroglycerin is extremely sensitive to shock, friction, and rapid heating, and impure material is even more dangerous; it freezes at about 13 °C and is most treacherous while partially thawing. Its detonation velocity is about 7,700 m/s. In propellants it is combined with nitrocellulose in double-base smokeless powders.

Medical use

In medicine, glyceryl trinitrate is a vasodilator used to relieve angina pectoris and manage heart failure and acute hypertension; it acts by releasing nitric oxide, relaxing vascular smooth muscle. It is administered sublingually as tablets or spray, by transdermal patch, or intravenously. Tolerance develops with continuous use. Workers in explosives factories famously experienced "Monday disease" — headaches and withdrawal symptoms as their tolerance faded over weekends.
