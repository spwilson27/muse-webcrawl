Adenoviridae is a family of viruses, commonly known as adenoviruses, that infect vertebrates including humans, other mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians and fish. They are medium-sized (90–100 nm), non-enveloped viruses with an icosahedral nucleocapsid containing a double-stranded DNA genome. Their name derives from their initial isolation from human adenoids in 1953.

Adenoviruses are unusually stable to chemical and physical agents and to adverse pH conditions, allowing for prolonged survival outside the body. In humans, adenoviruses most commonly cause respiratory illness, ranging from the common cold to pneumonia, croup and bronchitis. Depending on the type, they may also cause gastroenteritis, conjunctivitis, cystitis and rash illness. Most infections are mild and self-limiting, though serious disease can occur in people with weakened immune systems.

Structure

The adenovirus particle consists of an icosahedral protein capsid built mainly from hexon proteins, with penton bases at the twelve vertices from which fibre proteins project. The fibres mediate attachment to host cell receptors, such as the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR) for many species. Inside the capsid lies a linear double-stranded DNA genome of roughly 26–45 kilobase pairs, associated with core proteins V, VII, Mu and the terminal protein.

Classification

The family is divided into several genera. Mastadenovirus infects mammals, including the human adenoviruses, which are classified into species A through G and more than 100 types. Aviadenovirus infects birds, Atadenovirus infects a broad range of hosts including ruminants, birds and reptiles, and Siadenovirus infects birds and amphibians. Additional genera include Ichtadenovirus of fish and Testadenovirus of turtles.

Replication

Adenoviruses enter cells by receptor-mediated endocytosis. The partially disassembled particle escapes the endosome and travels along microtubules to the nuclear pore, where the viral DNA enters the nucleus. Early genes (E1–E4) are transcribed first; E1A activates transcription and pushes the cell toward S phase, while E1B and E3 products counteract host defences and apoptosis. Viral DNA replication then proceeds by protein priming, using the terminal protein as primer, and late genes encode the structural proteins that assemble progeny virions.

Use as vectors

Because adenoviruses efficiently deliver DNA to many cell types and can be grown to high titres, replication-deficient adenoviruses are widely used as vectors in gene therapy, vaccine development and oncolytic therapy. Adenovirus-based vaccines were deployed during the COVID-19 pandemic, and modified adenoviruses are used experimentally to carry therapeutic genes into tumours and other tissues.
