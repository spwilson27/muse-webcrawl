Late Roman Empire

The Late Roman Empire is the period of the Roman Empire from the accession of Diocletian in 284 CE until the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE (and, in the East, until the Arab conquests of the seventh century transformed it into the Byzantine Empire). Historians distinguish it from the earlier Principate because of the more openly autocratic style of rule — the Dominate — a greatly expanded bureaucracy and army, deep administrative reforms, and the eventual triumph of Christianity as the state religion.

Diocletian and the Tetrarchy

Emerging from the Crisis of the Third Century, Diocletian (r. 284–305) reorganized the Empire: he divided provinces into smaller units grouped under dioceses, separated civil from military authority, reformed taxation and coinage, attempted to fix prices by edict, and divided imperial power in the Tetrarchy, with two senior augusti and two junior caesares. The system collapsed into civil war after his abdication, from which Constantine emerged sole ruler.

Constantine and Christianization

Constantine I (r. 306–337) legalized Christianity through the Edict of Milan (313), convened the Council of Nicaea (325), and founded Constantinople (330) as a "New Rome". His dynasty and successors progressively favored the Church; Theodosius I made Nicene Christianity the state religion with the Edict of Thessalonica (380). The pagan revival under Julian (361–363) was brief and unsuccessful.

Division and the fall of the West

On the death of Theodosius I in 395 the Empire was divided between his sons Arcadius (East) and Honorius (West), and never durably reunited. The West suffered the Rhine crossing of 406, the sacks of Rome in 410 (Visigoths) and 455 (Vandals), the loss of North Africa, and the catastrophic failure to recover it. A succession of young emperors dominated by generals gave way to deposition: in 476 the Germanic leader Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, conventionally marking the end of the Western Empire.

The East

The Eastern Empire, wealthier and more urbanized, survived the fifth century behind the walls of Constantinople, paid subsidies to Attila's Huns, and under Justinian I (527–565) briefly reconquered North Africa, Italy and part of Spain — overextending its resources. Plague (541–542), a long war with Sasanian Persia, and the Arab conquests of the 630s–640s reduced it to an Anatolian and Balkan state, the medieval Byzantine Empire.

Government and society

The Late Roman state was highly bureaucratic, with a professional civil service, a codified law (the Theodosian Code of 438 and Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis), and an army split between frontier troops (limitanei) and mobile field armies (comitatenses). Society became more rigidly stratified, with many occupations and tenancies (the colonate) made hereditary.

Historiography

The traditional narrative of "decline and fall", classically argued by Edward Gibbon, has been challenged by modern scholarship (notably Peter Brown's concept of Late Antiquity as a period of creativity and transformation, c. 150–750), which stresses continuity and religious and cultural vitality rather than decay.
