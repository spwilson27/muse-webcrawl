Bon Festival

Observed by: Japanese Buddhists

Type: Buddhist festival

Significance: Honouring the spirits of one's ancestors

Date: Mid-August (or mid-July in some regions)

The Bon Festival (盆, Bon), also known as Obon (お盆), is a Japanese Buddhist custom honouring the spirits of one's ancestors. It has been celebrated in Japan for more than 500 years and centres on the belief that during this period the spirits of the dead return to visit their living relatives. The festival has evolved into a major family reunion holiday, when people travel back to their ancestral homes to visit and clean the graves of their forebears.

Origins

Bon derives from the Ullambana Sutra, which tells of the monk Mokuren (Maudgalyayana), who used his supernatural powers to see his deceased mother suffering in the realm of the hungry ghosts. Directed by the Buddha to make offerings to monks completing their summer retreat on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, he freed her spirit and danced with joy — the legendary origin of the Bon Odori dance.

Observances

Families prepare a household altar with food offerings, clean ancestral graves, and light lanterns or small fires (mukaebi) to guide the spirits home. At the festival's end, floating lanterns (tōrō nagashi) or the great bonfires of Kyoto's Gozan no Okuribi guide the spirits back to the world of the dead. Community Bon Odori dances, performed in circles around a raised scaffold to music and taiko drums, are held in temple grounds, parks, and streets across Japan and in Japanese diaspora communities in Hawaii, Brazil, and North America.

Timing

Most of Japan observes Obon in mid-August, following the adoption of the Gregorian calendar in the Meiji era, while parts of Kantō and other regions keep the traditional mid-July date, and Okinawa and parts of China follow the lunar calendar.
