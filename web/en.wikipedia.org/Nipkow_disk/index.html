Nipkow disk

A Nipkow disk (sometimes Anglicized as Nipkov disk; patented in 1884), also called a scanning disk, is a mechanical, rotating image-scanning device, patented by German inventor Paul Gottlieb Nipkow. It consists of a disk pierced by a spiral of small holes (apertures), each tracing one line of the image as the disk rotates, so that the scene is scanned sequentially into a varying light signal. The Nipkow disk was a fundamental component in early mechanical television systems of the 1920s and 1930s.

Operation

As the disk spins, each aperture sweeps across the image area, passing a momentary sample of light to a photocell behind it. After one revolution the whole picture has been scanned line by line; a synchronised disk at the receiver, modulating a lamp, reconstructs the image. John Logie Baird's early television demonstrations used Nipkow disks, as did the first television broadcasts of the BBC and German services.

Limitations

The system suffered from low resolution (typically 30–60 lines), dim images, and flicker, and it could not easily be scaled to higher definition. It was rapidly displaced in the mid-1930s by fully electronic television using cathode-ray tubes, such as Vladimir Zworykin's iconoscope and Philo Farnsworth's image dissector. Nipkow's principle survives today in the spinning disks of confocal microscopy.
