Spanish–American War


The Battle of Manila Bay

The Spanish–American War (April 21 – August 13, 1898) was a conflict fought between Spain and the United States. It began in the aftermath of the explosion of the USS Maine in Havana Harbor in Cuba, leading to United States intervention in the Cuban War of Independence. The war ended Spanish colonial rule in the Americas and resulted in US acquisition of territories in the western Pacific and Latin America.

Background

Cuba had been fighting for independence from Spain since 1895. American public opinion, inflamed by sensational "yellow journalism" and reports of Spanish atrocities, favoured intervention. On 15 February 1898 the battleship USS Maine exploded and sank in Havana Harbor, killing 266 men; American newspapers blamed Spain, and the rallying cry "Remember the Maine, to hell with Spain!" swept the country. The United States Congress declared war in April 1898.

The war

The war was brief and one-sided. On 1 May, Commodore George Dewey's Asiatic Squadron destroyed the Spanish Pacific fleet at the Battle of Manila Bay in the Philippines. In Cuba, American forces landed in June and, with Cuban allies, won the battles of El Caney and San Juan Hill — where Theodore Roosevelt's Rough Riders won fame — while the US Navy destroyed the Spanish Caribbean squadron at Santiago de Cuba on 3 July. Puerto Rico was occupied in late July. An armistice was signed on 12 August 1898.

Treaty of Paris

Under the Treaty of Paris (1898), Spain relinquished Cuba, which became a US protectorate, and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States for a payment of $20 million. Secretary of State John Hay called it "a splendid little war". The conflict marked the emergence of the United States as a world and colonial power and foreshadowed the Philippine–American War that followed.
