Schwarzschild radius

The Schwarzschild radius (sometimes called the gravitational radius) is the radius of the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole. It is named after Karl Schwarzschild, who in 1916 found the first exact solution of the Einstein field equations of general relativity describing the gravitational field outside a spherical mass. Every physical object has an associated Schwarzschild radius, proportional to its mass; if the object were compressed within that radius, it would become a black hole.

Formula

rs = 2GM / c2

where G is the gravitational constant, M the mass of the object, and c the speed of light. The radius grows linearly with mass: roughly 2.95 kilometres for the Sun, about 8.9 millimetres for the Earth, and around 0.85 centimetres for a mass equal to that of a large mountain.

History

Schwarzschild derived his metric while serving in the German army during the First World War, only months after Einstein published general relativity; he died the following year. The significance of the coordinate singularity at the Schwarzschild radius was debated for decades. It was eventually understood that the surface is not a physical singularity — an infalling observer can cross it in finite proper time — but an event horizon: a one-way boundary from within which nothing, not even light, can escape. The true singularity lies at the centre, at r = 0.

Classification of objects

Objects whose actual radius exceeds their Schwarzschild radius are ordinary stars, planets and other bodies. Objects smaller than their Schwarzschild radius are black holes; depending on mass these are classified as stellar-mass black holes, intermediate-mass black holes, supermassive black holes (such as the one at the centre of the Milky Way, with a Schwarzschild radius of roughly 0.08 astronomical units) and, hypothetically, primordial black holes and micro black holes. For rotating or charged black holes the event horizon lies inside the Schwarzschild radius, as described by the Kerr and Reissner–Nordström metrics. Related concepts include the photon sphere, at 1.5 times the Schwarzschild radius, where light can orbit the hole.
