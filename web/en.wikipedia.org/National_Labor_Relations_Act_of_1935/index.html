National Labor Relations Act of 1935

The National Labor Relations Act of 1935, also known as the Wagner Act, is a foundational statute of United States labour law that guarantees the right of private sector employees to organise into trade unions, engage in collective bargaining, and take collective action such as strikes.

Provisions

Section 7 of the Act protects employees' rights to self-organisation and collective bargaining. Section 8 defines unfair labour practices by employers, including interfering with union organising and discriminating against union members. The Act created the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to enforce its provisions and conduct union representation elections.

History

Sponsored by Senator Robert F. Wagner of New York and signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 5 July 1935 as part of the New Deal, the Act was upheld by the Supreme Court in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp. (1937).

Amendments

The Taft–Hartley Act of 1947 amended the NLRA, adding union unfair labour practices and allowing states to pass "right-to-work" laws. The Act excludes agricultural and domestic workers, supervisors, and public-sector employees.
