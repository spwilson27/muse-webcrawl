Independent music

Independent music (commonly shortened to indie music, or simply indie) is music produced independently of commercial record labels or their subsidiaries — a process that may include an autonomous, do-it-yourself approach to recording and publishing. The term originally described artists signed to independent record labels, and independent labels remain a defining institution of the sector, but in the digital era many artists self-release without any label at all.

Although "indie" derives from "independent", the word has also come to denote a recognisable set of genres and aesthetics — indie rock, indie pop, indie folk and indietronica among them — so that major-label artists working in those styles are routinely described as "indie" even though they are not independently produced. Conversely, much genuinely independent music (in metal, hip hop, electronic and experimental scenes) is rarely labelled indie.

History

Independent labels have existed since the early recording industry, but the modern indie sector emerged from punk rock in the late 1970s. In the United Kingdom, the DIY ethic of punk — crystallised by the Buzzcocks' self-released Spiral Scratch EP (1977) and labels such as Rough Trade, Factory and Mute — built an alternative infrastructure of independent distribution (including the Cartel network), specialist record shops, fanzines and John Peel's BBC radio show. The UK Indie Chart, launched in 1980, measured this parallel economy.

In the United States, the 1980s underground — labels such as SST, Dischord, Touch and Go and Sub Pop, college radio, and touring networks documented in Michael Azerrad's Our Band Could Be Your Life — laid the groundwork for the alternative rock boom of the 1990s, after which major labels absorbed or imitated much of the indie sector. From the 2000s, MP3 blogs, MySpace, Bandcamp and streaming lowered the cost of self-release dramatically, shifting independence from a distribution question to questions of marketing, playlisting and ownership of masters.

Characteristics and economics

Independent artists typically retain greater creative control and a larger share of revenue per sale, but work with far smaller recording and promotion budgets than major-label acts. Trade bodies such as the Worldwide Independent Network (WIN) and the Association of Independent Music (AIM) in the UK, and A2IM in the United States, represent the sector; independent labels and distributors have consistently accounted for roughly a third or more of the global recorded-music market by value, depending on how ownership versus distribution is measured.
