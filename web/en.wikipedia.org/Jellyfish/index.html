Jellyfish

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumCnidaria
SubphylumMedusozoa
GroupsScyphozoa, Cubozoa, Hydrozoa, Staurozoa

Jellyfish, also known as sea jellies, are the medusa-phase of certain gelatinous members of the subphylum Medusozoa, part of the phylum Cnidaria. Jellyfish are mainly free-swimming marine animals with umbrella-shaped bells and trailing tentacles, although a few are anchored to the seabed by stalks rather than being mobile. The bell can pulsate to provide propulsion through open water. The tentacles are armed with stinging cells called cnidocytes, used to capture prey and defend against predators.

Anatomy and physiology

Jellyfish have no brain, heart, bones, or blood. Their bodies are composed largely of water—often more than 95 percent—and of a gelatinous substance called mesoglea sandwiched between two layers of epithelium. A simple nervous system, the nerve net, detects touch and chemicals. The mouth leads to a gastrovascular cavity that serves as both stomach and circulatory system. Many species are bioluminescent.

Life cycle

Most jellyfish alternate between a free-swimming medusa stage and a sessile polyp stage attached to surfaces. Polyps can bud off immature medusae (ephyrae), which grow into adults. The so-called immortal jellyfish, Turritopsis dohrnii, can revert from the medusa stage back to the polyp stage, in principle escaping death.

Ecology and human interaction

Jellyfish are important predators of plankton and small fish and are themselves prey for sea turtles, ocean sunfish, and some seabirds. Large aggregations called blooms can clog fishing nets and power-plant intakes. Some species, such as the Australian box jellyfish, deliver extremely painful and potentially fatal stings, while others are harvested as food in parts of Asia.
