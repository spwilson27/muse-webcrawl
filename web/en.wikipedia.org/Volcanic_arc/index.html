Volcanic arc

A volcanic arc is a chain of volcanoes formed above a subducting tectonic plate, arranged in an arc shape as seen from above. Volcanic arcs are divided into those in which the chain of volcanoes forms islands, called island arcs, and those that lie on a continental margin, called continental arcs.

Formation

Where one lithospheric plate subducts beneath another, the descending slab carries water and other volatiles into the mantle. At depths of roughly 100–150 km these fluids lower the melting point of the mantle wedge above the slab, generating magma that rises to build a line of volcanoes parallel to the deep-sea trench, typically 100–300 km away from it. The magmas are characteristically andesitic and rich in volatiles, making arc volcanism often explosive.

Examples

Well-known island arcs include the Aleutian Islands, the Mariana Islands, Japan, the Philippines and the Lesser Antilles. Continental arcs include the Andes of South America, formed by subduction of the Nazca Plate, and the Cascade Range of western North America. The "Ring of Fire" around the Pacific Ocean is a near-continuous series of such arcs.
