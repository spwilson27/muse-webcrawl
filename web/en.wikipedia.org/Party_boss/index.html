Party boss

A party boss is a person who controls a political party or local political organization, often through patronage and a network of loyal supporters rather than by holding elective office personally. The term is particularly associated with the political machines of nineteenth- and early twentieth-century American cities.

Role and power

Bosses controlled nominations, distributed jobs and contracts, and delivered services to constituents in exchange for votes. Their organizations, known as machines, dominated cities such as New York, Chicago, and Boston.

Notable examples

William M. Tweed of Tammany Hall in New York became the archetype of the corrupt boss in the 1860s and 1870s. Later figures included Richard J. Daley of Chicago, whose organization influenced Democratic politics well into the twentieth century.

Decline

Civil-service reform, direct primaries, welfare-state programmes, and rising affluence eroded the patronage foundations of boss rule during the twentieth century, though informal party leadership networks persist.
