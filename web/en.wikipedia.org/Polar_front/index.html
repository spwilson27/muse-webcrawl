Polar front

In meteorology, the polar front is the weather front boundary between the cold polar air mass and the warm tropical air mass at about 60° latitude in both hemispheres. It is a semi-permanent, fairly continuous boundary along which mid-latitude cyclones (extratropical cyclones) form and travel, making it one of the most important features of mid-latitude weather.

The polar front separates polar and tropical air masses.

Description

The polar front is strongest in winter, when the temperature contrast between polar and tropical air is greatest, and weakest or absent in summer as the gradient relaxes. Its position shifts seasonally, moving equatorward in winter and poleward in summer. At the surface the front may break into segments, but it is better defined aloft, where it is associated with the polar-front jet stream, a core of strong westerly winds near the tropopause that steers surface storm systems.

Polar front theory

The Bergen School of meteorology, led by Vilhelm Bjerknes and including Jacob Bjerknes and Tor Bergeron, developed the polar front theory of cyclones in the years following World War I. In this model, waves develop along the polar front and amplify into mature extratropical cyclones with warm and cold fronts, eventually occluding and dissipating. The theory remains the foundation of the Norwegian cyclone model used in weather analysis today.

Related features

In the Southern Hemisphere, the polar front is largely oceanic and is closely associated with the Antarctic Circumpolar Current's ocean fronts. Related atmospheric boundaries include the arctic front, which separates arctic from polar air, and the subtropical jet stream farther equatorward.
