A heroic couplet is a pair of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter, traditionally used for epic and narrative poetry in English. The form is called "heroic" because iambic pentameter (the "heroic line") was the meter of English epic verse. In its classical Augustan form the couplet is "closed": each pair forms a complete grammatical and rhetorical unit, often balanced around antithesis and a medial caesura.

History

Geoffrey Chaucer pioneered the rhymed pentameter couplet in works such as The Legend of Good Women and much of The Canterbury Tales. After sporadic use in the 16th century, the form was refined by Edmund Waller and John Denham and brought to perfection by John Dryden, whose translations of Virgil and satires such as Absalom and Achitophel (1681) established its norms. Alexander Pope made the closed heroic couplet the dominant vehicle of Augustan poetry in An Essay on Criticism (1711), the mock-epic The Rape of the Lock (1712–1714), his translations of the Iliad and Odyssey, and An Essay on Man (1733–1734).

"True wit is nature to advantage dressed,
What oft was thought, but ne'er so well expressed." — Pope, An Essay on Criticism

Characteristics

The closed couplet favours balance, antithesis, parallelism and epigrammatic compression: each line typically divides into two half-lines, and the couplet ends with a pointed, rhyming conclusion. Pope warned against monotony with devices such as varied caesura placement, enjambment across couplets and the occasional triplet or alexandrine. The Romantics later reacted against the form's polish — Wordsworth and Coleridge preferred blank verse and ballad meters — though couplets continued in the work of George Crabbe and Lord Byron.

In other traditions

Comparable couplet forms exist in many literatures, including the Persian masnavi and the French alexandrine couplets of Racine and Corneille, but the term "heroic couplet" refers specifically to the English iambic pentameter form.
