Romeo and Juliet

AuthorWilliam Shakespeare
Writtenc. 1591–1595
First published1597 (First Quarto)
SettingVerona and Mantua, Italy
GenreTragedy

Romeo and Juliet is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare early in his career about the romance between two Italian youths from feuding families. It was among Shakespeare's most popular plays during his lifetime and, along with Hamlet, is one of his most frequently performed. Today, the title characters are regarded as archetypal young lovers.

Plot

In Verona, the Montague and Capulet families are locked in an ancient feud. Romeo Montague, infatuated at first with Rosaline, sneaks into a Capulet ball and falls instantly in love with Juliet Capulet. That night, in the famous balcony scene, they exchange vows and are secretly married the next day by Friar Laurence, who hopes the union will end the feud. The plan unravels when Juliet's cousin Tybalt kills Romeo's friend Mercutio in a street fight; Romeo retaliates by killing Tybalt and is banished to Mantua. Juliet, ordered to marry Count Paris, drinks a potion from the Friar that makes her appear dead for 42 hours. The message explaining the plan fails to reach Romeo, who hears only that Juliet has died. He buys poison, goes to her tomb, kills Paris, and drinks the poison. Juliet awakes, finds Romeo dead, and stabs herself with his dagger. The grieving families reconcile over the bodies of their children.

Sources and themes

Shakespeare's principal source was Arthur Brooke's narrative poem The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet (1562), itself adapted from Italian and French versions of the story. The play explores themes of love, fate, and the destructiveness of inherited hatred; its chorus describes the couple as "star-cross'd lovers", and chance and haste drive the tragedy as much as the feud does. Critics have also noted the play's remarkable blending of comedy and tragedy in its first half, and its experimental use of sonnet forms in the lovers' dialogue.

Legacy

Romeo and Juliet has been adapted more often than almost any other Shakespeare play: in operas by Gounod and Bellini, Tchaikovsky's fantasy-overture, Prokofiev's ballet, Bernstein's musical West Side Story, and numerous films, including Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 version and Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet (1996). The names of the lovers have become synonymous with passionate young love in many languages.
