Rubber ducky antenna

TypeElectrically short monopole antenna
Invented1958, Richard B. Johnson
Typical useHandheld two-way radios, portable scanners
LoadingNormal-mode helix

The rubber ducky antenna (or rubber duck aerial) is an electrically short monopole antenna that functions somewhat like a base-loaded whip antenna. It consists of a springy wire in the form of a narrow helix, sealed in a rubber or plastic jacket to protect the antenna. It is the standard antenna supplied with most handheld two-way radios, portable radio scanners and other portable radio devices where size and robustness take priority over efficiency.

Design

Because a quarter-wave whip for VHF frequencies would be inconveniently long, the rubber ducky shortens the radiating element by winding it into a normal-mode helix, which adds inductance and brings the short element to resonance. The trade-off is reduced gain and efficiency — often several decibels below a full-size antenna — and substantial losses in the loading structure.

History

The design is generally credited to Richard B. Johnson, who described it in 1958 while working on antennas for portable radios. The flexible jacket made the antenna safe and durable for police and commercial hand-portables, and it became ubiquitous on walkie-talkies, early mobile telephones and Wi-Fi equipment.

Performance

Rubber ducky antennas are typically rated with gains around −5 to −2 dBd relative to a dipole. Performance improves when the radio's chassis provides an adequate counterpoise; held in the hand, the user's body becomes part of the antenna system, making real-world behaviour variable.
