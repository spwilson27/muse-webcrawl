Cuirassiers (from French cuirassier, "wearer of a cuirass") were cavalry equipped with a cuirass, sword and pistols. Cuirassiers first appeared in mid-to-late 15th-century Europe as a form of heavy cavalry, and were the successors of the fully armoured knights of the late Middle Ages. They were the senior heavy cavalry regiments of the French, Austrian, Prussian, Russian and other continental armies into the 20th century.

Development

As firearms made full plate armour impractical, cavalry armour shrank to the cuirass — a steel breastplate and backplate — worn over a buff coat, with a helmet such as the lobster-tailed pot or, later, a crested classical-style helmet. In the English Civil War, units like Haselrig's "Lobsters" fought nearly fully armoured. By the 18th century many armies had reduced or abandoned the cuirass, but Napoleon restored it in France, fielding regiments of cuirassiers whose massed charges at Borodino, Waterloo and elsewhere became iconic images of Napoleonic warfare.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

German, Russian and French cuirassiers retained polished steel breastplates through the Franco-Prussian War and into the First World War, when French cuirassiers rode to the front in 1914 still wearing cuirasses (covered in cloth) before discarding them for trench warfare. Their shock role passed to armoured vehicles, but the name survives in modern armoured regiments, and cuirassiers still appear ceremonially — for example in the French Republican Guard and Italy's Corazzieri, who guard the President of Italy.
