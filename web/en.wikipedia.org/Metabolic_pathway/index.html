Metabolic pathway

In biochemistry, a metabolic pathway is a linked series of chemical reactions occurring within a cell. The reactants, products and intermediates of each enzymatic reaction are known as metabolites, and each step is catalysed by a specific enzyme encoded by the cell's genes. Pathways convert a starting molecule through stepwise modifications into a final product, often releasing or storing energy along the way.

Types

Catabolic pathways break down molecules and release energy — glycolysis and the citric acid cycle feed electrons into oxidative phosphorylation to make ATP. Anabolic pathways consume energy to build complex molecules, such as gluconeogenesis, fatty-acid synthesis and photosynthesis's Calvin cycle. Amphibolic pathways, like the citric acid cycle, serve both directions. Pathways may be linear, branched or cyclic, and they interconnect into a metabolic network whose hubs include glucose-6-phosphate, pyruvate and acetyl-CoA.

Regulation

Cells control flux through pathways by regulating enzyme amounts (gene expression) and activity — through allosteric feedback inhibition by end products, covalent modification such as phosphorylation, compartmentation within organelles, and hormonal signals such as insulin and glucagon coordinating pathways across tissues. Most pathways have a small number of rate-limiting, effectively irreversible steps at which control is exerted.

Importance

Defects in pathway enzymes cause inborn errors of metabolism — phenylketonuria and Tay–Sachs disease are classic examples — while pathway rewiring underlies cancer metabolism (the Warburg effect) and is a major target of drug design, from statins blocking cholesterol synthesis to antibiotics exploiting bacterial pathways absent in humans. Systems biology reconstructs these networks from genomics data in databases such as KEGG.
