Usage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Usage guides record and evaluate how a language is actually written and spoken.

Usage is the manner in which written and spoken language is used, the "points of grammar, syntax, style, and the choice of words" and "the way in which a word or phrase is normally and correctly used". In everyday usage — and among linguists — the term also refers simply to the way people actually use language, regardless of whether that use conforms to any standard.

The concept of correct usage is complicated by the fact that usage varies between social groups, regions, registers and historical periods. Prescriptive authorities such as dictionaries and style guides codify the usage of educated speakers, while descriptive linguistics studies usage as it is actually practised. Much public debate about language — over split infinitives, singular they, or the word literally — turns on the tension between these two approaches.

History

Systematic codification of English usage began in the 18th century, with works such as Robert Lowth's A Short Introduction to English Grammar (1762) and Lindley Murray's English Grammar (1795). In the 20th century, H. W. Fowler's A Dictionary of Modern English Usage (1926) became the most influential English usage guide, followed by works such as Ernest Gowers's The Complete Plain Words (1954), the Chicago Manual of Style, and Bryan Garner's Modern English Usage.

Descriptive and prescriptive usage

Descriptivists hold that the only meaningful standard of correctness is the usage of native speakers; from this standpoint, "errors" are simply variants that have not (yet) gained acceptance. Prescriptivists argue that a standard language is a social tool — facilitating clear communication across regions and generations — and that teaching it is a legitimate educational goal. Most modern usage guides take a middle position: they report the facts of usage, note which forms are disputed, and advise writers on which choices are safe in formal contexts.

Usage labels in dictionaries

Dictionaries record usage with labels such as archaic, obsolete, colloquial, slang, dialect, nonstandard and offensive, based on evidence from corpora of published and spoken language. Large text corpora, such as the British National Corpus and the Corpus of Contemporary American English, have made usage judgements increasingly empirical.
