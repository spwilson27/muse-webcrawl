Taxus is a genus of coniferous shrubs and small trees in the yew family, Taxaceae, commonly known as yews. Species of Taxus are slow-growing and very long-lived, and occur across temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere in Europe, Asia, North Africa, and North America.

Description

Yews are evergreen, with flat, dark-green needle-like leaves arranged spirally on the stem but twisted to appear two-ranked. They are mostly dioecious: male trees produce small pollen cones, while female trees produce highly modified cones consisting of a single seed partly enclosed in a fleshy, bright-red cup called an aril, which is eaten and dispersed by birds.

Toxicity and medicine

Almost all parts of yew trees, except the aril flesh, contain poisonous taxine alkaloids that affect the heart; ingestion has killed livestock and humans. Paradoxically, the bark of the Pacific yew (T. brevifolia) yielded the anticancer drug paclitaxel (Taxol), now produced semi-synthetically and from other yew species and used against ovarian, breast, and lung cancers.

Cultural significance

Yews are closely associated with churchyards in Britain and Ireland, where some specimens are thousands of years old, and yew wood — hard, elastic, and durable — was prized for longbows and fine furniture. The Fortingall Yew in Scotland is among the oldest known trees in Europe.
