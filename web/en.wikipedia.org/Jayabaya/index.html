Jayabaya (Javanese: Jayabayå) was a king of Kediri in East Java who reigned from about 1135 to 1159. His reign is remembered in Javanese tradition as a golden age of peace, prosperity, and literary flowering, and his name is inseparably linked with the famous Jangka Jayabaya — a body of prophecies attributed to him that has influenced Javanese political culture for centuries.

Reign

Jayabaya ruled from Daha (Kediri), one of the successor kingdoms of the Mataram kingdom after its division under Airlangga. Under Jayabaya, Kediri extended its influence over much of Java and into maritime trade networks reaching the spice islands. His reign saw the production of important Old Javanese literature: the court poet Mpu Sedah began, and Mpu Panuluh completed, the Kakawin Bharatayuddha (1157), an Old Javanese rendering of the climactic war of the Mahabharata, while Panuluh also composed the Hariwangsa and is associated with the Gatotkacasraya. Jayabaya was regarded by tradition as an avatar-like, semi-divine ruler — in Javanese memory a manifestation of the god Vishnu.

The Jayabaya prophecies

The Jangka Jayabaya ("Jayabaya's Era/Prophecies") is a collection of prophetic verses in Javanese, in their surviving form largely compiled and reshaped much later — most scholars date the core texts to the 18th–19th centuries, notably in the milieu of the Surakarta court poet Ranggawarsita — but attributed to the 12th-century king. The prophecies describe ages of history unfolding toward a time of upheaval (zaman edan, the "mad age"), followed by the coming of a just king, the Ratu Adil, who would restore harmony and prosperity to Java.

Political influence

The prophecies have repeatedly shaped Indonesian political movements. They were invoked during the Java War of Prince Diponegoro (1825–1830); during the Japanese occupation (1942–1945), propaganda claimed the prophecies foresaw liberation from Dutch rule by a power from the north; and in the Sukarno era and after, speculation about the Ratu Adil and the sequence of rulers encoded in the prophecies — including the popular "Notonegoro" acrostic said to foretell presidential names — became a staple of Javanese political commentary. A modern grave complex and museum at Menang, near Kediri, commemorates Jayabaya as a pilgrimage site.
