Wealth

Measured asNet worth (assets minus liabilities)
ComponentsFinancial assets, real estate, goods
ContrastIncome (a flow, not a stock)

Wealth is the abundance of valuable financial assets or physical possessions which can be converted into a form that can be used for transactions. In modern economics, the wealth of an individual or household is usually measured as net worth: the total value of assets — such as savings, investments, property and durable goods — minus debts and other liabilities. Wealth is a stock, accumulated over time, whereas income is a flow received per period.

Measurement and distribution

Statisticians measure wealth through household surveys and balance-sheet data; global estimates are published in reports such as the UBS Global Wealth Report and the World Inequality Report. Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income: worldwide, the richest 1% of adults own roughly two-fifths of all personal wealth, while the bottom half own only a few percent. At the end of the 2010s, total global household wealth was estimated in the hundreds of trillions of U.S. dollars.

Accumulation

Wealth is accumulated by saving out of income, by capital gains on assets such as housing and equities, and by inheritance. Economists debate the roles of returns on capital (Thomas Piketty's r > g thesis), entrepreneurship, and institutions in driving concentration. Older traditions treated wealth more broadly: Adam Smith defined a nation's wealth by its productive capacity, and many cultures value non-material wealth such as health, knowledge and social standing — ideas formalised in concepts like human capital and natural capital.
