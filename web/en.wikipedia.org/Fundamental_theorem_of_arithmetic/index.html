Fundamental theorem of arithmetic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Factor tree decomposing 120 into prime factors 2³ × 3 × 5]

In number theory, the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, also called the unique factorisation theorem, states that every integer greater than 1 can be represented uniquely as a product of prime numbers, up to the order of the factors. For example,

1200 = 24 × 31 × 52 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 3 × 5 × 5

and no other factorisation of 1200 into primes exists. The primes themselves are considered products of a single prime factor. The theorem guarantees both the existence of a prime factorisation (proved by strong induction: a composite number splits into smaller factors, which eventually must be prime) and its uniqueness (proved using Euclid's lemma: if a prime p divides a product ab, then p divides a or p divides b).

History

Elements of the theorem appear in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC): Book VII contains the lemma bearing Euclid's name, and Book IX proves there are infinitely many primes and that composite numbers decompose into primes. The first full, explicit statement and proof is credited to Carl Friedrich Gauss in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801).

Applications

Unique factorisation underlies much of elementary number theory: canonical representations simplify proofs about greatest common divisors, least common multiples, perfect numbers, and rational numbers. It also motivates the generalisation to other rings: integral domains in which every element factors uniquely into irreducibles are called unique factorisation domains (UFDs); the theorem says the integers are a UFD. In rings where unique factorisation fails — famously ℤ[√−5] — the failure inspired Kummer's "ideal numbers" and Dedekind's theory of ideals.

Generalisation

The concept extends far beyond the integers: polynomial rings over fields and the ring of Gaussian integers are also unique factorisation domains, while every principal ideal domain is a UFD. The theorem thus anchors a large part of abstract algebra and algebraic number theory.
