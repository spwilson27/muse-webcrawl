A hieroglyph (from Greek hieros, "sacred", and glyphein, "to carve") is a stylised picture of an object representing a word, syllable, or sound, as found in several ancient writing systems. The term is used most often of the characters of the formal writing system of ancient Egypt, but also applies to other pictorial scripts, including Anatolian (Luwian) hieroglyphs, Cretan hieroglyphs, the Maya script, and the Mi'kmaq hieroglyphic writing of eastern Canada.

Egyptian hieroglyphs

The Egyptian system combined logographic, alphabetic, and determinative signs: a single sign could stand for a whole word (logogram), for one, two, or three consonants (phonograms), or silently classify the word it followed (determinative). Around 700 signs were in regular use in the classical Middle Egyptian period, growing to several thousand in the Ptolemaic era. Reading was usually right-to-left or left-to-right depending on the direction the signs faced. Knowledge of the script was lost by late antiquity and was only recovered after the discovery of the Rosetta Stone and its decipherment by Jean-François Champollion in the 1820s.

Other hieroglyphic scripts

The unrelated Anatolian hieroglyphs of the Hittite and Luwian world, the undeciphered Cretan hieroglyphs of Bronze Age Crete, and the logosyllabic Maya glyphs of Mesoamerica are also called hieroglyphic because of their pictorial character, as are the sujij characters used by the Mi'kmaq people, systematised by missionaries in the 17th century.
