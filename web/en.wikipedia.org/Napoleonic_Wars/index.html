The Napoleonic Wars (1803–1815) were a series of global conflicts pitting the French Empire and its allies, led by Napoleon I, against a fluctuating array of European states formed into various Coalitions. They were a continuation, fuelled by unresolved disputes, of the French Revolutionary Wars, which ran from 1792 to 1802. The wars revolutionised European armies and warfare: mass conscription, the corps system, and rapid manoeuvre allowed battles of unprecedented scale, while France spread revolutionary legal and administrative reforms — and provoked nationalist resistance — across the continent. The conflict ended with Napoleon's defeat at Waterloo in June 1815 and the settlement of the Congress of Vienna.

Course of the wars

The Peace of Amiens collapsed in 1803, renewing war between Britain and France. In 1805 Napoleon destroyed the Third Coalition with the Ulm campaign and the victory at Austerlitz, while Britain secured naval supremacy at Trafalgar. Prussia was crushed at Jena–Auerstedt in 1806. The Treaty of Tilsit (1807) made France dominant in Europe; Napoleon then imposed the Continental System against British trade, invaded Iberia (the Peninsular War, 1808–1814 — his "Spanish ulcer"), defeated Austria again at Wagram (1809), and in 1812 invaded Russia with some 600,000 men. The Russian campaign destroyed the Grande Armée. A Sixth Coalition defeated Napoleon at Leipzig (1813), invaded France, and forced his abdication in April 1814. His return during the Hundred Days ended at Waterloo (18 June 1815), followed by exile to Saint Helena.

Military innovations

Napoleonic warfare relied on the levée en masse for manpower, divisional and corps organisation for flexible march and concentration, horse artillery, and living off the land to sustain speed. Opposing powers gradually reformed in response — Prussian staff reforms, British military professionalism under Wellington, and coalition strategy that avoided battle with Napoleon himself while defeating his marshals.

Consequences

The wars redrew Europe's map, dissolved the Holy Roman Empire (1806), spread the Napoleonic Code and abolition of feudal privileges in many regions, stimulated German and Italian nationalism, weakened Spain and Portugal (triggering Latin American independence wars), and elevated Britain to a century of naval and industrial predominance. The Congress of Vienna (1814–15) constructed a balance-of-power order intended to prevent another general European war, and the "Concert of Europe" broadly kept the great-power peace for decades.
