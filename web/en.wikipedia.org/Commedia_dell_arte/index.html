Commedia dell'arte

Commedia dell'arte was an early form of professional theatre that originated in Italy in the 16th century and spread throughout Europe. Its performances were improvised around a brief scenario (canovaccio) by troupes of actors playing recognisable stock characters, many of them masked. The form influenced dramatists from Molière and Shakespeare to modern physical theatre and clowning.

Stock characters

The masked vecchi (old men) included the miserly Venetian merchant Pantalone and the pedantic Bolognese Doctor (Il Dottore). The zanni were scheming servants such as Arlecchino (Harlequin) and Brighella, whose acrobatics and comic lazzi drove the plots. Unmasked characters included the young lovers (innamorati) and the boastful Spanish captain, Il Capitano. Columbina, the witty servant girl, often proved the cleverest figure on stage.

Performance practice

Troupes of ten or twelve actors travelled between towns, performing in marketplaces and courts. Each actor specialised in one role for life, memorising stock speeches, songs, and comic routines to weave into each improvised scenario. Women performed on stage in commedia troupes at an unusually early date for European theatre.

Legacy

By the 18th century the form declined as scripted comedy rose, but its characters survive in Punch and Judy shows, pantomime, Harlequinade, and the plays of Carlo Goldoni and Carlo Gozzi, who debated its reform. Twentieth-century practitioners such as Jacques Lecoq revived its mask and improvisation techniques.
