First Czechoslovak Republic

The First Czechoslovak Republic (Czech and Slovak: První československá republika) was the first Czechoslovak state that existed from 1918 to 1938, when it was compelled to cede the Sudetenland to Nazi Germany in the Munich Agreement. The state was commonly called Czechoslovakia (Československo).

Foundation and character

The republic was proclaimed on 28 October 1918 as Austria-Hungary collapsed at the end of the First World War, uniting the Czech lands of Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia with Slovakia and (from 1919) Subcarpathian Ruthenia. Its first president was the philosopher Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, succeeded in 1935 by Edvard Beneš. Czechoslovakia was a parliamentary democracy — the only one in Central and Eastern Europe to survive through the interwar period — with a strong industrial base inherited from the Habsburg lands and an influential network of alliances, notably the Little Entente and a treaty with France.

Minorities and crisis

The state's central difficulty was its multiethnic composition: ethnic Germans made up roughly a quarter of the population, concentrated in the borderlands (the Sudetenland), alongside Hungarian, Polish and Ruthenian minorities. In the 1930s the Sudeten German Party, subsidised by Berlin, campaigned for autonomy and then union with Germany. The Munich Agreement of September 1938, concluded by Germany, Britain, France and Italy without Czechoslovak participation, forced the republic to surrender the Sudetenland; Poland and Hungary also seized territory. The rump "Second Republic" lasted only until Germany occupied Prague in March 1939.
