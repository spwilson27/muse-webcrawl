United States Mint

The United States Mint is a bureau of the Department of the Treasury responsible for producing coinage for the United States and for controlling the movement of bullion. It operates four active production facilities — in Philadelphia, Denver, San Francisco and West Point — and the bullion depository at Fort Knox. Created by the Coinage Act of 1792, the Mint strikes billions of circulating coins each year for the Federal Reserve, and also produces commemorative coins, medals, and bullion coins such as the American Eagle.

History

The Constitution gave Congress the power "to coin Money." The Coinage Act of 2 April 1792 established the Mint in Philadelphia, then the national capital, and defined the dollar on a bimetallic standard of gold and silver. President Washington appointed the scientist David Rittenhouse as its first director, and the first coins — Flowing Hair silver dollars and cents — appeared in 1793–94. Branch mints followed the gold rushes (Charlotte, Dahlonega, New Orleans, San Francisco, Carson City, Denver), several closing or being converted over the 19th and 20th centuries. The Mint also struck coins for foreign governments for much of its history.

Products

Beyond circulating coinage — pennies, nickels, dimes, quarters (including the 50 State, America the Beautiful and American Women quarter programmes), half dollars and dollar coins — the Mint produces proof and uncirculated collector sets, congressional gold medals, and bullion products: American Eagle gold, silver, platinum and palladium coins, American Buffalo gold coins, and (since 2021) modern strikings of the Morgan and Peace dollars. Seigniorage — the difference between a coin's face value and production cost — returns billions of dollars to the Treasury, though the cent and nickel have for years cost more to make than their face value.
