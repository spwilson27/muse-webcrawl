Dimensional regularization

In theoretical physics, dimensional regularization is a method introduced by Gerard 't Hooft and Martinus J. G. Veltman for regularising integrals in quantum field theory that yield divergent results, the so-called loop integrals. The method proceeds by evaluating the integrals not in the physical four dimensions of spacetime, but in a generic number of dimensions d, treated as a continuous complex parameter.

How it works

Loop integrals that diverge in four dimensions are convergent for slightly smaller values of d. Results are then analytically continued towards d = 4, where ultraviolet divergences appear as poles in the small parameter ε = 4 − d. These poles can be absorbed into redefinitions of the theory's parameters (renormalisation), or cancel between diagrams in physically finite quantities. Because the continuation preserves the symmetries of the theory — in particular gauge invariance and Lorentz invariance — dimensional regularisation avoids many artifacts of momentum-cutoff schemes.

History and use

't Hooft and Veltman proposed the method in 1972, and it played a central role in their proof that non-abelian gauge theories are renormalisable, work recognised by the 1999 Nobel Prize in Physics. Independently, similar ideas were published by Carlos Bollini and Juan Giambiagi. Dimensional regularisation, especially in its modified minimal subtraction (MS-bar) form, is the standard regulator in perturbative calculations for the Standard Model, including multi-loop computations of anomalous dimensions and scattering amplitudes.

Limitations

Objects intrinsically tied to four dimensions — such as the Levi-Civita symbol and γ5 in chiral theories — require special prescriptions when continued to d dimensions, leading to variants such as the 't Hooft–Veltman scheme and dimensional reduction. The analytic continuation also has no literal geometric picture of fractional dimensions, though fractal geometry offers suggestive analogies.
