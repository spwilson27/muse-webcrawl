Extended family

An extended family is a family that extends beyond the nuclear family of parents and their children to include grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins and other relatives, often living in the same household or in close proximity. In anthropology and sociology the term contrasts with the nuclear family, which consists of two parents and their children only.

Forms and distribution

Extended families may be organised around a stem family, in which one married child remains in the parental home, or a joint family, common historically in South Asia, in which several married siblings share a household and property. Multi-generational households remain the norm in much of Asia, Africa, the Middle East and Latin America, and they were also widespread in pre-industrial Europe, although historians debate how common they were in northwestern Europe, where late marriage and neolocal households led the demographer John Hajnal to identify a distinct "European marriage pattern".

Functions

Extended kin networks pool income, share childcare and elder care, transmit property and skills, and provide a safety net in the absence of state welfare. Sociologists note that even where relatives do not co-reside — for example in contemporary Britain and the United States — extended families often remain important sources of support, a pattern described as the "modified extended family". Rising housing costs and population aging have contributed to a renewed growth of multi-generational living in many developed countries.
