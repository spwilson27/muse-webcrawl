Wealth inequality in the United States

Wealth inequality in the United States concerns the unequal distribution of assets among residents of the United States. Wealth—net worth, meaning assets minus debts—is much more concentrated than income. According to Federal Reserve data, the top 1 percent of households hold roughly a third of the nation's wealth, while the bottom half of the population holds only a few percent.

Measurement

The Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, conducted every three years, is the standard source for household wealth data, supplemented by the Distributional Financial Accounts. Economists such as Edward Wolff, and Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, have produced long-run estimates showing that wealth concentration in the early twenty-first century reached levels comparable to the late 1920s.

Trends

Wealth inequality declined from the Great Depression through the 1970s, as the middle class accumulated housing equity and pensions, and then rose steadily from around 1980. Drivers commonly cited include rising values of equities and businesses owned disproportionately by the affluent, stagnant wages and low saving rates among lower-income households, student and other consumer debt, and tax policy. Homeownership is the principal channel of wealth accumulation for the middle class, so housing price changes strongly affect measured inequality.

Racial and demographic gaps

Median white household wealth has long been several times median Black and Hispanic household wealth, a gap attributed to historical discrimination in housing, credit, and inheritance. Wealth also varies sharply by age and education.

Policy debate

Proposals debated in response include progressive taxation of income and estates, wealth tax proposals advanced by some economists and legislators, expanded retirement savings programs, and "baby bonds". Critics of intervention argue that wealth concentration partly reflects age, entrepreneurship, and differences in saving behavior, and that mobility between wealth classes remains substantial.
