Graph limit

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a graph limit is a limit object for a sequence of graphs. The theory of graph limits is used to study large graphs by approximating them with continuous objects, most commonly graphons in the theory of dense graphs. The subject grew rapidly in the 2000s, notably through work of László Lovász and coauthors.

Dense graph limits

A sequence of dense graphs (graphs whose number of edges grows quadratically with the number of vertices) is said to converge if, for every fixed small graph, the densities of copies of that small graph in the sequence approach a limit. Lovász and Balázs Szegedy showed that every convergent sequence of dense graphs has a limit represented by a graphon: a symmetric measurable function W from the unit square to [0, 1]. A graphon can be regarded as a "continuous graph" from which finite random graphs can be sampled by choosing random vertices in [0, 1] and connecting pairs with probability given by the function.

Applications

Graph limits provide a framework for extremal graph theory, property testing and the study of large networks. Concepts such as the cut distance between graphs, Szemerédi's regularity lemma and random graph models are unified in this theory. Limits for sparse graphs require different tools, including local weak convergence and the objects known as graphings.

Key figures

The theory was developed by Christian Borgs, Jennifer Chayes, László Lovász, Vera Sós, Balázs Szegedy and Katalin Vesztergombi, among others. Lovász's book Large Networks and Graph Limits (2012) is the standard reference.
