The mines in the Battle of Messines were a series of deep tunnels dug by British Empire tunnelling companies beneath German positions on the Messines–Wytschaete ridge south of Ypres in Belgium, and filled with explosives. At 03:10 on 7 June 1917, nineteen of the mines were detonated simultaneously at the start of the Battle of Messines, killing an estimated 10,000 German soldiers in one of the largest non-nuclear explosions in history and demolishing the ridge defences before the infantry assault.

Craters such as the Spanbroekmolen "Pool of Peace" survive as memorials.

Background and digging

The ridge had been in German hands since 1914, dominating the Ypres Salient. From 1915–16, under the direction of Major-General (later Sir) John Norton-Griffiths and the Engineer-in-Chief's staff, specialist tunnelling companies recruited from coal miners, sewer workers and "clay-kickers" drove galleries through the deep blue Ypresian clay, in places more than 30 metres below the surface, to chambers beneath German strongpoints from Hill 60 in the north to Ploegsteert in the south. German counter-miners fought a silent underground war; several British mines were discovered, fought over or damaged, and one at La Petite Douve was abandoned after a German camouflet broke into it — its charged chamber remains buried today.

Detonation

At zero hour the mines were fired in sequence along 9 miles of front. The largest charge, at St Eloi, was about 43 tonnes; the Spanbroekmolen mine held some 41 tonnes of ammonal and left a crater 130 metres wide, now the "Pool of Peace". Eyewitnesses described the earth heaving "like the swell of the sea"; the sound was reportedly heard in London and Dublin. Prime Minister David Lloyd George later wrote that the explosion would be counted among the great events of the war. General Plumer is said to have remarked to his staff the night before, "Gentlemen, we may not make history tomorrow, but we shall certainly change the geography."

Aftermath

The stunned German front-line garrisons were overrun; the Second Army captured the ridge by the end of the day, one of the clearest British successes of the war to that point, at a cost of some 25,000 casualties against about 25,000 German. Two mines were not fired: one at La Petite Douve (abandoned) and one near Ploegsteert. In 1955 a lightning strike detonated the remaining Birdcage 3 charge near Ploegsteert Wood, killing a cow and leaving a crater, a reminder that the unexploded La Petite Douve mine still lies beneath a farm.
