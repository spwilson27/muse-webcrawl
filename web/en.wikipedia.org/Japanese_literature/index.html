Japanese literature spans nearly two millennia, from the myth-histories of the 8th century to the global readership of contemporary novelists. Strongly influenced in its early centuries by Chinese literature, it developed distinctive forms—the court diary, the linked verse, the haiku, and the "I-novel"—while absorbing and transforming foreign models.

Classical period

The earliest extant works are the Kojiki (712) and Nihon Shoki (720), chronicles mixing myth and imperial history, and the Man'yōshū (c. 759), an anthology of some 4,500 poems. In the Heian period (794–1185), court women writing in the vernacular kana script produced masterpieces: Sei Shōnagon's The Pillow Book and Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji, a vast psychological narrative. Later medieval literature includes war tales such as The Tale of the Heike and the essays of the hermit Kamo no Chōmei and the monk Yoshida Kenkō.

Early modern and modern literature

During the Edo period (1603–1868), popular fiction flourished alongside haiku by Matsuo Bashō, who perfected the form, and the puppet and kabuki dramas of Chikamatsu Monzaemon. After the Meiji Restoration (1868), writers such as Natsume Sōseki and Mori Ōgai confronted Westernisation; Sōseki's novels remain canonical. The confessional I-novel dominated the early 20th century.

Postwar authors include Yukio Mishima, Yasunari Kawabata and Kenzaburō Ōe (both Nobel laureates), and, in the contemporary era, Haruki Murakami, whose works have achieved worldwide popularity, along with Banana Yoshimoto and Yōko Ogawa. Japanese poetry, drama, manga, and light novels form part of a diverse literary culture.
