Pilotage

Pilotage is the practice of navigating a vessel through dangerous or congested waters — such as harbours, river mouths, and channels — with the assistance of a maritime pilot, a mariner with expert local knowledge of depths, tides, currents, hazards, and port procedures. In most major ports pilotage is compulsory for large vessels.

The maritime pilot

Pilots are typically master mariners who have served at sea and then trained intensively for a particular district. The pilot boards the ship — often climbing a rope ladder from a fast pilot boat, sometimes in rough seas — and advises the master, who retains legal command. This division of responsibility, centred on the pilot's local expertise and the master's knowledge of the ship, is a distinctive feature of the system; the word "pilotage" also describes the fee charged for the service.

Regulation and organization

Pilotage is usually organized by port or national authorities, historically by bodies such as Trinity House in England (chartered 1514). Compulsory pilotage districts, licensing standards, and exemptions for regular callers are set by law. The term "pilotage" is also used in aviation and inland navigation for navigating by visual landmarks, and in law for the guiding of a bill through a legislature.
