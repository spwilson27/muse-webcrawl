Thermus aquaticus is a species of extremely thermophilic bacterium that can tolerate high temperatures. It is the source of the heat-resistant enzyme Taq DNA polymerase, one of the most important enzymes in molecular biology, essential for the polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Discovery and biology

The bacterium was discovered in 1969 by Thomas D. Brock and Hudson Freeze in the hot springs of Yellowstone National Park, notably Mushroom Spring, and described in 1969. T. aquaticus is a rod-shaped, Gram-negative, aerobic heterotroph that thrives between about 50 and 80 °C, with an optimum near 65–70 °C — temperatures once thought incompatible with life. Its proteins and membranes are adapted to remain stable and functional in near-boiling water.

Taq polymerase and PCR

A DNA polymerase isolated from T. aquaticus in 1976, later called Taq polymerase, survives the repeated heating to ~95 °C used in PCR. Its use, described by researchers at Cetus Corporation in the 1980s, allowed the reaction to be automated in a thermal cycler, transforming genetics, medicine, and forensics. The discovery and commercialization of Taq polymerase became the subject of famous patent disputes, and Kary Mullis received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for inventing PCR.
