Legend

A legend is a genre of folklore that consists of a narrative featuring human actions, believed or perceived to have taken place in human history. Narratives in this genre may demonstrate human values, and possess certain qualities that give the tale verisimilitude. Legend, for its active and passive participants, may include miracles. Legends may be transformed over time to keep them fresh and vital. Many legends operate within the realm of uncertainty, never being entirely believed by the participants but also never being resolutely doubted.

Etymology and history

The word legend comes from the Latin legenda, "things to be read", originally referring to written lives of saints read aloud in churches and refectories — collections such as Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend (c. 1260) were among the most popular books of the Middle Ages. In modern usage, the Broader definition covers any traditional story popularly regarded as historical but unauthenticated.

Legend versus myth and folktale

Folklorists distinguish the genres by setting, characters and attitude: myths are sacred narratives set in a remote, qualitatively different past, involving gods and explaining the world; folktales (including fairy tales) are avowedly fictional, set in an indeterminate time and place; legends are told as true and are anchored to specific places, people and events of the historical past. In practice the categories blur: Arthurian material, Robin Hood, the Trojan War cycle and tales of King Arthur's court have elements of all three.

Types

Common categories include saint's legends (hagiography), heroic and historical legends (El Cid, William Tell, Joan of Arc), local or place legends attached to landmarks and treasures, etiological legends explaining names or customs, and contemporary or urban legends — plausible but false stories (poisoned Halloween sweets, alligators in sewers) that circulate as news and are spread today by social media. A person may also be called "a legend" for extraordinary achievement, and "legend" denotes the explanatory key on a map or chart, as well as the inscription on a coin.

Study

The academic study of legends began with the Brothers Grimm, whose Deutsche Sagen (1816–1818) collected German legends as counterparts to their fairy tales. Modern legend scholarship (e.g., Linda Dégh, Jan Harold Brunvand) examines legends as expressions of belief and social anxiety, describing the process of ostension, in which people act out legendary narratives in real life.
