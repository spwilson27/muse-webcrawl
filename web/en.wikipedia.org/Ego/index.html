Ego

The ego (Latin for "I") is, in psychoanalytic theory, the component of the psyche that mediates between the instinctual id, the moralizing superego, and external reality. The term is also used more generally to refer to a person's sense of self or self-importance.

Freudian model

In Sigmund Freud's structural model of the psyche, introduced in The Ego and the Id (1923), the ego develops out of the id in infancy as the infant learns to negotiate with the outside world. Operating according to the reality principle, the ego delays or redirects the id's drives (the pleasure principle) into socially acceptable forms. The superego, formed later through internalized parental and social standards, judges the ego's actions and produces feelings such as guilt and pride.

The ego employs defense mechanisms — including repression, projection, denial, rationalization, and sublimation — to manage anxiety arising from conflicts among the id, superego, and reality.

Later developments

Ego psychology, developed by Anna Freud, Heinz Hartmann, and Erik Erikson, emphasized the ego's autonomous functions such as perception, memory, and adaptation. Carl Jung used "ego" for the centre of consciousness, distinct from the Self. In everyday usage, "ego" often denotes excessive self-regard, and "egotism" is a related concept.

In other traditions

In many Eastern spiritual traditions, the ego is viewed as an illusory sense of separate self whose transcendence is a goal of practice. Transpersonal psychology and modern mindfulness-based approaches similarly discuss loosening identification with the ego.
