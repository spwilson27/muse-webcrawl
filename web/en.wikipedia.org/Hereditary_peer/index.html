Hereditary peer

The peerage is the collective term for the system of titles used in the United Kingdom. A hereditary peer is a peer whose dignity may be inherited; the holder is a member of the peerage whose title passes by descent, usually to an heir determined by the terms of the original grant (most commonly primogeniture among male-line descendants).

Hereditary peerages are one of the five ranks of the British nobility: duke, marquess, earl, viscount, and baron. Until the House of Lords Act 1999, all hereditary peers (with certain exceptions, such as peeresses in their own right before 1963) had the right to sit in the House of Lords. The 1999 Act removed the automatic right, but allowed 92 hereditary peers to remain: 90 elected by and from the hereditary peers, plus the Earl Marshal and the Lord Great Chamberlain, who retain their seats by virtue of their offices.

History

The hereditary peerage evolved from the feudal baronage of Norman England: tenants-in-chief who held land directly from the king were summoned to his council, and from the 13th century these summonses developed into hereditary rights to sit in Parliament. Peerages were later created by letters patent with defined remainders. For centuries the House of Lords was overwhelmingly hereditary; the Life Peerages Act 1958 introduced the modern life peerage, appointed for the holder's lifetime only, and membership gradually shifted toward appointed life peers.

The 92 remaining hereditary peers

The 92 seats retained after 1999 are filled by internal by-elections among the hereditary peers when a vacancy occurs — a mechanism that has been repeatedly criticised as anachronistic and, because almost all hereditary peers are men (most titles descending only in the male line), as reinforcing the chamber's gender imbalance. Successive governments have proposed removing the remaining hereditary peers; legislation to end the by-elections and phase out the reserved seats was introduced in the 2024 Parliament as part of the House of Lords (Hereditary Peers) Bill.

Creation of new hereditary peerages

No new hereditary peerages have been created for non-members of the royal family since 1964, when Harold Wilson's government established the convention against them; a handful were granted under Margaret Thatcher in the 1980s (including the earldom of Stockton for Harold Macmillan). Hereditary peerages continue to be conferred on members of the royal family, traditionally upon marriage.

Styles and privileges

Hereditary peers are styled according to rank — "His Grace" for dukes, "the Most Honourable" for marquesses, and "the Right Honourable" for earls, viscounts, and barons. Historical privileges of the peerage, such as trial by fellow peers and freedom from arrest in civil cases, have largely been abolished. Peers may disclaim their peerages for life under the Peerage Act 1963, as Tony Benn famously did.
