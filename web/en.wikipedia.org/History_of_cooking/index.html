History of cooking

The history of cooking begins with the controlled use of fire by early humans — arguably a decisive step in human evolution — and encompasses the development of agriculture, the rise of the world's culinary traditions, the professionalisation of cooking, and the global exchange of ingredients and techniques.

Origins: fire and early humans

Evidence for controlled fire dates back at least several hundred thousand years, and anthropologist Richard Wrangham has argued that cooking softened food, released more calories, and helped drive the evolution of larger brains and smaller guts in the genus Homo. Roasting over open flames was followed by earth ovens, hot stones and, with pottery from around 20,000 years ago in East Asia, boiling and steaming.

Agriculture and ancient cuisines

The Neolithic Revolution brought domesticated grains, legumes and animals, bread, beer, cheese and fermented foods. The earliest surviving recipes are Mesopotamian cuneiform tablets (c. 1700 BCE) listing stews and breads. Ancient Egyptian, Greek and Roman cuisines developed bread-making, olive oil, wine and garum; Apicius preserves Roman fine cooking. In China, the stir-fry and noodle traditions matured over the Han and Song dynasties; Indian cooking developed its distinctive spice blends; and Mesoamerica built a cuisine on maize, beans and chillies.

Medieval and early modern cooking

Medieval European court cooking, recorded in books such as Le Viandier and The Forme of Cury, favoured spices, sweet–sour flavours and spectacle; Islamic and Persian cuisines strongly influenced Sicily and Spain. The Columbian exchange after 1492 transformed world cooking, moving tomatoes, potatoes, chillies and maize to the Old World and wheat, livestock and sugar to the Americas. Professional guilds of cooks formed, and printed cookbooks spread technique to literate households.

Modern cooking

In the nineteenth century, French haute cuisine was codified by Antonin Carême and Auguste Escoffier, whose brigade system and Guide Culinaire (1903) set restaurant standards worldwide. Industrialisation brought canning, refrigeration, gas and electric stoves, and convenience foods; domestic cookbooks by authors such as Isabella Beeton and Julia Child shaped home cooking. The late twentieth century saw nouvelle cuisine, fusion cooking, and molecular gastronomy (Ferran Adrià, Heston Blumenthal), while twenty-first-century cooking is marked by global street-food culture, renewed interest in fermentation and local sourcing, and television and internet food media.
