The Eleanor crosses were twelve ornate stone monuments erected by King Edward I of England between 1291 and 1294 to mark the nightly resting places of the funeral cortège of his wife, Queen Eleanor of Castile, who died at Harby in Nottinghamshire on 28 November 1290. Her body was carried to Westminster Abbey for burial, and a cross was raised at each stop along the roughly 200-mile route.

The twelve crosses

The crosses stood at Lincoln, Grantham, Stamford, Geddington, Hardingstone (Northampton), Stony Stratford, Woburn, Dunstable, St Albans, Waltham, Westcheap (Cheapside, London) and Charing (Charing Cross, London). Each was a tall, tiered Gothic structure decorated with statues of the queen, heraldry and pinnacles, built by leading royal masons and sculptors of the day.

Survival and legacy

Only three original crosses survive, at Geddington, Hardingstone and Waltham Cross; the rest were destroyed, mostly in the Civil War era. Several were later replaced by memorials, most famously the Victorian replica at Charing Cross in London (1865). The crosses are considered masterpieces of early English Gothic sculpture and a striking royal memorial to a queen whom Edward mourned deeply.
