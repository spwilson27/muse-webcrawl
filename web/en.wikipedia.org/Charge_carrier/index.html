Charge carrier


Charge carriers are particles or quasiparticles that carry electric charge through a medium.

A charge carrier is a particle or quasiparticle that is free to move, carrying an electric charge, especially through electrical conductors. Examples include electrons, ions and holes. The term is used most commonly in solid-state physics and semiconductor science.

In different media

In metals, the charge carriers are electrons, one or two from each atom being free to move through the material as a "sea" of conduction electrons. In electrolytes and ionised gases (plasma), the carriers are positively charged cations and negatively charged anions, which move in opposite directions. In a vacuum, electrons emitted by thermionic emission or field emission carry the current, as in cathode-ray tubes.

In semiconductors

In semiconductors, current is carried both by electrons in the conduction band and by holes — the vacancies left in the valence band, which behave as mobile positive charges. Carriers present in the greater concentration are called majority carriers and the others minority carriers; in n-type material the electrons are the majority carriers, and in p-type material the holes. Carrier concentration, mobility and lifetime determine a semiconductor's conductivity and the behaviour of devices such as diodes and transistors.

Generation and recombination

Carriers are generated when electrons absorb energy — thermal, optical or electrical — and are removed when electrons and holes recombine, processes that underlie light-emitting diodes, solar cells and photodetectors.
