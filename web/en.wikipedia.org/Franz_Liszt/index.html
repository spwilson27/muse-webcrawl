Franz Liszt (Hungarian: Liszt Ferenc; 22 October 1811 – 31 July 1886) was a Hungarian composer, virtuoso pianist, conductor, and teacher of the Romantic era. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest pianists of all time, and as a composer he was one of the most prominent representatives of the New German School, inventing the symphonic poem.

Early life and virtuoso years

Born in Raiding (then Doborján), Liszt was taught piano by his father Ádám and later studied in Vienna with Carl Czerny and composition with Antonio Salieri. Moving to Paris, he became the archetypal touring virtuoso of the 1830s and 1840s; the frenzy of his audiences was dubbed "Lisztomania" by Heinrich Heine. His Transcendental Études and operatic paraphrases stretched piano technique to new limits.

Weimar years

In 1848 Liszt settled in Weimar as Kapellmeister, giving up the virtuoso circuit. There he composed most of his mature orchestral works, including twelve symphonic poems such as Les préludes and Mazeppa, the Faust and Dante symphonies, and the Piano Sonata in B minor. He championed the music of Wagner — his future son-in-law — and Berlioz, and taught a generation of pianists free of charge.

Later life

From 1861 Liszt divided his time among Rome, Weimar, and Budapest (the so-called vie trifurquée). He took minor orders in the Catholic Church in 1865 and composed much sacred music. His late piano works, sparse and harmonically daring, anticipated twentieth-century music. He died of pneumonia in Bayreuth in 1886 while attending the Wagner festival.

Legacy

Liszt's output includes the nineteen Hungarian Rhapsodies, the Années de pèlerinage, two piano concertos, and some 1,400 works in total. He is credited with inventing the masterclass and the solo recital format.
