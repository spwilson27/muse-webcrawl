Mechanism design

Mechanism design: a designer chooses the rules of a game so that self-interested agents reach a desired outcome.

Mechanism design, sometimes called implementation theory or institution design, is a field in economics and game theory that explores the design of rules, institutions, and algorithms to achieve specific goals, taking into account the strategic behaviour of agents with private information. It has been called "reverse game theory": instead of analysing a given game, the designer engineers the game itself.

The 2007 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences was awarded to Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin, and Roger Myerson "for having laid the foundations of mechanism design theory."

Key concepts

The revelation principle

The revelation principle states that for any outcome implementable by some mechanism, there is a "direct mechanism" in which agents simply report their private information (their "types") and telling the truth is an equilibrium. This simplifies the designer's problem to finding mechanisms satisfying incentive-compatibility and participation constraints.

Incentive compatibility

A mechanism is incentive compatible if agents do best by revealing their information truthfully. The classic example is the Vickrey (second-price) auction, in which bidding one's true value is a dominant strategy.

Foundational results

  • The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: with unrestricted preferences over at least three outcomes, the only strategy-proof social choice function is dictatorial.
  • The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanisms: a family of mechanisms implementing efficient outcomes in dominant strategies when quasi-linear transfers are possible.
  • The Myerson–Satterthwaite theorem: no bilateral trading mechanism can be simultaneously efficient, incentive compatible, individually rational, and budget balanced.
  • Myerson's optimal auction theory, characterising revenue-maximising auctions.

Applications

Mechanism design underlies spectrum auctions, school-choice and kidney-exchange matching (algorithmic mechanism design), electricity markets, voting-system analysis, online advertising auctions, and the design of cryptocurrencies and peer-to-peer systems.
