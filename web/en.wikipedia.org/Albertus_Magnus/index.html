Albertus Magnus (c. 1200 – 15 November 1280), also known as Saint Albert the Great, was a German Dominican friar, philosopher, scientist and bishop. He was one of the most universal thinkers of the Middle Ages, earning the titles Doctor universalis and Doctor expertus, and was the teacher of Thomas Aquinas.

Life

Albert was born in Lauingen in Bavaria, studied at the University of Padua, and joined the Dominican Order in 1223. He taught at Cologne, Paris and other studia, holding the chair of theology at the University of Paris. In 1260 he became Bishop of Regensburg, but resigned after three years to return to teaching and preaching. He attended the Second Council of Lyon in 1274 and died in Cologne in 1280.

Works and thought

Albert wrote commentaries on nearly the whole corpus of Aristotle, integrating newly translated Arabic and Greek learning into Latin Christian thought. His works covered logic, metaphysics, physics, astronomy, chemistry, mineralogy, botany, zoology, psychology and ethics. He insisted that natural science required observation and experience — "the aim of natural science is not simply to accept the statements of others, but to investigate the causes at work in nature." He was the first to isolate arsenic and wrote extensively on minerals and alchemy.

Legacy

Albert laid the groundwork for the scholastic synthesis completed by his pupil Aquinas. He was canonized and declared a Doctor of the Church by Pope Pius XI in 1931 and is the patron saint of scientists. Numerous schools, colleges and the Albertus Magnus College in Connecticut bear his name.
