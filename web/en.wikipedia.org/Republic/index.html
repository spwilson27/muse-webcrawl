Republic

A republic, based on the Latin phrase res publica ("public affair"), is a form of government in which the country is considered a "public matter", not the rulers' private concern. In a republic, political power is held by the people and their elected representatives, and the head of state is not a hereditary monarch but typically an elected or nominated president.

Head of stateUsually a president

History

The archetypal early republic was the Roman Republic (509–27 BC), governed by magistrates and a Senate. Medieval and early-modern city-states such as Venice and the Dutch Republic sustained republican government for centuries. The concept was theorised by writers from Cicero to Machiavelli and later by Enlightenment thinkers.

Modern republics

The American (1787) and French (1789) revolutions spread republican government widely. Most states in the world today are formally republics. They vary enormously: parliamentary republics (Germany, India) vest executive power in a prime minister responsible to the legislature, while presidential republics (the United States, Brazil) give a directly elected president executive authority; semi-presidential systems (France) divide power between the two.

Republics and democracy

A republic need not be democratic — some republics restrict the franchise or concentrate power in practice — but modern usage usually assumes representative democracy, constitutional limits, and the rule of law. Such states are often termed liberal democratic republics.
