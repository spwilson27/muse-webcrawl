The American Federation of Labor and Congress of Industrial Organizations (AFL-CIO) is the largest federation of unions in the United States. It is made up of 60 national and international unions, together representing more than 12 million active and retired workers.

History

The AFL-CIO was formed in 1955 by the merger of the American Federation of Labor (AFL, founded 1886 and led for decades by Samuel Gompers) and the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO, founded 1935 under John L. Lewis). George Meany became the merged federation's first president, serving until 1979; he was succeeded by Lane Kirkland, John Sweeney, Richard Trumka and, from 2021, Liz Shuler, the federation's first woman president.

Structure and politics

The federation engages in political education, lobbying and electoral mobilization, historically supporting Democratic Party candidates while formally endorsing candidates of any party who back its agenda. Affiliated unions retain autonomy over collective bargaining; state federations of labor and central labor councils coordinate local activity. In 2005 several unions disaffiliated to form the rival Change to Win federation, though some later rejoined.
