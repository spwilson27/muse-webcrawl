James–Lange theory

The James–Lange theory is a hypothesis on the origin and nature of emotions, independently proposed by the American philosopher and psychologist William James (1884) and the Danish physiologist Carl Lange (1885). It holds that emotion is the perception of physiological changes in the body: an external stimulus first triggers bodily responses — such as a racing heart, trembling or sweating — and the feeling of emotion is the mind's awareness of those changes.

The theory

James famously inverted common sense: we do not tremble because we are afraid; we feel afraid because we tremble. In his formulation, "the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and our feeling of the same changes as they occur is the emotion." Lange emphasised vasomotor changes in particular.

Criticism

Walter Cannon criticised the theory in the 1920s, arguing that bodily changes are too slow and too undifferentiated to account for the speed and variety of emotional experience, that artificially induced arousal does not produce full emotions, and that animals with severed visceral feedback still display emotion. The Cannon–Bard theory proposed instead that emotion and bodily response occur simultaneously, coordinated by the thalamus.

Legacy

Though superseded in its strong form, the James–Lange theory influenced later two-factor (Schachter–Singer) and somatic-marker and facial-feedback research, which retain the idea that bodily feedback contributes to emotional experience.
