Activity theory

Activity theory (AT) is a psychological and multidisciplinary framework rooted in the Soviet cultural-historical psychology of Lev Vygotsky and developed by Alexei Leont'ev and later scholars such as Yrjö Engeström. It understands human consciousness and behaviour as embedded in goal-directed, socially situated activity systems mediated by tools and signs.

Core concepts

The unit of analysis is the activity system, comprising a subject (individual or group) working on an object (the motive or goal of the activity) to produce an outcome. The relationship between subject and object is mediated by tools and artefacts, including language. In Engeström's expanded model, the system also includes the community, the rules governing interaction, and the division of labour.

Generations

Researchers commonly distinguish generations of activity theory: a first generation centred on Vygotsky's concept of mediated action; a second, based on Leont'ev's distinction between activity, action and operation and Engeström's activity-system model; and a third generation that studies networks of interacting activity systems and dialogue between them. Contradictions within and between systems are seen as sources of learning and change, a process Engeström calls expansive learning.

Applications

Activity theory has been applied in human–computer interaction, education, organizational studies, information systems and developmental work research, where it offers a way to analyse practice without reducing it to isolated individual cognition.
