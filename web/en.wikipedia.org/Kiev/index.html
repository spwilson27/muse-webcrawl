Kyiv (also spelled Kiev, the older romanisation from Russian) is the capital and most populous city of Ukraine. It is located in north-central Ukraine along the Dnieper River and is one of the oldest cities in Eastern Europe, having passed through several stages of great prominence, from its medieval heyday as the capital of Kievan Rus' to its modern role as Ukraine's political, cultural, and economic centre.

Name

The name is traditionally linked to Kyi, the legendary founder of the city. "Kiev" is the transliteration from the Russian name and was the standard English form during the Russian Empire and Soviet periods; "Kyiv" transliterates the Ukrainian name. Following Ukrainian independence in 1991, and especially after 2014, Ukrainian authorities promoted "Kyiv" internationally, and it has since been adopted by most English-language reference works, governments, and news organisations.

History

According to legend, Kyiv was founded by the brothers Kyi, Shchek, and Khoryv and their sister Lybid. The city became the capital of Kievan Rus', a federation of Slavic and Finnic peoples, in the late 9th century, and flourished under Vladimir the Great, who adopted Orthodox Christianity in 988, and Yaroslav the Wise. The Mongol invasion destroyed the city in 1240. Kyiv later came under Lithuanian, Polish, and then Russian rule, industrialising in the late 19th century. It suffered enormously in the Second World War, including the Babi Yar massacre of 1941. After Ukraine's independence in 1991, Kyiv was the centre of the Orange Revolution (2004) and the Euromaidan protests (2013–14), and it withstood a Russian assault in the opening weeks of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Landmarks

The city is known for its golden-domed churches, including Saint Sophia Cathedral (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra monastery complex, and St Michael's Golden-Domed Monastery. Major thoroughfares and sites include Khreshchatyk, Maidan Nezalezhnosti (Independence Square), and the Motherland Monument.
