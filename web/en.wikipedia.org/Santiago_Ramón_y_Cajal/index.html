Santiago Ramón y Cajal

Born1 May 1852, Petilla de Aragón, Spain
Died17 October 1934 (aged 82), Madrid, Spain
FieldNeuroscience, histology, pathology
Known forThe neuron doctrine; mapping the nervous system
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1906), shared with Camillo Golgi

Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1 May 1852 – 17 October 1934) was a Spanish neuroscientist, pathologist and histologist regarded as the father of modern neuroscience. His exquisitely detailed drawings of the microscopic structure of the brain and nervous system — based on thousands of histological preparations — established that the nervous system is made of discrete individual cells, the neurons, a principle known as the neuron doctrine.

Early life

Ramón y Cajal was born in Petilla de Aragón, the son of a country surgeon who had risen from barber-surgeon to professor of anatomy. Rebellious and artistic as a boy — he was briefly imprisoned at age eleven for building a homemade cannon — he apprenticed as a shoemaker and barber before his father steered him to medicine. He studied at the University of Zaragoza, where his father taught, graduating in 1873. After serving as an army medical officer in Cuba, where he contracted malaria and tuberculosis, he completed a doctorate in Madrid and took successive chairs of anatomy and histology at Valencia, Barcelona and finally Madrid.

The neuron doctrine

In 1887 Ramón y Cajal encountered the silver-chromate staining method of the Italian scientist Camillo Golgi, which blackens a small random fraction of neurons in their entirety. Working with embryos and young animals, whose less-myelinated fibres were easier to trace, he vastly improved the technique and applied it across the retina, cerebellum, olfactory bulb, hippocampus and spinal cord. Where Golgi and most contemporaries saw a continuous network (the reticular theory), Ramón y Cajal showed that nerve cells are separate units that communicate by contact, not continuity, and he formulated the law of dynamic polarisation: signals flow from dendrites and cell body out along the axon. His 1894 Croonian Lecture to the Royal Society presented the neuron doctrine to the international scientific world.

Nobel Prize and later work

In 1906 Ramón y Cajal and Golgi shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — ironically, since Golgi used his acceptance lecture to defend the reticular theory that Cajal's work had refuted. Cajal went on to study degeneration and regeneration of the nervous system, proposing chemotactic guidance of growing nerve fibres, and produced his monumental Histology of the Nervous System of Man and Vertebrates (1899–1904). The Spanish government founded the Cajal Institute in Madrid for him in 1920. He also wrote an autobiography, scientific memoirs and fiction, and campaigned throughout his life for the modernisation of Spanish science and education.

Legacy

Many structures and cell types bear his name, including the interstitial cells of Cajal in the gut and the horizontal cells of Cajal in the cerebral cortex. His original drawings and microscope slides are preserved in Madrid, and his illustrations — hundreds of them — remain in use in neuroscience textbooks for their accuracy and clarity more than a century after they were made.
