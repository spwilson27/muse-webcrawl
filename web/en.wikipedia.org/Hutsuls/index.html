Hutsuls

The Hutsuls (Ukrainian: гуцули, hutsuly) are an ethnic group inhabiting the Carpathian Mountains in western Ukraine and northern Romania, in the region known as Hutsulshchyna. Closely related to other Ukrainian highlander groups such as the Boykos and Lemkos, the Hutsuls have traditionally been shepherds and lumberjacks, and speak a distinctive dialect of Ukrainian marked by Romanian and other influences.

Origin and history

The origins of the Hutsuls are debated; theories propose descent from Slavic settlers, Dacian tribes, or displaced peoples who fled to the mountains from feudal oppression in the lowlands. From the 14th to the 18th centuries, Hutsul territory lay at the junction of Poland, Moldavia, and Hungary, and the region was famous for the opryshky — outlaw bands whose most celebrated leader, Oleksa Dovbush (1700–1745), became a folk hero comparable to Robin Hood.

Culture

Hutsul culture is noted for its vibrant folk art: intricately decorated Easter eggs (pysanky), wood carving, brass and metalwork, weaving, embroidery, pottery, and the distinctive long trumpet called the trembita. Traditional dress features richly embroidered shirts and woven postoly footwear. The Hutsul horse breed, a hardy mountain pony, takes its name from the group. Music performed on the tsymbaly, sopilka flute, and fiddle accompanies dances such as the arkan.

The Hutsul region around Kosiv, Yaremche, and Vorokhta in Ivano-Frankivsk Oblast remains a centre of Ukrainian folk culture and mountain tourism, including the ski resort of Bukovel.
