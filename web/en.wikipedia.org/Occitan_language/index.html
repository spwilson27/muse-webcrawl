Occitan (Occitan: occitan [utsiˈta], lenga d'òc [ˈleŋɡɔ ˈðɔk]) is a Romance language spoken in Southern France, Monaco, Italy's Occitan Valleys, and Spain's Val d'Aran; collectively, these regions are sometimes referred to as Occitania. It is also spoken in Calabria (in a few communities, notably Guardia Piemontese) by a small community of Waldensian origin. The name derives from òc, the Occitan word for "yes", contrasting the language with the northern French langues d'oïl — a distinction already made by Dante.

History

In the Middle Ages Old Occitan (as Old Provençal) was one of Europe's great literary languages, the medium of the troubadours whose lyric poetry profoundly influenced the literature of France, Italy, Spain and Germany. It was also a language of law and administration across the south. The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229) and the progressive annexation of the southern counties by the French crown began its decline; the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) required French in administration. The language, stigmatised as a patois, receded sharply under the centralised French state and compulsory French-medium schooling in the 19th–20th centuries. The Félibrige movement of Frédéric Mistral (Nobel Prize in Literature 1904) led a literary revival in the 19th century.

Dialects and standardisation

Occitan is usually divided into six main dialect groups: Provençal (including Niçard), Languedocien, Gascon (including Aranese), Limousin, Auvergnat and Vivaro-Alpine. Two writing systems compete: the classical norm (nòrma classica), based on medieval spelling and used for pan-Occitan reference works, and the Mistralian norm used by some Provençal writers. Languedocien is often treated as the reference dialect for the standard language. Catalan is Occitan's closest relative; the two are sometimes grouped as Occitano-Romance.

Status today

UNESCO classifies Occitan as endangered (several dialects severely so). France has never ratified the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages in full, and the 2021 law on regional languages was partially struck down by the Constitutional Council in respect of immersive public schooling; nevertheless, immersion schools (Calandretas) and bilingual streams teach thousands of children. In Spain's Val d'Aran, Aranese is co-official and taught in schools, and Occitan as a whole is co-official in Catalonia. Estimates of speakers in France range from under 100,000 fluent daily speakers to several hundred thousand with active competence, plus millions with passive knowledge.
