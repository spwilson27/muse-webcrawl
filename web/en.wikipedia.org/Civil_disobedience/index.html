Civil disobedience

Civil disobedience is the active, and professed refusal of a citizen to obey certain laws, demands, orders or commands of a government (or any other authority). By some definitions, civil disobedience has to be nonviolent to be called "civil". Hence, civil disobedience is sometimes equated with peaceful protests or nonviolent resistance.

History of the term

Henry David Thoreau's essay Resistance to Civil Government, published posthumously in 1866 under the title Civil Disobedience, is often regarded as the founding text. Thoreau refused to pay the poll tax in protest against slavery and the Mexican–American War, spending a night in jail in 1846. He argued that a person should follow conscience over the laws of the state, writing that "under a government which imprisons any unjustly, the true place for a just man is also a prison."

Practice and theory

The concept was taken up globally. Mohandas Gandhi developed it into the philosophy of satyagraha, employing mass nonviolent law-breaking — most famously the 1930 Salt March against the British salt tax — in the Indian independence movement. Martin Luther King Jr., in his 1963 Letter from Birmingham Jail, argued that "one has a moral responsibility to disobey unjust laws." Civil disobedience shaped the American civil rights movement (sit-ins, freedom rides, marches), resistance to apartheid, anti-war and anti-nuclear campaigns, and later movements such as the suffragettes (who partly preceded these theories), the Velvet Revolution, and climate protest groups.

Philosophers such as John Rawls define civil disobedience as a public, nonviolent, conscientious breach of law undertaken to appeal to the sense of justice of the majority, within fidelity to law overall — including acceptance of legal punishment. Critics and theorists continue to debate its boundaries with lawful protest, conscientious objection, and revolutionary resistance.
