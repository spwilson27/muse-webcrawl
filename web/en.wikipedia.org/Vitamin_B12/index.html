Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12
ClassWater-soluble vitamin (cobalamins)
Recommended intake2.4 µg/day (adults, US)
Rich sourcesMeat, fish, dairy, eggs; fortified foods and supplements
DeficiencyMegaloblastic anaemia, neurological damage

Vitamin B12, also known as cobalamin, is a water-soluble vitamin involved in metabolism. It is one of eight B vitamins, and it is essential for DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation, and the normal functioning of the nervous system.

Function and absorption

B12 acts as a cofactor for two enzymes: methionine synthase, which recycles homocysteine to methionine, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Absorption from food requires gastric acid to release the vitamin from protein and intrinsic factor, a protein secreted by the stomach, for uptake in the ileum. The liver stores several years' supply, so deficiency develops slowly. The vitamin supports production of white blood cells and other blood cells in the bone marrow.

Deficiency

Deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia — historically called pernicious anaemia when due to loss of intrinsic factor — and can cause irreversible damage to the brain and nervous system, with symptoms including numbness, memory problems and glossitis. Groups at risk include older adults, people after gastric surgery, and those following a vegan diet without supplements, since B12 is made only by certain bacteria and archaea and reaches humans mainly through animal foods.

History

B12 was the last vitamin to be discovered; it was isolated in 1948 after the finding that raw liver cured pernicious anaemia. Dorothy Hodgkin determined its complex structure — the largest and most complex of any vitamin — by X-ray crystallography, work that contributed to her 1964 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
