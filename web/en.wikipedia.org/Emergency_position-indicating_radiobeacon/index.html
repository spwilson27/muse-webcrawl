Emergency position-indicating radiobeacon

An emergency position-indicating radiobeacon station (EPIRB) is a type of emergency locator beacon, a portable battery-powered radio transmitter used in emergencies to locate aviators, mariners, and people in distress and in need of immediate rescue. In the event of an emergency, such as a ship sinking or an aircraft crash, the transmitter is activated and begins transmitting a continuous radio signal, which is used by search and rescue teams to quickly locate the emergency and render aid. Modern 406 MHz beacons are detected by the international Cospas-Sarsat satellite system, whose ground stations compute the beacon's position and alert rescue coordination centres.

Types

Distress beacon families share the same satellite system:

  • EPIRBs for maritime use, registered to a vessel; Category I units float free and activate automatically when submerged (via a hydrostatic release), while Category II units are manually deployed.
  • ELTs (emergency locator transmitters) for aircraft, usually triggered by crash forces or manually.
  • PLBs (personal locator beacons), small units carried by individuals on land, registered to a person.
  • SSAS ship security alerts and submarine beacons serve specialised roles.

Frequencies and the Cospas-Sarsat system

First-generation beacons transmitted on 121.5 MHz, the aviation distress frequency; satellites could locate them only roughly, false alerts were common (up to 97–98 percent of activations), and satellite processing of 121.5 MHz ceased on 1 February 2009. Current beacons transmit a digital signal on 406 MHz (406.02–406.08 MHz) containing a unique identifier linked to registration data, allowing rescue centres to contact the vessel's operator and verify distress. Position is obtained either from an integrated GNSS receiver encoded in the message — accurate to about 100 metres — or by Doppler processing of the signal by polar-orbiting satellites, accurate to a few kilometres. Newer medium-Earth-orbit (MEOSAR) payloads on GPS, Galileo, and GLONASS satellites provide near-instantaneous global detection, and Galileo adds a return-link acknowledgement to the beacon. A low-power 121.5 MHz homing signal remains for final-stage location by rescue aircraft and vessels.

Regulation and use

EPIRBs are required on most commercial vessels under the SOLAS convention and the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) rules adopted in the 1990s, and on many pleasure craft under national law. Beacons must be registered (free of charge) with national authorities so their identifier resolves to owner and vessel information. Correct registration, battery replacement, and mounting are critical: most alerts are false, usually from mishandling or disposal of old units, yet every alert must be investigated. When genuinely activated, EPIRBs have contributed to the rescue of tens of thousands of people worldwide since the Cospas-Sarsat system began in 1982.
