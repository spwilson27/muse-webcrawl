Simpson's paradox

Simpson's paradox is a phenomenon in probability and statistics in which a trend appears in several groups of data but disappears or reverses when the groups are combined. This result is often encountered in social-science and medical-science statistics, and is particularly problematic when frequency data is unduly given causal interpretations. The paradox can be resolved when confounding variables and causal relations are appropriately addressed in the statistical modelling.

It was first described by Karl Pearson in 1899 and by Udny Yule in 1903, and is named after Edward H. Simpson, who described it in a 1951 paper.

Example: kidney stone treatment

In a well-known study of kidney stone treatments, open surgery appeared better than percutaneous nephrolithotomy for both small and large stones separately, yet worse when the results were pooled, because the two treatments were given to patients with very different mixes of stone sizes:

TreatmentSmall stonesLarge stonesOverall
Open surgery93% (81/87)73% (192/263)78% (273/350)
Percutaneous nephrolithotomy87% (234/270)69% (55/80)83% (289/350)

Interpretation

The paradox arises from a lurking (confounding) variable distributed unequally between groups. Whether the aggregated or disaggregated figures are the correct ones depends on the causal structure of the problem: for judging a treatment, the subgroup comparisons may be appropriate; for other questions the aggregate may be. The paradox is therefore a standard illustration of why correlation and aggregation must be handled with care in observational data.
