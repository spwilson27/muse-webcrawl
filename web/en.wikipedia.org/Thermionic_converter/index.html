Thermionic converter

A thermionic converter consists of a hot electrode which thermionically emits electrons over a potential energy barrier to a cooler electrode, producing a useful electric power output. The device is essentially a heat engine that converts heat directly into electricity with no moving parts, exploiting the phenomenon of thermionic emission first described by Thomas Edison.

Caesium vapour is commonly used to reduce the emitter work function and to provide ions that neutralise the electron space charge between the electrodes, allowing practical current densities. Practical converters operate with emitter temperatures of roughly 1,500–2,000 K and collector temperatures several hundred degrees lower, achieving efficiencies in the range of 5–20 percent.

History

The idea of the thermionic converter was first proposed by Schlichter in 1915, and the first practical devices were developed in the 1950s, driven largely by interest in space power systems. Thermionic converters were flown on Soviet TOPAZ nuclear reactor satellites, converting reactor heat directly into electricity for spacecraft. Research programmes in the United States, the Soviet Union and Europe explored applications in nuclear power, solar concentrators and fossil-fuel topping cycles, in which a thermionic stage sits atop a conventional steam plant to raise overall efficiency.

Principle of operation

Electrons evaporate from the hot emitter surface, traverse the inter-electrode gap (typically a fraction of a millimetre, or transported through a low-pressure plasma), and condense on the cooler collector at a lower electrical potential. The difference in work functions and electron energies between the electrodes appears as a voltage, typically around 0.5–1 volt per cell, so practical systems connect many cells in series.
