A Yagi–Uda antenna, commonly called a Yagi antenna, is a directional antenna consisting of a driven element — usually a dipole or folded dipole — together with parasitic elements: one or more reflectors behind it and a row of directors in front, all mounted parallel on a boom. It was invented in 1926 by Shintaro Uda of Tohoku University, Japan, and described in English by his colleague Hidetsugu Yagi, whose name became attached to the design.

Operation

The parasitic elements are not connected to the transmitter or receiver. Currents induced in them re-radiate the signal with phase shifts determined by their lengths and spacing: the slightly longer reflector reinforces radiation forward, while slightly shorter directors draw the beam forward, producing a directional pattern with gain typically of 7–15 dBi and a strong front-to-back ratio.

History and uses

The design saw military radar use in the Second World War and became ubiquitous as the standard rooftop television receiving antenna in the VHF/UHF era; millions were installed worldwide. Yagi arrays remain common for FM radio reception, amateur radio, point-to-point links and RFID readers. Its relative narrow bandwidth is its main limitation compared with log-periodic designs.
