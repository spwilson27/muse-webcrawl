Konrad Zacharias Lorenz (1903–1989) was an Austrian zoologist, ethologist, and ornithologist. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern ethology, the study of animal behaviour. In 1973 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Nikolaas Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch "for their discoveries concerning organization and elicitation of individual and social behaviour patterns".

Imprinting

Lorenz is best known for his work on imprinting, a form of rapid learning in young animals, worked out in his studies of greylag geese. He showed that newly hatched goslings instinctively bond to the first moving object they see during a brief critical period after hatching — in famous experiments, Lorenz himself, whose boots the goslings followed as they would a parent. His 1935 paper on the companion in bird life laid the foundation of the concept.

Career

Trained in medicine and zoology at the University of Vienna, Lorenz held posts at Königsberg and later directed the Max Planck Institute for Behavioural Physiology at Seewiesen in Bavaria. From his family estate at Altenberg in Austria he kept a celebrated menagerie of free-flying birds and other animals, the setting for his popular books.

Writings

Lorenz wrote widely read popular works, including King Solomon's Ring (1949), Man Meets Dog (1950), and On Aggression (1963), in which he applied ethological ideas to human behaviour and warned about the effects of modern civilization. His wartime politics and membership of the Nazi Party have been the subject of later critical discussion.
