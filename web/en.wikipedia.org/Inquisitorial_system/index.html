Inquisitorial system

An inquisitorial system is a legal system in which the court, or a part of the court, is actively involved in investigating the facts of the case. It is the primary model of criminal procedure in civil law countries such as France, Germany, Italy and Spain. It is contrasted with the adversarial system used in common law countries, in which the parties present their cases to a passive judge or jury and the court's role is largely that of an impartial referee.

Features

In an inquisitorial proceeding, the investigation is directed by a public official — typically a prosecutor and, in serious cases, an investigating judge (such as the French juge d'instruction) — who gathers both incriminating and exculpatory evidence and assembles a written dossier. At trial the presiding judge takes the lead in questioning witnesses and the defendant, rather than leaving examination to opposing counsel. There is generally a weaker role for plea bargaining, fewer exclusionary rules of evidence, and legal reasoning that gives greater weight to the written case file.

Rationale

Supporters argue that the system is oriented toward discovering the material truth: because inquiry is conducted by a neutral magistrate rather than by two partisan sides, outcomes are less dependent on the wealth or skill of advocates. Critics argue that concentrating investigative and judicial power in the same hands risks bias and weakens the defence, and that the presumption of innocence may be diluted when the judge who decides guilt has already directed the investigation.

Scope

The inquisitorial approach predominates in criminal procedure in civil law jurisdictions and in some international tribunals in modified form. In practice most modern systems are mixed: civil law countries have adopted adversarial elements such as party questioning and negotiated dispositions, while common law systems give judges some active case-management roles. The distinction is used mainly for criminal proceedings; civil proceedings in civil law countries also typically involve active judges, while common law civil trials remain party-driven.

History

The model descends from the Romano-canonical inquisitio procedure developed by the medieval Church from the twelfth century onward, in which a judge could initiate proceedings on public repute rather than awaiting a private accuser. It was codified in continental Europe in instruments such as the French Ordinance of 1670 and the Napoleonic Code d'instruction criminelle of 1808, which created the modern investigating magistrate.
