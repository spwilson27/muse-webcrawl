Sales taxes in the United States

Sales taxes in the United States are taxes placed on the sale or lease of goods and services. Unlike many countries, the United States has no national sales tax or value-added tax; instead, sales taxes are levied by 45 states and the District of Columbia, and by thousands of local jurisdictions — counties, cities, transit authorities, and special districts — producing a highly fragmented system.

Structure

Statewide base rates range from 2.9% in Colorado to 7.25% in California, with Tennessee, Indiana, Mississippi, and Rhode Island at 7%. When local taxes are added, combined rates can exceed 10% in parts of some states (for example, in Chicago and parts of Louisiana and Alabama). Five states — Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire, Delaware, and (for most goods) Alaska — impose no statewide general sales tax, though Alaskan localities may do so.

Most states exempt some necessities or tax them at reduced rates: many exempt most groceries and prescription medicines, while services are often outside the base, a growing distortion as consumption shifts toward services. Businesses collect the tax from purchasers at the point of sale and remit it to the taxing authority; purchases for resale and manufacturing inputs are typically exempt to avoid tax cascading.

Use tax and remote sales

A complementary use tax applies to goods bought out of state for use in state. Historically, states could not require remote sellers without a physical presence ("nexus") to collect the tax, under Quill Corp. v. North Dakota (1992). The Supreme Court reversed course in South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc. (2018), allowing states to require collection by sellers with substantial economic activity in the state. Marketplace-facilitator laws now require platforms such as Amazon and eBay to collect on behalf of their sellers in nearly all sales-tax states. See also Use tax and South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.
