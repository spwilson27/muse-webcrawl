Manzanar

Manzanar, photographed by Ansel Adams in 1943
Location:Owens Valley, California
Operated:March 1942 – November 1945
Peak population:10,046
Status:National Historic Site (1992)

Manzanar is the best known of the ten American concentration camps where more than 120,000 Japanese Americans were incarcerated during World War II. Located at the foot of the Sierra Nevada in California's Owens Valley, between the towns of Lone Pine and Independence, it held 10,046 people at its peak, most of them from the Los Angeles area.

History

Following the attack on Pearl Harbor, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066 in February 1942, authorizing the forced removal of all persons of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast. Manzanar received its first internees in March 1942. Families were housed in tar-paper barracks in 36 blocks, surrounded by barbed wire and guard towers, in a harsh high-desert environment of dust storms, summer heat, and cold winters.

Despite conditions, residents built schools, hospitals, newspapers, sports leagues, and celebrated gardens and ponds. Tensions over collaboration with administrators erupted in the Manzanar Riot of December 1942, in which military police killed two internees. The camp closed on 21 November 1945.

Documentation and legacy

The camp was documented by photographers including Dorothea Lange and Ansel Adams, whose 1943 photographs of Manzanar became iconic. Toyo Miyatake, himself an internee, secretly photographed camp life. Since 1969 an annual pilgrimage has been held at the site. Manzanar was designated a California Historical Landmark in 1973 and a National Historic Site in 1992; the National Park Service operates a visitor centre in the preserved camp auditorium and has reconstructed barracks, a mess hall, and guard tower.
