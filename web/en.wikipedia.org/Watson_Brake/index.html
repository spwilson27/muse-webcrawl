Watson Brake is an archaeological site in present-day Ouachita Parish, Louisiana, consisting of eleven mounds connected by a ridge, forming an oval nearly 900 feet (270 m) across. Dated to about 5,400 years ago (roughly 3500 BC), it is among the oldest large-scale mound constructions in North America — predating the Egyptian pyramids and, within the Americas, earlier than the better-known complex at Poverty Point by more than a millennium.

Discovery and investigation

The site sits on a terrace overlooking the Ouachita River floodplain. Local resident Reca Jones identified the site's significance and brought it to archaeologists' attention; excavations in the 1990s led by Joe W. Saunders of the University of Louisiana at Monroe produced soil cores and radiocarbon dates that established the Middle Archaic age of the mounds, announced in a widely cited 1997 paper in Science. The mounds range from about 3 to 25 feet (1–7.6 m) in height.

Builders and subsistence

Remarkably, Watson Brake was built by hunter-gatherers, not agriculturalists. Excavations found no evidence of farming; the inhabitants subsisted on fish, mussels, game, and wild plant foods such as chenopod and knotweed gathered from the rich riverine environment. Tools included stone drills, spear points, and fired earth objects. The discovery overturned the long-held assumption that monumental earthworks required agricultural surplus and chiefdom-level organization, showing instead that seasonally mobile foraging peoples could coordinate large communal construction projects, possibly for ceremonial or social purposes.

Preservation

The site was held in private hands for decades — the Watson family, for whom it is named, protected it from looting — and most of it was acquired by The Archaeological Conservancy to ensure its permanent preservation. Watson Brake is closed to general visitation but continues to be studied.
