Synapomorphy

In phylogenetics and cladistics, a synapomorphy is a shared, derived character state—a trait that is present in two or more taxa, and which was inherited from their most recent common ancestor, in which the trait first evolved. Synapomorphies are the fundamental evidence used to infer that a group of organisms forms a clade (a monophyletic group).

The term was introduced by the German entomologist Willi Hennig, the founder of cladistics. "Synapomorphy" is a compound of the Greek syn ("together"), apo ("away from"), and morphe ("form").

Related concepts

An apomorphy is any derived trait differing from the ancestral condition (plesiomorphy). An apomorphy shared by two or more taxa is a synapomorphy; one unique to a single taxon is an autapomorphy and provides no information about grouping. A shared ancestral trait is a symplesiomorphy, which likewise cannot be used to establish clades, because it may be inherited from a much older ancestor shared with many other groups. Shared derived traits that arise independently by convergence or reversal are called homoplasies and can mislead analyses.

Examples

The presence of mammary glands and hair are synapomorphies of mammals; feathers are a synapomorphy of birds; flowers define the angiosperms; and among vertebrates, the amniotic egg is a synapomorphy of the amniotes. At finer scales, the loss of gill slits and the development of lungs-plus-limbs patterns have served as nested synapomorphies uniting tetrapod subgroups. In molecular phylogenetics, synapomorphies may be particular nucleotide or amino-acid substitutions shared by a clade.

Use in analysis

Cladistic analysis codes characters across taxa, distinguishes derived from ancestral states using outgroup comparison, and seeks the branching diagram (cladogram) most consistent with the distribution of synapomorphies, typically under the principle of maximum parsimony. Because relationships are hierarchical, a trait that is a synapomorphy at one level of analysis is an autapomorphy or plesiomorphy at another.
