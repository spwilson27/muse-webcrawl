Chinese poetry

Chinese poetry is poetry written, spoken, or chanted in the Chinese language, one of the world's oldest continuous poetic traditions, extending over some three thousand years. It encompasses the anthology traditions of the Classic of Poetry (Shijing), the great lyric flowering of the Tang dynasty, and the song-lyric forms of the Song dynasty, down to modern vernacular verse.

Early forms

The earliest surviving collection, the Shijing (c. 11th–7th centuries BCE), contains 305 folk songs, court odes, and hymns. The Chu Ci (Songs of Chu), associated with Qu Yuan, introduced longer, more personal and shamanic verse. During the Han dynasty the Music Bureau (Yuefu) collected folk ballads that influenced later narrative poetry.

Tang and Song golden ages

The Tang dynasty (618–907) is regarded as the golden age of Chinese poetry. Regulated verse (lüshi) and quatrains (jueju) reached their height in the works of Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei. In the Song dynasty the lyric form known as ci, set to musical patterns, flourished in poets such as Su Shi and Li Qingzhao, and the qu arias of the Yuan dynasty added a further dramatic lyric form.

Modern poetry

The May Fourth era brought free verse in the vernacular (baihua), pioneered by Hu Shi and developed by Xu Zhimo and the Misty Poets of the late 20th century. Classical forms continue to be written alongside modernist verse, and poetry remains central to Chinese literary culture.
