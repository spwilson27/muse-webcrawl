Great Glen

LocationScottish Highlands, Scotland
Length~100 km (62 mi)
FeaturesLoch Ness, Loch Oich, Loch Lochy; Caledonian Canal

The Great Glen (Scottish Gaelic: An Gleann Mòr), also known as Glen Albyn or Glen More, is a glen in Scotland running for 100 kilometres (62 mi) from Inverness on the Moray Firth in the northeast to Fort William at the head of Loch Linnhe in the southwest. It follows a major geological fault, the Great Glen Fault, which cuts across the Scottish Highlands.

Geography

The glen contains a chain of freshwater lochs linked as a natural waterway: Loch Ness — the largest by volume in Britain — Loch Oich and Loch Lochy. The Caledonian Canal, engineered by Thomas Telford and completed in 1822, joins these lochs with locks to create a navigable route for shipping between the North Sea and the Atlantic. The valley is a strategically vital corridor and has historically been the main route across the Highlands; the A82 road runs its length.

Geology

The Great Glen Fault is a strike-slip fault that displaced the rocks to its northwest by about 100 km relative to those to the southeast. Active during and after the Caledonian orogeny some 430–390 million years ago, the fault zone was later excavated by glaciers during the last ice age, leaving the deep, straight trough occupied by the lochs. Small earthquakes still occasionally occur along the fault.

The glen's western end lies near Ben Nevis, the highest mountain in Britain. The Great Glen Way, a long-distance trail between Fort William and Inverness, opened in 2002.
