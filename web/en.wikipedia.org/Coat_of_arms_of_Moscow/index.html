Coat of arms of Moscow

The coat of arms of Moscow depicts Saint George the Victorious in silver armor on a silver horse, striking a black dragon (often described heraldically as a winged serpent) with a golden spear, all on a red field. The image of the horseman—known in Russian tradition as Saint George—has symbolized Moscow for centuries and also appears at the center of the coat of arms of Russia.

History

The mounted dragon-slayer appeared on the seals of Moscow's princes from the fifteenth century, notably those of Vasily I and Ivan III, as Moscow rose to lead the gathering of the Russian lands. Under Ivan III the rider was combined with the double-headed eagle adopted from Byzantium, and the figure was increasingly identified with Saint George. The arms were formally established for the city in the eighteenth century; an early official description dates to 1781 under Catherine the Great.

Soviet period and restoration

In 1924 the Soviet authorities replaced the arms with a new emblem featuring a monument to the Constitution, a hammer and sickle, and industrial motifs, reflecting the regime's rejection of religious and tsarist imagery. The historic arms were restored in 1993, after the dissolution of the Soviet Union, by a law of the city of Moscow; the official design shows Saint George in blue cloak and silver armor spearing the dragon.

Symbolism

Saint George, patron saint of Moscow and of its founder Yuri Dolgorukiy (whose name is the Russian form of George), represents the victory of good over evil. The emblem is displayed on the city's flag, on official buildings, and on Moscow's municipal insignia.
