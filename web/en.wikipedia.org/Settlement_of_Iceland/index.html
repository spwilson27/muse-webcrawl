Settlement of Iceland

Norse settlers arriving in Iceland

The settlement of Iceland (Icelandic: Landnámsöld, the Age of Settlement) is generally believed to have begun in the second half of the 9th century, when Norse settlers migrated across the North Atlantic. The reasons for the migration are uncertain, though later medieval sources attribute it variously to the tyranny of King Harald Fairhair of Norway, the search for new farmland, and the pull of lands discovered by earlier voyagers.

Sources

The settlement is described in two principal medieval works: the Íslendingabók ("Book of the Icelanders") by Ari Þorgilsson, written in the early 12th century, and the Landnámabók ("Book of Settlements"), which lists more than 400 settlers and some 3,000 people in total, describing where they settled and their descendants. Although written centuries after the events, these works are thought to preserve genuine oral tradition.

Discovery

According to tradition, Iceland was sighted successively by Naddodd, Garðar Svavarsson — who circumnavigated it and gave it the name Garðarshólmur — and Flóki Vilgerðarson, who is said to have named it "Iceland" after seeing a fjord full of ice. Irish monks known as papar may have lived on the island before the Norse arrived, but they either left or were few in number; Ari reports they departed because they did not wish to live among pagans.

Ingólfur Arnarson

The first permanent settler is traditionally said to be Ingólfur Arnarson, who built his farm at Reykjavík ("Smoky Bay," named for the steam from nearby hot springs) around 874 AD. Legend holds that he threw his high-seat pillars overboard as he approached land and vowed to settle wherever they washed ashore. Tephrochronology — dating by volcanic ash layers, especially the "settlement layer" (landnámslag) from an eruption dated to about 871 ± 2 — broadly supports this chronology.

Course of settlement

The main settlement period is conventionally dated from about 870 to 930 AD, when most habitable land had been claimed. Settlers came not only from Norway but also from Norse colonies in Ireland and Scotland, bringing Celtic wives, slaves, and livestock; genetic studies show that a substantial fraction of the founding population — particularly women — had Gaelic ancestry. By about 930 the settlers established the Althing, one of the world's oldest parliaments, marking the end of the Settlement Age and the beginning of the Icelandic Commonwealth.

Environmental impact

Settlement transformed Iceland's ecology. At the time of arrival, perhaps a quarter to two-fifths of the island was covered in birch woodland; settlers cleared the woods for pasture and fuel, and grazing by sheep prevented regeneration, leading to severe soil erosion that continues to shape the landscape today.
