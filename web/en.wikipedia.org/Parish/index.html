Parish

A parish is a local church community and, in many church traditions, a territorial division of a diocese. The term derives from the Greek paroikia, meaning a sojourning or dwelling beside. In the Catholic Church, canon law defines a parish as a community of the faithful stably constituted within a diocese and entrusted to a parish priest (pastor) under the authority of the diocesan bishop.

Ecclesiastical use

Parishes are the basic unit of pastoral care in the Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Orthodox, and several other churches. In the Church of England, the parish system covers the entire country: every resident lives within an ecclesiastical parish served by a parish church and clergy, and parochial church councils administer parish affairs with the incumbent. Historically, parish membership and residence determined obligations such as tithes and poor relief.

Civil use

The church parish became a unit of civil administration in England and Wales, evolving into the modern civil parish, the lowest tier of local government, governed by parish councils or meetings. The word is also used for civil subdivisions elsewhere: Louisiana's counties are called parishes, a legacy of French and Spanish colonial church divisions, and parishes are administrative divisions in countries including Jamaica, Barbados, and the Channel Islands.

History

The parish system developed in the early Middle Ages as Christianity spread into the countryside and local churches with baptismal and burial rights were established, each supported by tithes from a defined territory. Reforms over the centuries — including urbanisation — have repeatedly reshaped parish boundaries, but the parish remains a fundamental building block of church life.
