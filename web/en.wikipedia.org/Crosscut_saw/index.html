Crosscut saw

A crosscut saw (thwart saw) is any saw designed for cutting wood perpendicular to (across) the wood grain. Crosscut saws have teeth shaped like tiny chisels or knives that slice the wood fibres, in contrast to rip saws, whose teeth pare away material along the grain. The term covers small hand saws used in carpentry as well as the great two-man saws once used to fell trees and buck logs.

Types

Two-man crosscut saws, typically 1.2 to 3.6 metres long with a handle at each end, were the standard felling tool of the timber industry from the mid-19th century until the spread of chainsaws after the Second World War. One-man crosscuts, with an auxiliary handle, served smaller work. Tooth patterns — such as the "champion," "lance," and "perforated lance" arrangements of cutter teeth and rakers — were specialised for hardwoods, softwoods, and frozen timber.

Modern use

Although chainsaws dominate commercial logging, crosscut saws remain in use in designated wilderness areas in the United States, where motorised equipment is prohibited, and trail crews are trained and certified in their use and maintenance. Sharpening a crosscut is a skilled trade in itself, requiring jointing, shaping, setting, and filing of the teeth.
