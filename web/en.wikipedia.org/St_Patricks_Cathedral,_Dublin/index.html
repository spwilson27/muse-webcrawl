St Patrick's Cathedral, the National Cathedral of the Church of Ireland.

Saint Patrick's Cathedral (Irish: Ard-Eaglais Naomh Pádraig) in Dublin, Ireland, founded in 1191 as a Roman Catholic cathedral, is currently the National Cathedral of the Church of Ireland. Christ Church Cathedral, also a Church of Ireland cathedral in Dublin, is designated as the local cathedral of the Diocese of Dublin and Glendalough. At 91 metres (300 ft) in length, Saint Patrick's is the longest church building in Ireland.

History

The cathedral stands beside a well where, according to tradition, Saint Patrick baptised converts in the 5th century. John Comyn, the first Anglo-Norman Archbishop of Dublin, raised the church to cathedral status in 1191, and the present building in the Early English Gothic style was constructed between about 1220 and 1270. After the English Reformation it became an Anglican cathedral. In the 18th century Jonathan Swift, author of Gulliver's Travels, served as Dean from 1713 to 1745; he and his companion Esther Johnson ("Stella") are buried in the cathedral. In the 1860s the building underwent a major restoration funded by the brewer Benjamin Guinness, which saved it from ruin but replaced much medieval fabric.

Features

The cathedral contains over 300 monuments, including the Boyle monument, the tombs of Archbishop Tregury, and memorials to figures such as Douglas Hyde, first President of Ireland. Its choir stalls carry the banners of the Knights of St Patrick, an order founded in 1783. The cathedral choir school, founded in 1432, is the oldest in Ireland, and Handel's Messiah received its first performance in Dublin in 1742 with choristers from St Patrick's and Christ Church. A curiosity is the "Chapter House door" with a hole cut in it, through which, in 1492, the Earl of Ormond and the Earl of Kildare shook hands to end a feud — the origin of the phrase "to chance your arm".
