Indo-Aryan migration

The Indo-Aryan migration is the widely accepted model in linguistics, archaeology and archaeogenetics according to which speakers of Indo-Aryan languages — the branch of the Indo-European language family that includes Sanskrit, Hindi, Bengali and Punjabi — entered the Indian subcontinent from Central Asia during the 2nd millennium BCE. It is part of the broader Indo-European migrations out of the Pontic–Caspian steppe. The incoming groups are associated with the composition of the Rigveda in the Punjab region (c. 1500–1200 BCE) and with the foundations of Vedic culture.

Evidence

Linguistics. Sanskrit is demonstrably related to Old Iranian (Avestan) and, more distantly, to Greek, Latin and other Indo-European languages. Indo-Aryan shows substrate influence from non-Indo-European languages of the subcontinent — notably retroflex consonants and loanwords attributable to Dravidian and to a lost language ("Language X") of the Indus region — implying that Indo-Aryan speakers arrived in an already populated land and absorbed words from its inhabitants.

Textual and Near Eastern evidence. A treaty of c. 1380 BCE between the Hittites and the Mitanni invokes the Indo-Aryan gods Mitra, Varuna, Indra and the Nasatyas, and the Kikkuli horse-training text uses Indo-Aryan numerals and technical terms, showing Indo-Aryan speakers in the Near East by the mid-2nd millennium BCE — consistent with a movement from the steppe via the Sintashta and Andronovo cultures of Central Asia.

Archaeology. The spread of chariot technology, horse remains and new burial customs in the late Indus and post-Indus periods, together with cultures such as Gandhara Grave and Painted Grey Ware, is debated but broadly compatible with incremental migration and cultural blending after the decline of the Indus Valley Civilisation (after c. 1900 BCE).

Genetics. Ancient-DNA studies (notably Narasimhan et al., Science, 2019) show that steppe pastoralist-related ancestry entered South Asia in the 2nd millennium BCE and is present, in varying proportions, in most modern South Asian populations, mixed with ancestry related to Indus Valley and indigenous hunter-gatherer populations. This supports migration and admixture rather than either complete population replacement or no movement at all.

Scholarly consensus and controversy

The mainstream scholarly position is of migration followed by acculturation and language shift over centuries, not a single violent "invasion" — the older "Aryan invasion theory" in that form was abandoned by scholars in the mid-20th century. Alternative "Out of India" models, which propose an indigenous origin of Indo-European languages in South Asia, are rejected by the great majority of linguists and geneticists, though the topic remains politically charged in India, where the migration model features in debates about national identity, caste and religion.
