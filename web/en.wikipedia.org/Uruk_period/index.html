The Uruk period (c. 4000 to 3100 BC; also known as the Protoliterate period) existed from the protohistorical Chalcolithic to the early Bronze Age in the history of Mesopotamia, named after the Sumerian city of Uruk. This period saw the emergence of urban life in Mesopotamia and the Sumerian civilization. The late Uruk period (34th to 32nd centuries BC) saw the gradual emergence of the cuneiform script and corresponds to the early Bronze Age.

Urbanization

Uruk grew into the world's largest city of its time, with a population estimated at 40,000–80,000 and massive monumental architecture including the Eanna precinct and the Anu ziggurat (White Temple). Population concentrated in cities supported by irrigation agriculture, specialized crafts, and long-distance trade in timber, stone, and metals, which southern Mesopotamia lacked.

The "Uruk expansion"

Material culture associated with Uruk — bevelled-rim bowls, cylinder seals, and administrative tablets — spread across the Near East to sites such as Habuba Kabira in Syria and Godin Tepe in Iran. Scholars debate whether this expansion represented colonies and trade enclaves of southern Mesopotamians or local emulation.

Technology and administration

The period saw the invention of the potter's wheel in mass production, wheeled transport, the plough, and the sailboat. Administration used tokens, numerical tablets, and then proto-cuneiform writing (c. 3400–3300 BC), initially for accounting of goods, rations, and land. Bevelled-rim bowls, produced in enormous quantities, are often interpreted as ration vessels for dependent laborers.
