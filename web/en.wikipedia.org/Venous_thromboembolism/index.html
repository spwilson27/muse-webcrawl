Venous thromboembolism

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is a condition in which a blood clot (thrombus) forms in a vein. It comprises two main manifestations: deep vein thrombosis (DVT), in which a clot forms in a deep vein, usually in the legs, and pulmonary embolism (PE), in which part of a clot breaks off and travels to the lungs. VTE is a common and potentially fatal condition, and is a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

DVT typically causes pain, swelling, redness and warmth in the affected limb, although it may be asymptomatic. Pulmonary embolism commonly presents with sudden shortness of breath, chest pain that worsens with breathing, rapid heart rate, and cough, sometimes with blood-stained sputum. Large emboli can cause collapse and death.

Causes and risk factors

Clot formation is classically explained by Virchow's triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Important risk factors include major surgery, trauma, prolonged immobility (including long-haul travel and hospitalisation), active cancer, pregnancy and the postpartum period, use of estrogen-containing contraceptives or hormone therapy, obesity, smoking, and inherited thrombophilias such as factor V Leiden.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines clinical prediction rules (such as the Wells score) with D-dimer blood testing and imaging. Compression ultrasonography is the standard test for suspected DVT, while CT pulmonary angiography is generally used to confirm pulmonary embolism.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is based on anticoagulant medications, including heparins, warfarin, and direct oral anticoagulants such as rivaroxaban and apixaban, usually for at least three months. Severe pulmonary embolism may require thrombolysis or surgical or catheter-based intervention. Prevention in hospitalised and surgical patients uses risk assessment, early mobilisation, mechanical compression, and prophylactic anticoagulation where appropriate.
