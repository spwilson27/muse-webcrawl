George Arthur Akerlof (born June 17, 1940) is an American economist and university professor at Georgetown University and the University of California, Berkeley. He shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information.

The Market for Lemons

Akerlof's most famous paper, "The Market for 'Lemons': Quality Uncertainty and the Market Mechanism" (1970), used the used-car market to show how information asymmetry between buyers and sellers can drive good products out of a market: because buyers cannot distinguish good cars from defective "lemons", prices fall to an average level, driving sellers of high-quality cars away — a process of adverse selection that can cause markets to unravel entirely. The paper was initially rejected by several journals as too trivial before being published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics.

Other work

Akerlof developed efficiency wage theory with Janet Yellen, arguing that employers may rationally pay above market-clearing wages. His later work brought psychology and sociology into macroeconomics: Animal Spirits (2009, with Robert Shiller) examined the role of confidence, fairness, and stories in economic fluctuations, and Identity Economics (2010, with Rachel Kranton) integrated social identity into economic models. He also wrote Phishing for Phools (2015) with Shiller.

Personal life

Akerlof is married to Janet Yellen, former Chair of the Federal Reserve and U.S. Secretary of the Treasury. His brother, Carl Akerlof, is a physicist.
