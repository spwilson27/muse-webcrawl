The Cassegrain reflector is a type of reflecting telescope that uses a combination of a primary concave mirror and a secondary convex mirror, often used in optical telescopes and radio antennas. The design was published in 1672 and is attributed to the French priest and physicist Laurent Cassegrain, a contemporary of Isaac Newton.

Design

In the classical Cassegrain, the primary mirror is a paraboloid with a hole at its centre. Light collected by the primary is reflected toward a convex hyperboloidal secondary mirror mounted before the primary's focal point; the secondary reflects the converging beam back through the central hole to a focus behind the primary mirror, where eyepieces and instruments are mounted. The secondary magnifies the effective focal length — typically three to five times the primary's — so a long focal length is folded into a short, compact tube.

Variants

The Ritchey–Chrétien variant uses hyperboloidal primary and secondary mirrors and eliminates coma, making it the standard for professional telescopes, including the Hubble Space Telescope and most large observatory instruments. The Dall–Kirkham uses an ellipsoidal primary and spherical secondary, easier to make but with more off-axis coma. The Schmidt–Cassegrain and Maksutov–Cassegrain combine the layout with corrector plates and dominate the amateur telescope market. "Cassegrain focus" positions are also standard on large reflectors such as those of the Very Large Telescope, where heavy instruments can be mounted at the stable focus behind the primary cell.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include a folded, compact optical path, convenient access to the focal point, and long effective focal lengths well suited to planetary and lunar observing and imaging. Disadvantages include the central obstruction caused by the secondary mirror (reducing contrast), tighter collimation tolerances, and — in the classical form — off-axis coma.

Radio applications

The same geometry is used in radio astronomy and satellite communications: a feed at the vertex of a parabolic dish is replaced by a sub-reflector (the Cassegrain configuration), placing the receiver at a more accessible position and improving illumination control.
