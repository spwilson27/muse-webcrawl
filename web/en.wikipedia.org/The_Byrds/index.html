The Byrds

The Byrds were an American rock band formed in Los Angeles, California, in 1964. The band underwent multiple lineup changes, with frontman Roger McGuinn remaining the sole consistent member. They are widely regarded as among the most influential bands of their era, pioneering the folk rock and country rock genres.

History

Formation and folk rock breakthrough (1964–1966)

The original quintet — McGuinn, Gene Clark, David Crosby, Chris Hillman, and Michael Clarke — achieved worldwide fame with their 1965 recording of Bob Dylan's "Mr. Tambourine Man", which topped the charts and defined the folk rock sound with McGuinn's jangling 12-string Rickenbacker guitar. Their Dylan cover "Turn! Turn! Turn!" also reached number one.

Psychedelia and country rock (1966–1973)

With "Eight Miles High" (1966) the band moved into psychedelic rock and raga rock. After Clark and Crosby departed, the Byrds recorded Sweetheart of the Rodeo (1968) with Gram Parsons — a landmark of country rock. Guitarist Clarence White joined later that year. The band dissolved in 1973 after McGuinn decided to pursue a solo career; White was killed by a drunk driver later that year.

Legacy

The Byrds' influence extends through generations of jangle pop, alternative country, and indie rock acts, including Tom Petty, R.E.M., and The Smiths. They were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1991.
