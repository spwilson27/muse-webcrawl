Assassination of Reinhard Heydrich

LocationPrague-Libeň, Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia
Date27 May 1942 (attack); 4 June 1942 (Heydrich's death)
TargetReinhard Heydrich
PerpetratorsJozef Gabčík and Jan Kubiš (Operation Anthropoid)
OutcomeHeydrich killed; Nazi reprisals including the Lidice massacre

The assassination of Reinhard Heydrich took place on 27 May 1942 in Prague during the Second World War, when Czechoslovak soldiers Jozef Gabčík and Jan Kubiš, trained by the British Special Operations Executive and sent by the Czechoslovak government-in-exile, attacked Heydrich, the acting Reich Protector of Bohemia and Moravia, chief of the Reich Security Main Office and one of the principal architects of the Holocaust. Heydrich died of his wounds on 4 June 1942. He was the highest-ranking Nazi official to be assassinated during the war. The operation was codenamed Operation Anthropoid.

The attack

Gabčík and Kubiš parachuted into the Protectorate on the night of 28–29 December 1941. On the morning of 27 May 1942 they ambushed Heydrich's open-top Mercedes-Benz as it slowed at a hairpin bend in the Holešovice/Prague-Libeň district on his daily drive from Panenské Břežany to Prague Castle. Gabčík stepped in front of the car and attempted to fire his Sten gun, but it jammed. As Heydrich ordered his driver to stop and stood to return fire with his pistol, Kubiš threw a modified anti-tank grenade at the car. The explosion drove fragments — including parts of the car's upholstery — into Heydrich's body. Both attackers escaped. Heydrich initially seemed likely to recover but developed septicaemia, probably from the wound contamination, and died on 4 June after slipping into a coma.

Reprisals

The Nazi response was savage. Hitler ordered mass reprisals; on 9–10 June 1942, SS forces destroyed the village of Lidice, shooting its men, deporting its women and children (most of the children were murdered), and razing the village to the ground, in the mistaken belief that its inhabitants had aided the assassins. The village of Ležáky was likewise annihilated later that month. In total, thousands of Czechs were executed or died in the reprisals. Betrayed by fellow parachutist Karel Čurda, the assassins and five comrades were cornered in the Orthodox Cathedral of Saints Cyril and Methodius in Prague on 18 June 1942; after a six-hour gun battle in the crypt, they took their own lives rather than be captured.

Consequences

Politically, the assassination achieved the exiled Czechoslovak government's aim of demonstrating resistance at home: Britain and France subsequently repudiated the Munich Agreement of 1938, greatly strengthening Czechoslovakia's post-war claims. Heydrich's death removed one of the Third Reich's most capable and feared administrators; his name had been given to the "Heydrichiáda" terror in the Protectorate, and a covert follow-up action, Operation Reinhard — the extermination of the Jews of occupied Poland — was named in his memory.
