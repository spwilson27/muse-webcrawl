Richardsonian Romanesque is a style of Romanesque Revival architecture named for the American architect Henry Hobson Richardson (1838–1886), who adapted the medieval Romanesque architecture of southern France and Spain into a distinctive, personal idiom. Richardson's mature manner — beginning with Trinity Church, Boston (1872–1877), and developed in libraries, railroad stations, courthouses and commercial buildings — was widely imitated across the United States and Canada in the 1880s and 1890s, making it one of the first American architectural styles to influence Europe in return.

Trinity Church, Boston (1877), Richardson's breakthrough building.

Characteristics

  • Massive, heavy masonry construction, often of rough-faced (rock-faced) stone in contrasting colours.
  • Broad, round Romanesque arches, especially large entrance arches resting on short, squat columns.
  • Deeply recessed windows and entrances, cylindrical towers with conical caps, and asymmetrical massing.
  • Polychromatic stonework and simplified medieval detailing without archaeological copying.

Major works

Richardson's key buildings include Trinity Church, Boston; the Allegheny County Courthouse and Jail in Pittsburgh (1884–1888); the Marshall Field Wholesale Store in Chicago (1885–1887, demolished), a landmark of commercial design; a series of small libraries in Massachusetts (Winn Memorial, Crane Memorial, Ames Free Library); the Ames Gate Lodge; and picturesque suburban railway stations for the Boston and Albany Railroad. After Richardson's death in 1886, his successors Shepley, Rutan and Coolidge continued the style, which other firms applied to courthouses, city halls, churches, university buildings and depots nationwide.

Influence and decline

The style signalled a move away from the picturesque eclecticism of High Victorian Gothic toward bolder, more unified massing, and it influenced the young Louis Sullivan and Frank Lloyd Wright. By the 1900s it was being displaced by Beaux-Arts classicism and the Colonial Revival for public buildings, though its rugged stone aesthetic persisted in park structures and National Park Service rustic architecture.
