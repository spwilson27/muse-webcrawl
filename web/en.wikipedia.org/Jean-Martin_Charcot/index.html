Jean-Martin Charcot (French: [ʒɑ̃ maʁtɛ̃ ʃaʁko]; 29 November 1825 – 16 August 1893) was a French neurologist and professor of anatomical pathology. He is often regarded as the founder of modern neurology. His work at the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris greatly influenced the developing fields of neurology and psychology, and he was called "the Napoleon of the neuroses."

Career

Charcot took his medical degree in Paris in 1853 and in 1862 became physician to the Salpêtrière, a vast hospital-asylum housing thousands of women. Working with his collaborator Désiré-Magloire Bourneville, he systematically observed, classified, and photographed patients, correlating clinical symptoms with post-mortem lesions. In 1882 a chair of diseases of the nervous system — the first of its kind — was created for him, and his Tuesday lessons (leçons du mardi) drew students from across Europe, among them Sigmund Freud, Joseph Babinski, Georges Gilles de la Tourette, and Pierre Janet.

Contributions

Charcot gave classic descriptions of multiple sclerosis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis ("Charcot's disease"), Parkinson's disease, Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy), Charcot joints in tabes dorsalis, and numerous other conditions. His clinicopathological method — carefully recording symptoms during life and verifying them against autopsy findings — became the template for neurological research.

Hysteria and hypnosis

Late in his career Charcot studied hysteria and hypnotism, presenting hysteria as a genuine neurological disorder that could affect men as well as women, and staging dramatic demonstrations of hysterical attacks. His interpretations were later criticized — notably by the Nancy school of Hippolyte Bernheim and by his own former student Babinski — as artifacts of suggestion, and his reputation in this area declined after his death, though his anatomical and clinical neurology endured.

Legacy

Charcot's name survives in many eponyms in medicine. His son Jean-Baptiste Charcot became a noted polar explorer. The clinical tradition he founded at the Salpêtrière shaped neurology worldwide, and his influence on the young Freud helped seed psychoanalysis.
