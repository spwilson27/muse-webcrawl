Papal primacy

Papal primacy, also known as the primacy of the bishop of Rome, is the theological and juridical doctrine that the pope, as bishop of Rome and successor of Saint Peter, holds a position of leadership, authority and jurisdiction over the whole Christian Church. The scope of that primacy — whether a primacy of honour or of universal jurisdiction — is one of the principal points of division between the Roman Catholic Church and other Christian traditions.

Catholic doctrine

The Catholic Church teaches that Christ conferred a unique primacy on Peter (Matthew 16:18–19), that this primacy continues in his successors at Rome, and that the pope possesses "full, supreme, and universal power over the whole Church" (Vatican II, Lumen gentium). The First Vatican Council (1870), in Pastor aeternus, defined papal primacy of jurisdiction and papal infallibility as dogmas.

Other views

The Eastern Orthodox Church acknowledges the bishop of Rome as primus inter pares ("first among equals") among the patriarchs of the ancient pentarchy, granting him a primacy of honour but rejecting universal ordinary jurisdiction over all churches; disagreement over primacy was a major factor in the East–West Schism of 1054. Protestant traditions generally reject papal primacy altogether, holding Scripture rather than any office as the supreme authority. Ecumenical dialogues, including the 2007 Ravenna document, continue to examine the role of a universal primate.
