Anti-ship missile

An anti-ship missile (AShM or ASM) is a guided missile designed for use against ships and large boats. Most anti-ship missiles are of the sea-skimming variety, flying low over the water to delay detection, and many use a combination of inertial guidance and active radar homing in the terminal phase. They can be launched from aircraft, ships, submarines, helicopters and shore batteries.

History

Precursors appeared in the Second World War — German guided bombs sank the Italian battleship Roma in 1943 — but the missile age opened in 1967, when Egyptian Styx missiles sank the Israeli destroyer Eilat, the first ship sunk by a naval guided missile. In the 1982 Falklands War, Argentine Exocet missiles sank HMS Sheffield and Atlantic Conveyor, demonstrating the threat to modern warships. Well-known types include the American Harpoon, the French Exocet, the Norwegian Naval Strike Missile and various Russian and Chinese families such as the Kalibr and YJ-18.

Defences

Ships defend themselves with surface-to-air missiles, close-in weapon systems such as Phalanx, electronic warfare, chaff and decoys, and manoeuvre. The growth of long-range, fast and stealthy anti-ship missiles — including supersonic and ballistic types — is a central concern of modern naval warfare. See also cruise missile.
