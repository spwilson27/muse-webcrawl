Uniform Commercial Code

The Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), first published in 1952, is one of a number of uniform acts that have been established as law with the goal of harmonizing the laws of sales and other commercial transactions across the United States through adoption by every state within its territory. Because the United States Constitution leaves most contract and commercial law to the states, the UCC is a model code rather than federal law; each state adopts it, often with minor variations.

History

The code was developed by the Uniform Law Commission in cooperation with the American Law Institute, with legal scholar Karl Llewellyn serving as chief reporter. It drew on earlier uniform acts such as the Uniform Sales Act of 1906. Pennsylvania was the first state to adopt the UCC, in 1953; by the late 1960s every state except Louisiana — whose civil-law tradition differs — had adopted most of it. Louisiana has enacted most articles but not Article 2 (sales of goods) or Article 2A in full.

Articles

  • Article 1 — General provisions and definitions.
  • Article 2 — Sales of goods.
  • Article 2A — Leases of goods.
  • Article 3 — Negotiable instruments (checks, notes).
  • Article 4 — Bank deposits and collections.
  • Article 4A — Funds transfers.
  • Article 5 — Letters of credit.
  • Article 6 — Bulk sales (repealed or revised in most states).
  • Article 7 — Documents of title (warehouse receipts, bills of lading).
  • Article 8 — Investment securities.
  • Article 9 — Secured transactions.

Significance

The UCC governs an enormous share of American commerce, from sales of goods over $500 and secured lending to the clearing of checks. Article 9 filings — financing statements recorded with secretaries of state — provide the public notice system by which creditors perfect security interests in personal property. Periodic revisions, including the 1998 revision of Article 9 and the 2022 amendments addressing digital assets and controllable electronic records, keep the code current.
