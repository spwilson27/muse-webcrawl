Dative case

The dative case (abbreviated DAT) is a grammatical case used in some languages to indicate the recipient or beneficiary of an action, as in "Maria Jacobo potum dedit" (Latin for "Maria gave Jacob a drink"). In English the role is usually played by the indirect object, expressed with word order or prepositions such as to and for.

Typical functionIndirect object; recipient; beneficiary
Found inLatin, Greek, German, Russian, Sanskrit, Japanese (particle), Turkish, many others

Uses

The core use marks the indirect object of ditransitive verbs: German Ich gebe dem Mann das Buch ("I give the man the book"). Many languages extend the dative to other functions:

  • Dative of possession, as in Latin mihi est liber ("I have a book," literally "to me is a book").
  • Dativus ethicus ("ethical dative"), a pronoun indicating a person interested in the action.
  • Dative of agent with passive constructions in Greek and Latin.
  • Government by particular verbs and prepositions — German verbs like helfen ("to help") and prepositions like mit and zu take the dative; Russian uses the dative for age, necessity constructions, and many prepositions.

In specific languages

Ancient Greek and Sanskrit had distinct dative cases; in Latin the dative merged some functions of the older instrumental and locative. In Modern English the dative survives only vestigially in pronouns (e.g., whom) and in fixed expressions. In German, articles and adjectives inflect distinctly for the dative, and case errors are a classic shibboleth. Hungarian and Finnish express similar meanings with separate cases (dative and allative), and Japanese uses the particle ni.

History of the term

The name comes from Latin dativus, a loan translation of Greek dotikḗ (ptôsis), "pertaining to giving," coined by ancient grammarians.
