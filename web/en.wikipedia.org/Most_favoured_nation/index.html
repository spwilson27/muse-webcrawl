Most favoured nation

In international economic relations and international politics, most favoured nation (MFN) is a status or level of treatment accorded by one state to another in international trade. The term means the country which is the recipient of this treatment must receive equal advantages as the "most favoured nation" by the country granting such treatment (trade advantages include low tariffs or high import quotas). In effect, a country that has been accorded MFN status may not be treated less advantageously than any other country with MFN status by the promising country. There is a debate in legal circles as to whether MFN clauses in bilateral investment treaties include dispute settlement provisions.

Under the World Trade Organization (WTO) agreements, countries cannot normally discriminate between their trading partners. If a country lowers a trade barrier or opens up a market, it has to do so for the same goods or services from all its trading partners, whether rich or poor, weak or strong. Membership in the WTO, such as the United States is a member, means that a country, if granted MFN status, should not be treated less advantageously than other WTO members that are also accorded MFN status.

GATT and the WTO

Under World Trade Organization rules, a member of the WTO is not allowed to discriminate between trade partners. If a member grants another country a special favour (such as a lower customs duty rate for one of their products), then the member must do the same for all other WTO members. This principle is known as most-favoured-nation treatment and is one of the pillars of trade in the WTO.

The most favoured nation principle originates from Article I of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT 1994). Its exceptions are under Article XXIV for customs unions and free trade areas, and under the Enabling Clause for preferential trade arrangements in trade in goods between developing countries.

History

The MFN clause has been present in trade agreements since the early modern period. An early form can be found in the Treaty of Madrid (1526). In the 19th century, the Cobden–Chevalier Treaty of 1860 between Britain and France popularized the unconditional MFN clause, under which concessions granted to any third party automatically extended to the treaty partner.

In the United States, MFN status was long granted annually to countries such as China, subject to congressional review under the Jackson–Vanik amendment. In 1998, the United States changed the terminology in its domestic law from "most favoured nation" to "normal trade relations" (NTR), although the underlying WTO concept retained the MFN name.

Exceptions

Despite the general rule, the WTO agreements allow exceptions. Countries can set up a free trade agreement that applies only to goods traded within the group, can give developing countries special access to their markets, can raise barriers against products from specific countries considered to be traded unfairly, and, for services, can grant limited discrimination.
