Interferon gamma (IFN-γ) is a dimerized soluble cytokine that is the only member of the type II class of interferons. It plays an important role in innate and adaptive immunity against viral, some bacterial and protozoal infections and in the control of tumors. IFN-γ is produced predominantly by natural killer (NK) cells as part of the innate immune response, and by CD4+ Th1 and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes once antigen-specific immunity develops.

Function

IFN-γ binds to the interferon gamma receptor (IFNGR), activating the JAK-STAT signalling pathway — chiefly STAT1 — and inducing the transcription of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes. Its principal effects include:

  • Activation of macrophages, greatly enhancing their microbicidal activity (the classical "macrophage-activating factor")
  • Increased antigen presentation through up-regulation of MHC class I and II molecules
  • Promotion of Th1 differentiation and suppression of Th2 responses
  • Direct antiviral and immunoregulatory effects

Clinical significance

Defects in the IFN-γ pathway cause Mendelian susceptibility to mycobacterial disease, with severe infections by weakly virulent mycobacteria such as BCG. Excessive IFN-γ contributes to autoinammatory and autoimmune pathology and to the cytokine-release syndrome seen in haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH); the monoclonal antibody emapalumab, which neutralizes IFN-γ, is approved for primary HLH. Recombinant interferon gamma-1b (Actimmune) is used to reduce infections in chronic granulomatous disease and in severe malignant osteopetrosis. Measurement of IFN-γ release by T cells in response to tuberculosis antigens forms the basis of the interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) used to diagnose latent TB infection.
