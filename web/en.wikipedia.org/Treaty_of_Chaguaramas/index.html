The Treaty of Chaguaramas is the founding treaty of the Caribbean Community (CARICOM). It was signed on 4 July 1973 at Chaguaramas, Trinidad and Tobago, by the prime ministers of Barbados (Errol Barrow), Guyana (Forbes Burnham), Jamaica (Michael Manley) and Trinidad and Tobago (Eric Williams), and entered into force on 1 August 1973. The treaty established CARICOM and the Caribbean Common Market, replacing the earlier Caribbean Free Trade Association (CARIFTA).

Provisions

The treaty set out three pillars of regional integration: economic integration through a common market, with a common external tariff and harmonised economic policies; functional cooperation in areas such as education, health, transport and meteorology, building on institutions like the University of the West Indies; and coordination of foreign policy among member states. It created institutions including the Conference of Heads of Government as the supreme organ, ministerial councils, and a Secretariat based in Georgetown, Guyana. Membership expanded over the following years to most of the English-speaking Caribbean, later joined by Suriname (1995) and Haiti (2002), bringing CARICOM to fifteen member states.

Revised Treaty

By the late 1980s the integration movement was judged to have stalled; the West Indian Commission's 1992 report Time for Action urged deeper integration. The result was the Revised Treaty of Chaguaramas, signed in 2001 and provisionally applied from 2002, which established the CARICOM Single Market and Economy (CSME) — providing for the free movement of goods, services, capital and skilled persons — and created new institutions including the Caribbean Court of Justice, inaugurated in 2005, which serves both as the court of final appeal for some states and as the tribunal interpreting the treaty. Implementation of the single economy has proceeded unevenly, and the free movement of people remains limited to certain categories of workers.
