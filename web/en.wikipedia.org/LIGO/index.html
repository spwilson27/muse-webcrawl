The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) is a large-scale physics experiment and observatory designed to detect cosmic gravitational waves and to develop gravitational-wave observation as an astronomical tool. Its two identical detectors, in Hanford, Washington, and Livingston, Louisiana, are the largest and most sensitive gravitational-wave detectors ever built.

Principle

Each detector is a giant Michelson interferometer: laser light is split between two perpendicular vacuum arms, each 4 km long, and bounced between mirrors at the ends. A passing gravitational wave stretches one arm while squeezing the other, altering the interference pattern by an amount corresponding to a displacement a thousand times smaller than a proton's diameter. Fabry–Pérot cavities and power recycling amplify the signal; the twin sites, 3,000 km apart, allow coincident detection that rejects local noise and helps localise sources.

History and detections

Conceived in the 1970s by Rainer Weiss and developed by Caltech and MIT with Kip Thorne, Ronald Drever and others, LIGO was funded by the National Science Foundation and began science runs in 2002. The upgraded Advanced LIGO achieved the first direct detection of gravitational waves on 14 September 2015: the signal GW150914, from the merger of two black holes about 1.3 billion light-years away, announced in February 2016 — a landmark confirmation of a century-old prediction of Einstein's general relativity. Weiss, Thorne and project director Barry Barish received the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Subsequent observing runs, jointly with the European Virgo detector (and later Japan's KAGRA), have catalogued scores of events, overwhelmingly binary black hole mergers, plus binary neutron star mergers — including GW170817 (August 2017), whose light was observed across the electromagnetic spectrum, launching multi-messenger astronomy and identifying such mergers as factories of heavy elements like gold.

Future

Upgrades (A+) aim to increase the detection volume several-fold, and a third LIGO detector, LIGO-India, is under construction in Maharashtra. Proposed next-generation observatories include the underground Einstein Telescope in Europe and the 40-km Cosmic Explorer in the United States.
