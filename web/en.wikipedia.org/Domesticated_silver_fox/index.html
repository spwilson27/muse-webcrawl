Domesticated silver fox

The domesticated silver fox is a form of the silver fox (Vulpes vulpes) that has been domesticated through a long-running selective-breeding experiment at the Institute of Cytology and Genetics in Novosibirsk, Russia. Begun in 1959 by Soviet geneticist Dmitry Belyayev and continued by Lyudmila Trut, the experiment selected foxes solely for tameness toward humans and produced, within a few dozen generations, animals that actively seek human contact like domestic dogs.

The experiment

Belyayev hypothesised that selection for tameness — specifically, reduced fear and aggression — was the key driver in the domestication of the dog from the wolf. Each generation, fox pups were tested for their reactions to handlers and only the tamest (eventually fewer than 10 per cent) were bred. A parallel control line was selected for aggression, producing foxes that fiercely resent handling.

Domestication syndrome

Along with behaviour, the tame foxes developed a suite of correlated physical changes: floppy ears, curled tails, shorter snouts, piebald coats, and altered reproductive timing — traits collectively termed "domestication syndrome," also seen across domesticated species. Physiological studies showed changes in stress hormones and in serotonergic and other neurotransmitter systems. The experiment is a classic demonstration of how selection on a single behavioural trait can have wide-ranging developmental effects, though recent scholarship has debated aspects of the syndrome's framing.

Legacy

Tame foxes from the project have occasionally been sold as exotic pets under restrictions, and the population remains an active research resource for the genetics of behaviour and domestication.
