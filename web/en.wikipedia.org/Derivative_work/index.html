Derivative work

Translations, adaptations, sequels and remixes are classic examples of derivative works in copyright law.

In copyright law, a derivative work is an expressive creation that includes major copyrightable elements of an original, previously created first work (the underlying work). The derivative work becomes a second, separate work independent from the first. The transformation, modification or adaptation of the work must be substantial and bear its author's personality so as to be original and thus protected by copyright. Translations, cinematic adaptations, stage dramatizations, fictionalizations, musical arrangements, sound recordings, art reproductions, abridgments and condensations are all classic examples.

Legal framework

Under the U.S. Copyright Act of 1976, the copyright owner has the exclusive right "to prepare derivative works based upon the copyrighted work" (17 U.S.C. § 106(2)), and "derivative work" is defined in § 101. Internationally, Article 2(3) of the Berne Convention provides that translations, adaptations, arrangements of music and other alterations of a literary or artistic work are protected as original works without prejudice to the copyright in the original work. Unauthorized derivative works may infringe, unless a limitation or exception such as fair use applies — a defense tested in major cases including Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music (1994) on parody and Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) on the limits of transformative use.

Copyright in derivative works

Copyright in a derivative work extends only to the new material contributed by its author and does not imply any exclusive right in the preexisting material. In free and open-source software and content licensing, the concept maps onto copyleft obligations: licenses such as the GNU General Public License and Creative Commons ShareAlike require derivatives to be distributed under the same terms.
