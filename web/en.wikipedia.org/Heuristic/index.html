Heuristic

A heuristic is any approach to problem solving or self-discovery that employs a practical method that is not guaranteed to be optimal, perfect, or rational, but is nevertheless sufficient for reaching an immediate, short-term goal or approximation. Heuristics are mental shortcuts that ease the cognitive load of making decisions; the word comes from the Greek heurískō, "I find" or "discover" — the same root as Archimedes' "Eureka".

In psychology

Research by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman identified heuristics people use in judgment under uncertainty: representativeness (judging probability by resemblance), availability (judging likelihood by how easily examples come to mind), and anchoring and adjustment (relying too heavily on an initial value). These shortcuts are usually serviceable but can produce systematic biases. Gerd Gigerenzer's research program on "fast and frugal heuristics" argues that simple rules can be ecologically rational, sometimes outperforming complex models in uncertain environments.

In computer science

In algorithm design, a heuristic function estimates the cost of reaching a goal and guides search. In the A* algorithm, an admissible heuristic — one that never overestimates the true cost — guarantees an optimal path. Heuristic methods such as greedy algorithms, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, and tabu search are used for hard optimization problems where exact solutions are infeasible. In software, "heuristic" also describes the pattern-matching used by antivirus programs to detect previously unseen malware.

In mathematics and education

George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945) popularized heuristic strategies for mathematical problem solving — work backwards, consider a simpler problem, draw a diagram — that are now standard in mathematics education. Here "heuristic" denotes teachable, experience-based techniques rather than formal proof procedures.
