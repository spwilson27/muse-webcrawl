Antiknock agent

Antiknock agent
PurposeRaise the octane rating of gasoline; suppress engine knocking
Classic exampleTetraethyllead (TEL)
Modern alternativesMTBE, ethanol, alkylates, aromatics, ferrocene, MMT

An antiknock agent is a gasoline additive used to reduce engine knocking (the uncontrolled detonation of the fuel–air mixture in a spark-ignition engine) by increasing the fuel's octane rating — its resistance to auto-ignition under compression. Higher octane allows higher compression ratios and more efficient, powerful engines. Antiknock agents are among the most consequential, and controversial, chemicals of the twentieth century.

Tetraethyllead

The most famous antiknock agent is tetraethyllead (TEL), discovered in 1921 by Thomas Midgley Jr. and colleagues at General Motors. Marketed from 1923 as "Ethyl" gasoline, TEL was extraordinarily effective in tiny concentrations, decomposing in the engine to form lead oxide particles that interrupt the radical chain reactions leading to detonation. Its toxicity was known from the start — workers died in its manufacture in the 1920s — and over the following decades leaded petrol dispersed lead throughout the global environment, raising blood-lead levels in children and contributing to neurological harm on a vast scale. Research by Clair Patterson and others drove regulation: the United States phased out leaded road fuel between the 1970s and 1996, the European Union banned it in 2000, and the worldwide phase-out for road vehicles was completed in 2021, when Algeria, the last country, stopped sales. TEL remains in use in aviation gasoline (avgas) for piston-engined aircraft.

Other agents

After lead, refiners raised octane by reformulating gasoline itself — increasing branched alkanes (alkylate), aromatics and reformate — and by oxygenate additives such as MTBE (later restricted because of groundwater contamination), ETBE and ethanol, which is now the principal octane booster in many markets. Other metallic additives include MMT (a manganese compound, used in some countries) and ferrocene (iron-based). Modern engines with knock sensors and direct injection also tolerate lower-octane fuel more gracefully.
