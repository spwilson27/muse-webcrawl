Apoha

Apoha (Sanskrit for "exclusion") is a theory of meaning and concept formation developed in Indian Buddhist philosophy, principally by the logicians Dignāga (5th–6th century CE) and Dharmakīrti. According to the theory, words and concepts do not refer to positive universals; instead, a word such as "cow" functions by excluding everything that is not a cow (the "exclusion of the other", anyāpoha).

Rationale

Buddhist metaphysics holds that reality consists of momentary, unique particulars (svalakṣaṇa) and denies the existence of real universals posited by schools such as Nyāya. The apoha theory explains how language can be meaningful and general without committing to such universals: meaning is a double negation constructed by the mind.

Debate

The theory was elaborated by later thinkers such as Ratnakīrti and was vigorously criticised by Hindu philosophers including Kumārila and Udayana, generating centuries of debate about semantics, perception and conceptual content in Indian philosophy.
