Bereavement in Judaism

Bereavement in Judaism (avelut, Hebrew: אבלות, mourning) encompasses a structured system of rituals and practices observed after the death of a close relative. Jewish law (halakha) designates seven relatives for whom mourning is obligatory: father, mother, spouse, son, daughter, brother, and sister. The practices are traditionally divided into graduated stages of decreasing intensity, intended to honour the dead and to ease the mourner gradually back into normal life.

Before and at burial

Between death and burial the mourner is an onen, exempt from most positive commandments in order to attend to funeral arrangements. The body is watched over (shemirah), ritually washed (taharah) by the burial society (chevra kadisha), and buried, traditionally in a simple shroud and plain coffin, as soon as reasonably possible. At the funeral mourners perform keriah, the tearing of a garment (or a ribbon in some modern practice), and recite psalms and the memorial prayer El Malei Rachamim. Mourners traditionally help fill the grave.

Stages of mourning

  • Shiva ("seven"): the seven days after burial, during which mourners remain at home, sit on low stools, cover mirrors, refrain from work and bathing for pleasure, and receive visitors who come to comfort them; the Mourner's Kaddish is recited at daily prayer services held in the home.
  • Sheloshim ("thirty"): the remainder of the first thirty days, when mourners return to work but traditionally avoid haircuts, shaving, celebrations, and live-music entertainment.
  • Shneim asar chodesh: for a deceased parent, mourning continues for twelve months, during which the Kaddish is recited (for eleven months) and festivities are avoided.

Yahrzeit and remembrance

The anniversary of the death (yahrzeit) is marked annually by lighting a 24-hour candle, reciting Kaddish, and often visiting the grave. The unveiling of the headstone (matzevah) commonly takes place within the first year. Communal remembrance prayers (Yizkor) are recited on Yom Kippur, Shemini Atzeret, the last day of Passover, and Shavuot.
