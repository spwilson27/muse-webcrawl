Isabella Augusta, Lady Gregory (née Persse; 15 March 1852 – 22 May 1932) was an Anglo-Irish dramatist, folklorist and theatre manager. With William Butler Yeats and Edward Martyn, she co-founded the Irish Literary Theatre and the Abbey Theatre, and wrote numerous short plays for the Abbey. She also produced a number of books of retellings of stories from Irish mythology. Born into a class that identified closely with British rule, she turned against it over several encounters in her life, and she came to see her family's home, Coole Park in County Galway, as a centre of the Irish literary revival.

Life

Augusta Persse grew up at Roxborough House in County Galway and in 1880 married Sir William Henry Gregory, a widower 35 years her senior, former Governor of Ceylon and MP for Galway, who owned the neighbouring estate of Coole Park. After his death in 1892 she edited his autobiography, travelled, and increasingly involved herself in Irish cultural life. Her meeting with W. B. Yeats in 1896, while he was staying at nearby Tulira Castle, began one of the most productive collaborations of the Irish revival: Yeats spent long periods at Coole, and Gregory collected folklore from the tenants and workhouse inmates around Gort, rendering their speech in the "Kiltartan" dialect of her retellings.

Work

Her mythological retellings include Cuchulain of Muirthemne (1902), praised by Yeats as the best book to have come out of Ireland in his time, and Gods and Fighting Men (1904). For the Abbey she wrote or adapted some forty plays, most famously the one-act comedy Spreading the News (1904) and the tragedy The Gaol Gate (1906), as well as The Rising of the Moon and The Workhouse Ward. As a director of the Abbey she defended the theatre through controversies including the Playboy riots of 1907 and the dispute over Hugh Lane's bequest of paintings to Dublin, in which she campaigned on behalf of her nephew.

Legacy

Gregory's journals, published after her death, are a major record of the revival. Although long overshadowed by Yeats and Synge, her work as a playwright, collector and impresario is now recognised as central to the creation of a modern Irish national theatre and literature.
