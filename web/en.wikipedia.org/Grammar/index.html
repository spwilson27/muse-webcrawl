In linguistics, grammar is the set of rules for how a natural language is structured, as demonstrated by its speakers or writers. Grammar rules may concern the use of clauses, phrases, and words. The term can also refer to the study of such rules, a field that includes phonology, morphology, and syntax, together with phonetics, semantics, and pragmatics.

Descriptive and prescriptive grammar

Every language has a grammar in the descriptive sense: the (usually tacit) system that native speakers know and use. Prescriptive grammar, by contrast, sets out rules of "correct" usage based on some standard variety; disputes over constructions such as split infinitives or stranded prepositions arise when prescriptive rules conflict with actual usage.

Components

Morphology studies the internal structure of words — roots, affixes, and inflection. Syntax studies how words combine into phrases and sentences: word order, agreement, case, and dependencies between sentence parts. Grammars also account for how sound (phonology) and meaning (semantics) interface with these structures.

Grammar across languages

Languages differ enormously in grammatical structure: some, like Latin or Turkish, pack meaning into rich word forms (synthetic languages), while others, like Mandarin or Vietnamese, rely mostly on word order and particles (analytic languages). Typologists classify languages by features such as basic word order (SOV, SVO, VSO...) and alignment of grammatical relations.

History of grammatical study

Formal grammar study begins with Pāṇini's description of Sanskrit (c. 4th century BCE), continues through Greek and Latin grammarians such as Dionysius Thrax and Priscian, and the medieval and Renaissance tradition. Modern linguistics, from the structuralism of Saussure to Noam Chomsky's generative grammar, treats grammar as a formal system modelling speakers' linguistic competence.
