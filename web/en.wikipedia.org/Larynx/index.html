Larynx

The larynx (plural: larynges or larynxes), commonly called the voice box, is an organ in the top of the neck involved in breathing, producing sound, and protecting the trachea against food aspiration. It manipulates the pitch and volume of sound and is the source of the voice in humans and other mammals. The larynx houses the vocal cords (vocal folds) and sits just below where the pharynx splits into the trachea and esophagus.

Structure

The laryngeal skeleton consists of nine cartilages: three single cartilages (the thyroid cartilage, which forms the Adam's apple; the ring-shaped cricoid cartilage; and the leaf-shaped epiglottis) and three paired cartilages (arytenoid, corniculate, and cuneiform). Intrinsic muscles adjust the position and tension of the vocal folds, which consist of the vocal ligament covered by mucous membrane. The recurrent laryngeal nerve, a branch of the vagus nerve, supplies nearly all the intrinsic muscles.

Function

During phonation, air expelled from the lungs sets the adducted vocal folds vibrating, producing sound that is shaped by the pharynx, mouth, and nasal cavities. The larynx also acts as a valve: the epiglottis and the closure of the vocal folds protect the airway during swallowing, and the cough reflex expels foreign material. Differences in vocal fold length and thickness account for the change in voice at puberty, especially in males.

Clinical significance

Disorders include laryngitis, vocal fold nodules and polyps, laryngopharyngeal reflux, and laryngeal cancer, which is strongly associated with smoking. Surgical removal of the larynx is called a laryngectomy.
