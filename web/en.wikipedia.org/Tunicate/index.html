Tunicate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tunicate is a marine invertebrate animal, a member of the subphylum Tunicata (also called Urochordata). It is part of the Chordata, a phylum which includes all animals with dorsal nerve cords and notochords (including vertebrates). Tunicates are named for their tough outer covering, the "tunic", which is formed of tunicin, a type of cellulose — unusual for an animal.

Anatomy and life cycle

Most tunicates, such as the sea squirts (class Ascidiacea), are sessile filter feeders as adults, drawing water through an oral siphon and expelling it through an atrial siphon while trapping plankton in a mucous net. Their free-swimming larva resembles a tiny tadpole and possesses a notochord, dorsal nerve cord and tail; at metamorphosis these chordate features are largely resorbed as the adult form develops.

Classes

  • Ascidiacea — sea squirts; solitary or colonial, benthic
  • Thaliacea — salps, doliolids and pyrosomes; free-floating, often bioluminescent
  • Appendicularia (Larvacea) — retain larval form as adults and secrete complex mucus "houses" for filter feeding

Ecology and significance

Tunicates are important filter feeders and, in the case of salps, contribute significantly to the ocean's biological carbon pump. Some species are invasive fouling pests on ships and aquaculture gear; others are eaten in various cuisines. Molecular studies place tunicates, surprisingly, as the closest living relatives of vertebrates.
