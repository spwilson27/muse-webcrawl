A human milk bank or breast milk bank is a service that collects, screens, processes and dispenses donated human breast milk, primarily for hospitalised preterm and medically fragile infants whose mothers cannot supply enough of their own milk. Health bodies including the World Health Organization recommend donor human milk as the preferred alternative when a mother's own milk is unavailable, ahead of infant formula, because it lowers the risk of necrotising enterocolitis in preterm babies.

Operation

Donors are screened by health questionnaire and blood tests for infections such as HIV, hepatitis and syphilis. Expressed milk is frozen, pooled where permitted, pasteurised (commonly by the Holder method, 62.5 °C for 30 minutes), tested for bacterial contamination, and dispensed on prescription, usually to neonatal intensive care units. Some banks also supply milk to older infants or adults with specific medical needs.

History and extent

The first milk bank opened in Vienna in 1909; by the mid-twentieth century hundreds existed, but numbers fell with the HIV crisis of the 1980s before recovering under modern screening. Brazil built the world's largest network, with over 200 banks integrated into its public health system. Co-ordinating bodies include the Human Milk Banking Association of North America (founded 1985) and the European Milk Bank Association (2010). Informal milk sharing outside banks — through peer networks and online marketplaces — remains common and controversial because of contamination and disease risks.
