Militarisation of space

The militarisation of space involves the placement and development of weaponry and military technology in outer space. From the earliest days of the space age, military uses — reconnaissance, communications, navigation, and early warning — have accompanied civilian exploration. The term is often distinguished from the weaponisation of space, which refers specifically to placing weapons in orbit or using them against targets in space or on Earth.

History

Early spaceflight grew directly out of ballistic missile programs, and by 1960 both the United States and the Soviet Union operated photographic reconnaissance satellites (Corona and Zenit). Military satellite communications, weather, and navigation systems followed; the American GPS constellation, built for military guidance, became a global civilian utility. Crewed military programs included the US Manned Orbiting Laboratory (cancelled 1969) and the Soviet Almaz stations of the 1970s, one of which test-fired a cannon in orbit.

Space weapons and ASAT

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty banned weapons of mass destruction in orbit but did not prohibit conventional weapons or military activity generally. Both superpowers developed anti-satellite (ASAT) weapons; the USSR tested co-orbital interceptors, and in 1985 a US F-15 destroyed a satellite with an air-launched missile. The Strategic Defense Initiative ("Star Wars", 1983) proposed space-based missile defences but was never deployed. China's 2007 destruction of its Fengyun-1C satellite created thousands of tracked debris fragments and drew international criticism; India conducted a similar test in 2019, and Russia destroyed a satellite in 2021.

Contemporary developments

Modern armed forces depend on space for intelligence, precision strike, communications, and missile warning, making satellites high-value targets and prompting investment in resilience, electronic warfare, and rendezvous operations. In December 2019 the United States established the United States Space Force as a separate service. Diplomats continue to debate new treaties on the prevention of an arms race in outer space (PAROS) amid concerns that debris-generating ASAT tests threaten all space activity via the Kessler syndrome.
