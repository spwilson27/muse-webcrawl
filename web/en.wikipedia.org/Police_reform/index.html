Police reform

Public debate over the role and accountability of police drives reform efforts.

Police reform refers to efforts to transform the values, policies, and practices of police organizations, typically in response to concerns about misconduct, use of force, corruption, or lack of accountability. Reform movements have recurred throughout the history of modern policing, from the professionalization campaigns of the early 20th century onward.

Common measures

Proposed and enacted reforms frequently include body-worn cameras, civilian review boards, revised use-of-force standards, duty-to-intervene policies, improved training in de-escalation and crisis intervention, bans or restrictions on particular tactics, changes to qualified immunity and disciplinary procedures, and greater transparency through public data on stops, searches, and complaints.

United States

In the United States, policing is largely local, with roughly 18,000 law-enforcement agencies, making reform fragmented. Major waves of reform followed the Kerner Commission (1968), the Christopher Commission after the beating of Rodney King (1991), and the federal pattern-or-practice investigations and consent decrees of the 1990s–2010s. The murder of George Floyd in 2020 by a Minneapolis officer prompted nationwide protests and a broad legislative debate about use of force, accountability, and the funding and scope of police responsibilities.

International examples

Other notable reforms include the transformation of the Royal Ulster Constabulary into the Police Service of Northern Ireland under the Patten Report (1999–2001), and Georgia's wholesale dismissal and rebuilding of its patrol police after 2003 to combat corruption.
