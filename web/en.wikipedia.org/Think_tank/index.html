A think tank, or policy institute, is a research institute that performs research and advocacy concerning topics such as social policy, political strategy, economics, military, technology, and culture. Most think tanks are not-for-profit organizations, and many receive funding from governments, businesses, and private donors. They produce reports, briefings, and books, and their staff frequently testify before legislatures and appear in the media.

History

Institutions resembling think tanks appeared in the 19th and early 20th centuries, such as Britain's Fabian Society (1884) and Royal United Services Institute (1831), and the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace (1910) and Brookings Institution (1916) in the United States. The RAND Corporation, founded in 1948 to advise the U.S. military, exemplified the "contract research" model. From the 1970s onward, explicitly ideological and advocacy-oriented tanks proliferated, such as the Heritage Foundation (1973), reshaping policy debates in Washington and, by imitation, worldwide. The phrase "think tank" itself derives from Second World War slang for a secure room where plans were discussed, and entered general use in the 1950s and 1960s.

Types and influence

Think tanks range from academic-style university-affiliated institutes to advocacy organizations promoting a defined ideology and government-run research bodies. Their influence is debated: supporters see them as bridges between scholarship and policy; critics note opaque funding and revolving-door staffing with governments and lobbying firms. Thousands exist worldwide, concentrated in the United States, Europe, China, and India.
