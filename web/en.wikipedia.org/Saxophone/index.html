The saxophone is a family of woodwind instruments invented by the Belgian maker Adolphe Sax in the 1840s and patented in Paris in 1846. Although its body is made of brass, it is classed as a woodwind because it is sounded by a single cane reed and mouthpiece like those of the clarinet. Its conical bore gives it a powerful, vocal tone between the brass and woodwind choirs.

Design and family

Sax designed two families of seven instruments each — one in B♭ and E♭ for military bands (the family in common use today) and one in C and F for orchestras. Modern players use chiefly the soprano (B♭), alto (E♭), tenor (B♭) and baritone (E♭), with sopranino, bass and contrabass instruments rarer. All saxophones are transposing instruments with a written range of about two and a half octaves, extended upward by the altissimo register.

History

Sax intended the saxophone for military bands and orchestral use; composers including Berlioz championed it, and a saxophone class was established at the Paris Conservatoire in 1857. It found its greatest role in the 20th century in jazz, where players such as Coleman Hawkins, Lester Young, Charlie Parker, John Coltrane and Sonny Rollins made the tenor and alto saxophones central voices of the music. Classical composers also wrote for it — in works by Ravel (Boléro), Prokofiev, Vaughan Williams and Glazunov, whose concerto remains a repertoire staple. The instrument is ubiquitous in concert bands, big bands, rock, funk and popular music.
