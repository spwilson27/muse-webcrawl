Section 8 of the Housing Act of 1937 (42 U.S.C. § 1437f), often called Section 8, as repeatedly amended, authorises the payment of rental housing assistance to private landlords on behalf of low-income households in the United States. It is operated through local public housing authorities (PHAs) under the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). By far the largest part of the programme is the Housing Choice Voucher programme — the successor to the "Section 8 certificate" and voucher programmes created in 1974 and merged in 1998 — under which families rent in the private market and pay roughly 30% of their income in rent, with the PHA paying the balance directly to the landlord, up to a locally set payment standard.

History

The Housing Act of 1937 created the public housing programme; "Section 8" in its modern form dates from the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974, which shifted federal strategy from building projects toward subsidising rents in existing private housing. The project-based side of the programme financed new or rehabilitated subsidised developments; from the 1980s policy moved decisively toward tenant-based vouchers, which advocates argue offer families choice and access to lower-poverty neighbourhoods — a conclusion supported by the Moving to Opportunity experiment of the 1990s, whose long-term follow-ups found large earnings gains for children who moved young to low-poverty areas.

Operation and criticism

Demand vastly exceeds supply: only about one in four eligible households receives federal rental assistance, waiting lists are often closed for years, and lotteries are common. To qualify, household income generally must not exceed 50% of the area median; PHAs must target 75% of new admissions to extremely low-income households (below 30% of median). Units must pass housing quality inspections and rents must be "reasonable". Landlord participation is voluntary in most jurisdictions; "source of income" discrimination laws in some states and cities prohibit refusing voucher holders. Recurring debates concern programme cost, neighbourhood concentration, inspection burdens, and whether vouchers raise rents for unassisted tenants; special voucher categories serve veterans (HUD-VASH), youth aging out of foster care, and people with disabilities. "Section 8" is also used pejoratively in American political discourse about subsidised tenants, a usage housing advocates criticise as stigmatising.
