In mathematics, a hypercomplex number is a traditional term for an element of a finite-dimensional unital algebra over the field of real numbers. The subject was developed in the 19th century as mathematicians sought number systems beyond the complex numbers, typically written as combinations a0 + a1i1 + … + anin of a basis of "imaginary units".

Examples

The best-known examples are two-dimensional: the complex numbers, the split-complex numbers, and the dual numbers. In four dimensions the quaternions, discovered by William Rowan Hamilton in 1843, are the most famous; they form a non-commutative division algebra. The Cayley–Dickson construction doubles dimensions repeatedly, producing the 8-dimensional octonions (non-commutative and non-associative) and the 16-dimensional sedenions, which contain zero divisors.

Classification results

The Frobenius theorem (1877) states that the only finite-dimensional associative division algebras over the reals are the real numbers, the complex numbers, and the quaternions. Hurwitz's theorem identifies the only normed division algebras as the reals, complexes, quaternions, and octonions. Hypercomplex systems such as Clifford algebras and bicomplex numbers remain important in geometry, physics, and signal processing.
