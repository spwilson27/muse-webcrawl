Goldberg polyhedron

A Goldberg polyhedron is a convex polyhedron made from hexagons and pentagons, named after the mathematician Michael Goldberg (1902–1990), who described the class in 1937. Goldberg polyhedra have exactly twelve pentagonal faces, with the remaining faces hexagonal, and possess icosahedral symmetry.

Construction

Goldberg polyhedra can be constructed by truncating an icosahedron or dodecahedron, or equivalently from a geodesic subdivision of an icosahedron in which each triangular face is divided into smaller triangles and the dual is taken. They are dual to geodesic polyhedra: interchanging faces and vertices converts one class into the other.

Parameters and applications

A Goldberg polyhedron is commonly indexed by two integers (m, n) describing the pattern of subdivision across each pentagonal region; the number of hexagonal faces grows with these parameters. Because their many faces approximate a sphere with near-uniform distribution, Goldberg polyhedra appear in the design of geodesic domes, in models of virus capsids and fullerenes, in the hexagonal tiling of some golf balls and soccer balls (the classic truncated icosahedron is the smallest interesting case), and in equal-area world map projections.
