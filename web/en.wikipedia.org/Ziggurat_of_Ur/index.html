Ziggurat of Ur

The Ziggurat of Ur (Sumerian: É-temen-ní-gùru, "house, mountain of dread") is a Neo-Sumerian ziggurat in what was once the city of Ur near Nasiriyah, in present-day Dhi Qar Governorate, Iraq. It was built during the Early Bronze Age, around the 21st century BCE, by King Ur-Nammu, who dedicated it to the moon god Nanna within the temple complex of the divine city. The structure made of mud brick is the best preserved ziggurat in Iraq, partly reconstructed in the 1980s under Saddam Hussein.

Construction and dimensions

The ziggurat originally consisted of three superimposed terraces of baked and mud brick, reaching a height estimated at about 30 metres; only the lowest terrace, measuring roughly 64 by 45 metres, survives substantially intact. A triple flight of monumental staircases converged at the first terrace, leading to a shrine at the summit. Its bricks bear stamped inscriptions of Ur-Nammu and his son Shulgi.

Rediscovery and restoration

The ruins were visited by European travellers from the 17th century onward, and excavated in the 1920s and 1930s by Sir Leonard Woolley's joint British Museum–University of Pennsylvania expedition to Ur. The facade and stairways of the lowest level were rebuilt in the 1980s. The ziggurat suffered minor damage during the Gulf wars but remains the iconic monument of the ancient city; in 2016 the site was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List as part of the Ahwar of Southern Iraq listing.
