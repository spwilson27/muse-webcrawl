The Soviet–Afghan War was a nine-year conflict in which Soviet forces intervened in Afghanistan to support the communist Democratic Republic of Afghanistan against Islamist and nationalist insurgents known as the mujahideen. It began with the Soviet invasion of December 1979 and ended with the withdrawal of the last Soviet troops on 15 February 1989.

Background and invasion

The Saur Revolution of April 1978 brought the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan to power; its radical reforms provoked widespread rebellion. Fearing the regime's collapse, the Soviet leadership invaded in December 1979, killing President Hafizullah Amin and installing Babrak Karmal. The invasion was condemned internationally: the United States boycotted the 1980 Moscow Olympics and began Operation Cyclone, a covert programme channelling weapons — later including Stinger anti-aircraft missiles — through Pakistan to the mujahideen.

Course and impact

Soviet forces, peaking at over 100,000 troops, controlled the cities and main roads but never pacified the countryside. The war devastated Afghanistan: estimates of Afghan deaths range from roughly 600,000 to two million, millions of land mines were laid, and about five million refugees fled to Pakistan and Iran. Some 15,000 Soviet soldiers were killed. Under Mikhail Gorbachev, the USSR signed the Geneva Accords in 1988 and withdrew, leaving the Najibullah government, which survived until 1992.

Legacy

The war contributed to the Soviet Union's economic strain and loss of legitimacy ahead of its collapse in 1991, and Afghanistan descended into civil war from which the Taliban emerged. Networks of Arab volunteers who fought in the war later fed into al-Qaeda, giving the conflict global significance far beyond its era.
