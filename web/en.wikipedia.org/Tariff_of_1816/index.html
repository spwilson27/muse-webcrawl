Tariff of 1816

The Tariff of 1816 was the first protective tariff enacted by the United States Congress, imposing duties averaging about 25 percent on imported manufactured goods in order to shield young American industries — especially cotton and woollen textiles — from a flood of cheap British imports that followed the end of the War of 1812.

Background

During the embargoes and war of 1807–1815, American manufacturing had grown behind an effective wall of protection. With peace in 1815, British merchants, holding accumulated inventories, sold goods in the United States at very low prices — some contemporaries alleged deliberate dumping intended to strangle American industry "in the cradle". Manufacturers petitioned Congress for relief.

Provisions and debate

The act, signed by President James Madison in April 1816, levied duties of about 25 percent on cotton and woollen goods (with a minimum-valuation device that made the duty especially heavy on cheap Indian cottons), and protective rates on iron, leather, paper, and other manufactures, while many raw materials and unlisted goods entered at lower revenue rates. Notably, the bill drew support from nationalists across sections — John C. Calhoun of South Carolina was a prominent supporter — in the postwar spirit of economic nationalism that also produced the Second Bank of the United States and Madison's (vetoed) Bonus Bill.

Legacy

The Tariff of 1816 established protection as a legitimate instrument of federal policy. Higher tariffs followed in 1824 and 1828 (the "Tariff of Abominations"), by which time sectional alignments had inverted: the South, now dependent on cotton exports, became the stronghold of free trade, a conflict that culminated in the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33.
