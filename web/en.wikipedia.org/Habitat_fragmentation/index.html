Habitat fragmentation

Habitat fragmentation describes the emergence of discontinuities (fragmentation) in an organism's preferred environment (habitat), causing population fragmentation and ecosystem decay. Causes of habitat fragmentation include geological processes that slowly alter the layout of the physical environment (suspected of being one of the major causes of speciation), and human activity such as land conversion, which can alter the environment much faster and cause the extinction of many species.

Effects

Fragmentation divides a large, continuous habitat into smaller patches separated by a matrix of different habitat. The principal consequences include reduction in total habitat area, increased edge effects, and isolation of populations. Smaller, isolated populations face higher risks of inbreeding, genetic drift, and local extinction, and may be unable to recolonize patches after local losses. Edge effects — changes in light, temperature, humidity, and predation near patch boundaries — can degrade the quality of the remaining interior habitat even where its area is unchanged.

Fragmentation is considered one of the major drivers of biodiversity loss worldwide. It interacts with habitat loss, climate change, and invasive species.

Mitigation

Conservation responses include protecting large habitat blocks, maintaining or restoring wildlife corridors and stepping-stone patches that reconnect fragments, buffer zones around reserves, and land-use planning that concentrates development. Corridors have been widely promoted, though their effectiveness varies by species and landscape.
