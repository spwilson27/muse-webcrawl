Fred Toyosaburo Korematsu (30 January 1919 – 30 March 2005) was a Japanese-American civil rights activist who defied the United States government's internment of Japanese Americans during the Second World War. His case, Korematsu v. United States (1944), in which the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion order, became one of the most controversial decisions in the Court's history.

Defiance and case

Korematsu was born in Oakland, California, to Japanese immigrant parents who ran a flower nursery. After Executive Order 9066 in 1942 required people of Japanese ancestry on the West Coast to report to assembly centres, Korematsu — who had been rejected for military service and worked as a welder — refused to comply, underwent plastic surgery and assumed an alias, but was arrested in May 1942 and convicted of violating the exclusion order. With the support of ACLU lawyer Wayne Collins he appealed; in December 1944 the Supreme Court, by 6–3, upheld the exclusion as a military necessity, in a decision later widely condemned.

Vindication

In 1983, based on evidence uncovered by researcher Peter Irons and the work of Aiko Herzig-Yoshinaga showing that the government had suppressed and altered key intelligence reports, a federal court vacated Korematsu's conviction through a writ of coram nobis. Korematsu thereafter campaigned for redress, contributing to the Civil Liberties Act of 1988, in which the US government formally apologised and paid reparations. President Bill Clinton awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1998. In 2018, in Trump v. Hawaii, the Supreme Court finally repudiated the Korematsu decision as gravely wrong.
