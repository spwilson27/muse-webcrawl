Star formation

Star formation is the process by which dense regions within molecular clouds in interstellar space collapse under their own gravity to form stars. It is a central topic of astrophysics, closely linked to the formation of planets, which condense from the leftover material in circumstellar disks around newborn stars.

Collapse of a molecular cloud

Star formation begins in giant molecular clouds — cold (10–20 K), dense concentrations of mostly molecular hydrogen. When a region exceeds the Jeans mass, disturbances such as supernova shock waves or galactic collisions can trigger gravitational collapse. As a fragment contracts it heats up and fragments further, forming a rotating protostar surrounded by an accretion disk. Bipolar outflows and jets expel angular momentum and excess material.

From protostar to main sequence

The collapsing core passes through observable stages — class 0 and I protostars deeply embedded in dust, then class II (classical T Tauri) and class III sources as the envelope disperses. When the core temperature reaches roughly ten million kelvin, hydrogen fusion ignites and the object becomes a main-sequence star in hydrostatic equilibrium. Objects below about 0.08 solar masses never ignite hydrogen and become brown dwarfs.

Rates and environments

The Milky Way forms roughly one to a few solar masses of stars per year, while "starburst" galaxies convert gas far faster. Feedback from massive young stars — radiation, winds and supernovae — both disrupts clouds and triggers new collapse, regulating the process. Famous nearby nurseries include the Orion Nebula and the Eagle Nebula's "Pillars of Creation", imaged by the Hubble and James Webb space telescopes.
