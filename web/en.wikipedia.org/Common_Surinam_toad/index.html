Common Surinam toad

Scientific name: Pipa pipa (Linnaeus, 1758)
Family: Pipidae
Range: Amazon Basin, South America

The common Surinam toad (Pipa pipa) is a species of frog in the family Pipidae, found in slow-moving waters of the Amazon Basin and elsewhere in northern South America. It is a highly aquatic, strikingly flattened frog whose mottled brown body resembles a dead leaf or stone on the riverbed — camouflage for an ambush predator that feeds on fish, crustaceans, and invertebrates drawn in by suction feeding.

Description

Adults reach 10–17 cm in length. The body is almost square and paper-thin in profile, with a triangular head, tiny eyes, no tongue, and no teeth. The hind feet are broadly webbed, while the forefeet bear star-shaped sensory organs on the fingertips that help detect prey in murky water.

Reproduction

The species is famous for its extraordinary breeding method. During amplexus, the pair somersaults through the water; the male fertilises the eggs and presses them onto the female's back, where they sink into the skin. The young develop entirely within these pockets — skipping the free-swimming tadpole stage — until fully formed froglets erupt from the mother's back weeks later.

In history

The toad's bizarre reproduction was illustrated by Maria Sibylla Merian in 1705 and long doubted by European naturalists before being confirmed.
