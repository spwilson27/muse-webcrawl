The tropics are the regions of Earth surrounding the Equator. They are defined in latitude by the Tropic of Cancer in the Northern Hemisphere at 23.43612° N and the Tropic of Capricorn in the Southern Hemisphere at 23.43612° S. The tropics are also referred to as the tropical zone and the torrid zone.

In terms of climate, the tropics receive sunlight that is more direct than the rest of Earth and are generally hotter and wetter, having little seasonal variation in temperature. The word "tropical" sometimes refers specifically to tropical climate, the typical climate of these regions, as opposed to the geographic zone.

Seasons and climate

Many tropical locations experience wet and dry seasons rather than the four seasons of temperate latitudes, driven by shifts of the Intertropical Convergence Zone and monsoon circulations. Under the Köppen climate classification, the tropics largely comprise tropical rainforest (Af), tropical monsoon (Am), and tropical savanna (Aw/As) climates. Mean monthly temperatures at low elevation stay above 18 °C (64 °F) year-round.

Ecosystems

Tropical ecosystems include rainforests, mangroves, coral reefs, and savannas, and the tropics harbor the majority of Earth's biodiversity, including roughly half of all species. Tropical rainforests such as the Amazon and Congo basins are major carbon stores and are threatened by deforestation.

Population and health

Roughly 40 percent of the world's population lives in the tropics, a share projected to rise. The region bears a disproportionate burden of infectious tropical diseases such as malaria and dengue, a circumstance linked to climate, vectors, and socioeconomic conditions; these are studied under the field of tropical medicine.
