Turbofan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The turbofan or fanjet is a type of airbreathing jet engine that is widely used in aircraft propulsion. The word "turbofan" is a combination of "turbine" and "fan": the turbine is the gas generator core, and the fan is a ducted fan at the front that uses the turbine's power to accelerate air.

Principle

Part of the air taken in passes through the core, where it is compressed, mixed with fuel and ignited; the remainder — the bypass air — flows around the core through a duct. The ratio of bypass air to core air is the bypass ratio. In modern high-bypass engines (ratios of 10:1 or more) most thrust comes from the fan, giving much better fuel efficiency and lower noise at subsonic speeds than a pure turbojet. Low-bypass turbofans, often with afterburners, power most modern fighter aircraft.

History

The first production turbofan, the Rolls-Royce Conway, entered service in 1960. The General Electric TF39 and its civil derivative the CF6 introduced the high-bypass era on aircraft such as the C-5 Galaxy and early wide-body airliners. Subsequent generations — including the Rolls-Royce Trent, GE GE90/GEnx and Pratt & Whitney PW1000G geared turbofan — have steadily raised bypass ratios and cut fuel burn and emissions.

Advantages

Accelerating a large mass of air by a small amount is more propulsive-efficient and quieter than accelerating a small mass by a large amount, which is why turbofans displaced turbojets on virtually all subsonic airliners.
