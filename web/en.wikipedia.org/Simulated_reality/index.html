A simulated reality would be indistinguishable from "true" reality to its inhabitants.

Simulated reality is the hypothesis that reality as we know it could be an artificial simulation — typically a computer simulation — advanced to such a degree that it is indistinguishable from "true" reality. In its strongest forms, the inhabitants of the simulation, including their conscious minds, are themselves part of the simulation.

The simulation argument

Philosopher Nick Bostrom's 2003 paper "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" argues that at least one of three propositions is almost certainly true: (1) almost all civilizations go extinct before becoming capable of running high-fidelity simulations of their ancestors; (2) almost all such advanced civilizations choose not to run such simulations; or (3) we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The argument builds on the assumption that sufficiently advanced "posthuman" civilizations would have enormous computing power and might run vast numbers of simulations.

History of the idea

The idea has ancient antecedents: Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, Plato's allegory of the cave, and Descartes' evil demon all question the reality of perceived experience. In science fiction it features in works by Stanisław Lem, Daniel F. Galouye's Simulacron-3, and the 1999 film The Matrix, which popularised the concept.

Criticism and debate

Critics dispute the argument's assumptions about the feasibility of simulating consciousness, the motivations of future civilizations, and the physics of computation. Some physicists have proposed searching for anomalies — such as cosmic-ray cutoffs consistent with a discrete lattice — as tentative empirical tests, though such proposals remain speculative.
