Hamilton is a port city in the Canadian province of Ontario. Located at the western end of Lake Ontario, roughly midway between Toronto and Buffalo, New York, it is the ninth most populous city in Canada. Hamilton has long been known for its steel industry — dominant employers historically included Stelco and Dofasco — earning it the nicknames "Steeltown" and "the Hammer".

History

The area was originally inhabited by the Neutral Nation (Attiwandaron) and later by Mississaugas of the Anishinaabe. The city was conceived by George Hamilton, a local landowner and politician, after the War of 1812, and was incorporated as a city in 1846. Its position on Burlington Bay and its rail connections made it an industrial and transportation hub. In 2001, Hamilton amalgamated with the surrounding municipalities of the former Hamilton-Wentworth region, including Dundas, Ancaster, Flamborough, Glanbrook and Stoney Creek.

Geography

Hamilton is bisected by the Niagara Escarpment, a UNESCO World Biosphere Reserve, which runs through the city and is locally called "the Mountain". The escarpment gives the city more than 100 waterfalls and cascades, including Albion Falls and Tew's Falls, leading to its promotion as a "city of waterfalls". The harbour, Burlington Bay, connects to Lake Ontario via the Burlington Canal.

Economy and education

While steel remains important, Hamilton's economy has diversified into health sciences, education and services. It is home to McMaster University, known for its medical school and research, Mohawk College, and major hospitals including Hamilton Health Sciences. The city is also a transport node with the John C. Munro Hamilton International Airport and the Port of Hamilton.

Culture and sports

Cultural institutions include the Art Gallery of Hamilton and the Royal Botanical Gardens (shared with Burlington). The Canadian Football Hall of Fame is located in Hamilton, and the city is home to the Hamilton Tiger-Cats of the Canadian Football League, among the league's oldest franchises. Dundurn Castle, a nineteenth-century neoclassical mansion, is a National Historic Site.
