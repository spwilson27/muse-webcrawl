Gradualism

Gradual change accumulating over long timescales

Gradualism, from the Latin gradus ("step"), is a hypothesis or principle asserting that change proceeds by small, incremental steps over long periods rather than by sudden, large transformations. The term is used in geology, evolutionary biology, and politics.

In geology

Geological gradualism descends from the uniformitarianism of James Hutton and Charles Lyell: the Earth's features are the product of slow, observable processes — erosion, sedimentation, uplift — acting over immense spans of time. This view displaced catastrophism, which attributed geological features to sudden violent events, although modern geology accepts that rare catastrophes such as asteroid impacts also play a role.

In biology

Phyletic gradualism is the model of evolution in which species change slowly and steadily through anagenesis, with speciation occurring by the gradual divergence of populations. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection was fundamentally gradualist — "Natura non facit saltum" ("nature does not make leaps") was a principle he repeatedly invoked. In 1972 Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould proposed punctuated equilibrium as an alternative, arguing that the fossil record shows long periods of stasis interrupted by rapid bursts of change; the two models are now generally seen as describing different patterns within a broader picture.

In politics

Political gradualism favours reform through successive small changes rather than revolution or abrupt restructuring — for example, the Fabian Society's strategy of gradual democratic socialism, or incremental approaches to abolishing slavery and extending the franchise.
