Omniscience

Omniscience is the capacity to know everything. In monotheistic religions, such as Christianity and Islam, omniscience is an attribute of God. In philosophy of religion the concept is usually defined as the state of knowing all true propositions, including those about past, present and future events.

Definitions

A common definition holds that a being x is omniscient if and only if, for every proposition p, if p is true then x knows p. This is called propositional omniscience. Philosophers distinguish it from knowing what experiences are like ("knowledge by acquaintance") and from practical knowledge of how to do things. Some theologians add "middle knowledge" — knowledge of what every free creature would do in any circumstance — as part of the divine knowledge.

Philosophical problems

Omniscience raises several classic puzzles:

  • Foreknowledge and free will — if God infallibly knows what a person will do, it is argued, that person cannot do otherwise. Responses include Boethius's claim that God sees all time at once, and the Ockhamist distinction between "hard" and "soft" facts about the past.
  • Self-knowledge paradoxes — Patrick Grim argued that there can be no set of all truths, so no being can know all truths, on analogy with Cantor's theorem.
  • Inherent omniscience — whether an omniscient being must know the subjective experiences of beings unlike itself.

In other contexts

In narratology, the "omniscient narrator" of a novel is a third-person voice that knows the thoughts and futures of all characters. In information theory and game theory, "perfect information" serves an analogous role. Many religious traditions beyond the Abrahamic ones also ascribe unlimited knowledge to supreme beings or to awakened figures such as the Buddha.
