The Totonac are an Indigenous people of Mexico who reside in the states of Veracruz, Puebla, and Hidalgo. They are one of the possible builders of the pre-Columbian city of El Tajín, and further maintained quarters in Teotihuacan. Until the mid-19th century they were the world's main producers of vanilla.

History

The Totonac region, known as Totonacapan, stretched along the Gulf coast of east-central Mexico. The great Classic-period city of El Tajín, famous for its Pyramid of the Niches and numerous ballcourts, flourished roughly between 600 and 1200 CE and is widely attributed to Totonac or closely related peoples. By the 15th century the Aztec Empire had conquered much of Totonacapan and extracted heavy tribute. When Hernán Cortés landed in 1519, the Totonac ruler at Cempoala — remembered by the Spaniards as the "Fat Chief" — allied with him against the Aztecs, and Totonac warriors accompanied the Spanish in the conquest of Tenochtitlan.

Culture

The Totonac are renowned for the Danza de los Voladores ("Dance of the Flyers"), in which five participants climb a pole 20–40 metres high and descend by ropes while one plays drum and flute at the top; UNESCO inscribed the ceremony on its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2009. The region around Papantla remains a centre of vanilla cultivation, an industry the Totonac dominated for centuries.

Language

The Totonac languages, together with Tepehua, form the Totonacan language family, unrelated to other Mesoamerican families. Several varieties are spoken today, some of them endangered.
