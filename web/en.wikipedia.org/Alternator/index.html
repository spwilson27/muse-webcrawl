Alternator

A cutaway of a typical car alternator.

An alternator is an electrical generator that converts mechanical energy to electrical energy in the form of alternating current. Most alternators use a rotating magnetic field with a stationary armature — the reverse of many DC machines — because the generated current can then be taken from fixed windings without passing the full output through brushes.

Principle of operation

A rotor carrying field windings (or permanent magnets) spins inside stator windings. The changing magnetic flux induces an alternating voltage in the stator. In large power-station machines the rotor is an electromagnet supplied with DC excitation; synchronous speed determines the output frequency. Practical alternators range from bicycle bottle dynamos (actually small permanent-magnet alternators) to turbogenerators of over a gigawatt.

Automotive alternators

In cars, a belt-driven alternator generates three-phase AC that is rectified by diodes to DC to charge the battery and power the electrical system, with a voltage regulator controlling the field current. Alternators replaced DC generators in automobiles in the 1960s because they are lighter, simpler and produce more power at idle.

History

The alternator principle grew out of Michael Faraday's discovery of electromagnetic induction in 1831. Hippolyte Pixii built an early alternator in 1832, and polyphase machines were developed in the 1880s by Nikola Tesla, Galileo Ferraris and others, enabling the AC power systems that won the "war of the currents".
