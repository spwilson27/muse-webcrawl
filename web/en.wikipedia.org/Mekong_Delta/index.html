The Mekong Delta is the region in southwestern Vietnam where the Mekong River approaches and empties into the South China Sea through a network of distributaries. Known in Vietnamese as the "Nine Dragons delta" (Cửu Long) for the river's many mouths, it is Vietnam's rice bowl, producing about half of the country's rice and much of its fruit and aquaculture output.

Geography

The delta covers Vietnam's southernmost provinces, a flat, low-lying plain of channels, canals, mangrove swamp, and paddy fields only a few metres above sea level. The Mekong splits near Phnom Penh into the main Mekong and the Bassac (Hậu) River, which subdivide further into a web of channels crossing the delta. Seasonal flooding — the annual "rising-water season" — deposits fertile silt and supports floating villages, orchards on raised mounds, and famous floating markets such as Cái Răng near Can Tho.

History

The delta was once part of the Cambodian world: the ancient port of Óc Eo belonged to the kingdom of Funan, and Khmer communities (Khmer Krom) remain a significant minority. Vietnamese settlement expanded southward in the 17th–19th centuries under the Nguyễn lords and emperors. During the Vietnam War the delta saw extensive guerrilla warfare; today it is a focus of concern over climate change, sea-level rise, saltwater intrusion, and reduced sediment flow caused by upstream dams in China and Laos, all of which threaten its agriculture and land itself.

Economy

Besides roughly half of Vietnam's rice, the delta produces most of its catfish (pangasius) exports, tropical fruit such as mango, durian, and dragon fruit, and coconut products from Bến Tre. Rivers and canals remain its highways, with boats serving transport, markets, and tourism.
