Bełżec extermination camp

LocationNear Bełżec, German-occupied Poland
Operated17 March 1942 – June 1943 (site dismantled)
CommandantsChristian Wirth; Gottlieb Hering
VictimsEstimated at least 434,508 people, overwhelmingly Jews

Bełżec was a Nazi German extermination camp built during the Second World War for the implementation of Operation Reinhard, the secret plan to murder the Jews of the General Government territory of German-occupied Poland. Located near the village of Bełżec in the Lublin district, it began gassing operations on 17 March 1942 — the first of the three Operation Reinhard camps to open — and by the time killing ended in December 1942, at least about 434,500 people had been murdered there, almost all of them Jews deported from Poland, with also Roma and others among the victims.

The memorial landscape covering the camp site today.

Operation

Bełżec was commanded first by SS-Hauptsturmführer Christian Wirth and from August 1942 by Gottlieb Hering, with a small German staff and Trawniki-trained auxiliaries. Victims arrived by freight train at a siding beside the camp, were told they had come to a transit camp, were forced to undress, and were driven through a fenced path — the "tube" — into gas chambers where they were killed with carbon monoxide from a tank engine. Bodies were buried in mass graves; later, as the camp was wound down, they were exhumed and burned on open-air pyres to destroy the evidence.

Deportations

The deportations to Bełżec came chiefly from the Lublin, Kraków and Lwów (Galicia) districts; the great waves of killing in the summer and autumn of 1942 emptied hundreds of ghettos. Among the transports were also Jews deported from Germany, Austria and Slovakia. Almost no one survived: only a handful of prisoners who escaped or were spared for work details lived to testify, most famously Rudolf Reder, whose account is a principal source for the camp's operation.

Closure and memory

Gassing operations ceased in December 1942. By June 1943 the site had been ploughed over, planted with trees and disguised with a farm to conceal the crime. Post-war investigations and later archaeological work located the mass graves. A major memorial and museum, opened in 2004 on the site, covers the entire camp area with a field of slag and a descending corridor, the "Crevasse", bearing the names of the victims' home towns.
