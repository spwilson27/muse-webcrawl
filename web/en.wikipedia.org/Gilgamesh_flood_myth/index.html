Gilgamesh flood myth

The Gilgamesh flood myth is a flood narrative found in the Epic of Gilgamesh, the ancient Mesopotamian poem about the hero-king Gilgamesh. It is one of the oldest surviving flood stories and is famous for its close parallels to the account of Noah's Ark in the Book of Genesis.

The story

In Tablet XI of the epic, Gilgamesh, seeking the secret of immortality, questions Utnapishtim, a survivor of the great flood. Utnapishtim tells how the gods decided to destroy humanity with a flood, but the god Ea warned him in secret and instructed him to build a great boat and load it with his family, craftsmen, and "the seed of all living things". A storm raged for six days and nights; when the waters subsided, the boat came to rest on Mount Nisir. Utnapishtim released a dove, then a swallow, then a raven — the raven did not return, showing the waters had fallen. After making sacrifices, the god Enlil, though angry that anyone had survived, granted Utnapishtim and his wife immortality.

Discovery and significance

The flood tablet was translated by George Smith of the British Museum, who announced the discovery in 1872 to enormous public interest, since the story predated the biblical account while resembling it in many details — a divinely warned hero, a vessel, animals saved, birds released, and a mountain landing. Earlier Mesopotamian versions of the tale occur in the Atrahasis epic and the Sumerian flood story, showing that the Gilgamesh version itself drew on an older tradition.

Scholars debate the relationship between the Mesopotamian and biblical flood narratives, but the Gilgamesh account remains a central text in the comparative study of ancient Near Eastern literature and flood myths worldwide.
