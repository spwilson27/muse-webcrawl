The walrus (Odobenus rosmarus) is a large pinniped mammal found in the Arctic Ocean and subarctic seas. It is the only living member of the family Odobenidae and is readily recognised by its long ivory tusks — elongated canine teeth present in both sexes — its prominent whiskered muzzle (vibrissae), and its great bulk.

Description

Adult males can exceed 1,200 kg. Walruses use their tusks to haul themselves onto ice, for defence against polar bears and orcas, and in social displays; the longest and thickest tusks usually denote the most dominant animals. The sensitive whisker pads allow the walrus to detect shellfish buried in seabed sediment. A thick layer of blubber — up to 10 cm — insulates the animal in cold water, and its nearly hairless skin becomes flushed with blood at the surface in warm conditions.

Behaviour and ecology

Walruses are highly social, hauling out in herds of hundreds on sea ice or beaches. They feed mainly on benthic molluscs, sucking the flesh from shells, and also take crabs, shrimp, worms, and occasionally fish or young seals. Mating takes place in the water in mid-winter; after a gestation of 15–16 months — including delayed implantation — a single calf is born on the ice in spring. Two subspecies are usually recognised: the Atlantic walrus (O. r. rosmarus) and the Pacific walrus (O. r. divergens).

Humans and conservation

Indigenous peoples of the Arctic have hunted walruses for millennia for meat, blubber, hides, and ivory. Commercial hunting in the 18th and 19th centuries severely depleted Atlantic populations. The species is now listed as Vulnerable, and the loss of summer sea ice to climate change — which forces vast herds onto crowded terrestrial haulouts — is its principal long-term threat.
