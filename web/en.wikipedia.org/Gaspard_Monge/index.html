Gaspard Monge

Gaspard Monge, Comte de Péluse (9 May 1746 – 28 July 1818), was a French mathematician, physicist, and chemist. He is best known as the inventor of descriptive geometry — the mathematical basis of technical drawing — and is considered a father of differential geometry.

Life

Born in Beaune, the son of a merchant, Monge was educated at the Oratorian college there and revealed extraordinary ability in drafting and mathematics. As a young draftsman at the École Royale du Génie at Mézières he devised a rapid method of constructing fortification works using what became descriptive geometry; the method was initially treated as a military secret.

Career and the Revolution

Monge became a professor at Mézières and later in Paris, publishing on partial differential equations, surfaces, and the theory of curves. During the French Revolution he served as Minister of the Navy (1792–93), helped reorganise science education, and co-founded the École Polytechnique, where he taught his descriptive geometry. He accompanied Napoleon on the Egyptian expedition of 1798, helping to found the Institut d'Égypte, and worked on the metric system reforms. Stripped of honours after the Bourbon Restoration, he died in Paris in 1818. His name is among the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.

Contributions

Beyond descriptive geometry, Monge contributed to the theory of partial differential equations (the Monge cone and characteristics), differential geometry of surfaces (Monge patch), transport theory (the Monge–Kantorovich problem), and early work on the composition of water and on metallurgy.
