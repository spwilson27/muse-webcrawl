Foveaux Strait (Māori: Te Ara a Kiwa) separates Stewart Island from the South Island of New Zealand. The strait stretches roughly 130 km from Ruapuke Island in the east to Solander Island in the west. It is about 23 km wide at its narrowest and is notorious for rough, stormy seas driven by the prevailing westerlies of the Roaring Forties.

History

Māori knew the strait as Te Ara a Kiwa, "the pathway of Kiwa". European charts long followed Captain Cook, who in 1770 reported Stewart Island as a peninsula. The strait's true nature became known to sealers around 1804–1806, and it is named for Joseph Foveaux, lieutenant-governor of New South Wales. The port of Bluff grew as its mainland gateway.

Oyster fishery

Foveaux Strait is famous for its oyster beds, harvested since the 1860s; the Bluff oyster (Tiostrea chilensis) is a seasonal delicacy dredged from March to August. The fishery has been repeatedly damaged by the parasite Bonamia ostreae, which devastated beds in the 1980s–90s and again from 2017, forcing closures and the removal of farmed oysters.

Transport and hazards

A ferry service crosses between Bluff and Oban (Halfmoon Bay) in about an hour, and a submarine power and communications cable links the islands. Strong tides, reefs and sudden gales have wrecked many vessels, and the strait remains one of New Zealand's most demanding stretches of water for small craft.
