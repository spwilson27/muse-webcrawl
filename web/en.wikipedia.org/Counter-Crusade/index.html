Counter-Crusade

The Counter-Crusade refers to the response of the Muslim world to the Crusades — the series of campaigns, movements, and ideological developments by which Muslim powers resisted, and eventually reversed, the Crusader presence in the Levant between 1096 and 1291. The concept is often treated as the subject of Carole Hillenbrand's influential study The Crusades: Islamic Perspectives.

Early disunity

The Crusaders arrived at a time of deep political fragmentation in the Near East: the Seljuk sultanate had fractured into competing atabegates, and Sunni–Shia rivalry between Baghdad and Fatimid Cairo hindered a coordinated response. Early resistance was local and sporadic, though figures such as Ilghazi won notable victories, including the Battle of the Field of Blood in 1119.

Unification under Zengi, Nur al-Din, and Saladin

Imad al-Din Zengi, atabeg of Mosul and Aleppo, began the systematic Counter-Crusade, capturing Edessa in 1144. His son Nur al-Din united Syria and incorporated Egypt, while propagating jihad ideology through madrasas, inscriptions, and court literature. Salah al-Din (Saladin), who took control of Egypt and Syria, destroyed the Crusader army at Hattin in 1187 and recaptured Jerusalem, provoking the Third Crusade.

Expulsion of the Crusader states

The Ayyubid and then Mamluk sultans of Egypt completed the process: al-Zahir Baybars and his successors captured the remaining Crusader strongholds, culminating in the fall of Acre in 1291, which ended the Crusader states on the Levantine mainland.
