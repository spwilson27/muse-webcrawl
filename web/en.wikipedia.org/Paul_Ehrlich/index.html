Paul Ehrlich (14 March 1854 – 20 August 1915) was a German physician and scientist who founded major fields of modern medicine, including hematology, immunology, and antimicrobial chemotherapy. He is famous for his "magic bullet" concept—the idea of a compound that selectively targets a disease-causing organism without harming the host—and for developing Salvarsan (arsphenamine), the first effective treatment for syphilis. In 1908 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Élie Metchnikoff for their work on immunity.

Early work and staining

Ehrlich was born in Strehlen, Silesia, and studied medicine at Breslau, Strasbourg, Freiburg, and Leipzig, earning his doctorate in 1878 with a thesis on the theory and practice of histological staining. Fascinated by the differential affinity of dyes for tissues, he classified white blood cells by their staining properties—work that laid the foundations of hematology and of diagnostic blood analysis. Working in Robert Koch's circle in Berlin, he developed a staining method for the tubercle bacillus and studied antitoxins for diphtheria and tetanus, devising methods to standardise serum potency.

Immunology

Ehrlich's side-chain theory (1897) proposed that cells bear chemical side-chains (receptors) that bind toxins and nutrients; when a toxin binds, the cell overproduces and sheds these receptors into the blood, where they act as antitoxins—an early and remarkably prescient model of antibodies and receptors. He also coined terms including "chemotherapy" and pioneered concepts of autoimmunity, which he called horror autotoxicus.

Salvarsan and chemotherapy

As director of the Georg-Speyer-Haus in Frankfurt from 1906, Ehrlich systematically screened arsenical compounds against the spirochete causing syphilis. With his collaborator Sahachiro Hata, he found that compound 606—arsphenamine, marketed as Salvarsan in 1910—effectively treated the disease. It was the first modern chemotherapeutic agent and the world's best-selling prescription drug until antibiotics. A more soluble successor, Neosalvarsan, followed in 1912. Ehrlich's approach of rational, large-scale compound screening foreshadowed the modern pharmaceutical industry.

Ehrlich died of a stroke in Bad Homburg in 1915. The Paul Ehrlich Institute, Germany's federal institute for vaccines and biomedicines, bears his name.
