Cherenkov radiation

Cherenkov radiation is electromagnetic radiation emitted when a charged particle (such as an electron) passes through a dielectric medium at a speed greater than the phase velocity of light in that medium. It is characterised by a distinctive blue glow, famously visible in the water surrounding the cores of nuclear reactors.

Physical origin

Although nothing can travel faster than the speed of light in vacuum, light travels more slowly in a material medium, at speed c/n, where n is the refractive index. A sufficiently energetic charged particle can exceed this reduced speed. As it does so, it polarises the molecules of the medium along its path; when those molecules return to their ground state they emit photons. The emissions interfere constructively along a cone trailing the particle, analogous to the shock wave of a sonic boom or the bow wave of a boat.

History

The effect is named after the Soviet physicist Pavel Cherenkov, who first characterised it rigorously in 1934. Ilya Frank and Igor Tamm developed the theoretical explanation in 1937, and all three shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1958.

Applications

Cherenkov radiation is widely used in particle physics: Cherenkov detectors identify high-energy particles by the angle and intensity of the emitted cone. Large water-filled detectors such as Super-Kamiokande and the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole rely on it to study neutrinos. In medicine, Cherenkov imaging is used in radiation therapy verification, and the glow in reactor pools serves as a sign of spent fuel radioactivity.
