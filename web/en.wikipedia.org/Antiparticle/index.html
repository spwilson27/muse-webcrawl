Antiparticle

Predicted1928, Paul Dirac
First observed1932, Carl D. Anderson (positron)
Key propertySame mass as particle; opposite charge and quantum numbers

In particle physics, every type of particle has an associated antiparticle with the same mass and spin but opposite electric charge and other internal quantum numbers. When a particle and its antiparticle meet, they annihilate each other, converting their mass into energy, usually in the form of photons or other particle–antiparticle pairs. Antiparticles are the building blocks of antimatter.

History

In 1928 Paul Dirac formulated his relativistic equation for the electron, which admitted solutions of negative energy. By 1931 Dirac interpreted these as predicting a positively charged "anti-electron" of the same mass as the electron. In 1932 Carl David Anderson, photographing cosmic rays in a cloud chamber, discovered this particle — the positron — earning the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physics. The antiproton was identified in 1955 by Emilio Segrè and Owen Chamberlain at the Berkeley Bevatron, and the antineutron followed in 1956.

Properties

Antiparticles are distinguished from their particle counterparts by opposite additive quantum numbers: electric charge, baryon number, lepton number, strangeness, and so on. A few particles — notably the photon, the Z boson, and the Higgs boson — are their own antiparticles. Whether the neutrino is its own antiparticle (a Majorana particle) remains an open experimental question. Under the CPT theorem of quantum field theory, particles and antiparticles must have exactly equal masses and lifetimes and opposite charges.

Production and applications

Antiparticles are produced naturally in cosmic-ray collisions, in radioactive beta-plus decay, and around black holes and pulsars, and artificially in particle accelerators via pair production, in which a high-energy photon converts into a particle–antiparticle pair. Positrons are used routinely in medicine in positron emission tomography (PET) scans. Antiprotons are produced, decelerated, and stored at facilities such as CERN's Antiproton Decelerator, where experiments combine them with positrons to form antihydrogen, first produced in 1995 and trapped magnetically in 2010.

Antimatter asymmetry

A central puzzle of cosmology is why the observable universe contains almost no primordial antimatter, although the Big Bang should have produced matter and antimatter in nearly equal amounts. This baryon asymmetry is attributed to small violations of charge–parity (CP) symmetry in the early universe, satisfying the conditions proposed by Andrei Sakharov in 1967.
