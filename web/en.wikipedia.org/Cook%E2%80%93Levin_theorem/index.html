Cook–Levin theorem

In computational complexity theory, the Cook–Levin theorem, also known as Cook's theorem, states that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete. This means that every problem in the complexity class NP can be reduced to SAT in polynomial time, and SAT itself is in NP. The theorem was proved independently by Stephen Cook in the United States (1971) and Leonid Levin in the Soviet Union (1973).

Significance

The Cook–Levin theorem established the first known NP-complete problem, giving a concrete anchor for the theory of NP-completeness. Once SAT was known to be NP-complete, thousands of other problems — from graph colouring to the travelling salesman problem's decision version — were shown NP-complete by reduction from it or from problems already known to be complete.

Proof idea

The proof encodes the computation of a nondeterministic Turing machine running in polynomial time as a Boolean formula: variables describe the machine's tape contents, head position, and state at each time step, and clauses enforce that the described computation is a valid accepting one. The formula is satisfiable exactly when the machine accepts its input. Whether NP-complete problems can be solved in polynomial time is the famous P versus NP problem. See also NP-completeness.
