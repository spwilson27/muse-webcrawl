Information seeking

FieldInformation science, psychology, human–computer interaction
Notable modelsWilson's model, Kuhlthau's Information Search Process, Ellis's behavioural model, Bates's berrypicking

Information seeking is the process by which people search for and make use of information to satisfy a need — to fill a gap in knowledge, solve a problem, or make a decision. It is a central topic in information science and library and user studies, and it informs the design of search engines, libraries, and other information systems.

Models of information-seeking behaviour

Several influential models describe how people seek information. T. D. Wilson's models (1981, revised 1996) place seeking within the context of a person's life and intervening variables such as psychological state, role, and environment. David Ellis identified recurring behavioural features — starting, chaining, browsing, differentiating, monitoring, and extracting. Carol Kuhlthau's Information Search Process emphasised the affective dimension: seekers commonly move from uncertainty and anxiety toward clarity and confidence as a task progresses. Marcia Bates's "berrypicking" model described real searches as evolving, bit-at-a-time journeys rather than single queries.

Types of seeking

Information seeking ranges from undirected browsing and everyday-life information seeking (described by Reijo Savolainen) to the systematic, goal-directed searching of professionals such as physicians, engineers, and scholars. It may consult interpersonal sources — colleagues and experts — as readily as documents and databases, a tendency captured by the principle of least effort.

Digital environments

The web and mobile search have reshaped seeking behaviour, making enormous collections instantly available but also introducing problems of information overload, misinformation, and reliance on ranked results. Research in information seeking guides interface design, search literacy education, and the evaluation of information systems.
