The Waitangi Tribunal (Māori: Te Rōpū Whakamana i te Tiriti o Waitangi) is a New Zealand permanent commission of inquiry established by the Treaty of Waitangi Act 1975. It inquires into claims by Māori that acts, omissions, policies or laws of the Crown have breached the principles of the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), and makes recommendations to the government. Except in a few specified cases, its findings are recommendatory rather than binding.

History

The Tribunal began with jurisdiction only over claims arising after 1975. In 1985 an amendment extended its reach back to 1840, opening the way for historical claims concerning land confiscations and other grievances. Early landmark reports included the Motunui–Waitara report (1983) on sewage discharge affecting Taranaki reefs, the Manukau report (1985), and the Orakei report (1987). The Tribunal's reports underpinned the major Treaty settlements of the 1990s, including the Waikato raupatu settlement (1995) and the Ngāi Tahu settlement (1998).

Process

Claims are lodged by Māori claimants and registered; hearings are held largely on marae and follow both legal procedure and Māori protocol (tikanga). Tribunal panels are drawn from a membership appointed for their expertise in Māori, historical, legal and other relevant matters, and are chaired by a judge or senior lawyer. Since 2008 the Tribunal has moved to complete its historical inquiries by district, alongside kaupapa (thematic) inquiries such as the Health Services and Outcomes Inquiry (Wai 2575).

Significance

The Tribunal has been the principal forum for investigating the history of Crown–Māori relations and the evidentiary basis of Treaty settlements worth billions of dollars. Its work has also shaped public understanding of New Zealand history and jurisprudence on the "principles of the Treaty".
