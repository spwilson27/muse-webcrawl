Woodward–Hoffmann rules

The Woodward–Hoffmann rules (or Woodward–Hoffmann selection rules) are a set of rules in organic chemistry devised by Robert Burns Woodward and Roald Hoffmann to predict the stereochemistry of pericyclic reactions based on orbital symmetry. Reactions that obey the rules are described as "symmetry-allowed," and those that violate them as "symmetry-forbidden."

Principle

The rules rest on the conservation of orbital symmetry: in a concerted pericyclic reaction, the symmetry properties of the molecular orbitals of the reactants must correlate with those of the products. Whether a reaction is allowed thermally or photochemically, and whether rings open or close in a conrotatory or disrotatory sense, depends on the number of π electrons involved.

Applications

The rules explain the stereochemical outcomes of electrocyclic reactions, cycloadditions such as the Diels–Alder reaction, and sigmatropic rearrangements. Hoffmann shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the work; Woodward, who died in 1979, was not eligible.
