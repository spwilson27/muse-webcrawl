Ugarit (Ugaritic: 𐎜𐎂𐎗𐎚, ʾUgarītu) was an ancient port city in northern Syria, in the outskirts of modern Latakia, discovered by accident in 1928 together with the Ugaritic texts. Its ruins are often called Ras Shamra after the name of the headland where they lie. Ugarit had close connections to the Hittite Empire, sent tribute to Egypt at times, and maintained trade and diplomatic connections with Cyprus (then called Alashiya), documented in the archives recovered from the site.

The kingdom controlled about 2,000 square kilometres (770 sq mi) at its height. During its high period, between about 1450 BC and 1200 BC, Ugarit was a wealthy cosmopolitan trading centre linking Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Egypt and the Aegean world.

History

Though the site was occupied from the Neolithic period, Ugarit was a major Bronze Age city. The earliest written evidence of the city comes from the Ebla archives of the third millennium BC. By the second millennium BC, Ugarit was ruled by kings known from the royal archives, including Ammittamru I, Niqmaddu II, Arhalba, Niqmepa, Ammittamru II, Ibiranu, Niqmaddu III and Ammurapi, the last king, who was contemporary with the fall of the city.

At the beginning of the 12th century BC, Ugarit was destroyed, probably by the Sea Peoples or by internal upheaval associated with the Late Bronze Age collapse. A famous letter written by King Ammurapi to the king of Alashiya, found in a kiln where it was being baked for dispatch, describes enemy ships burning Ugaritic towns while its own troops were away in Hittite service. The city was never reoccupied on a significant scale.

Excavations

The ruins were discovered in 1928 by a local farmer who accidentally opened an old tomb while ploughing a field. Excavations beginning in 1929, directed by Claude F. A. Schaeffer for the Musée du Louvre, uncovered a royal palace of 90 rooms arranged around eight courtyards, private residences, two libraries of cuneiform tablets (a royal library and a priestly one), temples of Baal and Dagon, and an acropolis. Finds included the Baal Cycle tablets, stelae, bronze figurines, and ivory carvings of exceptional quality.

Language and texts

The scribes of Ugarit created the Ugaritic alphabet around 1400 BC, a cuneiform abjad of 30 letters written on clay tablets. Texts were written in the local Ugaritic language as well as in Akkadian, Hurrian, Sumerian, Hittite and Egyptian, and in several scripts, reflecting the city's international character. The Ugaritic religious texts — the Baal Cycle, the Legend of Keret and the Tale of Aqhat — are of central importance for understanding Canaanite religion and the background of the Hebrew Bible.

Religion

The head of the Ugaritic pantheon was El, with his consort Asherah (Athirat). The storm god Baal (Hadad) was the most active deity in the myths; other gods included Anat, Astarte, Yam (Sea), Mot (Death), Dagon, Resheph and the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Khasis.
