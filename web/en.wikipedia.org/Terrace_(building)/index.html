Terrace (building)

A terrace in building terms can denote several related things: an element of a building consisting of a raised, flat, outdoor platform — such as a roof terrace or a paved area adjoining a house — or, in British usage, a row of connected houses sharing side walls, called a terrace of houses or terraced housing. The word comes from the Latin terra, earth, by way of French terrasse, an earthen mound or platform.

The architectural platform

As a platform, a terrace provides outdoor living space level with an interior floor, often paved and balustraded, and is common in Mediterranean and modernist architecture. Le Corbusier's roof terraces (the toit-jardin, one of his Five Points) reclaimed the building's footprint as garden space. In landscape design, terraces step down slopes in garden layouts, as in the hanging gardens and terraced water gardens of Mughal and Italian Renaissance design.

Terraced housing

In the United Kingdom and countries influenced by British building, a terrace is a row of attached houses of uniform design, each sharing party walls with its neighbours; the individual units are "terraced houses". Grand Georgian terraces, such as those of Bath and London's Regent's Park, were prestigious addresses, while vast numbers of modest Victorian and Edwardian terraces housed industrial workers. The form is space-efficient and remains a large share of the British housing stock; equivalent terms include row house in North America.
