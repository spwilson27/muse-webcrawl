Extensive farming

Extensive farming or extensive agriculture (as opposed to intensive farming) is an agricultural production system that uses small inputs of labour and capital relative to the land area being farmed. Extensive farming most commonly refers to sheep and cattle farming in areas with low agricultural productivity, but can also refer to large-scale growing of wheat, barley, and other grains in areas like the Murray–Darling Basin in Australia and the Canadian Prairies.

Characteristics

Because extensive farming uses little labour and capital per hectare, yields per unit of land are generally low, although output per worker may be high. It is typically practised where land is abundant and cheap relative to labour, and where low rainfall or poor soils limit what each hectare can produce. Nomadic herding, ranching, and shifting cultivation are traditional forms.

Examples

Extensive livestock stations in the Australian Outback and New Zealand high country, sheep farming in the Scottish Highlands, cereal farming on the Eurasian steppe, and cattle ranching in the North American Great Plains are classic examples. In such systems a single farm or station may cover tens of thousands of hectares.

Comparison with intensive farming

Intensive farming concentrates inputs — labour, fertilizer, irrigation, and machinery — on a small area to maximize yield per hectare. Extensive farming generally has lower environmental impact per hectare but requires more land overall for the same output, which can drive habitat conversion where it expands into wilderness.
