Sugar shack

A sugar shack (French: cabane à sucre), also known as a sap house, sugar house, sugar shanty, sugar camp, or maple house, is a semi-commercial establishment, primarily found in Eastern Canada and northern New England, where maple sap is boiled down into maple syrup. The process is known as "sugaring" and takes place in late winter and early spring, when sap flows in maple trees.

Operation

A sugar shack generally consists of a rudimentary building housing an evaporator, in which sap is boiled down over a wood fire. Traditionally, sap was collected in buckets hung on tapped trees and carried to the shack; modern operations use networks of plastic tubing and vacuum pumps. It takes roughly 40 litres of sap to produce 1 litre of maple syrup.

Cultural role

In Quebec, where the vast majority of the world's maple syrup is produced, visiting a cabane à sucre is a beloved spring tradition. Many sugar shacks serve hearty traditional meals — pea soup, baked beans, ham, omelettes, and maple taffy poured on snow — accompanied by folk music and dancing. The season is called le temps des sucres ("sugaring-off time").

History

Maple sugaring was practised by the Indigenous peoples of North America long before European settlement; French settlers adopted and adapted the practice in the 17th and 18th centuries. The sugar shack became an emblem of rural Quebecois and Franco-American culture.
