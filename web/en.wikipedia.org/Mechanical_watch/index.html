Mechanical watch

A mechanical watch is a watch that uses a clockwork mechanism to measure the passage of time, as opposed to quartz watches which function using the vibration of quartz or the movement of electrons. It is driven by a mainspring which must be wound either periodically by hand or via a self-winding mechanism. Its force is transmitted through a series of gears to power the balance wheel, a weighted wheel which oscillates back and forth at a constant rate. A device called an escapement releases the watch's wheels to move forward a small amount with each swing of the balance wheel, moving the watch's hands forward at a constant rate.

Components

  • Mainspring — a coiled spring that stores the energy to drive the watch.
  • Gear train — a series of wheels that transmit power and reduce its speed to turn the hands.
  • Escapement — usually a Swiss lever escapement, which meters out the release of energy in regular steps.
  • Balance wheel and hairspring — the timekeeping element, oscillating typically at 18,000–36,000 beats per hour.
  • Dial and hands — display the time; many watches add complications such as date windows, chronographs, or moon phases.

History

Mechanical watches developed from portable spring-driven clocks of the 15th century. Peter Henlein of Nuremberg is often credited with the earliest watches around 1505. The invention of the balance spring (hairspring) in the 1670s, credited to both Robert Hooke and Christiaan Huygens, greatly improved accuracy. Mechanical watches remained the standard until the quartz crisis of the 1970s, when cheap, accurate quartz watches from Japan devastated the Swiss industry. Since the 1980s, mechanical watches have revived as luxury goods, valued for their craftsmanship.

Types

Manual watches must be wound by the wearer turning the crown. Automatic (self-winding) watches use a pivoting rotor that winds the mainspring using the motion of the wearer's arm. Most modern mechanical watches are automatic, often with a hand-winding capability as well.
