Polygonal rifling

Polygonal rifling is a type of gun barrel rifling in which the traditional sharp-edged lands and grooves are replaced by a smoother, rounded polygonal cross-section, usually hexagonal or octagonal. Instead of cutting or engraving grooves into the bore, the barrel interior is formed with hills and valleys in a polygonal pattern, typically produced by cold hammer forging.

Description

In conventional rifling, rectangular lands and grooves grip the bullet and impart spin. In a polygonal barrel, the bore has a rounded profile with no sharp corners; the bullet is engraved and sealed by the gradual transitions. The concept dates to the mid-19th century: Joseph Whitworth's hexagonal-bored rifles of the 1850s were an early form, and the idea was later refined by German manufacturers.

Advantages and disadvantages

Proponents claim polygonal rifling deforms the bullet less, produces a better gas seal, yields slightly higher muzzle velocity, reduces fouling, and prolongs barrel life. Critics note that forensic identification of bullets fired from polygonal barrels can be more difficult because the rifling leaves fewer distinctive markings, and that some designs perform poorly with unjacketed lead bullets because of accelerated lead fouling in the absence of sharp grooves. Glock pistols are the best-known modern users of polygonal rifling; Heckler & Koch, Magnum Research, and others have also used it.
