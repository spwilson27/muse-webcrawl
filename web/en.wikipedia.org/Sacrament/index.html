Sacrament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sacrament is a Christian rite that is recognised as being particularly important and significant. The word derives from the Latin sacramentum, originally a soldier's oath of allegiance, used in the Latin Bible to translate the Greek mystērion ("mystery"). Most Christian traditions define a sacrament as an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace, instituted by Christ.

The seven sacraments

The Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, and Oriental Orthodoxy recognise seven sacraments:

  • Baptism — initiation into the Christian life through water
  • Confirmation (Chrismation) — strengthening with the Holy Spirit
  • Eucharist — communion in the body and blood of Christ
  • Penance (Reconciliation) — confession and absolution of sins
  • Anointing of the Sick — prayer and anointing for the ill
  • Holy Orders — ordination of bishops, priests, and deacons
  • Matrimony — marriage

Protestant views

Most Protestant traditions recognise only two sacraments as explicitly commanded by Christ in the Gospels: baptism and the Lord's Supper (Eucharist). Anglican formularies describe these two as "Sacraments of the Gospel" and five other rites as "commonly called Sacraments" but not of the same nature. Some traditions, such as Quakers and the Salvation Army, practise no outward sacraments at all.

Theology

Catholic theology teaches that the sacraments act ex opere operato — by the work performed — conveying grace through valid matter, form, and ministerial intention, a doctrine defined at the Council of Trent. Debates over the Eucharist — transubstantiation, consubstantiation, and memorialism — were central to the Reformation.
