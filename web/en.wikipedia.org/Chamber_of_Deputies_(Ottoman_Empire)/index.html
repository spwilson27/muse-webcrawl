Chamber of Deputies (Ottoman Empire)

The Chamber of Deputies (Ottoman Turkish: مجلس مبعوثان, Meclis-i Meb'ūsān; modern Turkish: Meclis-i Mebusan) was the lower house of the General Assembly, the parliament of the Ottoman Empire. Created by the Ottoman constitution of 1876 (Kanûn-ı Esâsî), it consisted of deputies elected by the empire's (male) subjects, while the upper house, the Senate (Heyet-i Ayan), was appointed by the Sultan. The Chamber sat during the two constitutional eras of the empire: the First Constitutional Era (1876–1878) and the Second Constitutional Era (1908–1920).

First Constitutional Era

Elections were held in early 1877 under a provisional electoral law: deputies were chosen indirectly by provincial councils, roughly one per 50,000 male inhabitants. The first parliament — remarkably diverse, with Turks, Arabs, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, Bulgarians, Serbs, Albanians and others — opened on 19 March 1877 in the Dolmabahçe Palace. It asserted itself by scrutinising budgets and criticising ministers during the disastrous Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78. Sultan Abdul Hamid II, using his constitutional power, prorogued the Chamber in February 1878; it did not meet again for thirty years, and Midhat Pasha, the constitution's architect, was exiled and later executed.

Second Constitutional Era

The Young Turk Revolution of July 1908 forced the restoration of the constitution. Elections in late 1908, conducted under an electoral law with (indirect, two-stage) male suffrage, returned a Chamber dominated by the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), alongside the liberal opposition. The parliament legislated extensively but politics was punctuated by crisis: the 31 March Incident and deposition of Abdul Hamid II (1909), the disputed "election of the stick" (1912), the CUP's 1913 Raid on the Sublime Porte, and one-party rule during the First World War. Deputies nonetheless represented the empire's provinces and communities, debated budgets and war measures, and included prominent figures such as the Arab and Armenian deputies who later figured in post-Ottoman states.

End

After the Armistice of Mudros (1918), Sultan Mehmed VI dissolved the Chamber in December 1918; a final parliament elected in 1919 adopted the National Pact (Misak-ı Millî) in January 1920. Allied occupation forces raided and formally closed the parliament in March 1920 and deported deputies to Malta; many fled to Ankara, where they formed the core of the Grand National Assembly of Turkey, which opened on 23 April 1920.

Elections and composition

Elections were indirect and restricted to tax-paying adult males; the electorate and deputies were organised by province and, in practice, by the empire's millet communities, though formally seats were territorial. Sessional composition changed with each election (1877, 1877–78, 1908, 1912, 1914, 1919). The Chamber's procedures — question hours, budget debates, interpellations — modelled themselves partly on European parliaments and its published minutes (the Zabıt Ceridesi) are a major historical source.
