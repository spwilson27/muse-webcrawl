UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage Lists

The UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage Lists are lists maintained by UNESCO under the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage, adopted in 2003 and in force from 2006. Their purpose is to ensure better protection of important intangible cultural heritage worldwide and to raise awareness of its significance.

"Intangible cultural heritage" includes practices, representations, expressions, knowledge and skills — as well as the instruments, objects, artefacts and cultural spaces associated with them — that communities, groups and individuals recognise as part of their cultural heritage. It covers domains such as oral traditions and expressions, performing arts, social practices, rituals and festive events, knowledge and practices concerning nature and the universe, and traditional craftsmanship.

The lists

The Convention maintains two principal lists:

  • The Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, which showcases the diversity of intangible heritage and raises awareness of its importance.
  • The List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in Need of Urgent Safeguarding, for elements whose viability is at risk despite the efforts of the community concerned.

A third mechanism, the Register of Good Safeguarding Practices, selects programmes and projects that best reflect the principles of the Convention.

History

Before the lists began in 2008, UNESCO ran a programme of Proclamations of Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, issued in 2001, 2003 and 2005, covering 90 elements. Those elements were incorporated into the Representative List when it was established in 2008.

By the mid-2020s, the lists contained more than 700 elements from over 140 countries. Well-known inscriptions include the Mediterranean diet, flamenco, yoga, tango, traditional Chinese tea processing, the French gastronomic meal, mariachi music, falconry (a multinational inscription shared by many countries), reggae music of Jamaica, and the culture of beer in Belgium.

Procedure

States parties to the Convention nominate elements, usually with the participation and consent of the communities concerned. An evaluation body examines the files and an intergovernmental committee, meeting annually, decides on inscriptions. Inscription does not imply ownership or exclusivity: several countries may share similar elements, and multinational nominations are encouraged.

Criticism

Scholars and commentators have debated the effects of listing, including the risk of "freezing" living traditions, encouraging tourism-driven commercialisation, and national competition for inscriptions. UNESCO emphasises that the lists aim at safeguarding viability rather than conferring prestige alone.
