Otto cycle

TypeThermodynamic cycle
Named forNikolaus Otto
ApplicationSpark-ignition petrol engines

The Otto cycle is the idealised thermodynamic cycle that describes the functioning of a typical spark-ignition piston engine, such as the petrol engines used in most cars. It is named after Nikolaus Otto, who built the first practical four-stroke engine in 1876.

Processes

The ideal cycle consists of four processes: isentropic (adiabatic reversible) compression; constant-volume heat addition (representing ignition); isentropic expansion (the power stroke); and constant-volume heat rejection (exhaust). Real four-stroke engines add intake and exhaust strokes handled by the piston moving the gas.

Efficiency

The thermal efficiency of the ideal Otto cycle depends only on the compression ratio r and the ratio of specific heats γ: η = 1 − r1−γ. Higher compression ratios give higher efficiency, though in practice knock limits the ratio for petrol engines.

Comparison

The analogous cycle for compression-ignition engines is the Diesel cycle, which differs in adding heat at constant pressure rather than constant volume.
