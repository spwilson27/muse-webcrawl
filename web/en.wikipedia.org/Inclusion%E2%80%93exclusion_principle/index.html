Inclusion–exclusion principle

In combinatorics, a branch of mathematics, the inclusion–exclusion principle is a counting technique which generalizes the familiar method of obtaining the number of elements in the union of two finite sets. It corrects for over-counting by successively adding the sizes of the individual sets, subtracting the sizes of pairwise intersections, adding back the sizes of triple intersections, and so on with alternating signs.

Statement

For two sets: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|.

For three sets: |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A ∩ B| − |A ∩ C| − |B ∩ C| + |A ∩ B ∩ C|.

In general, for finite sets A1, …, An, the cardinality of the union equals the sum of the cardinalities of all intersections of odd "multiplicity" (singles, triples, …) minus the sum of the cardinalities of all intersections of even multiplicity (pairs, quadruples, …).

Applications

  • Counting derangements — permutations with no fixed points — giving the formula involving the subfactorial and the approximation n!/e.
  • Euler's totient function, via inclusion–exclusion over the prime divisors of n.
  • The sieve of Eratosthenes and other sieve methods in number theory, generalized in the principle's application to probability.
  • Probability: the probability of a union of events follows the same formula with probabilities in place of cardinalities.

History

The principle is credited to Abraham de Moivre (1718), though it appears in earlier work by Nicholas Bernoulli and was stated in general form by Daniel da Silva and James Joseph Sylvester in the nineteenth century.
