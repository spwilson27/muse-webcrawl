Digital Millennium Copyright Act

EnactedOctober 28, 1998
Signed byPresident Bill Clinton
StatutePub. L. 105-304
Key provisionsAnti-circumvention (17 U.S.C. §1201); OSP safe harbors (§512)

The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) is a 1998 United States copyright law implementing two 1996 World Intellectual Property Organization treaties. It criminalizes production and dissemination of technology intended to circumvent digital rights management (DRM), and it heightens penalties for copyright infringement on the internet.

Main provisions

Title I adds anti-circumvention rules: bypassing access controls on copyrighted works is itself unlawful, subject to exemptions granted in triennial rulemakings by the Librarian of Congress. Title II, the Online Copyright Infringement Liability Limitation Act, creates "safe harbors" shielding online service providers from liability for users' infringing material if they respond to takedown notices — the basis of the DMCA notice-and-takedown system used by platforms such as YouTube.

Criticism

Critics argue the anti-circumvention provisions chill security research, repair, and fair use, and that takedown notices are abused to suppress lawful speech. Supporters credit the safe harbors with enabling the growth of user-generated-content platforms.
