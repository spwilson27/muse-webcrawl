An elliptical galaxy is a type of galaxy with an approximately ellipsoidal shape and a smooth, nearly featureless image. They are one of the three main classes of galaxy described by Edwin Hubble in his Hubble sequence, along with spiral and lenticular galaxies. Elliptical galaxies are designated by the letter E followed by an integer representing their degree of ellipticity on the sky, from E0 (nearly spherical) to E7 (highly elongated).

Characteristics

Unlike spiral galaxies, elliptical galaxies contain little cold gas and dust and show little or no ongoing star formation; their light is dominated by old, redder stars. Their stars move in largely random orbits rather than rotating in an ordered disc, so the galaxy is supported against gravity mainly by velocity dispersion. Surface brightness typically follows a de Vaucouleurs profile. Most elliptical galaxies are found in galaxy clusters.

Formation

The prevailing view is that large elliptical galaxies form through mergers of smaller galaxies, which scramble stellar orbits and consume or expel gas. The most massive ellipticals — giant and cD galaxies such as M87 in the Virgo Cluster — sit at the centres of clusters and appear to have grown by accreting many companions. They commonly host supermassive black holes, and some produce powerful radio jets.

Dwarf ellipticals

At the small end of the scale, dwarf elliptical and dwarf spheroidal galaxies are numerous but faint. Many orbit larger galaxies as satellites, and their properties suggest they may be shaped by tidal forces and the removal of gas.
