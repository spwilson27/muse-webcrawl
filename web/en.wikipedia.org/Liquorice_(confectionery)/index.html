Liquorice (confectionery)

Liquorice (in North American English, licorice) is a confectionery flavoured with the extract of the roots of the liquorice plant (Glycyrrhiza glabra), usually combined with sugar or molasses, a binder such as flour, starch or gum arabic, and often anise oil. The traditional product is black; "red liquorice" is a fruit-flavoured candy, such as strawberry or cherry ropes, that contains no liquorice extract at all.

Production and varieties

Liquorice extract is made by boiling the roots of the plant, which grows around the Mediterranean and in western Asia; the key flavouring compound, glycyrrhizin, is some 30–50 times sweeter than sugar. In Britain, Pontefract in Yorkshire became a centre of liquorice growing and manufacture, producing the stamped Pontefract cakes; liquorice allsorts, a mixture of liquorice and coloured fondant shapes, were popularised by Bassett's of Sheffield from 1899. The Netherlands is famous for drop, including salt liquorice (zoute drop) flavoured with ammonium chloride (salmiak), which is also beloved in Finland (salmiakki) and Scandinavia generally. Other varieties include soft Australian liquorice, Italian pure-extract pastilles, and long extruded ropes, twists and wheels sold worldwide.

Health effects

Glycyrrhizin can, in large quantities, cause hypertension, low potassium and related symptoms by interfering with cortisol metabolism, and health authorities advise moderation, particularly for people with high blood pressure; warnings appear on some salty liquorice products. Deglycyrrhizinated liquorice (DGL) is sold as a supplement without this effect. Liquorice root also has a long history in traditional medicine as a demulcent and expectorant.

History

Liquorice was known in ancient Egypt, Greece and China as a medicine and thirst-quencher — Alexander's and Napoleon's armies alike are said to have carried it — and it was cultivated by Dominican friars at Pontefract in the sixteenth century. Its conversion into a mass-market sweet came with the addition of sugar in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
