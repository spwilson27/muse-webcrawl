The Oder–Neisse line (German: Oder-Neiße-Grenze; Polish: Granica na Odrze i Nysie Łużyckiej) is the border between Germany and Poland, running along the Oder and Lusatian Neisse rivers. It was established at the Potsdam Conference in August 1945 as the provisional western border of Poland, implementing the Allied decision to compensate Poland in the west for territory annexed by the Soviet Union in the east. The territories east of the line — including Silesia, Pomerania and the southern part of East Prussia — were placed under Polish administration, and most of their German population fled or was expelled, in the largest forced migration in European history.

Origins

The idea of moving Poland's borders westward circulated among Allied planners during the Second World War. At the Tehran and Yalta conferences, the "Big Three" agreed in principle that Poland would lose territory east of the Curzon Line to the Soviet Union and be compensated with German territory in the north and west. The Potsdam Agreement of 2 August 1945 fixed the line along the Oder and the western (Lusatian) Neisse pending a final peace settlement with Germany.

Recognition

The German Democratic Republic (East Germany) recognised the line as permanent in the Treaty of Zgorzelec (1950). The Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany), where expellee organisations were politically influential, long refused formal recognition; under Chancellor Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik, the Treaty of Warsaw (1970) accepted the line as the inviolable western frontier of Poland, a position endorsed by the West German parliament in 1972. Definitive confirmation came during German reunification: the Two Plus Four Treaty and the German–Polish Border Treaty of 14 November 1990 recognised the Oder–Neisse line as the final border, and the Basic Law was amended accordingly.

Border today

The line runs for roughly 467 kilometres from the Baltic Sea near Świnoujście/Usedom southward along the Oder, then up the Lusatian Neisse to the Czech border near Zittau. Divided cities such as Görlitz/Zgorzelec and Frankfurt (Oder)/Słubice face each other across the rivers. Since Poland joined the European Union in 2004 and the Schengen Area in 2007, border controls have disappeared, and the crossing points inherited from the Cold War era stand open.
