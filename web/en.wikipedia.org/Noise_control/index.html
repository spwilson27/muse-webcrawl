Noise control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Noise control or noise mitigation is a set of strategies to reduce noise pollution or its impact, whether outdoor or indoor. Because excessive noise harms hearing, health, wildlife and quality of life, governments regulate environmental and occupational noise, and engineers design quieter machines, buildings, vehicles and infrastructure. The standard engineering approach addresses the source, the path and the receiver of sound, in roughly that order of preference.

Methods

At the source, noise is reduced by quieter machinery design, low-noise road surfaces and tyres, mufflers and silencers, vibration isolation and damping, and operational changes such as flight-path management around airports. Along the path, measures include distance, earth berms and noise barriers along highways and railways, building insulation and soundproofing, acoustic absorption in rooms, and duct silencers in ventilation systems. At the receiver, hearing protection — earplugs and earmuffs — is used in workplaces as a last resort within hearing conservation programmes. Active noise control supplements passive measures in some applications by generating an "anti-noise" signal that destructively interferes with unwanted sound, as in noise-cancelling headphones and some industrial duct systems.

Regulation

Occupational exposure limits, such as those enforced by OSHA in the United States and under EU directives, typically set an action level around 85 dBA for an eight-hour day. Environmental noise is addressed through zoning, building codes, vehicle and aircraft standards, and noise mapping; the World Health Organization has published guidelines linking chronic exposure to cardiovascular disease, sleep disturbance and cognitive effects in children.
