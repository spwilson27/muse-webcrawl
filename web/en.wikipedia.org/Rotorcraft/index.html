Rotorcraft

A helicopter, the most common type of rotorcraft

A rotorcraft or rotary-wing aircraft is a heavier-than-air aircraft that generates lift using rotating wings, called rotor blades, which revolve around a mast. The rotating assembly is known as a rotor system or simply a rotor. Unlike fixed-wing aircraft, rotorcraft can take off and land vertically, hover, and fly slowly or move in any direction, at the cost of lower speed and efficiency in forward flight.

Types

• The helicopter, in which the rotor is powered by the engine throughout flight, providing both lift and propulsion; torque is usually counteracted by a tail rotor or alternative systems such as NOTAR or coaxial rotors.
• The autogyro (gyrocopter or gyroplane), whose rotor is unpowered and spins by autorotation in flight, with thrust supplied by a separate propeller.
• The gyrodyne, combining a powered rotor for takeoff, hovering and landing with auxiliary propulsion for forward flight.
• Tiltrotor aircraft, such as the V-22 Osprey, which convert between helicopter and turboprop aeroplane configurations by tilting their proprotors.
• Compound helicopters and cyclogyros are further specialised forms.

Flight principles

Rotor blades are airfoils; as they sweep through the air they produce lift much as a fixed wing does. The pilot controls flight through the collective pitch (changing all blades' pitch together, to climb or descend), the cyclic pitch (tilting the rotor disc to move horizontally), and, in helicopters, anti-torque pedals. In forward flight, blades experience dissymmetry of lift between the advancing and retreating sides, managed by flapping and lead-lag hinges or flexible blade roots. Retreating blade stall and compressibility on the advancing blade limit maximum speed.

History

Practical rotorcraft arrived in the 1930s with Juan de la Cierva's autogyros and the first successful helicopters of Breguet, Focke and Igor Sikorsky, whose VS-300 (1939) established the dominant single-main-rotor-plus-tail-rotor configuration. Rotorcraft now serve in transport, search and rescue, medical evacuation, law enforcement, offshore support, agriculture and military roles.
