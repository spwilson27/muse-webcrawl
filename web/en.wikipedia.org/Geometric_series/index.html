Geometric series


The sum of the series 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... can be visualised as filling a unit square, giving the sum 1.

In mathematics, a geometric series is a series with a constant ratio between successive terms. For example, the series

1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/16 + ⋯

is geometric, because each successive term can be obtained by multiplying the previous term by 1/2. In general, a geometric series is written as a + ar + ar2 + ar3 + ..., where a is the coefficient of each term and r is the common ratio between adjacent terms.

Geometric series are among the simplest examples of infinite series with finite sums, although not all of them have this property. Historically, geometric series played an important role in the early development of calculus, and they continue to be central in the study of convergence of series. Geometric series are used throughout mathematics, and they have important applications in physics, engineering, biology, economics, computer science, queueing theory, and finance.

Common ratio

The terms of a geometric series form a geometric progression (GP), meaning that the ratio of successive terms in the series is constant. This relationship allows for the representation of a geometric series using only two terms, r and a. The term r is the common ratio, and a is the GP's scale factor.

Depending on the value of the common ratio, the terms of the series behave differently: if |r| > 1 the terms grow without bound; if r = 1 each term equals a; if −1 < r < 1 the terms decrease in absolute value toward zero; if r = −1 the terms oscillate; and if r < −1 the terms oscillate with growing magnitude.

Sum

The sum of the first n terms of a geometric series, with first term a and common ratio r ≠ 1, is

Sn = a (1 − rn) / (1 − r).

As n approaches infinity, if |r| < 1, the term rn approaches zero and the sum of the infinite series converges to

S = a / (1 − r).

For example, with a = 1/2 and r = 1/2, the sum is (1/2)/(1 − 1/2) = 1. If |r| ≥ 1 the series diverges: its partial sums do not approach a finite limit (except in the trivial case a = 0).

History

Elements of the theory date to antiquity: Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC) contains a summation of a geometric progression in Book IX, and Archimedes used the sum of an infinite geometric series to compute the area under a parabola (the quadrature of the parabola), an early example of summation of an infinite series. Nicole Oresme in the 14th century gave one of the first proofs of divergence of the harmonic series and studied geometric series. The formula and its convergence properties were developed further by mathematicians of the 17th century as infinite series became central to analysis.

Applications

Geometric series model repeated proportional change. In finance they underlie the present-value formulas for annuities and perpetuities and the analysis of compound interest. In computer science they appear in the analysis of divide-and-conquer algorithms and binary representations. The sum formula also resolves Zeno's dichotomy paradox, in which an infinite sequence of ever-smaller distances sums to a finite total.
