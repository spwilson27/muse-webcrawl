Prostaglandin

Prostaglandins are a group of physiologically active lipid compounds called eicosanoids that have diverse hormone-like effects in animals. They are derived enzymatically from the fatty acid arachidonic acid and are produced in almost all tissues, acting locally near their site of synthesis rather than travelling through the bloodstream like classic hormones. There are about ten major prostaglandin receptors and numerous subtypes.

History

Prostaglandins were first observed in the 1930s by Ulf von Euler, who found that human semen contained a substance that contracted smooth muscle and lowered blood pressure; believing it came from the prostate gland, he named it "prostaglandin". Sune Bergström and Bengt Samuelsson later isolated and determined the structures of the prostaglandins, and John Vane showed that aspirin-like drugs act by blocking their synthesis. The three shared the 1982 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Synthesis and function

Prostaglandins are synthesised when arachidonic acid is released from cell membranes and acted upon by cyclooxygenase enzymes, forming the intermediate PGH2, which is converted into specific prostaglandins (PGD2, PGE2, PGF2α, PGI2/prostacyclin, and thromboxane A2). Their effects include mediating inflammation, pain, and fever; regulating smooth muscle contraction (including uterine contraction in labour); protecting the stomach lining; modulating platelet aggregation and blood vessel tone; and influencing kidney function and the sleep–wake cycle.

Medical relevance

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and aspirin work by inhibiting cyclooxygenase, reducing prostaglandin production and thereby pain and inflammation. Synthetic prostaglandin analogues are used as medicines: misoprostol (a PGE1 analogue) for ulcer prevention and obstetric uses, latanoprost for glaucoma, and dinoprostone for induction of labour.
