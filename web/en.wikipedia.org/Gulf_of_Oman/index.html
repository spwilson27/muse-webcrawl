The Gulf of Oman or Sea of Oman (Arabic: خليج عمان khalīj ʿumān; Persian: دریای عمان daryā-ye ʿomān), also known as the Gulf of Makran or Sea of Makran, is a gulf that connects the Arabian Sea with the Strait of Hormuz, which then runs to the Persian Gulf. It borders Iran and Pakistan to the north, Oman to the south, and the United Arab Emirates to the west.

Geography

The International Hydrographic Organization defines the limits of the Gulf of Oman as follows: on the northwest, a line joining Ras al Hadd (22°32'N) in Arabia, and Ras Jiyuni (61°43'E) on the coast of Pakistan; on the southeast, a line joining Ras Jibsh (25°32'N) and Ras al Kuh (25°17'N) on the coast of Iran. The gulf is about 340 kilometres (210 miles) long and 240 kilometres (150 miles) wide at its broadest.

The gulf's northern coast belongs mainly to Iran and Pakistan, while the southern coast belongs to Oman and, near the Strait of Hormuz, the Musandam Peninsula exclave of Oman and a small part of the United Arab Emirates (Fujairah). Major ports on the gulf include Jiwani and Gwadar in Pakistan, Chabahar in Iran, and Sohar, Muscat, and Sur in Oman, as well as Fujairah in the UAE — one of the world's largest bunkering ports.

Strait of Hormuz

At its western end the gulf narrows to the Strait of Hormuz, through which a large share of the world's seaborne petroleum passes from Persian Gulf producers. Because of this, the Gulf of Oman is of major strategic importance, and naval forces of several countries patrol its waters. The deep-water port of Gwadar in Balochistan, Pakistan, developed with Chinese investment, sits near the gulf's eastern entrance.

Oceanography and ecology

The Gulf of Oman is influenced by the monsoon winds of the northern Indian Ocean. In summer, strong southwesterly winds drive upwelling along the Omani coast, bringing nutrient-rich water to the surface and supporting productive fisheries. The gulf contains one of the world's largest hypoxic "dead zones," a seasonal oxygen minimum zone in its deeper waters that has been documented since the 1960s. Coral communities, turtles, dolphins, and whales — including a unique non-migratory population of humpback whales, the Arabian Sea humpback whale — inhabit its waters.

History

The gulf has been a maritime trade route since antiquity, linking Mesopotamia, Persia, and Arabia with the Indus Valley and beyond. Alexander the Great's admiral Nearchus sailed through it in 325–324 BC on the return voyage from India. In later centuries Omani seafarers dominated trade in the western Indian Ocean from ports on the gulf.
