Prejudice

Prejudice involves prejudging a person based on perceived membership of a social group.

Prejudice is an affective feeling — favourable or, more commonly in usage, unfavourable — towards a person based on their perceived membership of a social group, formed before or without knowledge of the person's actual qualities. The word derives from the Latin praejudicium, "judgment in advance." In social psychology, prejudice (an attitude) is distinguished from stereotypes (beliefs about a group) and discrimination (behaviour), though the three are closely related.

Theories and research

Gordon Allport's The Nature of Prejudice (1954) framed prejudice as a product of normal categorisation processes combined with in-group favouritism, and proposed the contact hypothesis: intergroup contact under conditions of equal status, common goals, cooperation and institutional support reduces prejudice — a prediction supported by later meta-analyses. Other major accounts include realistic group conflict theory, social identity theory (Tajfel's minimal-group experiments showed bias from even trivial group labels), authoritarian personality research, and work on automatic or implicit bias, measured by tools such as the Implicit Association Test.

Forms

Common forms include racism, sexism, ageism, homophobia, xenophobia, religious prejudice and class prejudice. Prejudice may be explicit and openly expressed or subtle and aversive; historically it has justified slavery, segregation, colonialism and genocide, and modern anti-discrimination law in many countries restricts its behavioural expression in employment, housing and public services.
