Atropisomer

Atropisomers are stereoisomers arising from hindered rotation about a single bond, where steric strain is high enough that the rotational barrier allows the individual rotamers to be isolated or to persist for an appreciable time. The term was coined by Richard Kuhn in 1933 from the Greek a- (not) + tropos (turn), applied first to hindered biphenyl compounds. Unlike stereoisomers based on stereogenic centres, atropisomers owe their chirality to an axis of restricted rotation — axial chirality — common in biaryls such as BINAP and BINOL, in many natural products (e.g. vancomycin and the ancishelenes), and increasingly in drug molecules.

Stability and classification

Whether a compound is atropisomeric in practice is a question of kinetics: by a common convention, an atropisomer is isolable when its half-life for racemisation is at least about 1,000 seconds (the corresponding barrier is roughly 93 kJ/mol at room temperature). Chemists classify atropisomers into three stability classes: class 1 (rapidly interconverting, half-life seconds), class 2 (intermediate, hours to days — a grey zone), and class 3 (configurationally stable, years), the latter behaving essentially like conventional enantiomers. Bulky ortho substituents on biaryl bonds raise the barrier; heating lowers the timescale and can racemise even stable atropisomers.

Importance

Atropisomerism matters practically in several areas. Chiral biaryl ligands such as BINAP (used in the Nobel Prize-winning work of Ryōji Noyori on asymmetric hydrogenation) are axially chiral atropisomers. Many pharmaceuticals possess hindered axes, and regulatory agencies increasingly require that drug atropisomers be characterised because interconversion in the body can convert an active form into an inactive or differently active one; some drugs, like the hepatitis C agent sofosbuvir's analogues or the kinase inhibitors sotorasib (deliberately designed as a stable atropisomer), make explicit use of the phenomenon. Natural products containing atropisomeric biaryl or aryl–amide bonds are targets of dedicated atroposelective synthesis strategies.
