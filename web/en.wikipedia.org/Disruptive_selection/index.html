Disruptive selection

Disruptive selection

Type: Form of natural selection

Effect: Both extreme phenotypes favoured; intermediate phenotypes selected against

Also called: Diversifying selection

Disruptive selection, also called diversifying selection, is a mode of natural selection in which extreme values for a trait are favoured over intermediate values. In disruptive selection, the variance of the trait increases and the population may divide into two distinct groups, a process that can contribute to speciation.

Disruptive selection is one of three main modes of selection on quantitative traits, alongside directional selection, which favours one extreme, and stabilizing selection, which favours intermediate values.

Mechanism

When disruptive selection acts on a population, individuals at both tails of the trait distribution leave more offspring than individuals near the mean. This can occur when the environment is heterogeneous, so that different phenotypes are favoured in different patches, or when a resource spectrum rewards specialists rather than generalists.

Examples

A classic example is the African seedcracker finch (Pyrenestes ostrinus), in which birds have either large or small beaks. Large-beaked birds crack hard sedge seeds, while small-beaked birds handle softer seeds; birds with intermediate beaks are inefficient at both and suffer lower survival.

Disruptive selection is also implicated in the maintenance of colour polymorphisms, such as in the peppered moth, and in host-associated divergence in insects such as the apple maggot fly.

Significance

Because disruptive selection increases phenotypic and genetic variance, it is considered a possible engine of sympatric speciation, in which a single population splits into two reproductively isolated groups without geographic separation.
