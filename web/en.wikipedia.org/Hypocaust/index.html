Hypocaust

A hypocaust (Latin hypocaustum) is a system of central heating in a building that produces and circulates hot air below the floor of a room, and may also warm the walls with a series of pipes. The word derives from the Ancient Greek hypo meaning "under" and caust-, "burnt". The system was developed by the Romans and was used in private villas and, especially, in the public baths (thermae) throughout the Roman Empire.

Operation

A furnace (praefurnium) outside the room burned wood or charcoal; its hot air travelled beneath a floor raised on brick pillars called pilae stacks, then up through hollow box tiles (tubuli) in the walls, escaping through the roof. The floor, built of concrete over tiles, was warmed from below without smoke entering the room. Rooms needing the most heat were placed closest to the furnace, making it possible to grade temperatures from the hot caldarium to the cooler tepidarium and frigidarium of a bathhouse.

Decline and legacy

Hypocausts were labour-intensive and fuel-hungry and largely disappeared in western Europe with the fall of Rome, although underfloor heating survived in some regions and in the Korean ondol and Chinese kang systems. Modern radiant underfloor heating is a direct conceptual descendant.
