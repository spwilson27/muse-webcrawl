Climate ethics

Climate ethics is a field of applied ethics and political philosophy concerned with the moral problems raised by human-caused climate change. It examines who is responsible for causing climate change, who is harmed by it, what duties of mitigation, adaptation, and compensation are owed, and how the costs and benefits of climate policy should be distributed across countries, generations, and species.

Background

Human activities, principally the burning of fossil fuels and deforestation, have increased atmospheric concentrations of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane, warming the planet and disrupting weather patterns. The harms of climate change — sea level rise, heat waves, droughts, floods, and ecosystem loss — fall disproportionately on the poorest people, who have contributed least to the problem. This asymmetry is a central source of ethical concern.

Key questions

Philosophers and policy analysts debate several questions: whether historical emissions generate duties of compensation; how emissions rights should be allocated (for example, on equal per-capita terms); the moral status of future generations, who will bear the worst effects; the appropriate discount rate for future harms in economic models; and whether individual actions, such as flying or eating meat, carry moral significance given the collective scale of the problem.

Climate justice

Climate justice links climate policy with human rights and distributive justice. Concepts such as "common but differentiated responsibilities", embedded in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, hold that wealthy industrialised nations bear greater responsibility because of their historical emissions and greater capacity to act. Loss and damage mechanisms, agreed in international negotiations, aim to address harms that cannot be avoided through mitigation or adaptation.
