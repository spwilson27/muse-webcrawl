Progressive Conservative Party of Canada

The Progressive Conservative Party of Canada (PC Party; French: Parti progressiste-conservateur du Canada) was a centre to centre-right federal political party in Canada that existed from 1942 to 2003. One of only two parties to have governed Canada since Confederation — alternating in power with the Liberal Party — the Progressive Conservatives formed the government under leaders including John Diefenbaker, Joe Clark, Brian Mulroney, and Kim Campbell.

History

The party descends from the Conservative Party founded by John A. Macdonald, Canada's first prime minister. In 1942 the party adopted the name "Progressive Conservative" as the condition of Manitoba premier John Bracken, a Progressive, becoming leader. Under John Diefenbaker (1956–1967) the party won the largest majority then recorded, in 1958, championed the Canadian Bill of Rights, and extended the vote to First Nations people.

Brian Mulroney led the party to a record landslide in 1984 and a second majority in 1988, enacting free trade with the United States, the Goods and Services Tax, and attempting constitutional reform through the Meech Lake and Charlottetown Accords. The failures of those accords and a deep recession destroyed the party: in the 1993 election, under Kim Campbell — Canada's first woman prime minister — the PCs were reduced from 169 seats to two, the worst defeat of a governing party in Canadian history.

Merger and legacy

Weakened and split by competition with the Reform Party and its successor, the Canadian Alliance, the Progressive Conservatives under Peter MacKay agreed in 2003 to merge with the Alliance to form the modern Conservative Party of Canada. The merger ended the 136-year lineage of Macdonald's party as a separate organisation. Provincial Progressive Conservative parties — including those in Ontario, Nova Scotia, and Alberta's former PC party — survive independently of the federal merger.

The federal PCs were a classic "brokerage party," spanning Red Tories with interventionist instincts and western and fiscal conservatives; that internal tension defined much of 20th-century Canadian politics.
