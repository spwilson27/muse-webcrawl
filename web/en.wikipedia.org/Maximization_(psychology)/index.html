Maximization (psychology)

In the field of psychology, maximization is a style of decision-making characterised by seeking the best possible option. A maximizer seeks to evaluate all options to find the optimal choice, in contrast to a satisficer, who seeks a "good enough" option that meets an acceptable threshold. The concept was developed by psychologist Barry Schwartz and colleagues, building on Herbert Simon's work on bounded rationality.

The Maximization Scale

Schwartz and co-workers devised the Maximization Scale (2002) to measure individual differences in the tendency to maximize. The scale assesses three components: high standards, alternative search, and decision difficulty. A related two-component model separates "goal" maximization (wanting the best) from "strategy" maximization (exhaustively searching alternatives), finding that the strategy component accounts for most negative outcomes.

Consequences

Research associates maximizing tendencies with lower life satisfaction, reduced happiness, more regret, greater decision difficulty, and tendencies toward perfectionism and depression — a phenomenon explored in Schwartz's book The Paradox of Choice: Why More Is Less (2004). Maximizers spend more time comparing options, are more affected by opportunity costs, and are more likely to experience post-decision regret even when their objective outcomes are better than those of satisficers.

Criticism and refinement

Later psychometric work criticised the original scale's structure and proposed alternative measures distinguishing adaptive high standards from maladaptive exhaustive search. Cross-cultural studies suggest the costs of maximization vary with the number of choices environments provide.
