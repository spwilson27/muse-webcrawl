Ken Jacobs (25 May 1933 – 5 October 2025) was an American experimental filmmaker, a central figure of the New American Cinema and of structural film. Over a career of more than six decades he made films, shadow plays, and projection performances that interrogated the material nature of cinema itself.

Early work

Born in Brooklyn, Jacobs studied painting with Hans Hofmann and began filming in the 1950s. With Jack Smith he made Blonde Cobra (1963), a notorious underground classic, and Little Stabs at Happiness (1960). In 1966 he co-founded the Millennium Film Workshop's screening programme, and he was a founder of the cinema department at Binghamton University, where he taught for many years.

Tom, Tom, the Piper's Son

His best-known film, Tom, Tom, the Piper's Son (1969, revised 1971), rephotographs a 1905 Billy Bitzer one-reeler, slowing, enlarging, and re-framing fragments of the original until the film becomes an archaeology of early cinema and of the filmstrip itself. It is regularly cited among the greatest avant-garde films and was inducted into the US National Film Registry.

Nervous System and later work

From the 1970s Jacobs developed the "Nervous System", live performances using two projectors, variable-speed shutters, and his own manipulations to create flickering three-dimensional effects from flat footage — a method he later extended into digital "Eternalism" works. He continued to produce a large body of digital video into his nineties, receiving a John Simon Guggenheim Fellowship and major retrospectives. He died in New York City in 2025, aged 92.
