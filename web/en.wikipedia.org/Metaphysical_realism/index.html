Metaphysical realism

Metaphysical realism
FieldMetaphysics, philosophy of language
Opposed toAnti-realism, internal realism
Key criticsHilary Putnam, Richard Rorty

Metaphysical realism is the view that objects exist independently of perception, thought, or language, and that the world has a definite, mind-independent structure that divides it into kinds in exactly one privileged way. On this view, truth consists in a correspondence between statements and a ready-made world whose "joints" exist prior to any conceptual scheme.

Characterisation

Following Hilary Putnam's influential critique, metaphysical realism combines three theses: the world consists of a fixed totality of mind-independent objects; there is exactly one true and complete description of the way the world is; and truth involves a correspondence between signs and external things. Putnam attacked the position in the late 1970s with model-theoretic arguments and his "brain in a vat" scenario, proposing instead an "internal realism" in which objectivity is preserved within a conceptual scheme.

Defences and debates

Philosophers such as Michael Devitt have defended realism against Putnam, arguing that the causal theory of reference can fix reference to mind-independent kinds. The dispute connects to wider debates over scientific realism, natural kinds, and the role of language in ontology, and remains a touchstone for discussions of realism and anti-realism in contemporary metaphysics.
