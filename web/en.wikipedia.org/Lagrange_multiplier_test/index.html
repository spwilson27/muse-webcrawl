Lagrange multiplier test

The LM test evaluates the slope (score) of the likelihood at the restricted estimate

In statistics, the Lagrange multiplier test, also called the score test or the Rao score test, is a hypothesis test of restrictions on the parameters of a statistical model. It is one of the three classical approaches to hypothesis testing, alongside the likelihood ratio test and the Wald test.

Principle

The test asks how steeply the log-likelihood function is rising at the parameter values estimated under the null hypothesis (with the restrictions imposed). If the restrictions are true, the score — the gradient of the log-likelihood — should be close to zero at the restricted estimate, because the unrestricted maximum lies nearby. If the score deviates significantly from zero, the restrictions are rejected. Formally, the statistic is

LM = s(θ̃)ᵀ I(θ̃)−1 s(θ̃),

where s is the score vector evaluated at the restricted maximum likelihood estimate θ̃ and I is the Fisher information matrix. Under the null hypothesis the statistic is asymptotically chi-squared distributed with degrees of freedom equal to the number of restrictions. The test was developed by C. R. Rao (1948) and, in the Lagrange multiplier framing, by Samuel Silvey (1959).

Advantages and uses

The score test's great practical advantage is that it requires estimation only under the null hypothesis. It is therefore especially convenient for testing complicated alternatives — for example, testing for omitted variables, serial correlation (the Breusch–Godfrey test), heteroscedasticity (the Breusch–Pagan test) or spatial dependence, where fitting the unrestricted model may be infeasible. In large samples the three classical tests are asymptotically equivalent, though they may differ in finite samples.
