Victoria Cross

The Victoria Cross (VC) is the highest and most prestigious decoration of the British decorations system, awarded for "most conspicuous bravery, or some daring or pre-eminent act of valour or self-sacrifice, or extreme devotion to duty in the presence of the enemy". Instituted by Queen Victoria in 1856 to honour acts of valour during the Crimean War, it may be awarded to members of the British Armed Forces and, historically, to Commonwealth forces of any rank, and to civilians under military command.

The medal

The cross is a bronze cross pattée bearing the royal cipher and the inscription "For Valour", suspended from a crimson ribbon. Tradition holds that the bronze came from guns captured at Sevastopol, though research suggests a more varied provenance. The medals are hand-made by the jewellers Hancocks of London, and the reverse bears the date of the act of bravery.

Awards

Since 1856, 1,358 crosses have been awarded to 1,355 individuals, including three men who won the VC twice (receiving a bar). Sixty-four per cent of First World War awards were posthumous in some campaigns, reflecting the decoration's severe standard: the deed must be witnessed and survive intense scrutiny. The most recent British awards were for the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Australia, Canada, and New Zealand have instituted their own versions of the Victoria Cross within their national honours systems.

Collections

The largest public collection, assembled by Lord Ashcroft, is displayed at the Imperial War Museum in London. Recipients are entitled to the post-nominal letters VC, and annuities and salutes have historically accompanied the award.
