Conserved quantity

A conserved quantity is a property or value that remains constant over time in a physical system, even as other aspects of the system change. Conservation laws, which state that such quantities cannot be created or destroyed (within a closed system), are among the most fundamental principles of physics.

Examples

  • Energy — conservation of energy (the first law of thermodynamics).
  • Linear momentum and angular momentum.
  • Electric charge — conserved in all known interactions.
  • Mass–energy in relativity, where mass and energy are unified.
  • Approximately conserved quantities in particle physics, such as baryon number and lepton number; others, like strangeness, are conserved only by some interactions.

Noether's theorem

Emmy Noether proved in 1915 (published 1918) that every differentiable symmetry of a physical system's action corresponds to a conservation law: time-translation symmetry gives energy conservation; spatial-translation symmetry gives momentum; rotational symmetry gives angular momentum. This deep connection between symmetry and conservation organises much of modern physics.

Applications

Conservation laws allow physicists to predict outcomes of collisions, reactions and orbital motions without tracking every detail of the internal forces, and missing momentum or energy in experiments has repeatedly pointed to new particles — most famously the neutrino, proposed by Wolfgang Pauli to preserve energy conservation in beta decay.
