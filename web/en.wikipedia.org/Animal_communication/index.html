Animal communication

[Image placeholder]

Animal communication is the transfer of information from one or a group of animals (senders) to one or more other animals (receivers) that affects the current or future behaviour of the receivers. Information may be sent intentionally, as in a courtship display, or unintentionally, as when a predator gives off odours that betray its presence. Communication may occur within a species (intraspecific) or between species (interspecific).

Modalities

Animals communicate through many channels. Visual signals include the courtship dances of birds, the colour changes of cephalopods, and the facial expressions of primates. Acoustic signals range from bird song and whale song to the infrasonic rumbles of elephants and the alarm calls of vervet monkeys, which are functionally referential — different calls denote different predator types. Chemical communication via pheromones dominates in insects, coordinating the life of ant and bee colonies. Tactile signals include grooming in primates and the waggle dance of honeybees, which encodes the direction and distance of food sources. Some animals use electrical signals (weakly electric fish) or seismic signals transmitted through the ground.

Functions

Communication serves functions including mate attraction and courtship, territorial defence, alarm and predator deterrence, parent–offspring interaction, coordination of group hunting or migration, and the establishment and maintenance of dominance hierarchies. Signals may be honest indicators of quality (handicap principle) or deceptive, as in aggressive mimicry by predators and brood parasitism by cuckoos.

Study

The modern study of animal communication grew out of ethology — the work of Konrad Lorenz, Nikolaas Tinbergen and Karl von Frisch — and is now an interdisciplinary field linking behavioural ecology, neurobiology, linguistics and information theory. A debated question is how animal systems compare with human language: some, such as the dances of bees and the calls of prairie dogs, show elements of reference and productivity but lack the open-ended syntax of human language.
