The Battle of Dettingen (27 June 1743) was fought during the War of the Austrian Succession between the so-called Pragmatic Army — British, Hanoverian and Austrian troops — and the French army. The allies, commanded in person by King George II of Great Britain, defeated the French under the Duc de Noailles. It was the last time a reigning British monarch led troops into battle.

Background

The Pragmatic Army had advanced into southern Germany in support of Maria Theresa of Austria but found itself cut off from its supplies along the River Main, with Noailles's larger French army blocking its retreat near Aschaffenburg. Short of food, the allies began withdrawing westward, apparently into a trap.

The battle

The French plan unravelled when the Duc de Gramont, impatient, led his troops across the Main and abandoned the strong defensive position Noailles had prepared. George II, on foot after his horse bolted, rallied his infantry, and the allied line advanced, driving the French back across the river in disorder. Casualties on both sides ran to several thousand, but the road to Hanau and resupply was open.

Legacy

The victory was celebrated in Britain, and Handel composed the anthem Te Deum in D major ("Dettingen Te Deum") to mark it. Strategically, the battle did little to change the war in Germany, as the allies withdrew into winter quarters in the Low Countries that autumn.
