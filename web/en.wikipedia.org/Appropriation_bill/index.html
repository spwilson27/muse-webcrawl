Appropriation bill

An appropriation bill (also called a supply bill or spending bill) is proposed legislation that authorises the expenditure of government funds. It embodies the constitutional principle that public money may be spent only with the approval of the legislature — in the United States, the Appropriations Clause of the Constitution provides that no money shall be drawn from the Treasury except in consequence of appropriations made by law.

United States process

In the U.S. federal government, discretionary spending is provided through twelve regular appropriation bills covering areas such as defence, agriculture and transportation, drafted by the House and Senate Appropriations Committees. When Congress cannot pass them before the fiscal year begins on 1 October, it may enact a continuing resolution to keep agencies funded temporarily; failure to do either produces a funding gap and a government shutdown.

Other systems

In Westminster systems, supply bills are central to parliamentary control of the executive: a government that loses a vote on supply is generally expected to resign or call an election, as in the Australian constitutional crisis of 1975.
