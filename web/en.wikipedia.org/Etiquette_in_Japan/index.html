Etiquette in Japan

Key conceptsWa (harmony), tatemae/honne, keigo (honorific language)
GreetingBowing (ojigi)
Notable customsRemoving shoes indoors, gift-giving, chopstick taboos, quiet public transport

Etiquette in Japan forms common social expectations for behaviour in Japanese society, heavily influenced by Confucian ideas of hierarchy and by the value placed on wa — social harmony — and on avoiding embarrassment to oneself or others. Many customs derive from the distinction between uchi (inside, one's own group) and soto (outside), and between public presentation (tatemae) and private feeling (honne).

Bowing and greetings

Bowing (ojigi) is the standard greeting, apology, and expression of thanks, with depth and duration scaled to the relative status of the other person and the gravity of the occasion. Business card exchange (meishi kōkan) is a ritualized part of introductions: cards are presented and received with both hands and studied briefly, never immediately pocketed.

Language

Politeness is built into the Japanese language through keigo, the system of honorific, humble, and polite speech forms. Names are typically followed by suffixes such as -san; surnames, not given names, are the default form of address except among close intimates.

Homes, shoes, and gifts

Shoes are removed at the entrance (genkan) of homes, traditional inns, many restaurants, and temples, and indoor slippers are provided; special slippers are used in toilets. Gift-giving is elaborate: presents are wrapped carefully, offered with modesty, and generally not opened in the giver's presence; fruit, sweets, and regional specialties (omiyage) are common choices.

Eating and public behaviour

Chopstick taboos (kirai-bashi) include sticking chopsticks upright in rice (which recalls funeral offerings) and passing food from chopstick to chopstick. It is polite to say itadakimasu before eating and gochisōsama deshita afterwards; slurping noodles is acceptable. On trains and buses, talking on mobile phones is discouraged and priority seats are yielded to the elderly and pregnant. Blowing one's nose loudly in public is considered rude, and walking while eating is widely frowned upon.
