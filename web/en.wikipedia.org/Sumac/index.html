Sumac

Sumac (also spelled sumach) is any of about 35 species of flowering plants in the genus Rhus and related genera in the cashew family (Anacardiaceae). Sumacs are dioecious shrubs and small trees that can reach a height of one to ten metres (3–33 ft). Species occur in subtropical and temperate regions throughout the world, with the greatest diversity in Southern Africa.

Description

The leaves are usually pinnately compound, though some species have trifoliate or simple leaves. The flowers are small, greenish, creamy white or red, and produced in dense panicles or spikes 5–30 cm long, giving rise to dense clusters of red, hairy fruit called sumac bobs. Sumacs propagate both by seed (spread by birds) and by new shoots from rhizomes, forming large clonal colonies.

Culinary use

The dried, ground fruit of Rhus coriaria — Sicilian sumac — is a ruby-red spice used for millennia in Middle Eastern, Mediterranean, and Central Asian cooking. It has a sour, lemony flavour and is a key ingredient of the blend za'atar and a garnish for fattoush, hummus, and grilled meats. In North America, the berries of staghorn and smooth sumac are steeped to make a tart drink sometimes called sumac-ade or Indian lemonade.

Other uses and relatives

Sumac leaves and bark are rich in tannins and were used for tanning leather and as dyes and mordants. The genus Rhus formerly included the allergenic species poison ivy, poison oak, and poison sumac, now placed in the genus Toxicodendron; these produce the skin-irritating oil urushiol. Edible sumacs, by contrast, are harmless and their red berries are readily distinguished from the white fruit of poison sumac.
