Invasion literature

Invasion literature (also called invasion novels or invasion fiction) is a literary genre, popular in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, in which a nation is threatened by invasion from a foreign power. The genre is generally credited as beginning with The Battle of Dorking (1871) by George Tomkyns Chesney, which imagined a German invasion of Britain and became a bestseller that spawned hundreds of imitations.

History

Chesney's story, published anonymously in Blackwood's Magazine shortly after Prussia's victory in the Franco-Prussian War, warned that Britain's reliance on a small volunteer army left it vulnerable. Its success launched a wave of "future war" fiction, including William Le Queux's The Invasion of 1910 (1906) and Spies of the Kaiser (1909), which fed public anxiety about espionage and helped create political pressure for military preparedness in the years before the First World War.

Themes

Invasion literature typically combined adventure narrative with political argument: authors used the fictional invasion to campaign for conscription, naval expansion, or the reform of national defence. The feared invader changed with international politics — France, Germany, Russia, and later an imagined "Yellow Peril" all featured. H. G. Wells drew on the genre in The War of the Worlds (1898), turning the invasion narrative toward science fiction by substituting Martians for a human enemy.

Legacy

The genre declined after the First World War, as real invasion fears gave way to anxieties about aerial bombing, but its conventions persist in alternate history, techno-thrillers, and alien-invasion stories. Scholars regard invasion literature as an important record of Edwardian anxieties and a significant influence on British defence policy and popular culture.
