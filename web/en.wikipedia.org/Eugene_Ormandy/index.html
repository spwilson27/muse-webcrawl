Eugene Ormandy (born Jenő Blau; November 18, 1899 – March 12, 1985) was a Hungarian-American conductor and violinist, best known for his association with the Philadelphia Orchestra as its music director for 44 years. Along with his 64-year association with the Philadelphia Orchestra, his conducting is particularly identified with the Rachmaninoff compositions and especially the creation of the "Philadelphia Sound," a lush, string-heavy orchestral sonority that he cultivated.

Career

A child prodigy on the violin, Ormandy entered the Royal National Hungarian Academy of Music at age five and studied with Jenő Hubay. He immigrated to the United States in 1921 and soon turned to conducting, leading the Capitol Theatre orchestra in New York and making guest appearances with major orchestras. After a stint as music director of the Minneapolis Symphony Orchestra (1931–1936), he became co-conductor of the Philadelphia Orchestra in 1936 alongside Leopold Stokowski, succeeding him as sole music director in 1938.

Legacy

Under Ormandy, the Philadelphia Orchestra made hundreds of recordings, especially for Columbia Records and later RCA, and gained a worldwide reputation. He was a champion of Rachmaninoff, who dedicated works to the orchestra, and gave the U.S. premieres of many important pieces. He became a conductor laureate in 1980. Ormandy received numerous honours, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom (1970) and an honorary knighthood from Queen Elizabeth II (1976).
