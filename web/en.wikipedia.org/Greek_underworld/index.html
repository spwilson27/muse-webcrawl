The Greek underworld, or Hades, is the place of the dead in ancient Greek religion and mythology, a realm beneath the earth ruled by the god Hades and his queen Persephone. The dead crossed the river Styx (or Acheron) in the boat of the ferryman Charon, who required payment of an obol placed in the mouth of the corpse at burial; the three-headed dog Cerberus guarded the exit so that none could leave.

Geography

Greek conceptions of the underworld evolved over time. In Homer, the dead are witless shades in a gloomy common realm. Later tradition divided the underworld into regions: the Asphodel Meadows, where ordinary souls wandered; Elysium (or the Isles of the Blessed), reserved for heroes and the righteous; and Tartarus, a pit below Hades itself where the Titans were imprisoned and great sinners such as Tantalus and Sisyphus suffered exemplary punishments. Five rivers flowed through the realm: Styx, Acheron, Lethe (forgetfulness), Phlegethon (fire), and Cocytus (lamentation).

Judgement and cult

In Plato and later writers the dead were judged—by Minos, Rhadamanthus, and Aeacus—and rewarded or punished accordingly. The mystery cults, above all the Eleusinian Mysteries, promised initiates a happier lot after death, while Orphic gold tablets buried with the dead gave them instructions for the journey, including which spring to drink from and which to avoid.

Visits by the living

Myth records several descents (katabaseis) by living heroes: Odysseus consulted the shade of Tiresias; Heracles wrestled Cerberus as his twelfth labour and rescued Theseus; Orpheus charmed the underworld's gods with music but lost Eurydice by looking back; Aeneas, guided by the Cumaean Sibyl in Virgil's Aeneid, received there a vision of Rome's future.
