Chaconne

A chaconne (French; Italian: ciaccona) is a type of musical composition popular in the Baroque era, built as a series of continuous variations over a repeating chord progression or bass line (ostinato), usually in triple metre and in a major or minor key.


Bach's Chaconne in D minor

History

The chaconne originated as a dance-song in Spanish culture, possibly with Latin American roots, and spread through Europe in the early 17th century. Early examples by Monteverdi and Frescobaldi gave way to grander structures; Lully made it a stately closing dance in French opera. In Germany the form merged with the passacaglia, to which it is closely related; the distinction between the two remains debated.

Famous examples

The most celebrated chaconne is the final movement of J. S. Bach's Partita No. 2 in D minor for solo violin (BWV 1004), a vast set of 64 variations. Other notable examples include Pachelbel's Chaconne in F minor, Handel's Chaconne in G major, and, in later centuries, movements by Brahms and Britten.
