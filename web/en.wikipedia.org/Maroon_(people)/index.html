Maroons (people)

Maroons are descendants of Africans in the Americas and the Indian Ocean who escaped from slavery and formed their own settlements. They often mixed with indigenous peoples, eventually evolving into separate creole cultures such as the Garifuna and the Mascogos.

History

The English word "maroon" derives from the French marron, meaning feral or fugitive, itself possibly from the Spanish cimarrón. As early as 1512, enslaved Africans escaped from Spanish and Portuguese captors in the Caribbean and the Americas and either joined indigenous peoples or formed their own communities. Maroon communities offered havens for escaped slaves and played an important role in the histories of several Latin American and Caribbean countries.

Notable communities

In Jamaica, Maroon communities in Accompong and Moore Town won treaties with the British in 1739 after the First Maroon War, led by figures such as Queen Nanny and Cudjoe. In Suriname and French Guiana, Maroon peoples including the Saramaka, Ndyuka and Aluku maintained autonomous societies in the rainforest interior. In Brazil, the quilombo of Palmares resisted Portuguese forces for much of the 17th century under leaders such as Ganga Zumba and Zumbi.

Culture

Maroon cultures developed distinctive languages, such as Jamaican Maroon Creole and the creoles of Suriname, combining African, European and indigenous elements. Their traditions include distinctive music, cuisine, religious practices and craft traditions preserved over centuries of independence.
