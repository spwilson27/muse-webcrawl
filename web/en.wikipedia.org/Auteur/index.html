Auteur

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A film director at work

An auteur (French for "author") is an artist with a distinctive and influential style and approach, most often applied to a film director whose personal influence and artistic control over a movie are so great that the filmmaker is regarded as the author of the movie. In film criticism, auteur theory, arising in France during the late 1940s, holds that a film reflects its director's personal creative vision.

Origins

The theory grew out of Alexandre Astruc's notion of the caméra-stylo ("camera-pen") and the politique des auteurs of the critics of Cahiers du Cinéma, especially François Truffaut's 1954 essay "A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema". Truffaut and his colleagues championed studio directors such as Alfred Hitchcock and Howard Hawks as artists, a stance developed in English by Andrew Sarris in "Notes on the Auteur Theory" (1962).

Criticism

Pauline Kael and others objected that auteur theory downplayed the collaborative nature of filmmaking, elevating directors over screenwriters, cinematographers, and editors. Later scholarship has sought modified positions, recognizing directorial influence while acknowledging collaboration and industrial constraints.

Extension to other media

The term has been extended to television showrunners, video game designers such as Hideo Kojima, musicians, and "auteur" figures in other arts who exercise comprehensive creative control.
