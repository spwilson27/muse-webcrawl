Fannie Lou Hamer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fannie Lou Hamer (née Townsend; October 6, 1917 – March 14, 1977) was an American voting and women's rights activist, community organiser, and leader of the civil rights movement. She was the vice-chair of the Freedom Democratic Party, which she represented at the 1964 Democratic National Convention, and a co-founder of the National Women's Political Caucus. Hamer is best remembered for her powerful testimony and for her signature phrase, "I'm sick and tired of being sick and tired."

Early life

Born in Montgomery County, Mississippi, the youngest of twenty children of sharecroppers, Hamer began working in the fields at age six and left school at twelve. She married Perry "Pap" Hamer and worked as a sharecropper and timekeeper on a plantation near Ruleville, Mississippi. In 1961 she was subjected to a hysterectomy without her consent — a practice so common among poor Black women that it was called a "Mississippi appendectomy" — an experience that deepened her commitment to activism.

Activism

In 1962 Hamer attended a meeting led by James Bevel of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference and volunteers of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC), and volunteered to attempt to register to vote — an act that cost her job and her home and exposed her to death threats. In June 1963, after attending a citizenship workshop, she was arrested in Winona, Mississippi, and brutally beaten in jail by police and forced prisoner proxies, suffering permanent injuries.

As vice-chair of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party, Hamer testified before the Credentials Committee of the 1964 Democratic National Convention, describing the violence faced by Black Mississippians seeking the vote; her televised account so moved the nation that President Lyndon B. Johnson called an impromptu press conference to divert coverage. The MFDP's challenge helped pave the way for the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Hamer later ran for Congress, organised Freedom Summer, founded economic cooperatives including the Freedom Farm Cooperative and a "pig bank", and co-founded the National Women's Political Caucus in 1971. She died in 1977 and is buried in Ruleville.
