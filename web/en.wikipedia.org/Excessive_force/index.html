Excessive force

Excessive force refers to force used by law enforcement officers (or other authorities) that exceeds what is reasonably necessary to achieve a legitimate objective, such as effecting an arrest, preventing escape, or protecting life. It is a form of police misconduct and, when it violates constitutional rights, can give rise to civil lawsuits and criminal prosecution.

Legal standards

In the United States, claims of excessive force during an arrest or investigatory stop are evaluated under the Fourth Amendment's "objective reasonableness" standard set out in Graham v. Connor (1989). Courts weigh factors including the severity of the crime at issue, whether the suspect posed an immediate threat, and whether the suspect was resisting arrest or fleeing. Deadly force is governed by Tennessee v. Garner (1985), which held that an officer may not use deadly force against a fleeing felon unless the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury.

Virtually all police agencies maintain use-of-force policies, often expressed as a continuum from verbal commands through physical control to lethal force, and incidents are investigated by internal affairs units, civilian review boards, or outside agencies. The concept also appears in international law, where the UN Basic Principles on the Use of Force and Firearms by Law Enforcement Officials (1990) require that force be proportional and a last resort.

Remedies and reform

Victims may sue under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, though the doctrine of qualified immunity shields officers unless they violated clearly established law. High-profile incidents have driven reforms including body-worn cameras, duty-to-intervene policies, bans on chokeholds, and databases tracking officers disciplined for misconduct.
