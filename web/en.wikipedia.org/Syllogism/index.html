Syllogism

A syllogism (Greek: συλλογισμός, syllogismos, "conclusion, inference") is a kind of logical argument that applies deductive reasoning to arrive at a conclusion based on two propositions that are asserted or assumed to be true. In its earliest form, defined by Aristotle, it combines a major premise and a minor premise to produce a conclusion.

Structure

The classic example runs: "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal." Every categorical syllogism has three terms — the major term (predicate of the conclusion), the minor term (subject of the conclusion) and the middle term, which appears in both premises but not the conclusion. The arrangement of the middle term defines the syllogism's figure, and the quantity and quality of its propositions define its mood; of 256 possible forms, only a minority are valid.

History

Aristotle's Prior Analytics founded Western logic, and syllogistic remained its core for two thousand years, elaborated by medieval scholastics who gave each valid mood a mnemonic name such as Barbara and Celarent. From the 19th century, the system was generalised and partly superseded by predicate logic developed by Frege and others, though it survives as a complete, decidable fragment of first-order logic.

Types and fallacies

Beyond categorical syllogisms there are hypothetical and disjunctive forms. Common fallacies include the undistributed middle, illicit major or minor, and the fallacy of four terms. Venn diagrams provide a standard visual test of validity.
