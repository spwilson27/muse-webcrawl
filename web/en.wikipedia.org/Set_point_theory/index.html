Set point theory


Set point theory holds that body weight is defended around a regulated level.

Set point theory, as it relates to human body weight, proposes that each individual has a predetermined weight range — a "set point" — that the body actively defends through compensatory changes in appetite, metabolism and energy expenditure. According to the theory, developed from animal studies in the mid-20th century (notably Gordon Kennedy's lipostatic hypothesis of 1953 and the work of Keesey and others), the body regulates its fat stores much as a thermostat regulates temperature: weight loss triggers increased hunger and reduced metabolic rate, while weight gain produces the opposite responses.

Mechanisms

The discovery of the hormone leptin in 1994 supplied a plausible mechanism: adipose tissue secretes leptin in proportion to fat mass, and the hypothalamus adjusts food intake and energy expenditure in response. Other signals — including insulin, ghrelin and gut peptides — feed into the same regulatory circuitry, centred on hypothalamic nuclei such as the arcuate nucleus.

Evidence and criticism

Set point theory helps explain why deliberate weight loss is frequently followed by regain, and why metabolic adaptation persists after dieting. Critics argue for a "settling point" instead: weight stabilises passively where energy intake and expenditure balance, without active defence, and environmental changes can shift equilibrium indefinitely. Others propose dual-intervention-point models, in which the body defends upper and lower bounds rather than a single weight, with drift within the range. It is also debated why population weights have risen with the modern food environment if a fixed set point exists; proponents respond that the set point itself can drift upward under sustained environmental pressure.

Related uses

The term "set point" is also used more loosely in psychology — for example, a happiness set point to which subjective well-being tends to return after positive or negative life events — borrowing the homeostatic metaphor from physiology.
