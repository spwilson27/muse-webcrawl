Shark Bay is a World Heritage Site in the Gascoyne region of Western Australia. The 23,000-square-kilometre bay and its islands lie at the westernmost point of the Australian continent, roughly 800 km north of Perth. Inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991 — meeting all four natural criteria, a distinction shared by few sites — Shark Bay is celebrated for its living stromatolites, vast seagrass meadows, and exceptional marine fauna. The bay was named by the English explorer William Dampier in 1699, and was visited in 1616 by Dirk Hartog, whose inscribed pewter plate commemorates one of the earliest recorded European landfalls in Australia.

Natural values

Stromatolites

At Hamelin Pool, in the hypersaline southern reaches of the bay, colonies of cyanobacteria build dome-shaped stromatolites — layered structures similar to the oldest fossil evidence of life on Earth, some 3.5 billion years old. The extreme salinity, caused by the Faure Sill (a seagrass and sand bank restricting water exchange) and high evaporation, excludes most predators of the bacteria, allowing the structures to flourish.

Seagrass and dugongs

Shark Bay holds about 4,800 km² of seagrass meadows — among the largest and most diverse anywhere, with 12 species recorded in one area — including the Wooramel Seagrass Bank, the largest structure of its kind in the world. The meadows support an estimated 10,000 dugongs, roughly one-eighth of the world population, making the bay one of the species' last strongholds.

Other fauna

The bay and its islands and peninsulas support green and loggerhead turtles, bottlenose dolphins (famous at Monkey Mia, where wild dolphins come to the beach to be fed under ranger supervision), humpback whales, rays, and a rich fish fauna despite the name's association: Shark Bay is home to many shark species, including tiger and hammerhead sharks. Terrestrial reserves on Bernier, Dorre, and Dirk Hartog Islands preserve mammals — such as burrowing bettongs, banded hare-wallabies, and Shark Bay mice — that became extinct or endangered on the mainland because of introduced predators.

Human history

Aboriginal people of the Malgana, Nhanda, and Yingkarta groups have lived around Shark Bay for tens of thousands of years, and the area contains important cultural sites. European contact began with Hartog in 1616, followed by Dampier's detailed natural-history observations in 1699 and visits by French expeditions under Baudin (1801–1803) and Freycinet. The 19th and 20th centuries saw guano mining, pearling, fishing, and pastoralism; the small towns of Denham and Monkey Mia now serve tourism and fishing.

Conservation

Much of the site is protected in the Shark Bay Marine Park, Francois Peron National Park, and island nature reserves. Project Eden and the Dirk Hartog Island National Park ecological restoration programme aim to remove feral cats, goats, and sheep and to reintroduce locally extinct native mammals. In 2010–2011 a marine heatwave caused extensive seagrass die-off in the bay, a major setback still being studied for its effects on dugongs and the bay's carbon stores.
