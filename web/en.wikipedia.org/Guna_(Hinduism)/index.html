Guṇa (Hinduism)

Guṇa (Sanskrit: गुण) is a concept in Hinduism and Sikhism which can be translated as "quality, peculiarity, attribute, property". In Samkhya philosophy, the guṇas are the three fundamental tendencies or modes of nature (prakriti) that are present, in varying combinations, in all beings and things. The three guṇas are:

  • sattva — goodness, purity, lightness, harmony, balance, joy, and intelligence;
  • rajas — passion, activity, movement, energy, restlessness, and attachment;
  • tamas — darkness, ignorance, inertia, heaviness, laziness, and delusion.

Each of these qualities is present in everyone and everything, varying in proportion in different circumstances, and no one or anything is purely one or the other. The interaction of the guṇas creates the world of experience; spiritual development (sadhana) is understood as gradually increasing sattva and ultimately transcending all three guṇas.

Samkhya philosophy

In Samkhya, one of the six orthodox schools (darshanas) of Hindu philosophy, prakriti (nature or matter) is composed of the three guṇas in equilibrium. When this balance is disturbed, the evolution of the universe begins. The word guṇa literally means "strand" or "rope": the guṇas are pictured as the three strands twisted together into the single rope of manifest nature, each striving for dominance over the others. Consciousness (purusha) is distinct from prakriti and is not itself composed of guṇas.

Bhagavad Gita

The Bhagavad Gita devotes its fourteenth chapter, the Guṇatraya-Vibhāga Yoga, to the three modes of material nature. Krishna explains that sattva binds through attachment to happiness and knowledge, rajas through attachment to action, and tamas through heedlessness, indolence, and sleep. The Gita classifies food, sacrifice, austerity, charity, knowledge, action, and even happiness according to the guṇas (chapters 17 and 18). The sage who has transcended the three modes is called a guṇātīta.

Ayurveda and everyday application

In Ayurveda, diet and lifestyle are analysed by their guṇic qualities: sattvic foods are fresh, light, and nourishing; rajasic foods are stimulating and spicy; tamasic foods are stale, heavy, or processed. Yogic practice aims to cultivate sattva as a basis for meditation and self-realisation, and ultimately to go beyond all three modes toward liberation (moksha).
