Jiaohe

Jiaohe (Chinese: 交河; pinyin: Jiāohé), also known by its Uyghur name Yarkhoto, is a ruined ancient city on a leaf-shaped plateau between two river valleys about 10 kilometres west of Turpan in Xinjiang, China. Founded by the Jushi people and occupied from roughly the 2nd century BCE until its destruction by Mongol invasion in the 13th century, Jiaohe was a frontier garrison town of China's Han and Tang dynasties and later a centre of the Uyghur Qocho (Gaochang) kingdom. Because the city was built by digging into the raw loess earth rather than by laying bricks, it survives as one of the best-preserved earthen ruin cities in the world, and since 2014 it has formed part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing "Silk Roads: the Routes Network of Chang'an-Tianshan Corridor."

History

The name Jiaohe means "where rivers join" (or "river junction"), describing the twin streams that carve its island-plateau and make it a natural fortress. The site was the capital of the Anterior Jushi (Cheshi) kingdom, whose people spoke Tocharian, and came under Chinese influence when the Han dynasty contested the Tarim Basin with the Xiongnu. In 60 BCE the Han established the Protectorate of the Western Regions; Jiaohe became a military-agricultural colony (tuntian) and the seat of the Wu and Ji colonels who administered the region. The city passed between Chinese, Turkic, and Tibetan control during the centuries of division, returning to Chinese rule under the Tang dynasty, which made it a county seat under the Anxi Protectorate in the 7th century. After the Tang withdrawal it belonged to the Uyghur Qocho kingdom, a Buddhist (and later partly Manichaean and Church of the East) state, until Mongol attacks in the 13th century destroyed and finally emptied the city.

The site

Jiaohe's builders carved dwellings, temples, and streets downward into the compacted loess of the plateau, supplementing dug-out structures with rammed-earth walls. A main street runs the length of the island; archaeologists have identified over 300 structures including residential quarters of courtyard houses dug below plateau level, government offices, workshops, watchtowers, and a large Buddhist temple district in the northwest with a main hall, stupa court, and a great central stupa. Avenues of poplars today mark the edges of the plateau, which stands about 30 metres above the surrounding riverbeds. The dry Turpan climate preserved the earthwork city to a remarkable degree, though explorer-archaeologists such as Aurel Stein removed manuscripts and artefacts in the early twentieth century, and a cemetery area yielded mummified remains and documents shedding light on Jushi and Tang frontier life.

Preservation and visiting

Jiaohe is protected as a national key cultural relic of China and is open to visitors as an open-air archaeological park, reached from Turpan together with nearby Gaocheng (Qocho) ruins, the Bezeklik Thousand Buddha Caves, and the Flaming Mountains. Conservation focuses on stabilising the fragile loess against wind and the rare but destructive rainstorms. Its 2014 inscription on the UNESCO World Heritage List recognised Jiaohe as an outstanding example of an earthen Silk Road city and of Chinese frontier urbanism.
