In mathematics, a Diophantine equation is an equation, typically a polynomial equation in two or more unknowns with integer coefficients, for which only integer solutions are of interest. The word Diophantine refers to the Hellenistic mathematician Diophantus of Alexandria (3rd century AD), who studied such equations in his Arithmetica and was one of the first to introduce symbolism into algebra.

Examples

Linear Diophantine equations such as ax + by = c have solutions exactly when c is a multiple of gcd(a, b). The equation x² + y² = z² describes Pythagorean triples. Pell's equation x² − Dy² = 1 has infinitely many integer solutions for non-square D. Fermat's Last Theorem asserted that xⁿ + yⁿ = zⁿ has no positive integer solutions for n > 2 — proven only in 1994 by Andrew Wiles.

Hilbert's tenth problem

In 1900 David Hilbert asked for a general algorithm to decide whether any given Diophantine equation has integer solutions. Work by Martin Davis, Hilary Putnam, Julia Robinson, and Yuri Matiyasevich culminated in 1970 with the MRDP theorem: no such algorithm exists; the set of Diophantine equations is undecidable in general.

Diophantine analysis

The study of Diophantine equations — Diophantine analysis — asks how many solutions exist, whether solutions can be found explicitly, and how solutions distribute. Modern approaches use algebraic geometry, as in the study of rational points on elliptic curves and Faltings's theorem.
