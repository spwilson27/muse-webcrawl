Funding Act of 1790

The Funding Act of 1790, the full title of which is An Act making provision for the [payment of the] Debt of the United States, was enacted by the United States Congress on August 4, 1790. The act implemented Alexander Hamilton's plan, presented in his First Report on the Public Credit, for the federal government to assume and fund the debts incurred by the states during the American Revolutionary War, as well as the domestic and foreign debt of the Confederation.

Background

By 1790 the United States carried a large burden of debt: a foreign debt owed chiefly to France and the Netherlands, a domestic debt held in certificates and loan office paper, and substantial state debts. Much of this paper traded at deep discounts, and the credit of the new government was untested. Secretary of the Treasury Alexander Hamilton argued that funding the debt at par — paying interest regularly and exchanging old certificates for new federal securities — would establish public credit, encourage investment, and bind creditors to the success of the Union.

Provisions

The act authorised the President to borrow up to $12,000,000 to discharge arrears of interest and instalments of the foreign debt, and provided for subscriptions to a new domestic loan into which holders of existing certificates could exchange them for new stock bearing interest at reduced rates, partly deferred. It also authorised the assumption of state debts up to $21,500,000, with quotas assigned to each state. The assumption provision was highly controversial and was passed only as part of the Compromise of 1790, in which the permanent capital was sited on the Potomac River in exchange for southern votes.

Legacy

The Funding Act established the financial architecture of the early republic. Together with the charter of the First Bank of the United States and the federal excise, it formed the core of Hamilton's financial program and laid the groundwork for American public credit.
