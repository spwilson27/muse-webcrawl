Ralliement créditiste

The Ralliement créditiste (Créditiste Rally) was a provincial political party in Quebec, Canada, active from 1970 to 1978. It was the Quebec provincial wing of the Social Credit movement, which advocated monetary reform based on social credit theory. The party grew out of the Ralliement national and the Quebec organisation of the Social Credit Party of Canada, and was led first by Camil Samson.

History

Quebec's créditiste movement had roots in the 1930s–40s, when social credit ideas spread among French Canadians through figures such as Louis Even and the Pilgrims of Saint Michael. Réal Caouette led Quebec Social Credit candidates to federal success in the 1960s. Provincially, the movement organised as the Ralliement créditiste for the 1970 Quebec election, winning 12 seats with about 11 percent of the vote under Camil Samson's colourful leadership — a surprise breakthrough during a period of realignment in Quebec politics.

Decline

Internal divisions soon weakened the party. In 1972 dissidents under Yvon Dupuis formed the short-lived Parti créditiste, which later reunited with the Ralliement. In the 1973 election the party was reduced to two seats, and in 1976 it won a single seat (Fabien Roy in Beauce-Sud). Roy broke away to found the Parti national populaire. The Ralliement créditiste contested its last election in 1976 and was dissolved by 1978, as the rise of the Parti Québécois and the refashioned Union Nationale reshaped the provincial landscape.

Ideology

The party combined social credit monetary doctrines with social conservatism, Quebec nationalism of a traditionalist kind, and defence of rural and small-town interests, drawing support especially from regions such as Beauce and Abitibi.
