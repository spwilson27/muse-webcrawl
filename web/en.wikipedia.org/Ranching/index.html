Ranching

ActivityRaising livestock on grazing land
Main animalsCattle, sheep, horses; also bison, goats, alpacas
ProductsBeef, wool, hides, breeding stock

Ranching is the practice of raising herds of animals on large tracts of land. Ranchers commonly raise grazing animals such as cattle and sheep, though some also raise elk, bison, ostriches, emus and alpacas. The ranching and livestock industries grew alongside the expansion of railroads and markets for beef, hides and wool, and they remain central to the rural economies of the western United States, the Canadian prairies, Mexico, Argentina, Australia and South Africa.

Practices

Unlike intensive animal farming, ranching relies on animals harvesting forage from rangeland — native grassland, shrubland or desert — with relatively low inputs of labour and capital per animal. Herds are typically bred and raised on the ranch itself (cow–calf operations), then sold to feedlots for finishing or directly for slaughter. Seasonal work includes branding, castration, vaccination, weaning and moving herds between pastures, traditionally done on horseback with the help of dogs.

Environmental issues

Grazing management is the central environmental question in ranching. Poorly managed grazing can degrade soil, riparian areas and biodiversity, while well-managed grazing can maintain grassland ecosystems that evolved with large herbivores. Debates continue over public-lands grazing fees and permits, predator control (especially of wolves and coyotes), water use, and the greenhouse-gas footprint of ruminant livestock.

History

Ranching in the Americas began with Spanish missions and haciendas, spread through the vaquero tradition of northern Mexico, and reached its iconic form in the post-Civil War cattle boom of the Great Plains, followed by fenced ranching in the 20th century and today's increasingly consolidated beef industry.
