Cognitive linguistics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cognitive linguistics is an interdisciplinary branch of linguistics that investigates language as an integral part of human cognition. It holds that linguistic knowledge emerges from general cognitive abilities and language use, rather than from a specialised, autonomous language module, and that meaning is grounded in perception, bodily experience (embodiment) and conceptualisation. The approach arose in the 1970s and 1980s, in part as a reaction against the formalist assumptions of generative grammar.

Core commitments

Cognitive linguists commonly endorse three guiding hypotheses:

  • Language is not an autonomous cognitive faculty: grammar draws on general processes such as categorisation, imagery, analogy and attention.
  • Grammar is conceptualisation: grammatical structures symbolise ways of construing experience, so there is no sharp division between syntax, semantics and the lexicon.
  • Knowledge of language emerges from language use: usage-based models hold that speakers abstract patterns from experienced utterances, with frequency and entrenchment shaping grammar.

Major theories and concepts

  • Cognitive grammar (Ronald Langacker): grammar as inventories of symbolic units; concepts such as profiling, trajector/landmark alignment and construal.
  • Conceptual metaphor theory (George Lakoff and Mark Johnson, Metaphors We Live By, 1980): everyday thought is structured by systematic mappings from concrete source domains (e.g. journeys) to abstract targets (e.g. life, arguments).
  • Construction grammar: language as a network of learned form–meaning pairings ("constructions") at all levels of complexity.
  • Prototype theory and radial categories: categories organised around central exemplars with graded membership, following Eleanor Rosch's work.
  • Frame semantics (Charles Fillmore), mental spaces and conceptual blending (Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner), and image schemas such as CONTAINER, PATH and FORCE.

Methods and criticism

Cognitive linguists use introspective analysis alongside corpus linguistics, psycholinguistic experiments, gesture studies and neuroscience. Critics from the generative tradition have questioned the falsifiability of some claims and whether usage-based accounts can explain children's rapid acquisition (the "poverty of the stimulus" debate); proponents respond with computational modelling and empirical acquisition research.
