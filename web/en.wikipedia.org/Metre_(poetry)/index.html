Metre (poetry)
DefinitionThe regular rhythmic structure of a line of verse
UnitThe foot (in stress- and syllable-based systems)

Metre (American spelling meter) in poetry is the basic rhythmic structure of a verse or lines in verse. Many traditional forms of poetry prescribe a specific metre, usually defined by patterns of stressed and unstressed syllables (in English and similar languages), by syllable count (in French and Japanese poetry), or by syllable weight — long and short (in classical Greek, Latin, Arabic, and Sanskrit poetry). The study and use of metre is called prosody.

Feet and line lengths

The basic unit of metre is the foot. Common feet in English include:

  • Iamb — unstressed + stressed (da-DUM)
  • Trochee — stressed + unstressed (DUM-da)
  • Anapaest — two unstressed + stressed (da-da-DUM)
  • Dactyl — stressed + two unstressed (DUM-da-da)
  • Spondee — two stressed syllables

Lines are named by the number of feet: monometer (1), dimeter (2), trimeter (3), tetrameter (4), pentameter (5), hexameter (6). Iambic pentameter — five iambs per line — is the dominant metre of English verse, used by Chaucer, Shakespeare (in blank verse), and Milton. Classical epic used dactylic hexameter; French classical verse used the twelve-syllable alexandrine.

Practice and variation

Skilled poets vary the underlying pattern with substitutions, caesuras (pauses within a line), enjambment, and feminine endings, producing expressive tension between metre and natural speech rhythm. The systematic analysis of these variations is called scansion. In the twentieth century much poetry abandoned regular metre for free verse, though metrical forms remain in continuous use.
