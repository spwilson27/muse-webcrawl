Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ

Abū Muhammad ʿAbd Allāh Rūzbih ibn Dāduya (c. 721–757/759), known as Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, was a Persian writer and translator of the early Abbasid period, regarded as one of the founders of Arabic literary prose. Born in Gor (modern Firuzabad, Iran) to a Zoroastrian family, he converted to Islam and served as a secretary (kātib) under Umayyad and Abbasid governors in Iraq.

Works

His most famous work is Kalīla wa-Dimna, an Arabic version of the Indian animal fables of the Panchatantra, rendered from a Middle Persian translation. The book became a classic of world literature and a model of Arabic style, transmitting the fables across the Islamic world and, through later translations, into Europe. He also wrote works of adab and political counsel, including al-Adab al-Kabīr and the Risāla fī al-Ṣaḥāba, a memorandum on governance addressed to the caliph al-Manṣūr.

Death and legacy

Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ was executed in Basra around 757–759, reportedly at the instigation of the city's governor, amid suspicions of heresy (zandaqa) and the political fallout of his drafting of a letter of safe-conduct for a rebel. His elegant, clear prose style profoundly influenced the development of Arabic literature and the secretarial tradition.
