Prisoner-of-war camp

A prisoner-of-war camp (often shortened to POW camp) is a site for the containment of enemy fighters captured by a belligerent power in time of war. Conditions, organization, and treatment vary widely, but since 1929 they have been regulated by the Geneva Conventions, most comprehensively by the Third Geneva Convention of 1949.

Legal framework

The Geneva Conventions require that prisoners be treated humanely, supplied with adequate food, shelter, and medical care, protected from violence and public curiosity, and allowed to correspond with families and receive visits from the International Committee of the Red Cross. Prisoners may be required to give only name, rank, date of birth, and serial number.

In the World Wars

Both world wars saw vast camp systems: German camps such as Stalags (for enlisted men) and Oflags (for officers) in the Second World War, notorious escape attempts such as the "Great Escape" from Stalag Luft III, and Japanese and Soviet camps where mortality was extremely high. Camps also existed in the American Civil War, such as Andersonville, infamous for its death rate.
