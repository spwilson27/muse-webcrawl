Barolo

Barolo is a red wine produced in the Piedmont region of northwestern Italy from the Nebbiolo grape. It is one of Italy's best-known and most prestigious wines, frequently called "the king of wines" and "the wine of kings". Barolo was granted DOC status in 1966 and was among the first wines elevated to DOCG status in 1980.

Production

The production zone covers 11 communes in the Langhe hills southeast of Alba, including Barolo itself, La Morra, Monforte d'Alba, Serralunga d'Alba and Castiglione Falletto. Regulations require 100% Nebbiolo, a minimum of 38 months' ageing (at least 18 in wood) before release, and 62 months for wine labelled Riserva. The vineyards of the zone, with neighbouring Barbaresco, were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2014 as part of the Vineyard Landscape of Piedmont: Langhe-Roero and Monferrato.

Character

Barolo is pale garnet in colour, with high tannins and acidity, alcohol typically 13–15%, and characteristic aromas often described as "tar and roses", along with dried cherry, leather, liquorice and white truffle. Its structure makes it highly age-worthy; fine vintages improve for decades. In the 1980s the so-called "Barolo Wars" divided traditionalists, using long macerations and large Slavonian oak casks, from modernists favouring shorter macerations, barriques and earlier-drinking wines.

History

The modern dry style of Barolo was created in the mid-nineteenth century, associated with Giulia Falletti, Marchioness of Barolo, and the winemakers Paolo Francesco Staglieno and Louis Oudart, replacing an earlier sweet, sparkling wine.
