MMR vaccine controversy

The MMR vaccine controversy was a public health scandal and media-driven scare that began in 1998 with the publication of a fraudulent research paper in The Lancet linking the combined measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine to autism and bowel disease in children.

Course of the controversy

At a press conference accompanying the paper, lead author Andrew Wakefield suggested using single vaccines instead of the triple MMR jab. Media coverage was intense and often treated the claim as an open scientific question. MMR vaccination rates in the UK fell from about 92% to below 80% by 2003–04, and measles, declared eliminated in Britain, became endemic again by 2008.

Investigation and retraction

Investigative reporting by Brian Deer in The Sunday Times and the BMJ — which called the study an "elaborate fraud" — showed that Wakefield had been funded by lawyers preparing litigation against vaccine manufacturers and that patient data had been misrepresented. The UK General Medical Council found Wakefield dishonest and irresponsible in 2010; The Lancet retracted the paper, and Wakefield lost his medical licence. Litigation over the alleged vaccine injury collapsed for lack of evidence, and large studies consistently found no link.
