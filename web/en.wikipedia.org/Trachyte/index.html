Trachyte

Trachyte is an extrusive igneous rock, the volcanic equivalent of syenite, composed chiefly of alkali feldspar (sanidine or anorthoclase) with minor amounts of plagioclase, quartz, and mafic minerals such as biotite, amphibole, or pyroxene. Its name comes from the Greek trachys, "rough", referring to the typically rough, uneven texture of broken surfaces, produced by the flow-aligned arrangement of small feldspar laths known as trachytic texture.

Characteristics

Trachytes are generally light-coloured — white, grey, or pinkish — and fine-grained, often with visible phenocrysts of feldspar in a dense groundmass. Chemically they are intermediate to felsic but distinguished by high alkali content (sodium and potassium) and low quartz; varieties with more quartz approach rhyolite, while strongly undersaturated varieties grade into phonolite. Trachytic magmas are viscous and tend to form short, thick lava flows and domes.

Occurrence

Trachyte occurs in continental rift and oceanic-island settings, including the Eifel region of Germany, the Auvergne in France (where the Puy de Dôme is a trachyte dome), the Azores, and parts of the East African Rift. On Easter Island, trachyte was one of the stones used by the Rapa Nui. Trachyte has historically been used as a building and paving stone in parts of Germany and Italy.
