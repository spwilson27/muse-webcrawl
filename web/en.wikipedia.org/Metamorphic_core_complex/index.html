Metamorphic core complex

Metamorphic core complex
FieldStructural geology, tectonics
Key featureLow-angle detachment fault
Classic regionBasin and Range, western USA

Metamorphic core complexes are exposures of deep crust exhumed in association with largely amagmatic extension. They are a zone of high ductile strain, roughly dome-shaped, overlain by a low-angle normal fault — a detachment fault — that accommodated tens of kilometres of horizontal extension. Beneath the detachment, mylonitic and gneissic rocks of the lower plate record deformation at depth; above it, brittlely faulted upper-plate rocks are typically unmetamorphosed or only weakly metamorphosed.

Formation

Core complexes form during large-magnitude continental extension, when deep, hot middle-crustal rocks are drawn upward and exposed as the upper crust is pulled away along the detachment. Rolling-hinge and isostatic rebound models explain their domal geometry. The concept was developed in the 1970s and 1980s from studies of the Basin and Range Province of the western United States, notably by geologists such as George Davis, John Bartley, and Brian Wernicke.

Examples

Classic examples include the Snake Range in Nevada, the Whipple Mountains in California, the Ruby Mountains–East Humboldt Range in Nevada, and the D'Entrecasteaux Islands of Papua New Guinea, where core complexes are actively forming. Similar structures are recognised in the Aegean Sea, the North China Craton, and along the Atlantic margins.
