Extraterritoriality

Extraterritoriality is the state of being exempted from the jurisdiction of local law, usually as the result of diplomatic negotiations. Historically it applied to individuals and groups; in modern usage it also describes states applying their laws beyond their territory, and the immunities enjoyed by diplomats and foreign military forces.

Historical practice

Extraterritorial privileges were a hallmark of the "unequal treaties" imposed on Asian states in the nineteenth century. Under the Treaty of Wanghia (1844) and the Treaty of Tientsin (1858), American and European nationals in China were tried in consular courts under their home countries' laws rather than Chinese law; similar capitulations applied in the Ottoman Empire, Siam, Persia, and Japan. Japan abolished the system in 1899 after modernizing its legal codes, while extraterritoriality in China ended only in 1943, when the United States and Britain relinquished their rights by treaty.

Modern forms

Today the concept survives in diplomatic immunity under the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), in status-of-forces agreements governing troops stationed abroad, and in the extraterritorial reach of statutes such as the United States' Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the European Union's data-protection regulation, which can apply to conduct outside the enacting state's territory. Such assertions of jurisdiction remain a recurring source of international friction.
