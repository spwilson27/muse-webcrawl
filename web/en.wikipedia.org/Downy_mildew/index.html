Downy mildew

Downy mildew
Causal organismsOomycetes, order Peronosporales
Important generaPlasmopara, Peronospora, Bremia, Pseudoperonospora
Key hostsGrape, lettuce, cucurbits, onion, tobacco, soybean, basil
Famous speciesPlasmopara viticola (grape downy mildew)

Downy mildew refers to any of several types of disease caused by oomycetes ("water molds") in the order Peronosporales that parasitise plants. Downy mildews colonise plant tissue and appear characteristically as yellow to pale patches on the upper surface of leaves, with a greyish, downy growth of sporangia on the underside in humid conditions. Despite the name, they are not true fungi but relatives of brown algae, differing from fungi in having cellulose cell walls and motile zoospores.

Biology

Under cool, wet conditions the pathogens produce wind-borne sporangia that release swimming zoospores, which encyst near stomata and infect. Sexual oospores formed in host tissue can overwinter in soil and debris. Epidemics therefore explode during periods of leaf wetness — the classic forecast rule for grape downy mildew requires specific combinations of temperature and rain to trigger sprays.

Agricultural importance

Downy mildews are among the most destructive plant diseases. Plasmopara viticola, accidentally introduced from North America to Europe in the 1870s, devastated vineyards until controlled by the copper-based Bordeaux mixture, discovered by Millardet in 1885 and a founding moment of chemical plant protection. Other major diseases include lettuce downy mildew (Bremia lactucae), cucurbit downy mildew (Pseudoperonospora cubensis), onion downy mildew, tobacco blue mold (Peronospora tabacina), and basil downy mildew, which emerged globally in the 2000s.

Control

Management combines resistant cultivars, canopy and irrigation practices that shorten leaf wetness, and fungicides — protectant copper and multisite products, and systemic oomycete-specific compounds such as phenylamides and QoI fungicides, against which resistance readily evolves. Downy mildew should not be confused with powdery mildew, caused by true fungi of the order Erysiphales, whose white growth develops mainly on upper leaf surfaces.
