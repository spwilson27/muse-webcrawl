The Scheldt (French: Escaut; Dutch: Schelde) is a 360-kilometre river in northern France, western Belgium, and the southwestern Netherlands. It rises near Gouy in the department of Aisne, flows through Cambrai, Valenciennes, Tournai, and Ghent, and passes Antwerp—one of Europe's largest ports—before reaching the North Sea through the Westerschelde estuary near Flushing (Vlissingen).

Course and estuary

Below Ghent the river becomes tidal. At Antwerp it is a broad navigable waterway; downstream, it divides around islands in the province of Zeeland. Historically the Scheldt reached the sea through several mouths, including the Oosterschelde, but since the nineteenth century only the Westerschelde has served as the main shipping channel, while the Oosterschelde was partly closed by the Delta Works after the North Sea flood of 1953.

History

Control of the Scheldt estuary was a strategic and commercial prize for centuries. After the fall of Antwerp in 1585, the Dutch Republic closed the river to shipping—a closure confirmed by the Peace of Münster in 1648—which crippled Antwerp's trade for two centuries. The river was reopened under French revolutionary occupation in the 1790s, and free navigation was definitively arranged by treaties in the nineteenth century. In 1944 the estuary was the scene of the Battle of the Scheldt, in which Canadian and Allied forces cleared German defences so that Antwerp's port could supply the Allied advance into Germany.

Economy and environment

The Scheldt basin is densely populated and industrialized. The ports of Antwerp, Ghent, and Vlissingen-Terneuzen form a major maritime cluster. The estuary is a protected Natura 2000 site known for its tidal mudflats and salt marshes; deepening projects for larger container ships have long been balanced against ecological concerns shared by Belgium and the Netherlands.
