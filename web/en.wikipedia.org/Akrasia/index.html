Akrasia

Akrasia (Greek: ἀκρασία, "lacking command" or "weakness", occasionally transliterated as acrasia or Anglicised as acrasy) is a lack of mental strength or of willpower, or the tendency to act against one's better judgment. The opposite attitude is enkrateia, a state sometimes called continence or self-control.

Classical accounts

The problem of akrasia was central to Socratic moral philosophy: Socrates, as presented in Plato's dialogues, denied that anyone could knowingly act against what they believe to be best, treating apparent weakness of will as a failure of knowledge. Aristotle gave a more permissive account in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics, analysing how appetite can override rational choice.

Modern philosophy

Donald Davidson's essay "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?" (1969) revived the topic in analytic philosophy, arguing that akratic action is both intentional and irrational. Later writers debate whether akrasia is genuinely possible, whether it differs from compulsion, and how it relates to addiction and self-deception.
