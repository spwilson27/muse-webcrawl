Federal jurisdiction (United States)

Federal courts exercise jurisdiction over cases defined by the Constitution and Congress.

In the United States, federal jurisdiction refers to the authority of the federal courts to hear and decide cases. Unlike state courts, which are courts of general jurisdiction, federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction: they may hear only the categories of cases authorised by Article III of the U.S. Constitution and by statutes enacted by Congress.

Categories

The principal heads of federal subject-matter jurisdiction are federal question jurisdiction (cases arising under the Constitution, federal laws or treaties) and diversity jurisdiction (cases between citizens of different states, or between citizens and foreign nationals, where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000). Other categories include admiralty and maritime cases, bankruptcy, patent and copyright cases, cases in which the United States is a party, and cases between states.

Concurrent and exclusive jurisdiction

Many cases may be brought in either state or federal court (concurrent jurisdiction), and defendants in such cases may often remove them from state to federal court. Some matters are reserved exclusively to federal courts, including federal criminal prosecutions, bankruptcy, patent, copyright and antitrust cases. Questions of personal jurisdiction over the parties, venue and standing must also be satisfied before a federal court may proceed.
