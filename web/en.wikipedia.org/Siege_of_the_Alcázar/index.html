Siege of the Alcázar

The Siege of the Alcázar (Spanish: Asedio del Alcázar de Toledo) was a highly symbolic victory for the Nationalist faction during the Spanish Civil War. It occurred at the Alcázar of Toledo, a fortress that housed the Toledo Infantry Academy, in July and August 1936, immediately after the military uprising that began the war.

Forces loyal to the Spanish Republic besieged the fortress, which was held by a mixed force of Civil Guards, army cadets, soldiers, and Falangist and monarchist volunteers under the command of Colonel José Moscardó. Inside the fortress were also hundreds of women and children, the families of the defenders. The siege ended on 27 September 1936 when Nationalist troops of the Army of Africa, commanded by General José Enrique Varela, relieved the garrison.

Background

The uprising of 17–18 July 1936 found Toledo's military governor, Colonel Moscardó, in control of the Alcázar, which served as an infantry academy. After declaring a state of war on 21 July in support of the rebels, Moscardó and his men were driven back into the fortress by superior Republican forces, including militia columns and troops loyal to the government in Madrid.

The siege

Within days the defenders were cut off and besieged. The Republican forces, unable to take the heavily built fortress by direct assault, subjected it to artillery bombardment and aerial bombing; much of the building was reduced to rubble. Attempts were also made to undermine the fortress with mining, and in September two large mines were detonated under its towers. Nevertheless, the garrison held out, rationing food and water and enduring severe deprivation.

The Moscardó telephone call

The most famous episode of the siege was a telephone conversation on 23 July 1936 between Moscardó and a Republican militia chief, who threatened that Moscardó's captured 16-year-old son, Luis, would be shot if the Alcázar did not surrender within ten minutes. Moscardó asked to speak to his son and, according to the Nationalist account, told him: "Commend your soul to God, shout 'Viva España!' and die like a hero." Luis was in fact shot a month later, in reprisal for an air raid. The episode became central to Nationalist propaganda and the cult of the Alcázar's defenders.

Relief and aftermath

General Francisco Franco diverted the Army of Africa from its drive on Madrid to relieve Toledo, a decision of propaganda value though arguably costly strategically. On 27 September, Varela's columns entered Toledo and lifted the siege; the surviving defenders, emaciated and exhausted, were famously paraded before Franco. The ruins of the Alcázar were preserved as a Nationalist shrine, and the episode—popularized in numerous books and the 1940 film Sin novedad en el Alcázar—became one of the founding myths of Francoist Spain.
