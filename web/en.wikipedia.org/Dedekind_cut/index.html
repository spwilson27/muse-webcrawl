Dedekind cut

In mathematics, Dedekind cuts, named after German mathematician Richard Dedekind (but previously considered by Joseph Bertrand), are а method of constructing the real numbers from the rational numbers. A Dedekind cut is a partition of the rational numbers into two sets A and B, such that all elements of A are less than all elements of B, and A contains no greatest element.

Definition

A cut is a subset A of the rationals that is nonempty, not all of the rationals, closed downwards (if x is in A and y < x, then y is in A), and has no largest element. The set B of remaining rationals may or may not have a smallest element; if it does, the cut corresponds to that rational number, and if it does not, the cut defines a new, irrational number. The cut for the square root of 2, for example, takes A to be the rationals whose square is less than 2 or which are negative.

Construction of the reals

Identifying each real number with the cut it determines gives a complete ordered field: arithmetic operations can be defined on cuts, and every nonempty set of cuts bounded above has a least upper bound, filling the "gaps" in the rationals. Dedekind published the idea in his 1872 pamphlet Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen ("Continuity and irrational numbers"), independently of but at about the same time as constructions by Cantor and others.

Generalizations

The idea generalises to cuts in any totally ordered set, and the Dedekind–MacNeille completion extends it to partially ordered sets, embedding a poset into a complete lattice.
