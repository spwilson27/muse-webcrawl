The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) is a civil rights law of the United States that prohibits discrimination based on disability. Modelled in part on the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, it guarantees people with disabilities equal opportunity in employment, public services, public accommodations and telecommunications. It is regarded as the most comprehensive disability rights legislation in the world at the time of its passage.

President George H. W. Bush signing the ADA on the South Lawn of the White House, 26 July 1990.

Background

The disability rights movement built momentum through the 1970s and 1980s, winning Section 504's ban on disability discrimination by federal recipients and pushing for broader protections. Activists staged high-profile protests, including the 1990 "Capitol Crawl", in which wheelchair users pulled themselves up the steps of the U.S. Capitol to dramatise architectural barriers. After years of negotiation with business groups, Congress passed the ADA with broad bipartisan support, and President George H. W. Bush signed it on 26 July 1990.

Structure

The Act is divided into five titles:

  • Title I — Employment: employers with 15 or more employees must not discriminate against qualified individuals with disabilities and must provide reasonable accommodations, unless doing so causes undue hardship.
  • Title II — Public services: state and local governments must make their programmes and public transport accessible.
  • Title III — Public accommodations: businesses open to the public — shops, restaurants, hotels, theatres and more — must remove architectural barriers where readily achievable and provide auxiliary aids; new construction must be accessible.
  • Title IV — Telecommunications: telephone companies must provide relay services for people with hearing or speech disabilities.
  • Title V — Miscellaneous: protections against retaliation, and provisions on insurance and other statutes.

The ADA defines disability as a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities, a record of such an impairment, or being regarded as having one. After Supreme Court decisions narrowed that definition, the ADA Amendments Act of 2008 restored a broader interpretation.

Impact

The ADA transformed the built environment in the United States — wheelchair ramps, accessible toilets, Braille signage and curb cuts became standard — and influenced disability legislation internationally, including the framework leading to the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Debates continue over its effects on employment rates for disabled people and over its application to websites and digital services.
