Hubble sequence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hubble sequence is a scheme for classifying galaxies according to their visual appearance, invented by Edwin Hubble in 1926 and presented in his 1936 book The Realm of the Nebulae. Because its diagram resembles a tuning fork, it is often called the Hubble tuning-fork diagram.

The scheme

Elliptical galaxies lie along the handle of the fork, classed E0 (spherical) to E7 (highly flattened) by their apparent ellipticity. The two prongs are formed by spiral galaxies: normal spirals (S) and barred spirals (SB), each running from tightly wound arms with large central bulges (Sa, SBa) to loosely wound arms with small bulges (Sc, SBc). Lenticular galaxies (S0), with a disc but no spiral arms, sit at the junction of the fork. Galaxies that fit none of these categories are classed irregular (Irr).

Significance and refinements

Hubble cautiously called the left of the diagram “early” and the right “late”, terminology that survives even though the sequence is not an evolutionary track. Later astronomers — notably Gérard de Vaucouleurs — expanded the system with rings, intermediate spiral types and finer bars. Morphological type correlates strongly with colour, star-formation rate and gas content: ellipticals are generally red and gas-poor, spirals blue and star-forming. The scheme remains the most widely used shorthand for galaxy morphology.
