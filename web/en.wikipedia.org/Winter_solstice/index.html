Winter solstice

The winter solstice is the moment when one of Earth's poles reaches its maximum tilt away from the Sun, producing the shortest day and longest night of the year for that hemisphere. It occurs in December in the Northern Hemisphere (around December 21–22) and in June in the Southern Hemisphere (around June 20–21).

Astronomy

Because Earth's axis is tilted about 23.4° relative to its orbital plane, at the December solstice the North Pole points furthest from the Sun; the Sun appears at its southernmost point, directly overhead at noon at the Tropic of Capricorn. Above the Arctic Circle the Sun does not rise at all (polar night), while the Antarctic experiences the midnight sun. The word "solstice" derives from Latin solstitium, "the Sun stands still," referring to the brief pause before the Sun's apparent path reverses.

Cultural significance

Midwinter has been marked since Neolithic times; monuments such as Stonehenge and Newgrange are aligned to the solstice sunrise or sunset. Festivals include the Roman Saturnalia and Sol Invictus, Germanic Yule, the Chinese Dongzhi Festival, Persian Shab-e Yalda, and many modern pagan observances. Christmas, celebrated on December 25, falls close to the solstice, and several of its customs absorbed older midwinter traditions. After the solstice, days gradually lengthen toward spring.
