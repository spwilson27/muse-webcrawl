Appeal of murder

The appeal of murder was an archaic legal procedure in English common law by which the widow or next of kin of a murdered person could bring a private prosecution — an "appeal" — against the accused, even after the accused had been acquitted on a public indictment. The accused could respond by electing trial by battle, wagering physical combat against the accuser or a champion.

The Thornton case

The procedure became notorious in 1817–1818, when Abraham Thornton, acquitted of the murder of Mary Ashford, was appealed by her brother and claimed his right to trial by battle, which the family declined. The case, Ashford v Thornton, exposed the survival of the medieval remedy and caused a public outcry.

Abolition

Parliament abolished appeals of murder and other felonies, along with trial by battle in civil cases, by statute in 1819 (59 Geo. 3 c. 46). The episode is frequently cited in discussions of obsolete legal forms and the transition to a purely state-run system of criminal prosecution.
