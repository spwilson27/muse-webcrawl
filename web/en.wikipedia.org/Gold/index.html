Gold is a chemical element with the symbol Au and atomic number 79. A bright, slightly yellow, dense and highly malleable metal, it has been prized since prehistory for jewellery, coinage and art, and remains a store of value and an industrial material.

Properties

Gold is the most malleable of metals: one gram can be beaten into a sheet of one square metre or drawn into nearly three kilometres of wire. It is an excellent conductor of heat and electricity and resists corrosion and oxidation, reacting with few substances other than aqua regia and cyanide solutions. Its purity is measured in karats (24 karat being pure) or fineness.

Occurrence and production

Gold occurs in veins with quartz and as alluvial nuggets and grains; it is also extracted from seawater only in uneconomic traces. Roughly 200,000 tonnes have been mined throughout history. Leading producers include China, Australia and Russia; major deposits include South Africa's Witwatersrand basin, which has yielded a large fraction of all gold ever mined.

Uses

Beyond jewellery and central-bank reserves, gold is used in electronics (bonding wires and connectors), dentistry, aerospace coatings, and medicine, including gold salts and nanoparticles. Its monetary history — from ancient coinage to the gold standard — underpins its continuing role as a safe-haven asset.
