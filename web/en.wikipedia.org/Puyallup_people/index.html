Puyallup people

The Puyallup people (Lushootseed: spuyaləpabš, "people of the Puyallup") are a Coast Salish Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest whose traditional territory encompasses the Puyallup River valley and the shores of Commencement Bay in present-day Washington State, including the site of modern Tacoma. Today their descendants are principally enrolled in the federally recognized Puyallup Tribe of Indians.

Traditional life

Before colonization, the Puyallup lived in permanent winter villages of cedar longhouses along the river and bay, with seasonal camps for fishing, hunting, and gathering. Salmon—taken with weirs, nets, and spears at river mouths—formed the dietary and ceremonial core, supplemented by shellfish, waterfowl, deer, elk, roots, and berries. Cedar provided houses, canoes, clothing fiber, and baskets. Society was organized by kinship, with wealthy families holding potlatches to validate status and distribute goods. Their language is a dialect of Lushootseed, the language of the southern Coast Salish peoples of Puget Sound.

Contact and treaty era

Contact with Euro-American traders and settlers intensified after the founding of Fort Nisqually in 1833. In 1854 the Puyallup, together with neighboring peoples, signed the Treaty of Medicine Creek, ceding vast lands while reserving a reservation along Commencement Bay and the right to fish at "usual and accustomed" places. Disputes over the treaty's terms contributed to the Puget Sound War of 1855–56. Allotment under federal policy shattered the reservation land base, and much of it passed to non-Native ownership and to the growing city and Port of Tacoma.

Modern history

Puyallup fishers resisted state restrictions for decades, culminating in the federal Boldt decision of 1974 upholding treaty fishing rights. The 1989 settlement act compensated the tribe for port-related land claims and helped rebuild its land base and institutions. Contemporary Puyallup identity is carried forward through the tribal government, language revitalization in Lushootseed, fisheries co-management, and cultural programs centered on the river and its salmon.
