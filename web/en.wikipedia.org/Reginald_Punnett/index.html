Reginald Crundall Punnett (1875–1967) was a British geneticist who, with William Bateson, helped establish the science of genetics at the University of Cambridge. He is best remembered for the Punnett square, the diagram still used to predict the outcomes of genetic crosses.

Career

Punnett studied zoology at Cambridge and worked on marine worms before turning to Mendelian inheritance. With Bateson he reported some of the first cases of genetic linkage (in sweet peas) and epistasis (in poultry comb shape). In 1910 he became the first Professor of Genetics at Cambridge.

Work and publications

His popular book Mendelism (1905) introduced Mendel's ideas to a wide audience. When G. H. Hardy was asked why allele frequencies remain stable, the resulting letter produced the Hardy–Weinberg principle; Punnett had posed the question, reportedly over cricket conversation. Punnett also researched the genetics of poultry and butterflies.
