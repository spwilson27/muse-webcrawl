Cognitive apprenticeship

Cognitive apprenticeship is a theory of learning that extends the model of traditional apprenticeship — in which a novice learns a craft by working alongside a master — to cognitive skills such as reading, writing and mathematics. It was articulated principally by Allan Collins, John Seely Brown and Susan Newman in the late 1980s.

Rationale

In traditional apprenticeship, thinking is visible: the learner can observe the expert's processes and outcomes. In school subjects, by contrast, expert thinking is largely internal and invisible. Cognitive apprenticeship therefore aims to make thinking visible, so that students can observe, enact and refine cognitive strategies with feedback.

Methods

The framework's teaching methods are modelling (the expert performs while verbalising their reasoning), coaching (guidance and feedback as students practise), scaffolding (support that is gradually withdrawn, or "faded"), articulation (students explain their thinking), reflection (comparing one's performance with an expert's), and exploration (independent problem solving). Sequencing moves from increasing complexity and diversity to global before local skills. The theory is closely related to situated learning and legitimate peripheral participation.
