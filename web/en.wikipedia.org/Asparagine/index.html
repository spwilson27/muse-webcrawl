Asparagine

AbbreviationsAsn, N
FormulaC4H8N2O3
Molar mass132.12 g/mol
ClassificationProteinogenic amino acid; polar, uncharged
CodonsAAU, AAC

Asparagine (symbol Asn or N) is an α-amino acid used in the biosynthesis of proteins. It contains an α-amino group, an α-carboxylic acid group, and a side-chain carboxamide, making it a polar, aliphatic, non-essential amino acid in humans, who can synthesize it from oxaloacetate. It is encoded by the codons AAU and AAC.

History

Asparagine was the first amino acid to be isolated: in 1806 the French chemists Louis Nicolas Vauquelin and Pierre Jean Robiquet crystallized it from asparagus juice, from which it takes its name. It was also the first amino acid whose structure and synthesis were later established from natural sources.

Functions

Asparagine is required by the nervous system and plays a role in the synthesis of ammonia and in nitrogen transport in plants, where it is a major storage and transport compound. In proteins, asparagine residues are common sites of N-linked glycosylation, which occurs at the Asn-X-Ser/Thr sequon. With age, asparagine (and aspartate) residues spontaneously deamidate, a process used as a molecular clock of protein ageing. When heated with reducing sugars, asparagine participates in the Maillard reaction and is the precursor of acrylamide formed in fried and baked starchy foods.

Sources

Dietary sources include dairy products, meat, eggs, seafood, legumes, nuts, seeds and asparagus. The enzyme L-asparaginase, which depletes asparagine, is used in the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, whose malignant cells depend on external asparagine.
