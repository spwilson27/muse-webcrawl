Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice is the act of killing one or more humans as part of a ritual, intended as an offering to a god, spirit, or ancestor, or as a retainer sacrifice accompanying a ruler's burial. It has been practised in many cultures throughout history and is generally regarded today as an extreme form of ritual murder.

Ancient world

Retainer sacrifice — the killing of servants, concubines, or guards to accompany a dead ruler — is attested in early dynastic Egypt, the royal tombs of Ur in Mesopotamia, Shang-dynasty China, and Scythian burials. In the Mediterranean world, Phoenician and Carthaginian child sacrifice has been debated by historians, and human victims are reported in Celtic and Germanic rites by Roman authors. In several traditions, animal or symbolic offerings gradually substituted for human victims.

The Americas

The Aztec (Mexica) empire practised human sacrifice on a large scale, typically by heart extraction atop temple pyramids, feeding the blood to the sun god Huitzilopochtli to sustain the cosmos; victims were displayed on skull racks (tzompantli). Sacrifice also occurred among the Maya, the Mississippian culture (for example at Cahokia), and in the Inca empire, where capacocha ceremonies sacrificed chosen children on high mountain shrines, leaving naturally mummified remains.

Asia, Africa, and decline

Documented or reported practices include sati in India, sacrifices in Dahomey and Asante, and ritual killings in parts of Oceania. The practice declined worldwide through the spread of world religions condemning it, state suppression, and colonial-era prohibition, though isolated ritual murders motivated by sacrificial beliefs have occurred into modern times and are prosecuted as crimes.
