Grimm's law, also known as the First Germanic Sound Shift, is a set of systematic sound changes that affected all the stop consonants of Proto-Indo-European as they developed into Proto-Germanic, in the 1st millennium BC. It was described in detail by Jacob Grimm in the 1822 second edition of his Deutsche Grammatik, building on observations by the Danish philologist Rasmus Rask.

The changes

The shift proceeded in three phases: voiceless stops became voiceless fricatives (e.g. *p → f, as in Latin piscis vs. English fish); voiced stops became voiceless stops (e.g. *d → t, as in Latin duo vs. English two); and voiced fricatives became voiced stops. Apparent exceptions—in which a voiceless fricative remained voiced—were later explained by Verner's law in terms of the position of the Indo-European accent.

Significance

Grimm's law was a landmark in the development of historical linguistics: it showed that sound change could proceed with near-mechanical regularity, laying the groundwork for the Neogrammarian doctrine of exceptionless sound laws, and it established the Germanic languages as a distinct branch of the Indo-European family.
