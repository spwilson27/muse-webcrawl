Old Europe (archaeology)

Old Europe is a term coined by the archaeologist Marija Gimbutas to describe a relatively homogeneous pre-Indo-European Neolithic and Chalcolithic culture in southeastern Europe, roughly 7000–3500 BCE (also 9,000–5,500 years ago). In her interpretation these societies were sedentary farming communities, characterised by large tell settlements, sophisticated pottery and early metallurgy, and a religion centred on female deities.

Cultures

Old Europe encompassed cultures such as the Vinča culture of Serbia, the Cucuteni–Trypillia culture of Romania, Moldova, and Ukraine (famous for its very large settlements and painted pottery), the Varna culture of Bulgaria (whose necropolis contains some of the world's oldest worked gold), and the Gumelnița culture. These societies practised early copper and gold metallurgy and used enigmatic sign systems, such as the Vinča symbols, which Gimbutas interpreted as a form of script.

Gimbutas's interpretation

Gimbutas argued that Old European society was peaceful, egalitarian, and "gynocentric", in contrast to the patriarchal, warlike Proto-Indo-European ("Kurgan") pastoralists who, in her Kurgan hypothesis, invaded from the Pontic steppe in waves between about 4400 and 2800 BCE and transformed Europe. Her broader claims about a goddess-centred civilisation have been criticised by many archaeologists as speculative, although the steppe-migration model itself has gained support from ancient DNA research, and the material sophistication of the Balkan Chalcolithic is widely acknowledged.
