Shoshone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Shoshone encampment in the Wind River mountains]

The Shoshone (Shoshoni: Newe, "People") are a Native American tribe with four large cultural and linguistic divisions: the Eastern Shoshone of Wyoming, the Northern Shoshone of eastern Idaho, the Western Shoshone of Nevada and Utah, and the Goshute of western Utah and eastern Nevada. Their traditional homelands spanned the Great Basin and Rocky Mountains, and their language belongs to the Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family, closely related to Comanche and Ute.

The Shoshone are famous in American history through Sacagawea, the Lemhi Shoshone woman who guided the Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806); her brother Cameahwait was a chief of her band. The Eastern Shoshone under Chief Washakie were allies of the United States and received the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming under treaties of 1863 and 1868.

Culture and history

Western Shoshone and Goshute groups practised a desert way of life based on seeds, roots, jackrabbits, and communal drives, while Eastern bands adopted horses and bison hunting in the 18th century. Conflicts of the 1860s, including the Bear River Massacre of 1863 in which U.S. troops killed hundreds of Northwestern Shoshone, devastated many communities.

Today federally recognised Shoshone tribes include the Eastern Shoshone of the Wind River Reservation, the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes of Fort Hall, Idaho, and numerous Western Shoshone bands and colonies in Nevada. Efforts to revitalise the Shoshoni language continue in tribal schools.
