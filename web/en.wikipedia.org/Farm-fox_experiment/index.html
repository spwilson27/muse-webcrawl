Farm-fox experiment

The farm-fox experiment is a long-running study of the evolutionary and genetic effects of domestication, conducted since 1959 at the Institute of Cytology and Genetics in Novosibirsk, Russia (then the Soviet Union). Initiated by geneticist Dmitry Belyayev and continued by Lyudmila Trut, the experiment selectively bred silver foxes (Vulpes vulpes) solely for tameness—and observed the emergence of traits associated with domestication.

Design

Starting from foxes raised on fur farms, the researchers each generation selected the animals least fearful and aggressive toward humans for breeding, using a standardised hand-feeding and handling test. A control population was bred without selection for comparison.

Results

Within about ten generations, selected foxes began to seek human contact—whimpering, tail-wagging and licking handlers. By the 2010s, a large majority of the experimental population was classified as "domesticated elite". Strikingly, the tame foxes also developed physical changes typical of domestication syndrome:

  • Floppy ears and curled tails
  • Piebald (spotted) coat colouration
  • Shorter snouts and smaller teeth
  • Changes in reproductive timing (breeding outside the normal season)
  • Lower levels of stress hormones (cortisol)

A parallel line selected for aggression produced foxes that fiercely attacked humans.

Significance

The experiment is a key evidence base in the study of how selection on behaviour alone can drive broad developmental and hormonal change, informing accounts of the domestication of dogs and other animals. It is popularised in the book How to Tame a Fox (and Build a Dog) (2017) by Trut and Lee Alan Dugatkin.
