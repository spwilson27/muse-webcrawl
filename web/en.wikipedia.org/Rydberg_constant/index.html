The Rydberg constant, symbol R∞, is a physical constant relating to the electromagnetic spectrum of an atom. Named after the Swedish physicist Johannes Rydberg, it appears in the Rydberg formula, published in 1888, which predicts the wavelengths of the spectral lines of hydrogen and other hydrogen-like atoms. It is one of the most precisely measured constants in physics.

The visible Balmer series of hydrogen, whose wavelengths the Rydberg formula predicts.

The Rydberg formula

The wavelengths λ of hydrogen's spectral lines obey:

1/λ = R (1/n1² − 1/n2²)

where n1 and n2 are integers with n2 > n1. Setting n1 = 1 gives the Lyman series (ultraviolet), n1 = 2 the Balmer series (visible), and n1 = 3 the Paschen series (infrared). For a nucleus of finite mass M, the constant is adjusted to RM = R∞ / (1 + me/M), which differs slightly between hydrogen, deuterium, and helium.

Theoretical basis

In the Bohr model of the atom the constant is expressed in terms of fundamental constants:

R∞ = m_e e⁴ / (8 ε₀² h³ c) ≈ 1.0973731568157 × 10⁷ m⁻¹

Niels Bohr's 1913 derivation of this expression from first principles was a landmark success of early quantum theory. Because precision spectroscopy of hydrogen measures the Rydberg constant so accurately, it plays a central role in testing quantum electrodynamics and in determining other fundamental constants; discrepancies in measurements contributed to the "proton radius puzzle" of the 2010s.
