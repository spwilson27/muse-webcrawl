Castilla–La Mancha

Castilla–La Mancha is an autonomous community of Spain. Comprising the provinces of Albacete, Ciudad Real, Cuenca, Guadalajara and Toledo, it covers most of the southern half of the Iberian Peninsula's central plateau. Its capital and largest city is Toledo, a historic city and former imperial capital. The region is famous worldwide as the setting of Miguel de Cervantes's novel Don Quixote.

Geography

Castilla–La Mancha is dominated by the Meseta Central, an extensive plateau averaging about 600–700 metres above sea level. The plain of La Mancha, in the south-centre of the region, is one of the largest natural plains in Spain. Mountain systems frame the plateau: the Sierra Morena to the south, the Sistema Ibérico to the east and the Montes de Toledo in the west. The Tagus, Guadiana and Júcar rivers drain the region, flowing respectively to the Atlantic and the Mediterranean.

History

The region was contested between Christians and Muslims during the Middle Ages; Toledo was taken by Alfonso VI of Castile in 1085 and Cuenca in 1177. The area formed the southern part of the Crown of Castile (New Castile). Under Spain's 1978 constitution and the 1982 Statute of Autonomy, Castilla–La Mancha became an autonomous community; the province of Madrid, historically part of New Castile, formed its own separate community.

Economy and culture

Agriculture remains important: the region is one of the world's great vineyard areas and produces Manchego cheese, saffron and olive oil. Wind turbines dot the plains, continuing the landscape Cervantes populated with windmills that Don Quixote mistook for giants. Toledo, Cuenca and Almagro are major cultural destinations, and Toledo's historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
