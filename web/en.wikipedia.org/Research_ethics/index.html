Research ethics

Research ethics is the application of fundamental ethical principles to research activities, including the design and implementation of research involving human participants, animal experimentation, academic integrity, and the handling of data. Its central concerns include informed consent, confidentiality, the minimisation of harm, and scientific honesty.

History

Modern research ethics emerged in response to abuses, most notoriously the medical experiments conducted by Nazi physicians, which led to the Nuremberg Code (1947). The Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and the Belmont Report (1979) — which articulated the principles of respect for persons, beneficence, and justice — followed revelations such as the Tuskegee syphilis study.

Oversight

Research involving human subjects is typically reviewed by an institutional review board (IRB) or research ethics committee. Key requirements include voluntary informed consent, risk–benefit assessment, fair selection of participants, and protection of vulnerable populations. Research misconduct — fabrication, falsification, and plagiarism — is policed by institutions and funders.
