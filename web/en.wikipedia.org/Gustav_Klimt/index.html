Gustav Klimt (14 July 1862 – 6 February 1918) was an Austrian symbolist painter and one of the most prominent members of the Vienna Secession movement. His work is noted for its decorative patterns, use of gold leaf, and frank eroticism, and his paintings include portraits, landscapes, allegories, and murals.

Early career

Klimt was born in Baumgarten, near Vienna, the son of a gold engraver — a background that later informed his use of gold in painting. He studied at the Vienna School of Arts and Crafts and, with his brother Ernst and Franz Matsch, formed an artists' company that received numerous commissions for decorative painting in theatres and public buildings across the Austro-Hungarian Empire.

Vienna Secession and the Faculty Paintings

In 1897 Klimt co-founded and became the first president of the Vienna Secession, which broke from the conservative artists' establishment. His ceiling paintings for the University of Vienna — Philosophy, Medicine, and Jurisprudence — provoked scandal for their pessimistic, erotic imagery and were never installed; they were destroyed by fire in 1945.

Golden Phase

Klimt's "Golden Phase" produced his most famous works, including Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I (1907) and The Kiss (1908–09), with surfaces patterned in gold and silver leaf and ornamental motifs. The Kiss, depicting two lovers kneeling on a flower-strewn meadow, has become an icon of Austrian art. He also painted landscapes in a mosaic-like style during summers at Lake Attersee.

Death and legacy

Klimt died of pneumonia following a stroke in February 1918. Many of his works have achieved record prices; Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I, looted by the Nazis and recovered by the sitter's niece Maria Altmann after a celebrated legal case, was sold in 2006 for a then-record sum.
