Indo-Islamic architecture

RegionIndian subcontinent
Period12th century – present
Notable examplesQutb Minar, Taj Mahal, Red Fort, Gol Gumbaz

Indo-Islamic architecture is the architectural tradition produced by Islamic patrons and builders on the Indian subcontinent from the 12th century onward, blending Islamic forms — arches, domes, minarets, geometric and calligraphic ornament — with Indian materials, craftsmanship and spatial traditions.

Early Sultanate period

The Delhi Sultanate introduced monumental mosques and tombs, beginning with the Quwwat-ul-Islam mosque and Qutb Minar in Delhi (1190s). Early structures reused carved temple elements, while later Sultanate buildings developed true arches and domes. Regional sultanates in Gujarat, Bengal, Jaunpur and the Deccan produced distinctive local styles.

Mughal architecture

Under the Mughals (1526–1857) the style reached its height: Humayun's Tomb introduced the Persian charbagh tomb-garden; Akbar built Fatehpur Sikri in red sandstone; and Shah Jahan's reign produced the Taj Mahal at Agra, the Red Fort and Jama Masjid in Delhi, famed for white marble, pietra dura inlay, bulbous double domes and rigorous symmetry.

Later developments

Regional courts at Lucknow, Hyderabad and Mysore continued the tradition, and the British Raj fused it with Gothic and classical elements in the Indo-Saracenic style seen in railway stations, universities and palaces. Indo-Islamic monuments remain central to South Asia's built heritage and tourism.
