Agrochemical

An agrochemical (or agrichemical), a contraction of agricultural chemical, is any chemical product used in industrial agriculture. Such chemicals include pesticides (insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, nematicides), synthetic fertilizers, plant growth regulators, hormones used in livestock production, and antibiotics used to control disease in animals. Most agrochemicals are designed to improve crop yields or protect crops from pests, weeds, and disease, though their use has raised significant environmental and public-health concerns.

Types

Pesticides are substances used to control organisms harmful to cultivated plants or animals. Herbicides are the most widely used class worldwide, followed by insecticides and fungicides. Fertilizers supply essential plant nutrients, principally nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, in forms that crops can absorb. Plant growth regulators modify plant development, while feed additives and veterinary chemicals support intensive livestock production.

History

The use of chemicals in agriculture is ancient: sulfur was used as a fumigant in antiquity, and arsenic-based compounds were applied as insecticides in the nineteenth century. The modern agrochemical industry emerged after the Second World War, when compounds developed as chemical weapons or from wartime research — notably the organochlorine insecticide DDT and the phenoxy herbicide 2,4-D — were redirected to farming. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring publicized the ecological harms of indiscriminate pesticide use and helped launch the modern environmental movement.

Environmental and health effects

Agrochemical runoff can contaminate soil and groundwater, harm pollinators such as bees, and promote the evolution of pesticide-resistant pests. Chronic exposure has been linked to health problems in farm workers. These concerns have driven the development of integrated pest management, precision application technologies, and organic farming, which restricts synthetic agrochemical use.
