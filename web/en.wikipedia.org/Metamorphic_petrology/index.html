Metamorphic petrology

Metamorphic petrology is the branch of petrology that studies metamorphic rocks — rocks that have been transformed (metamorphosed) from a pre-existing protolith by heat, pressure, deformation and chemically active fluids, without melting. Petrologists in this field reconstruct the pressure-temperature-time (P-T-t) histories of rocks to understand mountain building, subduction, crustal thickening and other tectonic processes.

Concepts

Central ideas include metamorphic grade (the intensity of metamorphism), metamorphic facies — sets of mineral assemblages that form under similar pressure and temperature conditions, such as greenschist, amphibolite, granulite and eclogite facies — and index minerals such as chlorite, biotite, garnet, staurolite, kyanite and sillimanite, whose first appearance marks isograds on a map. The aluminosilicate polymorphs andalusite, kyanite and sillimanite are classic indicators of pressure-temperature conditions.

Methods

Work combines field mapping, optical microscopy of thin sections, electron-microprobe analysis of mineral compositions, geothermobarometry (calculating the temperature and pressure at which minerals equilibrated), phase-equilibrium modelling (pseudosections), and geochronology to date metamorphic events. Textures such as schistosity, gneissic banding, porphyroblasts and reaction rims record how minerals grew and reacted during deformation and heating or cooling.

Types of metamorphism

Regional metamorphism affects large volumes in orogenic belts; contact metamorphism occurs around igneous intrusions; burial, hydrothermal, shock (impact) and dynamic (fault-zone) metamorphism are also recognised. Ultrahigh-pressure metamorphism, marked by coesite or diamond in crustal rocks, proves that continental material has been subducted to mantle depths and returned to the surface.
