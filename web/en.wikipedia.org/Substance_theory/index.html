Substance theory

Substance theory, or substance–attribute theory, is an ontological theory positing that objects consist of a purported substratum or "substance" in which properties "inhere". The substance is said to stand under, and to support, the qualities or accidents that we perceive, while persisting through change.

History

The theory originates with Aristotle, who in the Categories distinguished primary substances (individual things such as a particular man or horse) from the species and genera to which they belong, and held that a substance is the bearer of attributes without itself being predicated of anything else. Medieval scholastics developed the doctrine in discussing the Eucharist: in transubstantiation, the substance of bread and wine was held to change while their accidents (taste, colour, texture) remained.

Early modern philosophers recast the theory. Descartes distinguished thinking substance (res cogitans) from extended substance (res extensa). Spinoza argued there is only one substance, God or Nature. Leibniz posited infinitely many simple substances, the monads. John Locke described substance in general as an obscure "something, I know not what" that supports observable qualities.

Criticism

George Berkeley and David Hume attacked the notion of material substance as unperceivable and unnecessary; Hume argued we have no impression of any substratum distinct from a bundle of perceptions. Bundle theory, the chief modern rival, holds that objects are nothing over and above their properties in relation.
