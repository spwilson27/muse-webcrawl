Marble

Type: Metamorphic rock
Composition: Recrystallised calcite or dolomite
Protolith: Limestone or dolomite
Famous sources: Carrara (Italy), Penteli (Greece), Makrana (India)

Marble is a metamorphic rock formed when limestone or dolomite is subjected to heat and pressure, recrystallising into an interlocking mosaic of calcite or dolomite crystals. Valued since antiquity for its beauty, translucency and workability, it is a classic material of sculpture and monumental architecture.

Formation and characteristics

During metamorphism, the original sedimentary textures and fossils are usually obliterated. Pure marble is white; mineral impurities such as clay, iron oxides and serpentine produce the coloured veins and swirls — grey, pink, green, black — prized in decorative stone. Marble is relatively soft (about 3 on the Mohs scale for calcite marble), taking a high polish but weathering and staining readily outdoors, especially under acid rain.

History and uses

Greek temples such as the Parthenon used Pentelic marble, Roman builders spread its use across an empire, and Renaissance sculptors — above all Michelangelo — sought the white statuario of Carrara in Tuscany. The Taj Mahal is clad in Makrana marble. Today marble is used for flooring, cladding, countertops, monuments and sculpture, and ground marble serves as a filler and pigment.

Production

Major producers include Italy, China, India, Spain, Turkey and Greece. Blocks are extracted in quarries with wire saws and channeling machines, then sawn into slabs and polished.
