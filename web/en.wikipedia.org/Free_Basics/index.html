Free Basics

Free Basics was a mobile application and website initiative launched by Meta (then Facebook) in partnership with mobile phone providers, offering free access to a limited selection of websites and services in developing countries without data charges — a practice known as zero-rating. It was the consumer-facing part of the Internet.org initiative, announced in August 2013, and was rebranded from "Internet.org" to "Free Basics" in September 2015.

The platform allowed users with compatible mobile carriers to browse a curated set of sites — including news, health, education, jobs, and Facebook itself — with images and scripts restricted to keep data use low. By 2016, Free Basics had launched in more than 40 countries across Africa, Asia, and Latin America.

Controversy and net neutrality

Free Basics became a flashpoint in global net neutrality debates. Critics argued that zero-rating a selected bundle of services violated net neutrality principles, distorted competition, gave Facebook gatekeeper power over which services reached new users, and created a "walled garden" version of the Internet for the poor. Supporters countered that some connectivity was better than none and that the platform was open to any service meeting its technical guidelines.

The dispute came to a head in India, one of Facebook's largest prospective markets, where a mass public campaign by net neutrality advocates led the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India to ban discriminatory data pricing in February 2016, effectively blocking Free Basics there. Similar concerns led Egypt and other countries to discontinue the service. Free Basics continued operating in many markets but remained a prominent case study in debates over zero-rating, digital colonialism, and equitable Internet access.
