Flapper

Flappers were a subculture of young Western women prominent after the First World War and throughout the Roaring Twenties, who wore short skirts (knee length was considered short in those days) and bobbed hair, listened to jazz, and flouted social and sexual norms of the time. Flappers were considered brash for wearing excessive makeup, drinking alcohol, smoking cigarettes in public, driving automobiles, treating sex in a casual manner, and otherwise scoffing at conventional morality. The image is strongly associated with the United States in the era of Prohibition and the Jazz Age, and with parallel movements in Britain, France (the garçonne), Germany, and elsewhere.

Origins and etymology

The word "flapper" earlier meant a teenage girl or a young prostitute in British slang, and by one theory derives from a young bird flapping its wings as it learns to fly. Its modern sense was popularised in the 1920s by the press and by films such as The Flapper (1920) with Olive Thomas and the Clara Bow vehicle It (1927). F. Scott Fitzgerald, whose short stories in Flappers and Philosophers (1920) chronicled the type, is credited with defining the flapper for American readers; Zelda Fitzgerald was often described as the archetype.

Fashion and lifestyle

Flapper fashion rejected the corseted Victorian silhouette for straight, loose dresses with dropped waists, worn with rolled stockings, T-strap or Mary Jane heels, cloche hats, and long strings of beads. Bobbed hair (the Eton crop, the shingle), heavy eye makeup, and rouge signalled modernity. Freed in part by new economic and political rights — American women won the vote in 1920 — flappers worked in offices and department stores, danced the Charleston and the Black Bottom in speakeasies, drank bootleg gin, smoked through long cigarette holders, and embraced new technologies from cars to radios. Older commentators and moral reformers condemned the phenomenon as reckless; anti-flirt and modesty campaigns attempted, with little success, to legislate hemlines and behaviour.

Legacy

The Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the ensuing Great Depression ended the flapper era; hemlines dropped and the mood sobered. Nonetheless, the flapper remains a durable cultural symbol of youthful rebellion and women's emancipation, and her silhouette — bob, beads, and fringe — recurs in fashion revivals and popular depictions of the 1920s.
