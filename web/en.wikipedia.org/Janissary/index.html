The Janissaries (Ottoman Turkish: Yeniçeri, "new soldier") were an elite infantry corps that formed the Ottoman Sultan's household troops and bodyguards. Founded in the 14th century under Sultan Murad I, they are considered one of the first modern standing armies in Europe: salaried, uniformed, and living in barracks. Originally recruited from Christian boys taken through the devşirme (child levy) in the Balkans, converted to Islam, and trained for total loyalty to the Sultan, the Janissaries became a decisive military and political force before their violent abolition in 1826.

Origins and the devşirme

The corps originated in the pençik system of taking one-fifth of war captives as the Sultan's share, and was systematised under Murad I (r. 1362–1389) with the advice of Çandarlı Kara Halil. With the devşirme, Christian boys — chiefly from the Balkans, roughly aged eight to eighteen — were periodically levied, converted to Islam, taught Turkish, and given rigorous training in the acemi oğlan (novice) schools, the most gifted going on to palace service in the Enderun, others to the Janissary corps. In principle celibate and forbidden to trade, the Janissaries were legally slaves of the Sultan (kul), yet could rise to the highest offices of the empire; several Grand Viziers, including Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, came through the system.

Military role

Armed initially with bows and later among the earliest infantry to adopt firearms, the Janissaries formed the disciplined core of the Ottoman field army, fighting in the centre of the battle order around the Sultan. They were central to Ottoman expansion — present at Kosovo (1389), the conquest of Constantinople (1453), and campaigns in Hungary, Persia, and Egypt — and were celebrated for cohesion and ferocity in close combat. Their distinctive headgear, the börk, was said to recall the sleeve of the dervish saint Haji Bektash, patron of the corps, and their regiments (orta) were organised around the communal soup cauldron (kazan), symbol of the Sultan's provision.

Decline and political power

From the late 16th century the corps's recruitment rules relaxed: Muslim-born boys and Janissaries' sons gained entry, celibacy lapsed, members engaged in trades, and the rolls swelled with men seeking the corps's pay and privileges while its battlefield effectiveness waned. Politically powerful in the capital, Janissaries repeatedly mutinied over pay and debased coinage, deposing and killing sultans — most notoriously Osman II in 1622 — and blocking military reform, as in the revolt that deposed Selim III in 1807.

Abolition

Sultan Mahmud II, having carefully built a loyal modern artillery and prepared public and religious support, provoked a final Janissary revolt in June 1826 by announcing a new-model army. Loyal troops bombarded the Janissary barracks in Constantinople; thousands were killed and the survivors executed or exiled. The destruction of the corps — officially the Vaka-i Hayriye, the "Auspicious Incident" — removed the greatest obstacle to the Ottoman Empire's 19th-century modernisation (the Tanzimat era).
