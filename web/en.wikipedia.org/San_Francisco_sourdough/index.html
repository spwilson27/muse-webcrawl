San Francisco sourdough is a type of sourdough bread originating in San Francisco, California, characterised by a pronounced tangy flavour, crisp crust and chewy, open crumb. It became a signature food of the city during the California Gold Rush of 1849, when miners and settlers carried starters westward; "sourdough" itself became a nickname for old-time prospectors.

History

The Boudin Bakery, founded in 1849 by Isidore Boudin, is credited with the famous "mother dough" still in use, said to descend from a Gold Rush-era starter saved by Louise Boudin from the 1906 earthquake fire. French bakers brought sourdough technique to California, where the mild, foggy climate suited the slow fermentation that produces the bread's acidity.

Microbiology

The distinctive flavour is attributed to the local balance of wild yeast and lactic-acid bacteria in the starter. For decades the dominant bacterium was named Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis (now Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis) after the city, and the typical yeast is Kazachstania humilis. Although early folklore held that the bread could not be reproduced outside a 50-mile radius of San Francisco, the same organisms occur in starters worldwide; the flavour depends mainly on process, hydration and fermentation time.
