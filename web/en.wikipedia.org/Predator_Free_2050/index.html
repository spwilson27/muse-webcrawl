Predator Free 2050 is a New Zealand government-backed conservation programme launched in 2016 with the goal of eradicating introduced mammalian predators from the entire country by 2050. The initial targets are rats, stoats and brushtail possums, which together are estimated to kill tens of millions of native birds and other wildlife each year.

Background

New Zealand's native fauna evolved without land mammals (other than bats), leaving endemic birds such as the kiwi and kākāpō highly vulnerable to predators introduced by humans. Conservationists had already cleared predators from many offshore islands; Predator Free 2050 extends the ambition to the mainland.

Implementation

The programme is delivered through Predator Free 2050 Limited, a Crown-owned company, alongside the Department of Conservation, regional councils, iwi and thousands of community trapping groups. Intermediate goals include suppressing predators across large mainland areas and eradicating them from expanding "island" peninsulas and defended zones.

Methods and debate

Current methods rely on trapping and toxins such as 1080 (sodium fluoroacetate), whose aerial use is effective but controversial. The programme also funds research into new tools, including species-specific toxins, improved lures and genetic approaches. Commentators debate the feasibility of complete eradication and its large projected cost, estimated in the billions of dollars.
