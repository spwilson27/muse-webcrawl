Ma'am

Ma'am (pronounced /mæm/, and in some varieties rhyming with "ham" or "ma'am" reduced to a quick /məm/) is a polite form of address for a woman, used especially in North American English and in formal or service contexts. It is a contraction of madam, which derives from the French ma dame, "my lady."

Usage

Ma'am is the feminine counterpart of sir and is used when addressing a woman formally, respectfully, or at a courteous distance—for example, "Yes, ma'am" or "Excuse me, ma'am." In much of the southern United States it is an everyday marker of politeness used toward women of any adult age, including by children addressing their mothers or teachers. In military and uniformed-service contexts in the United States, "ma'am" is the prescribed form of address for female superior officers.

Regional and social variation

Attitudes toward the word vary. In parts of the northern United States and among some speakers, being called ma'am can be perceived as implying older age, and some people dislike it for that reason. In British usage, "ma'am" (pronounced "mahm") is notably the traditional form of address to female members of the royal family after first being addressed as "Your Majesty" or "Your Royal Highness," and is also used to female officers in the British armed forces and police. The unreduced form madam remains common in formal letters ("Dear Madam") and in addressing presiding officers and officials.

Etymology

Madam entered Middle English from Old French ma dame. The contracted form ma'am is attested from the seventeenth century onward and appears frequently in representations of respectful or deferential speech in literature and drama.
