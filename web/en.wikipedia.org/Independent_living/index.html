Independent living

Independent living means disabled people controlling their own lives and support, not necessarily doing everything unaided.

Independent living is a philosophy and a movement of people with disabilities who work for self-determination, equal opportunity and self-respect. It holds that disabled people are the best experts on their own needs and should be able to live in the community — making their own choices about housing, work, education and personal assistance — rather than being confined to institutions or having their lives managed by professionals.

Philosophy

In the independent living framework, "independence" does not mean doing everything without help; it means control over the help one needs and over one's own life. The problem is located not in the individual's impairment but in social barriers — inaccessible buildings, transport and attitudes — an outlook that helped give rise to the social model of disability. The movement rejects the medical and charitable framing of disability in favour of civil rights.

History

The movement grew out of the experience of students with significant disabilities at the University of California, Berkeley, in the 1960s, led by Ed Roberts, who had polio and used a wheelchair and respirator. The "Rolling Quads" campaigned for accessible housing and personal attendant services, and in 1972 founded the Center for Independent Living (CIL) in Berkeley — the first organisation of its kind, run by and for disabled people. The model spread across the United States and internationally; in the US, CILs were given a statutory basis by amendments to the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, and hundreds now provide core services including information and referral, peer counselling, independent living skills training, individual and systems advocacy, and transition support out of institutions.

Independent living today

The philosophy underpins policies such as consumer-directed personal assistance and direct payments (pioneered in the UK by disabled activists in the 1980s–90s), deinstitutionalisation, and Article 19 of the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), which recognises the right to live independently and be included in the community. The phrase is also used more loosely — for example for retirement communities offering "independent living" housing for older people — a usage distinct from the disability-rights movement.
