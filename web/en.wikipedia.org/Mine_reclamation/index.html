Mine reclamation

Mine reclamation is the process of restoring land that has been mined to a natural or economically usable state. Modern reclamation — usually planned before mining begins and carried out progressively during the mine's life — involves reshaping spoil and pits to stable contours, replacing topsoil, re-establishing vegetation, controlling erosion and acid drainage, and monitoring the site until it meets agreed standards for grazing, forestry, wildlife habitat, wetlands, recreation or development. Mining companies commonly post financial bonds to guarantee the work.

A former surface mine reshaped, topsoiled and revegetated as grassland.

Stages

  1. Planning — baseline studies of soil, water, ecology and heritage; an approved reclamation plan and bond.
  2. Earthworks — backfilling, regrading highwalls and spoil, burying toxic material, and restoring drainage patterns.
  3. Soil reconstruction — spreading salvaged topsoil or manufactured growing media; ameliorating acidity with lime.
  4. Revegetation — seeding grasses, legumes and native species, planting trees; controlling invasive weeds.
  5. Monitoring and relinquishment — demonstrating stable landforms and self-sustaining ecosystems before the bond is released.

Legislation

In the United States, the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 (SMCRA) requires permits, reclamation to approximate original contour, and restoration of land use capability for coal mines; an Abandoned Mine Land fund, financed by a per-ton fee, addresses sites orphaned before the law. The Superfund programme covers some of the worst hard-rock sites. Other jurisdictions with comprehensive regimes include Canada, Australia, Germany (where lignite regions such as Lusatia have been converted to lake districts), and the European Union. Enforcement quality and the treatment of acid mine drainage — which can persist for centuries from sulfide waste — remain major challenges.

Outcomes and examples

Successful projects have created farmland in Appalachia, the Lusatian Lake District in Germany, wetlands and nature reserves (e.g. former quarries hosting rare orchids and birds in Britain), vineyards on restored spoil, and solar farms and data centres on mined land. The Eden Project in Cornwall occupies a reclaimed china-clay pit. Critics note that "mountaintop removal" valley fills in the US and unrehabilitated artisanal mining in developing countries show the gap between modern standards and practice.
