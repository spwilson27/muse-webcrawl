Occam's razor

Occam's razor (also spelled Ockham's razor) is the problem-solving principle that recommends searching for explanations constructed with the smallest possible set of elements. It is also known as the principle of parsimony or the law of parsimony (Latin: lex parsimoniae). Attributed to William of Ockham, a 14th-century English philosopher and theologian, it is frequently cited as Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem — "Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity."

Interpretation

The principle does not state that the simplest explanation is always correct, but that among competing hypotheses that make the same predictions, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected — or, in Newton's phrasing, nature should not be assumed to do more than is necessary to explain appearances. It functions as a heuristic guiding scientists toward more economical theories.

"We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances." — Isaac Newton

Applications and criticism

Occam's razor is invoked in model selection, statistics (overfitting), medicine, and philosophy. Critics note that nature is under no obligation to be simple, and that overly parsimonious models can omit real complexity.
