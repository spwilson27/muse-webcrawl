SignificanceStart of organised Nonconformity in England

The Great Ejection followed the Restoration of the English monarchy in 1660 and the passing of the Act of Uniformity 1662, which required every minister in the Church of England to give "unfeigned assent and consent" to everything in the revised Book of Common Prayer, to be ordained by a bishop if not already so ordained, and to renounce the Solemn League and Covenant. Ministers who could not comply by St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August 1662, were deprived of their livings; approximately 2,000 ministers — Puritans, Presbyterians, Congregationalists and others — left or were expelled from their parishes.

Background

During the Civil War and Interregnum, the Church of England had been reshaped under Presbyterian and Independent influence, episcopacy abolished and the Book of Common Prayer replaced by the Directory of Public Worship. The Savoy Conference of 1661 failed to reach a compromise acceptable to both Presbyterians and the restored Anglican bishops, and the Cavalier Parliament chose a settlement demanding full conformity, enforced by the series of statutes later known as the Clarendon Code.

Consequences

The ejected ministers, including figures such as Richard Baxter (who had already left Kidderminster), John Bunyan's contemporaries, and scholars like John Wallis's clerical counterparts, formed the backbone of Protestant Dissent. Many preached in conventicles despite persecution under the Conventicle Acts and the Five Mile Act, which forbade ejected ministers from coming within five miles of their former parishes or corporate towns. Their congregations became the Presbyterian, Congregational and Baptist churches of England and Wales, and the event is traditionally regarded as the birthday of Nonconformity. Farewell sermons preached in August 1662 were collected and widely published, and the ejection is still commemorated by Dissenting churches; its tercentenary in 1962 prompted calls for reconciliation with the Church of England.
