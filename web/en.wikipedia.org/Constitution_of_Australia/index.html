Constitution of Australia

The Constitution of Australia is the fundamental law that governs the political and legal structure of Australia. It is a written constitution that establishes the country as a federation under a constitutional monarchy, with the Commonwealth of Australia as the federal government and six states. The Constitution was drafted at conventions in the 1890s, approved by voters in the Australian colonies, and enacted as section 9 of the Commonwealth of Australia Constitution Act 1900 (UK). It came into effect on 1 January 1901.

Structure

The Constitution has eight chapters and 128 sections. It establishes the Parliament (Chapter I), the executive government (Chapter II), and the judicature (Chapter III), distributing legislative powers between the Commonwealth and the states. Section 51 lists the subjects on which the federal Parliament may legislate; state parliaments retain residual powers. Section 109 resolves inconsistencies in favour of Commonwealth law.

Key features

The High Court of Australia, created by the Constitution and established in 1903, is the final court of appeal and the arbiter of constitutional disputes. The Constitution contains few express rights — among them section 116's prohibition on Commonwealth laws establishing a religion, and section 51(xxxi)'s guarantee that property be acquired only "on just terms".

Amendment

Under section 128, the Constitution can only be altered by referendum, requiring a national majority of voters plus a majority in a majority of states. Of 45 referendum questions put since 1901, only eight have succeeded, most famously the 1967 referendum concerning Aboriginal Australians.
