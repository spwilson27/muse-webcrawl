William Temple Hornaday

[Image: William Temple Hornaday]

William Temple Hornaday (December 1, 1854 – March 6, 1937) was an American zoologist, conservationist, taxidermist, and author. He served as the first director of the New York Zoological Park (the Bronx Zoo) from 1896 to 1926 and was a leading figure in the early American conservation movement.

Career

Hornaday began his career as a taxidermist, working for Ward's Natural Science Establishment and later as chief taxidermist of the U.S. National Museum, where his display of American bison helped popularise realistic habitat groupings in museums. A survey trip to Montana in 1886 convinced him the bison was on the verge of extinction, and he became a tireless campaigner for wildlife protection.

Conservation work

He co-founded the American Bison Society in 1905 and helped establish herds that saved the species. As director of the Bronx Zoo, he built one of the world's great zoos. His book Our Vanishing Wild Life (1913) galvanised public support for game laws, and he campaigned successfully for restrictions on hunting, the protection of migratory birds, and the creation of wildlife refuges. His reputation is also complicated by the zoo's notorious 1906 exhibition of Ota Benga, a Congolese man, which Hornaday defended at the time.
