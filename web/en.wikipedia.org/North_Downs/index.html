The North Downs are a ridge of chalk hills in south-east England that stretch from Farnham in Surrey in the west to the White Cliffs of Dover in Kent in the east. They form the northern rim of the Weald, and run broadly parallel to the South Downs, which form the southern rim. Much of the range lies within two Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty, the Surrey Hills and the Kent Downs.

Geology

The Downs are formed from the Chalk Group, a thick deposit of white limestone laid down in a shallow sea during the Late Cretaceous period, between about 100 and 66 million years ago. Uplift and erosion of the great Wealden dome exposed the chalk as an inward-facing escarpment with a steep scarp slope to the south and a gentler dip slope falling northwards towards the Thames basin.

Features

The highest point is Botley Hill in Surrey at 267 metres. Prominent summits and viewpoints include Box Hill, Leith Hill (on an adjacent Greensand ridge), Newlands Corner and Wrotham Hill. The ridge is crossed by several river gaps, including those of the Wey, Mole, Darent, Medway and Stour. The North Downs Way, a long-distance national trail of about 153 miles (246 km), follows the ridge from Farnham to Dover.

History and ecology

The downs have been grazed by sheep for centuries, producing a species-rich chalk grassland notable for orchids and butterflies such as the Adonis blue and chalkhill blue. In places the grassland gives way to beech and yew woodland — Box Hill is famous for its native box trees. The Pilgrims' Way, the ancient trackway along the southern slopes, was traditionally followed by pilgrims travelling to the shrine of Thomas Becket at Canterbury.
