Battle of Bouvines

Date27 July 1214
LocationBouvines, County of Flanders (now France)
ResultDecisive French victory
BelligerentsKingdom of France vs. coalition of the Holy Roman Empire, England, Flanders, and Boulogne
CommandersPhilip II of France; Otto IV, John of England (absent), Ferrand of Flanders, Renaud of Boulogne

The Battle of Bouvines was fought on 27 July 1214 near the village of Bouvines in Flanders, concluding the Anglo-French War of 1213–1214. A coalition army of the Holy Roman Emperor Otto IV, King John of England's allies, Count Ferrand of Flanders, and Count Renaud of Boulogne attacked the army of King Philip II of France in an attempt to reverse French expansion and destroy Capetian power. Philip won a complete victory that is regarded as one of the most decisive battles of the European Middle Ages.

Background

Philip Augustus had stripped King John of most of his continental possessions — Normandy, Maine, Anjou, and Poitou — in the decade after 1202. John constructed a grand coalition to recover them, planning a two-pronged attack: he would land in Poitou while Otto and the counts struck from Flanders. John's southern offensive stalled after his defeat at Roche-au-Moine on 2 July 1214, but the northern coalition army advanced into Flanders and met Philip, who was marching back to meet it.

The battle

The armies were roughly comparable in size, perhaps 6,000–9,000 men each including large bodies of mounted knights. Fighting was heaviest on the wings: on the French left, the coalition's Flemish infantry and knights pressed hard; in the centre, Philip himself was unhorsed and nearly killed before being rescued by his household knights; on the French right the coalition collapsed and Count Ferrand was captured. Otto IV fled the field, losing the imperial standard, and Renaud of Boulogne made a last stand in a circle of pikemen before his capture.

Consequences

The defeat destroyed Otto IV's authority; he was deposed in favour of Frederick II. Ferrand and Renaud spent years in captivity. King John, his continental ambitions ruined, returned to England to face his rebellious barons, who forced him to seal Magna Carta in June 1215. Philip's prestige soared, and Bouvines became a founding triumph in French national memory, celebrated for centuries.
