Probabilistic method

The probabilistic method is a nonconstructive method, primarily used in combinatorics, pioneered by Paul Erdős, for proving the existence of a prescribed kind of mathematical object. It works by showing that if one randomly chooses objects from a specified class, the probability that the result is of the prescribed kind is strictly greater than zero.

Method

To prove that an object with certain properties exists, one defines a probability space of candidate objects and shows that the desired properties hold with positive probability. Although the proof shows existence, it usually does not give an explicit example. A basic tool is the expectation argument: if the expected number of "bad" features is less than one, some object must have none.

Examples

Classic applications include lower bounds on Ramsey numbers — Erdős proved in 1947 that R(k,k) grows at least exponentially, one of the first triumphs of the method — results on the girth and chromatic number of graphs, and property testing in theoretical computer science. Powerful refinements include the Lovász local lemma and the method of conditional expectations, which sometimes converts the existence proof into an efficient deterministic algorithm.

Notable texts

The Probabilistic Method by Noga Alon and Joel Spencer is the standard reference on the subject.
