The Niah Caves are an enormous limestone cave complex in Niah National Park in the Malaysian state of Sarawak, on the island of Borneo. The main chamber, the Great Cave, has one of the largest cave entrances in the world and contains archaeological evidence of human occupation spanning some 50,000 years—among the earliest evidence for modern humans in Southeast Asia. The caves are also famous for their edible-nest swiftlets, whose nests are harvested for bird's nest soup, and for vast bat colonies.

Archaeology

Excavations led by Tom Harrisson of the Sarawak Museum in the 1950s and 1960s uncovered the "Deep Skull" in 1958—a fragmentary human skull dated to roughly 40,000 years ago, once considered the oldest modern human fossil in Southeast Asia. Later work by the Niah Cave Project (2000–2003) refined the chronology and documented long sequences of occupation, burials, and subsistence, including evidence that early inhabitants exploited rainforest resources such as nuts and tubers and managed toxic plants for food. Neolithic burials and the painted walls of the separate Painted Cave, with red hematite images of boats and figures, attest to later use of the complex.

Bird's nests and guano

For centuries, local communities have harvested the nests of swiftlets from the cave ceilings using tall bamboo and rattan poles, a dangerous trade in a commodity prized in Chinese cuisine. The caves' huge bat and swiftlet populations also deposit guano, historically collected as fertiliser.

Conservation

The caves lie within Niah National Park, gazetted in 1974. In 2024 the archaeological heritage of Niah National Park was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List, recognising its outstanding significance for understanding human prehistory in the region.
