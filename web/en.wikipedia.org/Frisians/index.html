Frisians

The Frisians are an ethnic group indigenous to the coastal regions of the Netherlands and northwestern Germany. They inhabit an area known as Frisia, stretching along the North Sea coast from the Dutch province of Friesland through Groningen and into East Frisia and North Frisia in Germany. The Frisians speak, or historically spoke, the Frisian languages, which are the closest living relatives of English among the Germanic languages.

History

The Frisians are mentioned by Roman authors such as Tacitus and Pliny the Elder. In the early Middle Ages, Frisian merchants controlled much of the North Sea trade, and Frisian territory extended further than it does today. The so-called Frisian Freedom was a notable medieval arrangement under which much of Frisia had no feudal lord and was governed by free farming communities. From the late Middle Ages onward, Frisian lands came under the rule of neighbouring powers, and Low German and Dutch displaced Frisian in many areas.

Language and culture

Today West Frisian is an official language in the Dutch province of Friesland, spoken by several hundred thousand people. Saterland Frisian survives in a small part of Lower Saxony, and North Frisian dialects are spoken on the German North Frisian Islands and adjacent mainland. Frisian culture is marked by traditional sports such as fierljeppen (canal vaulting), a strong seafaring and dairy-farming heritage, and the Frisian horse breed.
