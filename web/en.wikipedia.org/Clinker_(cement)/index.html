Clinker (cement)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clinker is a solid material produced in the manufacture of Portland cement as an intermediary product. It occurs in the form of marble-sized nodules, typically 3–25 millimetres in diameter, formed by sintering limestone and alumino-silicate minerals such as clay during the cement kiln stage. Clinker is ground with gypsum and other additives to produce cement.

Production

Raw materials — mainly limestone with smaller amounts of clay, shale, sand, and iron ore — are ground and blended into a raw meal, which is fed into a rotary kiln. As the meal moves through the kiln it is dried, calcined (driving off carbon dioxide from limestone at about 900 °C), and finally heated to around 1,450 °C, where partial melting causes the nodules of clinker to form. The clinker is then rapidly cooled, traditionally in air-quenching coolers.

Composition

Typical Portland cement clinker contains four principal phases, given in cement chemists' notation: alite (tricalcium silicate, C3S, roughly 50–70%), belite (dicalcium silicate, C2S, 15–30%), tricalcium aluminate (C3A, 5–10%), and tetracalcium aluminoferrite (C4AF, 5–15%). On grinding and mixing with water, these phases hydrate to give cement its strength; alite is mainly responsible for early strength and belite for later strength gain.

Environmental impact

Clinker production is highly energy-intensive and is responsible for the majority of the cement industry's carbon dioxide emissions, both from fuel combustion and from the chemical calcination of limestone. Cement manufacture accounts for roughly 8% of global CO2 emissions. Reducing the clinker content of cement using supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash, slag, and calcined clay is a major decarbonisation strategy.
