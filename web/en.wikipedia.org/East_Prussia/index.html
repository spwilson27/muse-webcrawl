East Prussia (German: Ostpreußen) was a province of the Kingdom of Prussia and later of the Free State of Prussia within Germany, from 1773 to 1945. Its capital was Königsberg. The region lay on the southeastern coast of the Baltic Sea, its name deriving from the Old Prussians, a Baltic people conquered and assimilated by the Teutonic Order during the Northern Crusades of the 13th century.

History

The Duchy of Prussia, a Polish fief from 1525, was inherited by the electors of Brandenburg in 1618; Frederick I crowned himself "King in Prussia" at Königsberg in 1701. The province of East Prussia was formally created in 1773 from the duchy's lands. After the Treaty of Versailles (1919), most of West Prussia was ceded to Poland as the "Polish Corridor", leaving East Prussia an exclave of Weimar Germany, connected by transit routes and the sea. Plebiscites in Allenstein and Marienwerder voted overwhelmingly to remain German.

Second World War and aftermath

In 1939 East Prussia was a staging area for the invasion of Poland. In 1944–45 the Red Army's East Prussian Offensive overran the province in bitter fighting; Königsberg fell in April 1945 after a siege that destroyed the city centre. Hundreds of thousands of German civilians fled westward, many across the frozen Vistula Lagoon, and thousands died when the refugee ship Wilhelm Gustloff was torpedoed in January 1945 — the deadliest sinking in maritime history.

Division and expulsion

Under the Potsdam Agreement (1945), East Prussia was divided: the northern third, including Königsberg — renamed Kaliningrad in 1946 — went to the Soviet Union as part of the Russian SFSR, and the southern two-thirds to Poland. The entire German population, some two million people, was expelled westward by 1950 and replaced by Soviet and Polish settlers. The former East Prussia thus exists today only as a historical region, remembered for Kant's Königsberg, the Tannenberg memorial, Masurian lakes and amber coasts.
