The Roman Baths are a well-preserved thermae (public bathing complex) in the city of Bath, Somerset, England. Built around the only natural hot springs in Britain, the baths formed part of the Roman town of Aquae Sulis and were constructed from about 60 AD onwards, alongside a temple dedicated to the goddess Sulis Minerva.

The spring and temple

The King's Spring produces around 1.17 million litres of water per day at 46 °C, rich in minerals. The Romans enclosed the spring in a reservoir and built a temple in the classical style, unusual in Britain, to Sulis, a local Celtic deity identified with the Roman Minerva. Worshippers threw offerings into the spring, including coins and "curse tablets" — small sheets of lead or pewter inscribed with messages asking the goddess to punish thieves and wrongdoers; more than 130 of these tablets have been recovered.

The bathing complex

The baths included the Great Bath, a lead-lined open-air pool fed by the spring, together with heated rooms (a tepidarium and caldarium) using hypocaust underfloor heating, a cold plunge pool (frigidarium), and later additions such as circular baths and exercise yards. The complex functioned as a social and religious centre as much as a place of hygiene, comparable to Roman baths elsewhere in the empire.

Rediscovery

After the Roman withdrawal the baths fell into ruin and were built over. The spring was used through the medieval and Georgian periods, when Bath became a fashionable spa town. The Roman remains were rediscovered in stages from the 18th century, with major excavations in the 1880s revealing the Great Bath. The site is now a museum; visitors can see the baths and temple remains but may not enter the water.
