Detergent

A detergent is a surfactant or a mixture of surfactants with cleansing properties when in dilute solutions. There are a large variety of detergents, the most common family being the alkylbenzene sulfonates, which are soap-like compounds that are more soluble in hard water than ordinary soap, because the polar sulfonate group is less likely than the polar carboxylate of soap to bind to calcium and other ions found in hard water.

History

The first synthetic detergents were developed in Germany during the First World War in response to shortages of fats for soap. Commercial household detergents appeared in the 1930s and expanded rapidly after the Second World War, when branched alkylbenzene sulfonates were replaced by biodegradable linear alkylbenzene sulfonates in response to foaming pollution in rivers.

Composition

Household detergents are complex formulations: surfactants (anionic, nonionic, cationic, or amphoteric) lift soil and grease; builders such as zeolites or phosphates soften water and maintain alkalinity; enzymes digest protein and starch stains; and optical brighteners, fragrances, and corrosion inhibitors are added. Detergents are available as powders, liquids, gels, and unit-dose pods for laundry and dishwashing.

Environmental issues

Phosphate builders contributed to eutrophication of lakes and have been restricted or banned in many jurisdictions. Surfactant biodegradability is regulated to limit aquatic toxicity and persistent foam.
