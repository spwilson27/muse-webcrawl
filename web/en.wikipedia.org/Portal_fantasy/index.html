Portal fantasy

Portal fantasy is a subgenre of fantasy in which a character from an ordinary (often contemporary, real-world) setting travels through a portal or other gateway into a magical or fantastical world. The term was popularised by the critic Farah Mendlesohn, who classified portal fantasy as one of four rhetorical modes of the fantastic in her study Rhetorics of Fantasy (2008). In portal fantasy, the protagonist — and with them the reader — enters the fantastic world through a threshold and must learn its rules from guides and companions.

Characteristics

Classic portal fantasies often feature child protagonists who leave a mundane home for a secondary world, undergo adventures or a quest, and return changed. The portal itself may be a magical object or place: a wardrobe, a rabbit hole, a looking-glass, a tornado, or a railway platform. Because the viewpoint character is a newcomer, the narrative can explain the fantasy world to the reader naturally, making the subgenre especially common in children's and young adult literature.

Examples

Early examples include Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), L. Frank Baum's The Wonderful Wizard of Oz (1900), and E. Nesbit's stories. The best-known example is C. S. Lewis's The Chronicles of Narnia (1950–1956), in which children reach Narnia through a wardrobe. Modern examples include Philip Pullman's His Dark Materials, Cornelia Funke's Inkheart, Lev Grossman's The Magicians, and Seanan McGuire's Wayward Children series, which deconstructs the subgenre by focusing on children who have returned from portal worlds. The Japanese isekai genre is a closely related development in anime, manga, and light novels.
