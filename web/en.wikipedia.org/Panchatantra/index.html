The Panchatantra (Sanskrit: पञ्चतन्त्र, "Five Treatises") is an ancient Indian collection of interrelated animal fables in Sanskrit verse and prose, arranged within a frame story. It is traditionally attributed to Vishnu Sharma, a scholar said to have composed it to instruct three dull princes in the art of statecraft and practical wisdom (nīti) through entertaining stories. The surviving text is generally dated to between about 200 BCE and 300 CE, though it draws on older oral traditions.

Structure

The work is divided into five books, or tantras, each illustrating one theme of political and practical conduct:

  1. Mitra Bheda – the loss of friends (the estrangement of the lion and the bull)
  2. Mitra Samprapti – the gaining of friends (the dove, crow, mouse, tortoise and deer)
  3. Kakolukiyam – of crows and owls (war and diplomacy)
  4. Labdhapranasham – loss of gains (the monkey and the crocodile)
  5. Apariksitakarakam – ill-considered deeds (the Brahmin and the mongoose)

Influence

The Panchatantra is among the most widely translated works of Indian literature. A Pahlavi translation made in the sixth century under the Sasanian king Khosrow I led to Syriac and Arabic versions, the Arabic Kalīla wa Dimna becoming a classic in its own right. From these intermediaries the stories passed into Persian, Hebrew, Latin, and the vernacular literatures of Europe, influencing collections such as Aesop-derived fable traditions and, indirectly, works like Boccaccio's Decameron and La Fontaine's Fables. More than 200 versions are known in over 50 languages. Many of its stories — including "The Monkey and the Crocodile" and "The Blue Jackal" — remain popular as children's tales in India and beyond.
