Alkane

In organic chemistry, an alkane, or paraffin (a historical name that also has other meanings), is an acyclic saturated hydrocarbon. In other words, an alkane consists of hydrogen and carbon atoms arranged in a tree structure in which all the carbon–carbon bonds are single. Alkanes have the general chemical formula CnH2n+2. The alkanes range in complexity from the simplest case of methane (CH4), where n = 1 (sometimes called the parent molecule), to arbitrarily large and complex molecules, like pentacontane (C50H102) or 6-ethyl-2-methyl-5-(1-methylethyl)octane.

Nomenclature

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) defines alkanes as “acyclic branched or unbranched hydrocarbons having the general formula CnH2n+2, and therefore consisting entirely of hydrogen atoms and saturated carbon atoms”. The simplest alkanes are named methane, ethane, propane, butane, pentane, hexane, heptane, octane, nonane, and decane according to the number of carbon atoms.

Properties

Alkanes are relatively unreactive, nonpolar compounds. Their melting and boiling points increase with molecular mass. The first four alkanes are gases at room temperature; those with 5 to 17 carbons are liquids, and longer chains are waxy solids. Alkanes undergo combustion readily, releasing large amounts of heat, which makes them important fuels; they also undergo free-radical halogenation and cracking.

Occurrence and uses

Alkanes occur naturally in petroleum and natural gas. Methane is produced by methanogenic bacteria and is a major component of natural gas. Commercially, alkanes are used as fuels (liquefied petroleum gas, gasoline, diesel), lubricants, and feedstocks for the petrochemical industry.
