Nature reserve

A nature reserve (also known as a wildlife refuge, nature preserve, or natural reserve) is a protected area of importance for flora, fauna, fungi, or features of geological or other special interest, which is reserved and managed for purposes of conservation and to provide special opportunities for study or research. Nature reserves may be designated by governments, conservation charities, or private landowners.

History

The conservation impulse behind reserves is ancient — sacred groves and royal hunting forests preserved wildlife in many cultures. Modern reserves date from the 19th century: Drachenfels in Germany was protected in 1836, and figures such as Charles Waterton created early reserves in England. In Britain, the first national nature reserve was Beinn Eighe in Scotland (1951). The IUCN classifies strict nature reserves as Category Ia protected areas, managed mainly for science and wilderness protection.

Management

Reserves are typically managed to maintain habitats through grazing, coppicing, controlled burning, control of invasive species, and protection from development. Access is often permitted for recreation and education, though sensitive sites may restrict entry or require permits. Many reserves form part of larger networks, such as the Natura 2000 sites in the European Union or UNESCO biosphere reserves.

By country

Terminology varies: the United States has National Wildlife Refuges, Australia has nature reserves under state law, and the Netherlands has reserves managed by organisations such as Natuurmonumenten. Worldwide, protected areas now cover over 15% of the Earth's land surface.
