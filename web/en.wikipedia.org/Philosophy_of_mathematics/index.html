Philosophy of mathematics

The philosophy of mathematics is the branch of philosophy that studies the assumptions, foundations and implications of mathematics. It asks what mathematical objects are, how mathematical knowledge is possible, why mathematics is so effective in describing the physical world, and what makes a mathematical proof valid or convincing.

Major schools

  • Platonism holds that mathematical entities such as numbers and sets exist independently of human minds, and that mathematical truths are discovered rather than invented.
  • Logicism, associated with Frege, Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica, sought to reduce mathematics to logic.
  • Formalism, associated with Hilbert, treats mathematics as the rule-governed manipulation of symbols, with consistency of formal systems the central concern; Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed the limits of Hilbert's programme.
  • Intuitionism, founded by Brouwer, rejects non-constructive existence proofs and the unrestricted law of excluded middle, holding that mathematics is a mental construction.
  • Structuralism holds that mathematics studies structures or patterns, and that mathematical objects are merely places within structures.
  • Fictionalism and nominalism deny that mathematical objects exist, treating mathematics as a useful fiction or reformulating science without quantifying over abstract entities.

Key questions

Philosophers of mathematics debate the nature of mathematical truth and necessity, the apparent indispensability of mathematics to science (the Quine–Putnam argument), the epistemological problem of how humans can know about abstract objects (Benacerraf's problem), the role of proof and computation, and the explanation of mathematics' "unreasonable effectiveness" in the natural sciences, in Eugene Wigner's phrase.
