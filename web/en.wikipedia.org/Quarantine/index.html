Quarantine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A quarantine is a restriction on the movement of people, animals, and goods that is intended to prevent the spread of disease or pests. It is one of several basic measures of disease control, distinct from isolation, which separates people who are confirmed to be ill: quarantine applies to those who may have been exposed but are not yet symptomatic.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Venetian quarantena, "forty days": in 1377 the Republic of Ragusa (Dubrovnik) required ships arriving from plague-affected areas to wait thirty days, later extended to forty in Venice, before landing — one of the earliest formal quarantine systems. Lazarettos (quarantine stations) were established in ports throughout the Mediterranean and later worldwide.

Modern practice

Quarantine powers are used against diseases such as cholera, tuberculosis, Ebola, and, most prominently in recent memory, COVID-19, during which many countries imposed quarantine on travellers and contacts of cases. The International Health Regulations (2005) govern quarantine measures at borders, and most states maintain legal frameworks — and historical controversies — concerning the balance between public health and civil liberties. Quarantine is also applied to animals and plants to control invasive species and agricultural pathogens.
