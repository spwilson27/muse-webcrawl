Mass noun

Mass noun (uncountable noun, non-count noun)
Examples: water, furniture, information, rice, advice
Contrast: Count noun (e.g. chair, idea)

In linguistics, a mass noun, uncountable noun, non-count noun or uncount noun is a noun with the property that any quantity of it is treated as an undifferentiated unit rather than as something with discrete elements. Mass nouns cannot be counted directly and do not normally take a plural form or the indefinite article: one says "much water" rather than "*a water" or "*three waters", and quantities are measured with unit nouns such as "a glass of water" or "a grain of rice".

Properties

Mass nouns typically denote substances (water, gold, air), aggregates (furniture, luggage, traffic) and abstract concepts (information, advice, happiness). In English they combine with quantifiers such as much and little rather than many and few. Semantically, mass nouns have cumulative reference: any sum of parts of water is still water, and any part of water (down to chemical limits) is also water — a property called divisiveness that distinguishes mass reference from count reference.

Flexible usage and cross-linguistic variation

The mass/count distinction is grammatical, not purely physical, and nouns can shift categories: "cake" is countable when referring to whole cakes but mass when referring to the substance; "a coffee" coerces the mass noun into a serving unit. Languages differ in how rigidly they mark the distinction; Mandarin Chinese, for example, generally requires measure words (classifiers) for all nouns, blurring the mass–count boundary drawn in English.
