Inertial electrostatic confinement

Inertial electrostatic confinement (IEC) is a scheme for nuclear fusion in which electric fields heat and confine a plasma. The best-known IEC device is the Farnsworth–Hirsch fusor, invented by Philo T. Farnsworth (with Robert Hirsch) in the 1960s: two concentric spherical electrode grids inside a vacuum chamber are biased so the inner grid is held at a large negative voltage. Ions of fusion fuel (usually deuterium) are accelerated toward the centre, pass through the grid, and oscillate back and forth, building up a dense core where some collide and fuse.

Performance and applications

Fusors readily achieve fusion reactions and are built by hobbyists and universities as neutron sources (deuterium–deuterium fusion yields 2.45 MeV neutrons), but grid collisions and other losses mean they consume far more power than the fusion releases; no IEC device has approached net energy gain. Research variants, such as the Polywell concept of Robert Bussard, aim to avoid grid losses by confining electrons magnetically. Commercial IEC neutron generators are used for materials analysis, explosives detection and medical isotope research, and IEC theory underlies some ion-thruster and plasma-processing devices.
