Decoupling (particle physics)

In quantum field theory, decoupling refers to the suppression of the effects of heavy particles (or high-energy degrees of freedom) on low-energy physics. Intuitively, a particle whose mass is far above the energy scale being probed can be created only virtually and for very short times, so its influence on low-energy observables is small and can be absorbed into the parameters of an effective field theory.

Appelquist–Carazzone theorem

The decoupling theorem of Thomas Appelquist and J. Carazzone (1975) gives precise conditions under which heavy particles decouple in a renormalizable gauge theory: if a particle's mass arises in a gauge-invariant way (for example from a bare mass term), then at momenta far below its mass its effects on the renormalization of couplings and light-particle amplitudes are suppressed by powers of the ratio of the scales, up to finite renormalizations. The heavy particle can then be "integrated out" and its residual effects described by higher-dimensional operators in the effective theory.

Exceptions and applications

Decoupling fails to be complete when the heavy mass is generated by spontaneous symmetry breaking with couplings that grow with the mass. The top quark, whose Yukawa coupling is proportional to its mass, leaves unsuppressed contributions to certain observables — most famously the ρ parameter and corrections to Higgs-boson couplings. Similarly, chiral fermions and anomalies can obstruct naive decoupling.

Decoupling underlies the hierarchical structure of effective field theories across physics: heavy quarks are integrated out step by step in QCD (heavy-quark effective theory), the W and Z bosons are integrated out to give Fermi's theory of weak interactions, and hypothetical heavy particles beyond the Standard Model are often assumed to decouple, leaving only subtle effective-operator signatures at accessible energies.
