Chivalry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chivalry, or the chivalric code, is an informal and varying code of conduct developed originally for European medieval warfare, associated with the institution of knighthood. The code was influenced by the Crusades, epics, "courtly literature" such as the works of Chrétien de Troyes, and the chivalric orders. The virtues associated with chivalry include justice, valour, courtesy (courtoisie), honour, piety and mercy, as well as a duty to protect the weak — women, children, and the church.

Origins and development

Chivalry emerged in the 11th and 12th centuries in France, developing from the mounted warrior class. As knights became a distinctive social order, the Church sought to Christianize their violence through crusading ideology and the Peace and Truce of God movements. Codifications followed: Ramon Llull's Book of the Order of Chivalry (1274–76) and Geoffroi de Charny's Book of Chivalry (c. 1350) described the ideal knight's life. Knights' behaviour was further shaped by the court, where the conventions of courtly love (fin'amor) enjoined service to a lady.

Ideals and reality

The core tenets of chivalry included fighting boldly for one's lord, showing mercy to defeated enemies who asked for it, protecting the church, keeping one's word, and being generous with largesse. In practice, knights often violated these ideals — chevauchées devastated civilian populations in the Hundred Years' War, and the code's protection applied mainly to members of the knightly class. Historian Johan Huizinga saw late medieval chivalry as a beautiful but escapist veneer over a violent age.

Decline and legacy

Gunpowder, professional infantry armies and the decline of the feudal order rendered the knight militarily obsolete by the 16th century, but "chivalry" survived as a social ideal of gentlemanly conduct. The modern word itself comes from the Old French chevalerie, from cheval (horse). Chivalric orders survive in modern orders of knighthood (e.g., the Order of the Garter, founded 1348), and the ethos persists in romance literature, the Arthurian legend's enduring popularity, and modern informal notions of gentlemanly behaviour.
