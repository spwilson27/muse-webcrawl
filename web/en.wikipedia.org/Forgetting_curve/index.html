Forgetting curve

The forgetting curve describes the exponential decrease in the brain's ability to retain newly learned information over time when no attempt is made to review or retain it. It was first described by the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus in his 1885 book Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology, based on experiments in which he memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tested his own retention at intervals.

Ebbinghaus's findings

Ebbinghaus found that forgetting proceeds very rapidly at first—a large fraction of newly learned material is lost within the first hour and the first day—and then levels off, so that what remains after the initial steep decline decays much more slowly. He also measured "savings": relearning forgotten material took less effort than the original learning, showing that some trace of the memory persisted.

Implications

The forgetting curve underlies the technique of spaced repetition, in which reviews are scheduled at expanding intervals to counteract forgetting. Modern replications, such as a 2015 study by Murre and Dros, have broadly confirmed the shape of Ebbinghaus's curve. Retention is improved by meaningful material, elaborative encoding, sleep, and repeated retrieval practice.
