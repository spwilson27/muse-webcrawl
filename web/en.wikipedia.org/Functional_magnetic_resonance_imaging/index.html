Functional magnetic resonance imaging

fMRI
TypeNeuroimaging technique
SignalBOLD (blood-oxygen-level dependent) contrast
DemonstratedEarly 1990s (Ogawa; Kwong et al.)
UsesBrain mapping, cognitive neuroscience, presurgical planning

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) measures brain activity by detecting changes in blood flow. It relies on the principle that cerebral blood flow and neuronal activation are coupled: when an area of the brain is in use, blood flow to that region increases.

The BOLD signal

fMRI most often uses blood-oxygen-level dependent (BOLD) contrast, discovered by Seiji Ogawa. Oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin have different magnetic properties, so changes in local oxygenation slightly alter the MR signal. Because the haemodynamic response lags neural activity by several seconds, fMRI has millimetre spatial resolution but poor temporal resolution compared with EEG.

Methods and criticism

Subjects perform tasks or rest ("resting-state fMRI") while scanners record volumes every 1–3 seconds; statistics identify voxels whose signal correlates with the task. The field has faced criticism over statistical practices, small samples, and reverse inference — inferring a mental process from activation of a non-selective region — leading to reforms in analysis standards.

Applications

fMRI is used in cognitive neuroscience (memory, language, perception), clinical presurgical mapping of eloquent cortex, psychiatry research, and brain–computer interfaces.
