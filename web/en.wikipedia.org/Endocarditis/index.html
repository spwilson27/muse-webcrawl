Endocarditis is an inflammation of the inner layer of the heart, the endocardium. It usually involves the heart valves. Other structures that may be involved include the interventricular septum, the chordae tendineae, the mural endocardium, or the surfaces of intracardiac devices. Endocarditis is characterised by lesions, known as vegetations, which are masses of platelets, fibrin, microcolonies of microorganisms, and scant inflammatory cells.

Causes and classification

The most common form is infective endocarditis, caused by bacteria (frequently Staphylococcus aureus and streptococci) or, less often, fungi entering the bloodstream and lodging on damaged or prosthetic valves. Risk factors include congenital heart defects, artificial valves, intravenous drug use, indwelling catheters and poor dental health. Non-infective forms include Libman–Sacks endocarditis, associated with systemic lupus erythematosus, and nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis in advanced malignancy.

Signs, symptoms and diagnosis

Presentation ranges from an acute, rapidly progressive illness with high fever to a subacute course with low-grade fever, weight loss, night sweats and anaemia. Classic peripheral signs include splinter haemorrhages, Janeway lesions, Osler's nodes and Roth's spots. Diagnosis rests on blood cultures and echocardiography, formalised in the modified Duke criteria.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment is with prolonged high-dose intravenous antibiotics, guided by culture results; surgery to repair or replace the valve is required when there is heart failure, uncontrolled infection or recurrent embolism. Even with modern treatment, mortality remains substantial. Antibiotic prophylaxis before certain dental or surgical procedures is now restricted to patients at highest risk.
