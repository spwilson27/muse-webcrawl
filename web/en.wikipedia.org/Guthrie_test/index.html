Guthrie test

Blood spots collected on filter paper for newborn screening.

The Guthrie test is a medical test performed on newborn infants to detect phenylketonuria (PKU), a metabolic disorder that causes intellectual disability if untreated. It was developed in 1961–62 by the American microbiologist Robert Guthrie and is one of the founding procedures of modern newborn screening.

Method

A few drops of blood are taken from the infant's heel a few days after birth and dried on filter paper (the "Guthrie card"). In the original bacterial inhibition assay, discs of the dried blood are placed on agar seeded with Bacillus subtilis and an inhibitor, β-2-thienylalanine; excess phenylalanine in the blood of an affected infant overcomes the inhibition, and bacterial growth around the disc signals a positive result.

Legacy

Because the test was cheap and could be automated, it enabled population-wide screening and early dietary treatment of PKU, preventing thousands of cases of severe disability. Tandem mass spectrometry has since expanded the same dried-blood-spot card to screen for dozens of metabolic and genetic conditions.
