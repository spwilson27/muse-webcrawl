Drum

The drum is a member of the percussion group of musical instruments. In the Hornbostel–Sachs classification system it is a membranophone: it consists of a membrane, called a drumhead, stretched over a shell and struck with the hands, sticks, mallets, or brushes to produce sound by vibration. Drums are among the world's oldest instruments and are found in virtually every culture.

Construction

A drum typically consists of a hollow shell — of wood, metal, fibreglass, or ceramic — with a membrane stretched over one or both ends. The head, traditionally animal skin and now often synthetic film, is held by hoops and tensioned by lugs or rope. Tuning the head changes the drum's pitch and tone. Some drums, such as the talking drum of West Africa, can be squeezed while playing to vary pitch and imitate speech tones.

Types

Common types include the snare drum, bass drum, and tom-toms of the Western drum kit; the conga, bongo, and djembe played with the hands; frame drums such as the bodhrán and tambourine (the latter with jingles); the tabla of India; the timpani of the orchestra, which are tuned to definite pitches; and ceremonial and ritual drums worldwide. Drums are also named in the classification of non-musical containers, such as steel barrels, by analogy of shape.

Uses

Beyond music, drums have served communication (including "talking drums" and military signalling), religious ritual, dance, theatre, and warfare. In modern popular music the drum kit — bass drum, snare, toms, hi-hat, and cymbals played by a single drummer — provides the rhythmic foundation of rock, pop, jazz, and many other genres. Drum machines and electronic pads have supplemented or replaced acoustic drums in much recorded music since the 1980s.
