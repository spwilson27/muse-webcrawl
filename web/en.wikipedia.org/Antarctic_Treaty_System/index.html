The Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) comprises the Antarctic Treaty and related agreements, which together regulate international relations with respect to Antarctica, Earth's only continent without a native human population. The treaty, signed in 1959 by 12 nations active in Antarctica during the International Geophysical Year, entered into force in 1961 and has since been acceded to by many more states.

Main provisions

The treaty establishes Antarctica as a scientific preserve devoted to peace and science. It prohibits military activity, nuclear explosions and the disposal of radioactive waste; guarantees freedom of scientific investigation and cooperation; and sets aside (but does not settle) territorial sovereignty disputes — seven states maintain territorial claims, which are neither recognised nor denied under the treaty. Signatories may inspect each other's stations to verify compliance.

Related agreements

The system includes the Convention for the Conservation of Antarctic Seals (1972), the Convention on the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR, 1980), and the Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty (1991, the "Madrid Protocol"), which designates Antarctica as a "natural reserve, devoted to peace and science" and prohibits all mineral resource activities other than scientific research.

Meetings and membership

Consultative parties — those demonstrating substantial scientific activity — participate in the annual Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meetings, which adopt measures, decisions and resolutions governing activities on the continent.
