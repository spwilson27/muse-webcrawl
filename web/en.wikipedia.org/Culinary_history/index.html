Culinary history

Culinary history is the study of the origins and development of cooking, foodways, and cuisines over time. It draws on archaeology, written recipes, trade records, and art to trace how ingredients, techniques, and tastes have spread and changed — from humanity's first use of fire to the globalized food culture of today.

Prehistory and antiquity

Control of fire, perhaps a million years ago, made cooking possible, expanding the human diet and aiding digestion. The Neolithic Revolution (~10,000 BCE) brought agriculture, animal domestication, bread, beer, and cheese. The oldest known recipes are cuneiform tablets from Mesopotamia (c. 1700 BCE). Roman gastronomy is documented in the cookbook attributed to Apicius, and banquets expressed wealth and status throughout the ancient world.

Middle Ages and early modern period

Medieval European cooking used heavy spicing, sweet-and-sour combinations, and elaborate presentation; manuscripts such as Le Viandier and The Forme of Cury record court cuisine. The Columbian Exchange after 1492 transformed diets worldwide: tomatoes, potatoes, maize, and chilies spread to the Old World, while wheat, sugar, coffee, and livestock went to the Americas. In the 17th–19th centuries, French haute cuisine was codified by chefs such as François Pierre La Varenne, Marie-Antoine Carême, and Auguste Escoffier.

Industrial era to present

Industrialization brought canning, refrigeration, railways, and restaurants to a mass public; cookbooks by writers like Mrs Beeton and Julia Child shaped home cooking. The late 20th century saw nouvelle cuisine, fusion cooking, and the molecular gastronomy of Ferran Adrià and Heston Blumenthal. Historians such as those contributing to Food in History (Reay Tannahill) and the journal Gastronomica have made food a serious academic subject, while globalization, migration, and media continue to reshape what and how the world eats.
