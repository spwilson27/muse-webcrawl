Echo chamber (media)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An echo chamber is a metaphorical description for a situation in which beliefs are amplified or reinforced by communication and repetition inside a closed system, insulated from rebuttal. In media studies, participants in an echo chamber mainly encounter opinions that coincide with their own, so their existing views are reinforced and alternative ideas go unchallenged.

Concept

The term plays on the acoustic echo chamber, where a sound reverberates. Scholars and commentators use it to describe partisan news outlets, social media feeds and online communities in which algorithms and self-selection filter out dissenting information. Related concepts include the filter bubble, described by Eli Pariser, and Cass Sunstein's work on group polarisation in Republic.com.

Evidence and debate

Empirical research on echo chambers is mixed. Some studies find that social media users are predominantly exposed to like-minded content and that this exposure can increase political polarisation; others find that most people encounter more diverse views online than offline, and that heavy echo-chamber effects are concentrated among a small, highly partisan minority. Researchers caution that selective exposure is partly a matter of individual choice rather than algorithmic imposition alone.

Effects

Echo chambers are argued to contribute to misinformation spread, declining trust in shared facts, and political polarisation, and have been blamed for phenomena ranging from conspiracy theory communities to radicalisation. Suggested remedies include media literacy education, platform design changes and exposure to cross-cutting viewpoints.
