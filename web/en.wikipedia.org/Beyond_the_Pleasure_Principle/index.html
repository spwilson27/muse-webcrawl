Beyond the Pleasure Principle

Beyond the Pleasure Principle (German: Jenseits des Lustprinzips) is a 1920 essay by Sigmund Freud. It marks a turning point in Freud's theoretical work: although the work is often described as dealing with the pleasure principle, its actual argument introduces the idea of a death drive (German: Todestrieb) opposed to the life instincts, together with the concept of repetition compulsion.

Argument

Freud begins from the pleasure principle — the tendency of the mental apparatus to seek pleasure and avoid unpleasure — but notes phenomena that seem to contradict it: the recurring nightmares of traumatic neurosis (as seen in shell-shocked soldiers of the First World War), children's games that restage distressing experiences, and patients' tendency in analysis to repeat painful early experiences rather than remember them.

The famous example is the fort/da game of Freud's grandson, who repeatedly threw away a cotton reel on a string while saying fort ("gone") and pulled it back with a joyful da ("there"), restaging his mother's departures and thereby converting passive suffering into active mastery. Yet repetition seemed to Freud even more fundamental: he concluded that drives are inherently conservative, tending to restore an earlier state, and postulated a drive toward death and inorganic stillness — Thanatos — in perpetual conflict with Eros, the life drive.

Influence

The essay inaugurated Freud's final dual-drive theory and reshaped subsequent psychoanalysis, influencing his The Ego and the Id (1923) and Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), as well as later thinkers from Melanie Klein to Jacques Lacan and literary and cultural theory.
