Earmark (politics)

An earmark is a provision in United States congressional appropriations legislation that directs approved funds to be spent on specific projects, or that directs exemption from taxes or mandated fees. Earmarks allow legislators to steer federal money to projects in their own districts or states, and are a classic instrument of what critics call pork barrel spending.

Definition and practice

Unlike formula-based spending, an earmark names a recipient or project directly — for example, funds for a particular bridge, hospital or defence contractor. Supporters argue earmarks are a legitimate exercise of Congress's constitutional power of the purse and let elected representatives, rather than executive agencies, set priorities.

Controversy and reform

Critics associate earmarks with waste and corruption; the proposed "Bridge to Nowhere" in Alaska became a national symbol of the practice in 2005. Disclosure rules were tightened in 2007, and in 2011 both chambers adopted a moratorium on earmarks.

Restoration

In 2021 Congress restored the practice under new names with transparency requirements: members must post requests publicly, certify no financial interest, and total earmarks are capped at 1% of discretionary spending.
