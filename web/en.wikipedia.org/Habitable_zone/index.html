Habitable zone

Habitable zone
Also known asGoldilocks zone; circumstellar habitable zone (CHZ)
Solar System extentRoughly 0.95–1.67 AU (conservative estimates vary)
Key conditionStellar flux allows liquid water on a rocky planet's surface

In astronomy and astrobiology, the habitable zone (HZ), also called the Goldilocks zone, is the range of orbits around a star within which a planetary surface can support liquid water given sufficient atmospheric pressure. Liquid water is considered essential for life as we know it, so planets in this zone — especially Earth-sized rocky planets — are prime targets in the search for extraterrestrial life.

Definition and limits

The inner edge of the zone is set by the runaway greenhouse effect and loss of water to space; the outer edge is set by the maximum greenhouse warming that carbon dioxide can provide before it condenses out of the atmosphere. For the Sun, conservative estimates place the zone at roughly 0.95 to 1.37 AU and optimistic (empirical) estimates extend it from about 0.75 AU (based on early Venus) to 1.77 AU (based on early Mars). Earth's orbit, at 1 AU, sits within it.

The location of the zone scales with stellar luminosity and temperature: around small red dwarfs it lies very close to the star (raising issues of tidal locking and flare activity), while around hot, massive stars it is much farther out but the stars' short lifetimes limit the time available for life to evolve.

Exoplanet discoveries

NASA's Kepler mission showed that Earth-sized planets in habitable zones are common — occurring around a substantial fraction of stars — and raised the estimated number of such planets in the Milky Way to the billions. Notable systems include Proxima Centauri b (the nearest exoplanet, in its star's habitable zone, 4.24 light-years away), the TRAPPIST-1 system with several Earth-sized zone planets, and Kepler-186f, the first Earth-sized habitable-zone planet confirmed around another star (2014). Follow-up work with radial-velocity surveys and the James Webb Space Telescope aims to characterise their atmospheres.

Limitations of the concept

The classical habitable zone assumes Earth-like planets with carbon dioxide/water/nitrogen atmospheres and a functioning carbon–silicate cycle. Subsurface oceans (as on Europa and Enceladus, far outside the Sun's classical zone), hydrogen-rich atmospheres, and tidal heating can extend habitability well beyond it, so many researchers view the zone as a useful first filter rather than a strict requirement.
