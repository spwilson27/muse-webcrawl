Opportunity cost

Opportunity cost
FieldMicroeconomics
DefinitionValue of the next-best alternative forgone when a decision is made
Related conceptsScarcity, trade-off, production–possibility frontier

In microeconomic theory, opportunity cost is the value of the alternative that must be forgone in order to pursue a certain action, or the benefits a person, investor or business misses out on when choosing one alternative over another. Put another way, it is the loss of potential gain from other alternatives when one alternative is chosen.

Opportunity cost is a key concept in economics, and has been described as expressing "the basic relationship between scarcity and choice". If the next best use of time and resources is identified, the concept ensures that scarce resources are used efficiently.

Definition and examples

Formally, the opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative not taken. If a person spends $5 on a book rather than a sandwich, the opportunity cost is the sandwich (and the enjoyment it would have given). For a student, attending university has an opportunity cost in forgone earnings from employment. In finance, investments carry opportunity costs relative to the best alternative return available at similar risk.

History

The term was coined in 1914 by the Austrian economist Friedrich von Wieser in his Theorie der gesellschaftlichen Wirtschaft (Theory of Social Economics), building on earlier ideas in the work of John Stuart Mill and the Austrian School. The concept contrasts with sunk costs, which are already incurred and should not affect rational decisions.

Explicit and implicit costs

Economists distinguish explicit costs (direct monetary payments) from implicit costs (the value of resources used without payment, such as the owner's own time). Opportunity cost comprises both, which is why economic profit differs from accounting profit.
