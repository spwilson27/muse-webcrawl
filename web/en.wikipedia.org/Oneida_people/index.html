The Oneida people (Oneida: Onyota'a:ka, "the people of the standing stone") are a Native American people and one of the five founding nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), historically located in what is now central New York state, around Oneida Lake and the upper Mohawk Valley.

History

The Oneida traditionally held a territory of about six million acres. During the American Revolutionary War the Oneida (along with the Tuscarora, who had joined the Confederacy as the sixth nation) broke with most of the Haudenosaunee and allied with the American colonists, supplying warriors and corn to Washington's army, including during the winter at Valley Forge — a contribution carried by the Oneida woman Polly Cooper. Despite this alliance, postwar New York State pressed the Oneida into a series of land cessions that reduced their holdings to a few hundred acres by the 19th century.

Removal and present nations

In the 1820s–1830s many Oneida removed to Wisconsin, establishing the community that is now the Oneida Nation of Wisconsin near Green Bay; another group moved to Ontario, becoming the Oneida Nation of the Thames. Those who remained in New York form the Oneida Indian Nation, which in the late 20th century reasserted sovereignty — the U.S. Supreme Court's 1985 Oneida County v. Oneida Indian Nation decisions recognized its historic land claim — and developed enterprises including the Turning Stone Resort Casino. Land-claim settlements and agreements with New York State were reached in 2013.

Culture

Traditional Oneida society was matrilineal, organized into three clans — Wolf, Bear, and Turtle — with clan mothers holding significant authority. The Oneida language, a Northern Iroquoian language, is critically endangered but is the subject of active revitalization programs in all three communities.
