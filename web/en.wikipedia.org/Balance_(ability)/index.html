Balance (ability)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Balance, in biomechanics and physiology, is the ability to maintain the line of gravity (vertical line through the centre of mass) of a body within its base of support with minimal postural sway. It depends on the integration of visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive (somatosensory) information by the central nervous system, and on coordinated muscular responses. Balance is a component of all movement, from standing still to elite athletic performance.

Mechanisms

Three sensory systems inform the brain about body position: the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear detects head acceleration; vision reports orientation relative to the environment; and proprioceptors in muscles, joints, and the skin report limb position and pressure distribution. The cerebellum and brainstem integrate these signals and trigger automatic postural responses — ankle, hip, and stepping strategies — that return the centre of mass over the feet. Balance can be static (maintaining a position), dynamic (maintaining control while moving), or reactive (recovering from a disturbance).

Impairment and assessment

Balance deteriorates with ageing and is impaired by conditions such as vestibular disorders (e.g. Ménière's disease, benign paroxysmal positional vertigo), stroke, Parkinson's disease, peripheral neuropathy, and concussion. Poor balance is a major risk factor for falls in older adults. Clinicians assess it with tests such as the Romberg test, the Berg Balance Scale, the Timed Up and Go test, and force-plate posturography. Balance training — including tai chi, strength and proprioceptive exercises — can reduce fall risk and is widely used in rehabilitation and fall-prevention programmes.
