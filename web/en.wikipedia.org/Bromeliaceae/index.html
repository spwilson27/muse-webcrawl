Bromeliaceae

The Bromeliaceae (the bromeliads) are a family of monocot flowering plants comprising some 80 genera and more than 3,700 species, native mainly to the tropical and subtropical Americas, with a single species (Pitcairnia feliciana) in West Africa. The family, placed in the order Poales, ranges from desert-dwelling terrestrial xerophytes to rain-forest epiphytes; its best-known member is the pineapple (Ananas comosus), the source of the enzyme bromelain, and its most unusual is Spanish moss (Tillandsia usneoides).

Characteristics

Bromeliads typically bear stiff, often spiny-margined leaves arranged in a spiral rosette, frequently forming a central cup or "tank" that collects water and debris, which the plant absorbs through specialised scales called trichomes. Many species are epiphytes that anchor to trees without parasitising them, while the "air plants" of the genus Tillandsia absorb nearly all their water and nutrients through their leaves. Most bromeliads use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), opening their stomata at night to conserve water.

Ecology and uses

Tank bromeliads create miniature ecosystems whose pools shelter frogs, insects, and other invertebrates found nowhere else. Humans cultivate bromeliads as ornamentals worldwide for their long-lasting, vividly coloured flower spikes and bracts; historically, fibres from several species supplied textiles, and the family gave pineapple — the only bromeliad grown commercially for fruit — to world agriculture after Columbus encountered it in the Caribbean in 1493 and carried it back to Europe.
