Sir Alfred Joseph Hitchcock (13 August 1899 – 29 April 1980) was an English film director and producer widely regarded as one of the most influential filmmakers in cinema history. Known as the "Master of Suspense", he directed more than 50 feature films over six decades, pioneering techniques of suspense, voyeuristic camerawork and psychological tension.

British career

Hitchcock began in silent films in London, breaking through with The Lodger (1927) and Blackmail (1929), Britain's first "talkie". His 1930s thrillers — The Man Who Knew Too Much (1934), The 39 Steps (1935) and The Lady Vanishes (1938) — brought him to Hollywood's attention.

Hollywood

In America, Hitchcock's Rebecca (1940) won the Academy Award for Best Picture. A string of classics followed: Shadow of a Doubt (1943), Notorious (1946), Strangers on a Train (1951), Rear Window (1954), Vertigo (1958), North by Northwest (1959), Psycho (1960) and The Birds (1963). His television series Alfred Hitchcock Presents made his profile, voice and macabre humour familiar worldwide. Later films included Marnie (1964), Frenzy (1972) and his final feature, Family Plot (1976).

Style and legacy

Hitchcock's hallmarks include the MacGuffin, the innocent man on the run, meticulous storyboarding, cameo appearances in his own films, and suspense generated by giving the audience information the characters lack. Initially dismissed by some critics as an entertainer, he was reappraised by the French New Wave — notably in François Truffaut's 1966 book-length interview — and Vertigo topped the BFI's Sight and Sound critics' poll of the greatest films ever made in 2012. He was knighted shortly before his death in 1980.
