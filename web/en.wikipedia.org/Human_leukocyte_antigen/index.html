Human leukocyte antigen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system is a complex of genes on chromosome 6 in humans that encode cell-surface proteins responsible for regulating the immune system. The HLA system is the human version of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC), found in most vertebrates, and is the most polymorphic gene complex in the human genome.

[Image: Diagram of an HLA class I molecule presenting a peptide to a T cell]

Classes

HLA proteins fall into two main classes. Class I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) are found on nearly all nucleated cells and present peptides from inside the cell to cytotoxic T cells. Class II molecules (HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR) are found on antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells and macrophages and present peptides from outside the cell to helper T cells.

Functions

By displaying peptide fragments, HLA molecules allow the immune system to distinguish self from non-self and to mount responses against pathogens. The enormous diversity of HLA alleles in the population improves the species' ability to resist varied infectious diseases.

Clinical significance

HLA matching is critical in organ and stem-cell transplantation, where mismatches increase the risk of rejection and graft-versus-host disease. Particular HLA variants are associated with autoimmune diseases — such as HLA-B27 with ankylosing spondylitis — with adverse drug reactions, and with susceptibility or resistance to infections. HLA typing is also used in anthropology and forensic science.
