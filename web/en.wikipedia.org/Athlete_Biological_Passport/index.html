Athlete Biological Passport

The Athlete Biological Passport (ABP) is an individual, electronic record of an athlete's biological markers collected over time, used in anti-doping to detect the effects of doping indirectly rather than by identifying a prohibited substance itself. Introduced by the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA), the passport's first module — the haematological module, targeting blood doping — came into operation in 2009; a steroidal module followed in 2014.

How it works

Rather than a single threshold, the ABP uses a Bayesian statistical model to build an individual reference range for each athlete from their own previous results. For blood, markers include haemoglobin concentration and reticulocyte percentage; abnormal values that fall outside the athlete's expected range with a very high level of statistical specificity are flagged. An expert panel reviews flagged profiles, considering altitude exposure, illness, and other confounders, before any anti-doping rule violation is alleged.

Use and cases

The Union Cycliste Internationale was an early adopter, and ABP evidence has supported sanctions against athletes in cycling, athletics, and other endurance sports even without a positive test for a specific substance. Results management is handled by Athlete Passport Management Units (APMUs) attached to WADA-accredited laboratories. The passport complements traditional testing by deterring micro-dosing and autologous blood transfusions that direct tests miss.

Criticism

Commentators have raised questions about cost, the transparency of the statistical model, natural variability of biomarkers, and due process, though sports arbitration panels have repeatedly upheld ABP-based sanctions.
