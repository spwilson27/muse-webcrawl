Angevin Empire

The Angevin Empire is a modern term for the collection of territories ruled by the Plantagenet kings of England between 1154 and 1214 — and in diminished form until the mid-13th century. At its height it stretched from the Scottish border to the Pyrenees, comprising England, Normandy, Anjou, Maine, Touraine, Aquitaine, and Gascony, with claims of overlordship over Wales, Scotland, Brittany, and Ireland.

Formation

The empire was assembled by Henry II: he inherited Anjou from his father Geoffrey Plantagenet, gained Normandy and England through his mother Empress Matilda, and acquired Aquitaine through marriage to Eleanor of Aquitaine in 1152. His son Richard I (the Lionheart) spent much of his reign on crusade and defending the territories against Philip II of France.

Collapse

Under King John, the empire unravelled: Philip II conquered Normandy, Anjou, and Maine in 1204, and John's attempt to recover them ended with defeat at Bouvines in 1214. The Treaty of Paris (1259) confined English holdings in France essentially to Gascony, which England retained until 1453.

The "empire" had no common institutions; it was a personal union of lands held under different titles, administered separately by an itinerant court.
