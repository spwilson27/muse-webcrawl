Pericardium

The pericardium (from Greek peri, "around", and cardion, "heart") is a double-walled sac containing the heart and the roots of the great vessels. It consists of an outer fibrous pericardium and an inner serous pericardium, the latter having parietal and visceral layers enclosing the pericardial cavity.

Structure

The fibrous pericardium is a tough connective tissue sac fused inferiorly with the central tendon of the diaphragm and continuous with the adventitia of the great vessels. The visceral layer of the serous pericardium, also called the epicardium, adheres directly to the heart surface. The pericardial cavity normally contains 15–50 ml of serous fluid that lubricates the beating heart.

Function

The pericardium anchors the heart in the mediastinum, limits its acute distension, reduces friction, and provides a barrier against infection. It also influences the pressure relationships between the cardiac chambers.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the pericardium is called pericarditis. Accumulation of fluid in the sac (pericardial effusion) can compress the heart — cardiac tamponade — requiring emergency drainage by pericardiocentesis. Chronic inflammation may produce constrictive pericarditis.
