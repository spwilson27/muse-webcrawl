Ultra-prominent peak

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ultra-prominent peak, or Ultra for short, is a mountain summit with a topographic prominence of 1,500 metres (4,921 ft) or more; it is also called a P1500. The prominence of a peak is the minimum height of climb to the summit on any route from a higher peak, or from sea level if there is no higher peak. There are approximately 1,524 such peaks on Earth.

Some well-known Ultras include Mount Everest, Aconcagua, Denali, Mount Kilimanjaro and Mont Blanc. Many famous peaks that are not Ultras include K2 (prominence about 4,020 m — actually an Ultra), but also Mont Blanc's neighbours and peaks such as the Eiger and Kangchenjunga's subsidiary summits, whose prominence is limited by their connection to higher ground. Ultras are important to peak baggers, who attempt to climb all Ultras in a given region.

Definition

Topographic prominence measures the height of a mountain's summit relative to the lowest contour line encircling it but containing no higher summit within it. A threshold of 1,500 metres was chosen because it is a round number that yields a manageable list and roughly corresponds to the greatest prominence found in regions such as the British Isles. The term "Ultra" was coined by earth scientist Stephen Fry in the 1980s, from his study of the prominence of peaks in Washington state, where he originally applied it to peaks with at least 5,000 feet (1,524 m) of prominence.

Distribution

There are 1,524 Ultras on Earth, distributed across all continents. The greatest concentrations are in the great cordilleras of Asia (the Himalayas, Karakoram, and Hindu Kush), the Andes of South America, and the ranges of western North America. Europe has 113 Ultras, while Oceania and the polar islands contribute island high points, many of which are Ultras because they rise directly from the sea.

Peak bagging

Climbing all the Ultras of a region is a recognised peak-bagging objective. Several mountaineers have climbed all the Ultras of Europe or of Britain, and lists of ascents are maintained by prominence enthusiasts. Because Ultras are by definition separated from higher ground, the list emphasises independent mountains rather than subsidiary summits.
