Robert Henryson (Middle Scots: Robert Henrysoun; c. 1425 – c. 1500) was a Scottish poet who flourished in the second half of the fifteenth century and is regarded as one of the great makars of Middle Scots literature, alongside William Dunbar, who names him, with respect, in his Lament for the Makaris. Henryson appears to have been schoolmaster of the grammar school attached to Dunfermline Abbey and also practised as a notary public and solicitor.

Works

Three major works survive. The Testament of Cresseid (c. 1490s) continues the story of Chaucer's Criseyde, following her after Troilus's betrayal: stricken with leprosy and abandoned, she dies after composing a bitter "testament". It is celebrated for its tragic seriousness and was for centuries printed as Chaucer's own work, appearing in editions of Chaucer down to the eighteenth century. The Morall Fabillis of Esope the Phrygian retell thirteen of Aesop's fables in Scots verse with extended moralizations, displaying wit, sympathy for the poor, and sharp social observation. Orpheus and Euridice adapts the classical myth through the medieval commentary tradition. A handful of shorter poems are also attributed to him.

Reputation

Henryson's poetry combines Chaucerian inheritance with native Scottish tradition, legal training, and human compassion. Seamus Heaney, who translated The Testament of Cresseid, praised its "wintry" power. The exact dates of his birth and death are unknown; tradition recorded by Dunbar suggests he died before about 1505.
