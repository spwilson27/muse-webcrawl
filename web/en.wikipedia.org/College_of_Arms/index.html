The College of Arms (also known as the College of Heralds) is a royal corporation in London consisting of professional officers of arms who act, on behalf of the Crown, in matters of heraldry, genealogy and state ceremony. It is the official heraldic authority for England, Wales, Northern Ireland and some Commonwealth realms, including Australia and New Zealand, though its authority there is not exclusive in practice.

History

Kings of arms and heralds served English monarchs from at least the thirteenth century, regulating armorial bearings and organising tournaments and ceremonial. In 1484 Richard III incorporated the heralds as a college and granted them a house, Coldharbour, in the City of London. The college lost its charter under Henry VII but was reincorporated by Mary I and Philip in 1555, which also granted its present site. Its seventeenth-century building, erected after the Great Fire of London, survives today. From the sixteenth to the nineteenth centuries the heralds carried out county-by-county "visitations" recording pedigrees and arms, a rich source for genealogists.

Officers

The college is headed by the Earl Marshal, an office hereditary in the Duke of Norfolk. The thirteen officers of arms in ordinary comprise three kings of arms — Garter Principal King of Arms, Clarenceux King of Arms (for England south of the Trent) and Norroy and Ulster King of Arms (for the north and Northern Ireland) — six heralds (including Chester, Lancaster, Richmond, Somerset, Windsor and York) and four pursuivants (Bluemantle, Portcullis, Rouge Croix and Rouge Dragon). Officers are appointed by the Crown and also maintain private practices in heraldic and genealogical work.

Functions

The College of Arms grants new coats of arms to individuals and corporate bodies by letters patent, records pedigrees, advises on flags, badges and name changes by royal licence, and helps organise state ceremonial such as coronations and state funerals. Claims to existing arms and disputes over armorial bearings fall under the jurisdiction of the Court of Chivalry, which sits very rarely. Scotland has its own distinct authority, the Lord Lyon King of Arms.
