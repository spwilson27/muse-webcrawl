Naturalist

A naturalist is a person who studies or is an expert in natural history, particularly the observation of organisms and ecosystems in their environments. Before the professionalisation of science in the 19th century, naturalists were the principal investigators of the living world, combining fieldwork, collection, classification, and illustration.

History

The tradition of natural history stretches from Aristotle and Pliny the Elder through the Renaissance herbalists to the great systematist Carl Linnaeus, whose binomial nomenclature (1735–1758) organised the flood of new species revealed by global exploration. Voyaging naturalists such as Joseph Banks (aboard HMS Endeavour), Alexander von Humboldt, Charles Darwin (aboard HMS Beagle), and Alfred Russel Wallace gathered the observations that underpinned the theory of evolution by natural selection.

Roles

Many historical naturalists were clergymen, physicians, or wealthy amateurs — the "parson-naturalist" Gilbert White, author of The Natural History of Selborne (1789), is a classic example. Women naturalists such as Maria Sibylla Merian and Mary Anning made foundational contributions despite exclusion from scientific institutions. As biology specialised into disciplines like botany, zoology, and geology, the generalist naturalist gave way to professional scientists.

Modern usage

Today the term describes skilled field observers, museum and conservation staff, science communicators, and citizen scientists. Naturalists remain essential to biodiversity monitoring, ecological fieldwork, and the interpretation of protected areas such as nature reserves.
