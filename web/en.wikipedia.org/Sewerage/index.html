Sewerage

TypeInfrastructure
ComponentsSewers, manholes, pumping stations, outfalls
Types of systemCombined, separate (sanitary + storm), simplified
PurposeConvey wastewater to treatment or disposal

Sewerage (or a sewage system) is the infrastructure that conveys sewage — wastewater from homes, businesses and industries, and in some systems stormwater runoff — through networks of pipes, chambers and pumping stations to a point of treatment or disposal. The network of pipes itself is called the sewer system, and its construction, operation and maintenance are core tasks of municipal engineering and public-health protection.

Types of systems

In a combined sewer system, one pipe carries both sanitary sewage and stormwater; during heavy rain, combined sewer overflows (CSOs) may discharge diluted sewage directly to waterways, a persistent source of pollution that cities such as London are addressing with giant storage tunnels (the Thames Tideway Tunnel). In separate systems, sanitary sewers carry wastewater to treatment while storm drains discharge rainwater separately. Where gravity flow is impossible, pumping stations lift sewage; pressure and vacuum sewers serve flat or rocky terrain. Many low-income areas use simplified sewerage with small-diameter shallow pipes, or have no sewers at all, relying on on-site sanitation such as pit latrines and septic tanks.

History

Covered drains existed in the Indus Valley civilisation and in ancient Rome, whose Cloaca Maxima still partly functions. However, systematic sewerage arrived only in the mid-19th century, when Hamburg (1842) and then London, under engineer Joseph Bazalgette after the Great Stink of 1858, built comprehensive networks in response to cholera and typhoid. These systems, and the sewage treatment that followed, produced enormous gains in urban life expectancy.

Operation and problems

Modern sewerage faces ageing pipes, infiltration of groundwater, blockages caused by fats, oils and grease ("fatbergs") and wet wipes, odour and corrosion from hydrogen sulphide, and climate-driven increases in storm flows. Management increasingly uses CCTV inspection, hydraulic modelling and "smart" real-time control.
