History of Puerto Rico

The history of Puerto Rico begins with the settlement of the archipelago by Amerindian peoples, principally the Taíno, followed by Spanish colonization after Columbus's arrival in 1493. Puerto Rico remained a Spanish colony for four centuries until it was ceded to the United States in 1898 after the Spanish–American War; it remains a U.S. territory today.

Indigenous and early colonial period

The Taíno called the island Borikén. Columbus landed on 19 November 1493, and Juan Ponce de León founded the first Spanish settlement, Caparra, in 1508. Disease, forced labor in the encomienda system, and conflict devastated the Taíno population within decades. San Juan, founded in 1521, became a key defensive port for Spain's American empire, guarded by the fortresses of El Morro and San Cristóbal.

19th century

The Royal Decree of Graces of 1815 encouraged immigration and commerce, transforming Puerto Rico into a producer of sugar, coffee, and tobacco. Abolitionist and autonomist movements grew; slavery was abolished in 1873, and in 1897 Spain granted Puerto Rico an autonomic charter with substantial self-government.

American period

Under the Treaty of Paris (1898), Puerto Rico passed to the United States. The Foraker Act (1900) established civilian government, and the Jones Act (1917) granted Puerto Ricans U.S. citizenship. In 1952 Puerto Rico became a Commonwealth (Estado Libre Asociado) under its own constitution. The island's political status — statehood, independence, or continued commonwealth — remains debated in periodic plebiscites.

Recent history

The 2016 PROMESA act imposed a federal oversight board amid a debt crisis. Hurricane Maria in September 2017 caused catastrophic damage and thousands of deaths, prompting a prolonged recovery. Mass protests in 2019 forced Governor Ricardo Rosselló to resign.
