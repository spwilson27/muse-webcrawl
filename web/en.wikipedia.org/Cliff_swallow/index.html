American cliff swallow

Scientific namePetrochelidon pyrrhonota
FamilyHirundinidae (swallows)
LengthAbout 14 cm (5.5 in)
RangeBreeds in North America; winters in South America
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The American cliff swallow (Petrochelidon pyrrhonota) is a member of the swallow family of birds. Like other swallows, it is an aerial insectivore, catching flying insects on the wing. It breeds across North America and migrates to South America for the winter. The species is famous for its gourd-shaped mud nests, built in large colonies on cliff faces and, increasingly, on bridges, dams, and the eaves of buildings.

Description

The cliff swallow averages about 14 cm in length, with a steel-blue back and crown, a pale buff forehead patch, chestnut cheeks and throat, dark wings and tail, and a distinctive pale buff rump. The tail is square-ended. Sexes are similar. It is distinguished from the similar cave swallow by its dark throat and pale forehead.

Breeding and nests

Cliff swallows return to their colonies in spring, often to the same site used in previous years. Both sexes gather pellets of wet mud in their beaks and build a retort-shaped nest with a small tubular entrance, attached to a vertical surface beneath an overhang. Colonies may number hundreds or thousands of nests packed side by side. The female lays three to five eggs, which both parents incubate. The species is a classic subject for studies of coloniality, brood parasitism, and social information transfer among birds.

Folklore

The annual return of swallows to Mission San Juan Capistrano in California — popularly associated with cliff swallows — became a famous folk tradition, celebrated each March on St. Joseph's Day, though the birds have become less regular visitors as the area has urbanised.
