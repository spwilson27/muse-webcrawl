Little's law

In queueing theory, Little's law (also Little's theorem or Little's result) is a theorem by John Little which states that the long-term average number of customers in a stationary system L is equal to the long-term average effective arrival rate λ multiplied by the average time a customer spends in the system W:

L = λW

Significance

The result is remarkable for its generality: it holds for almost any queueing system, regardless of the arrival process, service distribution, or scheduling discipline, provided the system is stationary. It requires no knowledge of probability distributions — only averages.

Applications

Little's law is used in operations management, telecommunications, computer systems performance analysis, and lean manufacturing, where it relates work-in-process inventory, throughput, and cycle time. It also underlies simple estimates such as inferring the average number of shoppers in a store from arrival rates and average visit durations.

History

Although the result was used intuitively earlier, the first rigorous proof was published by John D. C. Little in 1961 in the journal Operations Research.
