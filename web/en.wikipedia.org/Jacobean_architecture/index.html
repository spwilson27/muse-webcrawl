Jacobean architecture

Jacobean architecture is the second phase of Renaissance architecture in England, following the Elizabethan style and named after King James I of England (Latin: Jacobus), whose reign (1603–1625) it roughly spans. It is characterised by the free application of classical orders and details — often drawn from Flemish and German pattern books rather than from Italy directly — to buildings whose underlying plans and massing remained essentially late-medieval, with strapwork ornament, shaped gables and large mullioned and transomed windows.

Characteristics

Jacobean great houses, the so-called "prodigy houses", display a love of display: symmetrical fronts (at least in intention), flat roofs with parapets and strapwork cresting, turrets and cupolas, columns and pilasters used decoratively, and elaborate carved stonework and plasterwork inside, with vast fireplaces and long galleries. The style continued and developed Elizabethan trends rather than breaking with them, and rustic, Gothic and classical motifs were often mixed freely.

Notable examples

  • Hatfield House, Hertfordshire (1607–1612), built for Robert Cecil.
  • Audley End, Essex, once among the largest houses in England.
  • Knole, Kent, remodelled in the Jacobean period.
  • Charlton House, London (1607–1612), one of the best-preserved Jacobean houses in the capital.
  • Holland House and Crewe Hall also show the style.

The period also saw the first work of Inigo Jones — the Queen's House at Greenwich (begun 1616) and the Banqueting House, Whitehall (1619–1622) — whose disciplined Italian Palladianism pointed away from Jacobean eclecticism toward a purer classicism.

Colonial Jacobean

The term is also applied to the earliest colonial architecture in British America, such as Bacon's Castle in Virginia (1665), the oldest surviving brick dwelling in the United States, with its characteristic shaped gables and triple chimney stacks.
