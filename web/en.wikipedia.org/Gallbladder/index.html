Gallbladder

In vertebrates, the gallbladder, also known as the cholecyst, is a small hollow organ that stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver before releasing it into the small intestine to aid digestion of fats. In humans the pear-shaped organ, about 7–10 cm long, sits in a shallow depression beneath the right lobe of the liver. Some species, including rats and horses, lack a gallbladder entirely.

Structure and function

The gallbladder consists of a fundus, body and neck, its wall lined with absorptive epithelium thrown into folds. Between meals it stores bile and concentrates it up to tenfold by absorbing water and electrolytes. After eating, the hormone cholecystokinin triggers contraction, expelling bile through the cystic duct into the common bile duct and hence the duodenum.

Clinical significance

The most common gallbladder disease is gallstones (cholelithiasis), which can obstruct the ducts and cause biliary colic, inflammation of the gallbladder (cholecystitis) or pancreatitis. Definitive treatment is usually surgical removal, cholecystectomy, most often performed laparoscopically and one of the commonest abdominal operations; people live normally without a gallbladder, though bile then drips continuously into the intestine.
