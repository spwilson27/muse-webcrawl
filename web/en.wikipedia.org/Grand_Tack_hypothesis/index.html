Grand Tack Hypothesis

The Grand Tack hypothesis proposes that after its formation in the gas disk of the early Solar System, the planet Jupiter migrated inward to a distance of about 1.5 AU (astronomical units) from the Sun, before reversing course and migrating outward again to its present location at 5.2 AU. The reversal of direction, analogous to the tacking of a sailboat, is proposed to have occurred after Saturn formed and migrated inward, becoming locked in an orbital resonance with Jupiter.

The hypothesis was first proposed in 2011 by Kevin Walsh, Alessandro Morbidelli, Sean Raymond, David O'Brien and Avi Mandell. It was developed to explain several otherwise puzzling features of the inner Solar System.

Features explained

The inward migration of Jupiter would have truncated the disk of material from which the terrestrial planets formed at about 1 AU. This explains why Mars is much smaller than Earth and Venus: rather than growing from an extended disk, Mars accreted from the truncated edge of the disk and was starved of material.

The "tack" also scatters planetesimals in both directions. Rocky material from the inner disk is scattered outward, while primitive, water-rich bodies from beyond Jupiter's original orbit are scattered inward. This mixing provides a natural explanation for the composition of the asteroid belt, which contains both dry, thermally processed asteroids and volatile-rich bodies, and for the delivery of water to the growing Earth.

Migration mechanism

In a gaseous protoplanetary disk, a growing giant planet exchanges angular momentum with the surrounding gas and tends to migrate. Jupiter is thought to have migrated inward (Type II migration) until Saturn, forming later and migrating faster, caught up and locked into a 3:2 mean-motion resonance. In this configuration the combined torque on the two planets can reverse, pushing both outward — the "tack" — until the gas disk dissipated.

Relation to other models

The Grand Tack is often discussed alongside the Nice model, which describes a later instability in the outer Solar System, and more recent "early instability" variants. Critics note that the hypothesis depends on particular assumptions about disk viscosity and the timing of Saturn's formation, and alternative models such as pebble accretion have been proposed to explain the small mass of Mars without large-scale migration of Jupiter.
