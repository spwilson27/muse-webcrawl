Septuagint

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Septuagint (from Latin septuāgintā, "seventy"), often abbreviated LXX, is the earliest extant Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible and related texts. Produced in Alexandria, Egypt, beginning in the 3rd century BC — the Torah first, the remaining books over the following two centuries — it served the Greek-speaking Jewish diaspora and became the Old Testament of the early Christian Church.

Origin legend

The Letter of Aristeas recounts that Ptolemy II Philadelphus commissioned 72 Jewish elders to translate the Torah for the Library of Alexandria, and that they produced identical versions — hence "the Seventy." Whatever the legend's embellishments, Alexandria's large Jewish community genuinely needed the scriptures in Greek.

Contents and influence

The Septuagint includes books not in the Hebrew canon — the deuterocanonical books such as Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, and 1–2 Maccabees — and sometimes differs substantially from the Masoretic Text (its Jeremiah is much shorter, for example). New Testament authors quote it extensively, and it remains the official Old Testament of the Eastern Orthodox Church. Key manuscripts include Codex Vaticanus, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Alexandrinus.
