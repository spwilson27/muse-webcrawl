Galaxy Zoo is a crowdsourced astronomy project that invites members of the public to classify the morphologies of large numbers of galaxies from telescope survey images. Launched in July 2007 by researchers including Kevin Schawinski and Chris Lintott, the project was created because classifying roughly 900,000 galaxies from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) by eye was impractical for the small research team — yet human classification was more reliable than the automated algorithms of the time.

History and results

Within 24 hours of launch the site was receiving 70,000 classifications per hour; over its first year more than 150,000 volunteers contributed over 50 million classifications. Galaxy Zoo data releases have supported dozens of peer-reviewed papers on galaxy evolution, including findings that red spirals are common, that bars are more frequent in redder galaxies, and on correlations between morphology, colour, and star formation. The project showed that volunteer votes, properly weighted for bias, could match expert classification accuracy.

Discoveries by volunteers

Volunteers have made notable individual discoveries. In 2007 Dutch schoolteacher Hanny van Arkel found a glowing gas cloud, Hanny's Voorwerp, later identified as a light echo of a quasar that has since faded. Volunteers also identified the "Green Pea" galaxies — compact, intensely star-forming galaxies — which became the subject of dedicated follow-up research. The unusual galaxy pair and overlap systems catalogued by users continue to be studied.

Legacy — the Zooniverse

Galaxy Zoo's success led to the Zooniverse platform, which hosts hundreds of citizen-science projects across astronomy, ecology, history, and medicine, and to Galaxy Zoo successors classifying images from the Hubble Space Telescope (Galaxy Zoo: Hubble), CANDELS, and DECaLS surveys. Later iterations combine human classification with machine learning, using volunteers to train models that classify the largest surveys.
