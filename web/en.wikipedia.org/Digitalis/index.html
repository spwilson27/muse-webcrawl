Digitalis

Digitalis is a genus of about 20 species of herbaceous perennial plants, shrubs, and biennials, commonly called foxgloves. The genus is native to western and eastern Europe, western Asia, and northwestern Africa. The flowers are produced on a tall spike, are tubular, and vary in color among species, from purple to pink, white, and yellow.

Digitalis purpurea, the common foxglove, in flower.

Medicinal use

Digitalis is the source of a group of medicines called digitalis glycosides, including digoxin and digitoxin, extracted chiefly from the leaves of Digitalis lanata and Digitalis purpurea. These cardiac glycosides increase the force of contraction of the heart muscle and are used to treat heart failure and certain irregular heart rhythms, particularly atrial fibrillation. The physician William Withering published an influential account of the medicinal use of foxglove in 1785.

Toxicity

All parts of the plant are toxic, containing cardiac glycosides that can cause nausea, vomiting, visual disturbances, dangerous arrhythmias, and death if ingested. Digitalis toxicity remains a clinical concern because the therapeutic dose is close to the toxic dose, a situation known as a narrow therapeutic index.

Cultivation

Foxgloves are popular ornamental garden plants, valued for their tall spires of bell-shaped flowers in early summer. They often self-seed freely and prefer partial shade and moist, well-drained soil.
