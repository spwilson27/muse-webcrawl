Acid–base reaction

An acid–base reaction is a chemical reaction that occurs between an acid and a base. It can be used to determine pH via titration. Several theoretical frameworks provide alternative conceptions of the reaction mechanisms and their application in solving related problems; these are called the acid–base theories, for example, the Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory.

Their importance becomes apparent in analysing acid–base reactions for gaseous or liquid species, or when acid or base character may be somewhat less apparent. The first of these concepts was provided by the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier, around 1776.

Theories

Arrhenius: Svante Arrhenius defined acids as substances that produce hydrogen ions (H+) in aqueous solution and bases as substances that produce hydroxide ions (OH−). This definition is limited to aqueous systems.

Brønsted–Lowry: In 1923, Johannes Nicolaus Brønsted and Thomas Martin Lowry independently proposed that acids are proton donors and bases are proton acceptors. An acid–base reaction is the transfer of a proton from an acid to a base, generating conjugate acid–base pairs. This framework extends to non-aqueous solvents and to reactions not involving hydroxide.

Lewis: Also in 1923, Gilbert N. Lewis proposed a more general theory in which an acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a base is an electron-pair donor. Lewis theory encompasses reactions such as the formation of coordination complexes and many reactions in organic chemistry that involve no protons at all.

Neutralisation

When a strong acid reacts with a strong base in water, the net ionic process is H+ + OH− → H2O, producing a neutral solution and a salt. Titration uses this neutralisation, monitored with indicators or a pH meter, to determine unknown concentrations. With weak acids or bases, the resulting solution may be acidic or basic because of hydrolysis of the conjugate ion, and buffer solutions resist changes in pH.
