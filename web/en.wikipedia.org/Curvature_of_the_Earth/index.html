Curvature of the Earth

The curvature of the Earth refers to the observation that the surface of the Earth curves away from an observer, causing distant objects to be progressively obscured and the horizon to drop below the line of sight as distance increases. Because the Earth is approximately a sphere with a radius of about 6,371 kilometres, its surface falls away by roughly 8 inches over a distance of one mile (the "eight inches per mile squared" rule of thumb).

Observable effects

The curvature can be observed in several ways. Ships disappear hull-first over the horizon when sailing away from shore. Distant mountains and city skylines are partly hidden below the horizon, with only their upper portions visible. Long bridges and causeways, such as the Lake Pontchartrain Causeway, are built with a slight arc to follow the planet's curve. Surveyors and engineers must account for curvature when laying out long, level structures such as canals, rail lines, and aqueducts.

History

The spherical shape of the Earth was known in antiquity; Eratosthenes measured its circumference around 240 BC by comparing shadows in Syene and Alexandria. The drop of the horizon and the hull-down appearance of ships were cited by Aristotle as evidence of a spherical Earth. In the nineteenth century, Samuel Rowbotham and other proponents of a flat Earth disputed these observations, prompting Alfred Russel Wallace to conduct the Bedford Level experiment in 1870, which confirmed the curvature over a six-mile stretch of the Old Bedford River.

Measurement

For an observer at height h above the surface, the distance to the horizon is approximately 3.57 times the square root of h in metres, giving kilometres. Atmospheric refraction bends light downward and slightly extends the visible horizon, typically by about 8 percent.
