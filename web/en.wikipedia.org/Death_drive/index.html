In classical Freudian psychoanalytic theory, the death drive (German: Todestrieb) is the drive toward death and destruction, often expressed through behaviours such as aggression, repetition compulsion, and self-destructiveness. Proposed by Sigmund Freud in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920), it stands opposed to Eros, the tendency toward survival, propagation, sex, and other creative, life-producing drives.

Origins

Freud had previously attributed nearly all mental life to the libido and the pleasure principle. The horrors of the First World War, the phenomenon of traumatic neuroses in which patients repeatedly dreamed of their trauma, and the repetitive "fort-da" game of his grandson led him to posit a compulsion to repeat that lies "beyond the pleasure principle." He theorised that organisms harbour an innate urge to return to an earlier, inanimate state: "the aim of all life is death." The term itself had been used earlier by Sabina Spielrein in her 1912 paper "Destruction as the Cause of Coming into Being." Freud later named the paired drives Eros and Thanatos (the latter term popularised by his followers rather than by Freud himself).

Manifestations

According to Freud, the death drive may be directed inward, appearing as masochism and self-harm, or outward as aggression and destructiveness when deflected by Eros. Civilisation, he argued in Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), depends on binding and redirecting these destructive impulses, whose frustration nonetheless leaves an enduring sense of discontent.

Criticism and influence

The death drive is among the most controversial of Freud's concepts. Many psychoanalysts, including most ego psychologists, rejected it as unfalsifiable speculation, while Melanie Klein and Jacques Lacan placed it at the centre of their theories. Lacan treated the death drive as bound up with the symbolic order and repetition in language. The concept has also influenced literary and cultural theory, where it is used to discuss self-destructive repetition in art and politics.
