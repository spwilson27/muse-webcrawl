Inverted yield curve

An inverted yield curve is a yield curve in which short-term interest rates exceed long-term interest rates for debt instruments of the same credit quality. Normally the curve slopes upward, because longer maturities command higher yields; an inverted curve is therefore an unusual and widely watched signal in bond markets.

Causes

Inversions typically arise when a central bank raises short-term policy rates to fight inflation while investors, expecting slower growth or future rate cuts, accept lower yields on long-term bonds. Under the expectations hypothesis, long-term rates reflect the average of expected future short-term rates, so expected rate declines pull the far end of the curve below the near end.

As a predictor of recession

An inverted curve between the 10-year and 2-year (or 3-month) US Treasury yields has preceded every US recession since the 1970s, usually by 6 to 24 months, with very few false signals. Research by economist Campbell Harvey in the 1980s documented the relationship, and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York publishes a recession-probability model based on the spread between the 10-year yield and the 3-month bill rate. The curve inverted in 2019 before the 2020 downturn and again from 2022 to 2024 — one of the longest inversions on record — followed by a re-steepening that some economists term the point of greatest risk.

Effects

An inverted curve squeezes banks' net interest margins, since banks borrow short and lend long, discouraging lending and tightening credit conditions throughout the economy. It can also reflect or amplify pessimism in financial markets.

Criticisms and limitations

Commentators note that quantitative easing and large central-bank balance sheets have distorted long-term yields, possibly reducing the signal's reliability, and that the lag between inversion and recession varies considerably, making the signal of limited use for timing.
