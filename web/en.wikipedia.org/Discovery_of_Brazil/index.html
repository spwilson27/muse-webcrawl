Discovery of Brazil

The discovery of Brazil by Europeans is traditionally dated to 22 April 1500, when a Portuguese fleet commanded by Pedro Álvares Cabral reached the coast of what is now the Brazilian state of Bahia. The land was claimed for the Portuguese Crown.

The voyage

Cabral's fleet of thirteen ships had set sail from Lisbon in March 1500, bound for India by the route around Africa pioneered by Vasco da Gama. To catch favorable winds and currents, the fleet swung far west into the Atlantic — a maneuver known as the volta do mar — and sighted land on 21 April. The next day the fleet anchored near a hill Cabral named Monte Pascoal, near modern Porto Seguro.

First contact and naming

The Portuguese made contact with the indigenous Tupí people and celebrated the first Mass on Brazilian soil on 26 April 1500. Believing the land to be an island, Cabral named it Ilha de Vera Cruz (Island of the True Cross); it was later renamed Terra de Santa Cruz, and eventually Brazil, after brazilwood, the valuable dyewood exported from its forests. A detailed letter by the fleet's scribe, Pero Vaz de Caminha, describing the land and its people, survives as the first written document of Brazilian history.

Context and debate

Historians debate whether the landfall was accidental or deliberate, since the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) had divided newly discovered lands between Spain and Portugal, and the Spanish navigator Vicente Yáñez Pinzón had reached the northern coast of Brazil earlier in 1500. Portuguese colonization of Brazil developed slowly at first, with captaincies established from the 1530s.
