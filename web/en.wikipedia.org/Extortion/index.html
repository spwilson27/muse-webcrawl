Extortion

Extortion is a criminal offence consisting of obtaining money, property or services from a person or organisation by coercion — typically by threats of violence, property damage, reputational harm or adverse official action. It differs from robbery in that the victim formally consents to hand over the property, though under duress, and the threatened harm need not be immediate.

Forms

When the threat is to reveal damaging information, the offence overlaps with blackmail — in England and Wales the Theft Act 1968 covers such demands under the offence of blackmail, while "extortion" in common-law usage usually involves abuse of office or threats of physical harm. Protection rackets, in which businesses pay organised crime groups for "protection" against harms the groups themselves threaten, are a classic form, as are extortionate demands by corrupt officials. In the United States, federal statutes such as the Hobbs Act (1946) criminalise extortion affecting interstate commerce, including under colour of official right.

Cyber extortion

The internet has produced new variants: ransomware attacks encrypt a victim's data and demand payment for the key, while denial-of-service extortion threatens to flood a website unless paid. Authorities generally advise against paying, since payment funds further crime and does not guarantee recovery.

Distinctions

Not every unpleasant bargain is extortion: hard negotiating tactics, threats to sue for a legitimate claim, and lawful strikes are generally protected, and the line is drawn at wrongful threats — demands to which the threatener has no legitimate claim — though its exact position varies between jurisdictions.
