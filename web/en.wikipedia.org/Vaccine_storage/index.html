Vaccine storage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A pharmaceutical refrigerator storing vaccine vials — placeholder]

Vaccine storage refers to the policies, equipment, and procedures used to keep vaccines within their required temperature ranges from manufacture to administration, preserving their potency. Because vaccines are biological products, exposure to heat, freezing, or light can irreversibly damage them; careful storage is a core element of the cold chain.

Temperature requirements

Most routine vaccines must be refrigerated at 2 °C to 8 °C (36 °F to 46 °F). Some, such as certain varicella and MMRV vaccines, require freezer storage, while the mRNA COVID-19 vaccines initially required ultra-low temperatures of −80 °C to −60 °C before formulations and guidance allowed longer refrigerated storage. Freeze-sensitive vaccines (including most aluminium-adjuvanted products) can be damaged by a single freezing event, detected historically with the "shake test" and by freeze indicators.

Equipment and monitoring

Guidelines from bodies such as the CDC and the WHO require purpose-built pharmaceutical-grade refrigerators rather than dormitory-style units, continuous temperature monitoring with digital data loggers, twice-daily checks where loggers are absent, and alarm systems for excursions. Vaccine vial monitors — heat-sensitive labels that change colour with cumulative heat exposure — are widely used in global immunization programmes.

Breaches and handling

When storage temperatures are exceeded, providers must consult manufacturers or immunization programmes before using or discarding stock, since stability varies by product. Maintaining the cold chain in low-resource settings is a major logistical challenge addressed by solar-powered refrigerators and controlled-temperature-chain strategies approved for certain vaccines.
