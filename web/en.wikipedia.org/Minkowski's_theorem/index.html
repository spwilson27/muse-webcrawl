Minkowski's theorem

In mathematics, Minkowski's theorem is a fundamental result in the geometry of numbers, proved by Hermann Minkowski in 1889 and published in his book Geometrie der Zahlen (1896). It states that any convex set in Rn that is symmetric about the origin and has volume greater than 2n must contain a non-zero point of the integer lattice Zn.

Statement

Let K be a convex subset of Rn which is symmetric with respect to the origin, meaning that if x is in K then −x is also in K. If the volume of K satisfies

vol(K) > 2n,

then K contains a non-zero point of the lattice Zn.

More generally, for any lattice Λ of determinant d(Λ), a symmetric convex set of volume greater than 2n·d(Λ) contains a non-zero point of Λ. A refinement, Minkowski's theorem on successive minima, gives a chain of related inequalities; the constant 2n cannot in general be improved.

Proof sketch

The standard proof relies on Blichfeldt's theorem, which says that any measurable set in Rn of volume greater than 1 contains two distinct points whose difference is an integer point. Applying this to the halved set (1/2)K, whose volume exceeds 1, gives points x and y in (1/2)K with x − y a non-zero lattice point. By symmetry and convexity, x − y = (2x + (−2y))/2 lies in K, completing the argument.

Applications

Minkowski's theorem can be used to give elegant proofs of:

  • Lagrange's four-square theorem (every natural number is a sum of four integer squares);
  • the existence of solutions to certain Diophantine approximation problems, including a proof of Dirichlet's approximation theorem;
  • key facts in algebraic number theory, such as the finiteness of the class number and Dirichlet's unit theorem, via Minkowski's bound.
