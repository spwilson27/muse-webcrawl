Scroll

A scroll (from the Old French escroe or escroue, a roll of parchment) is a roll of papyrus, parchment, or paper containing writing. Scrolls were the dominant form of book throughout the ancient world and remained in use in many cultures for millennia before being largely replaced by the codex — the bound book of pages — in late antiquity. Torah scrolls, Chinese and Japanese handscrolls, and some ceremonial documents still use the form today.

History

Egyptians were writing on papyrus scrolls by around 3000 BC. The format spread throughout the Mediterranean: Greeks and Romans stored volumina in cylindrical cases called capsae, reading by unrolling with one hand and rolling up with the other. Standard scrolls were 6–10 metres long; exceptional works filled many rolls, and libraries like Alexandria's held hundreds of thousands. In East Asia, mounted handscrolls on paper and silk became a major art form in China and Japan, meant to be viewed section by section, while hanging scrolls served for display.

From scroll to codex

Between the 1st and 4th centuries AD, the parchment codex displaced the scroll in the Roman world: it was more compact, allowed random access and writing on both sides, and suited the needs of early Christian communities, who adopted it early and probably for their scriptures. Scrolls nevertheless survived in Jewish liturgy — the Sefer Torah — and in legal and ceremonial niches, such as British Acts of Parliament, which were enrolled on parchment rolls into the modern era.

Notable scrolls

The most famous surviving ancient scrolls are the Dead Sea Scrolls (c. 250 BC – AD 68), discovered in the Qumran Caves from 1946–47, which include the oldest known biblical manuscripts. The carbonised Herculaneum papyri, buried by Vesuvius in AD 79, have recently been read in part using X-ray tomography and machine learning.
