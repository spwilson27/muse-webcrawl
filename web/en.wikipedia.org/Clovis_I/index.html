Clovis I

Clovis I (c. 466 – 27 November 511) was the first king of the Franks to unite all of the Frankish tribes under one ruler, changing the form of leadership from a group of petty kings to rule by a single king and ensuring that kingship passed down to his heirs. He is considered the founder of the Merovingian dynasty, which ruled the Frankish kingdom for the next two centuries, and is traditionally regarded as a founder of France — his name, Latinised as Clodovicus, evolved into the French name Louis.

Early reign and conquests

Clovis succeeded his father, Childeric I, as king of the Salian Franks at Tournai in 481 or 482, aged about fifteen. In 486 he defeated Syagrius, the last Roman commander in Gaul, at the Battle of Soissons, annexing the rump Roman state between the Seine and the Loire. He subsequently subdued the Thuringians and, after the Battle of Tolbiac (c. 496), the Alemanni of the upper Rhine. In 507 Clovis defeated the Visigoths under Alaric II at the Battle of Vouillé, annexing most of Aquitaine, although Theodoric the Great prevented him from reaching the Mediterranean coast.

Conversion to Christianity

Clovis married the Burgundian princess Clotilde, a Catholic Christian, who urged him to convert from the Frankish paganism (or Arian Christianity) he had practised. According to Gregory of Tours, Clovis vowed during the battle against the Alemanni that if victorious he would accept his wife's God, and was subsequently baptised at Reims by Bishop Remigius. The conventional date is 25 December 496, though some historians argue for 506 or 508. His conversion won him the support of the Gallo-Roman Catholic episcopate and distinguished the Franks from their Arian Gothic neighbours.

Administration and law

Clovis made Paris his capital and convened the first Council of Orléans in 511 to organise the Frankish church. Under his rule the Salic law (Lex Salica) was first committed to writing, codifying Frankish customary law with its tariff of fines (wergild) for injuries. The Eastern Roman emperor Anastasius I recognised Clovis with consular honours, a recognition Clovis used to bolster his legitimacy.

Death and succession

Clovis died in Paris in 511 and was buried beside Clotilde in the Church of the Holy Apostles, which he had founded. Following Frankish custom his kingdom was divided among his four sons — Theuderic, Chlodomer, Childebert, and Chlothar — a partition that shaped the political geography of the Merovingian world for generations.
