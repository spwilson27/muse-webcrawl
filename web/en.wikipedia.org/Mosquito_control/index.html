Mosquito control

Purpose: Reduce mosquito populations and mosquito-borne disease
Methods: Source reduction, larvicides, adulticides, biological control, genetic methods, personal protection
Target diseases: Malaria, dengue, Zika, West Nile virus, yellow fever

Mosquito control is the practice of reducing mosquito populations to protect public health from mosquito-borne diseases such as malaria, dengue fever, Zika fever, West Nile fever, and yellow fever, and to reduce the nuisance of biting mosquitoes. Modern programmes usually follow integrated pest management, combining surveillance with several control methods.

Methods

Source reduction eliminates standing water where mosquitoes breed—draining wetlands, emptying containers, and managing irrigation. Larvicides target aquatic immature stages and include the bacterial toxin Bti (Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis), methoprene, and surface oils; adulticides such as pyrethroids are sprayed by truck or aircraft against flying adults. Biological control employs predators such as mosquitofish (Gambusia) and copepods. At the household level, insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, repellents, and window screens are central tools, especially against malaria vectors.

Genetic and novel approaches

Newer approaches include the sterile insect technique, releasing mosquitoes infected with the bacterium Wolbachia to suppress virus transmission (used notably against dengue), and controversial gene drive technologies intended to suppress or modify wild populations, which remain largely experimental and subject to ecological and ethical debate.

History

Organised mosquito control expanded after the discovery in the late 19th century that mosquitoes transmit malaria and yellow fever. Drainage and oiling campaigns enabled the completion of the Panama Canal, and mid-20th-century use of DDT dramatically reduced malaria in many regions before resistance and environmental concerns—highlighted by Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962)—led to bans in many countries and a shift to integrated methods.
