Federal Communications Commission

The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) is an independent agency of the United States government that regulates communications by radio, television, wire, satellite and cable across the United States. Created by the Communications Act of 1934, the FCC succeeded the Federal Radio Commission. It is headed by five commissioners appointed by the president and confirmed by the Senate, one of whom serves as chair.

Jurisdiction and duties

The FCC licenses radio and television broadcasters, manages the radio spectrum through auctions and allocations, enforces rules on broadcast indecency and children's programming, and regulates interstate telephone and broadband services. Its authority over the internet has been politically contested, particularly in the debate over net neutrality: the FCC classified broadband as a common-carrier service under Title II of the Communications Act in 2015, reversed that classification in 2017, and restored it in 2024, with further litigation and policy changes following.

History

The Communications Act of 1934 consolidated federal regulation of telephony (previously under the Interstate Commerce Commission) and radio (under the Federal Radio Commission) in a single body. The FCC oversaw the breakup-era Bell System, the transition from analogue to digital television completed in 2009, and successive generations of mobile wireless licensing, including major spectrum auctions that have raised tens of billions of dollars for the U.S. Treasury.

Structure

The commission's work is carried out through bureaus including the Wireline Competition Bureau, the Wireless Telecommunications Bureau, the Media Bureau and the Enforcement Bureau. No more than three commissioners may belong to the same political party.
