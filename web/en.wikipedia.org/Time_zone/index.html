Time zone

A time zone is an area of the world that observes a uniform standard time for legal, commercial and social purposes. Time zones tend to follow the boundaries of countries and their subdivisions rather than longitude strictly, because it is convenient for areas in close commercial or other communication to keep the same time.

All time zones are defined as offsets from Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), ranging from UTC−12:00 to UTC+14:00. The offsets are usually a whole number of hours, but a few are offset by additional 30 or 45 minutes, such as in India, Sri Lanka, Nepal and some parts of Australia.

History

Standard time was first proposed by Sir Sandford Fleming in 1879, and the Greenwich prime meridian was adopted as the reference at the International Meridian Conference of 1884. Before standard zones were adopted, many localities set their clocks by local solar time, which made railway timetables in countries such as Britain and the United States extremely complicated.

Daylight saving time

Some time zones observe daylight saving time (DST) for part of the year, typically by advancing the clock by one hour during spring and summer to make better use of evening daylight.
