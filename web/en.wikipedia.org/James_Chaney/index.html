James Earl Chaney (May 30, 1943 – June 21, 1964) was an American civil rights activist from Mississippi. He was one of three Congress of Racial Equality (CORE) workers murdered by members of the Ku Klux Klan near Philadelphia, Mississippi, during the Freedom Summer campaign of 1964 — a crime that became known nationwide as the "Mississippi Burning" murders.

Early life

Chaney was born in Meridian, Mississippi, the son of a plasterer. As a teenager he was suspended from school for wearing a paper NAACP badge, and he became involved in civil rights organising at an early age. He joined CORE and worked alongside veteran organiser Dave Dennis in voter registration efforts in his home state.

Freedom Summer and murder

In 1964 Chaney helped organise Freedom Summer, a drive to register Black Mississippians to vote. On June 21, 1964, Chaney and two colleagues from New York, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, investigated the burning of Mount Zion Methodist Church in Neshoba County, a church that had agreed to host a Freedom School. On their way back they were arrested by Deputy Sheriff Cecil Price on a speeding charge, held in jail until night, then released. Price and other Klansmen intercepted the three men on a dark road; Chaney was beaten and all three were shot. Their bodies were buried in an earthen dam and were not found until August 4, after an FBI investigation (code-named MIBURN) and a tip from an informant.

Aftermath

The murders outraged the nation and helped build support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Because Mississippi declined to bring murder charges, the federal government prosecuted eighteen men for conspiracy to deprive the victims of their civil rights; seven were convicted in 1967. In 2005, Edgar Ray Killen, a former Klansman and part-time preacher, was convicted of manslaughter in state court for his role in the killings. Chaney is buried in Meridian, and the three men are commemorated by memorials in Mississippi and at the site of the murders.
