Ribosome

Ribosomes are macromolecular machines, found within all cells, that perform biological protein synthesis (messenger RNA translation). Ribosomes link amino acids together in the order specified by the codons of messenger RNA (mRNA) molecules to form polypeptide chains. Ribosomes consist of two major components: the small and large ribosomal subunits. Each subunit consists of one or more ribosomal RNA (rRNA) molecules and many ribosomal proteins.

The ribosome is a ribozyme: the peptidyl transferase reaction that joins amino acids is catalysed by rRNA, not by protein. Ribosomes occur free in the cytosol and bound to the rough endoplasmic reticulum; mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own smaller (70S-like) ribosomes, reflecting their bacterial origin.

A ribosome translating mRNA: tRNAs occupy the A, P and E sites between the small and large subunits.

Structure

Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S, composed of a 50S large subunit (23S and 5S rRNA) and a 30S small subunit (16S rRNA). Eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, with a 60S large subunit (28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNA) and a 40S small subunit (18S rRNA). The three tRNA binding sites are designated A (aminoacyl), P (peptidyl) and E (exit). High-resolution structures of the ribosome, obtained by X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy, earned Venkatraman Ramakrishnan, Thomas Steitz and Ada Yonath the 2009 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Function: translation

During translation, the small subunit binds mRNA and ensures correct codon–anticodon pairing by incoming transfer RNAs, while the large subunit catalyses peptide bond formation. The ribosome moves along the mRNA codon by codon (translocation), powered by elongation factors and GTP, until a stop codon triggers release of the completed polypeptide. Many antibiotics — including aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides and chloramphenicol — work by binding bacterial ribosomes and blocking these steps, exploiting differences from eukaryotic ribosomes.

History

Ribosomes were observed as dense particles by George Emil Palade in the 1950s using electron microscopy; the name "ribosome" was proposed in 1958. Their role as the site of protein synthesis was established through cell fractionation and radioactive amino-acid incorporation experiments.
