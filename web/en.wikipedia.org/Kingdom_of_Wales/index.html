Kingdom of Wales

There was never a single, enduring Kingdom of Wales in medieval history. Wales was instead divided among competing native kingdoms and principalities — principally Gwynedd, Powys, and Deheubarth — for most of the medieval period, although a few rulers briefly united most of the country.

Early medieval kingdoms

After the Roman withdrawal, Wales fragmented into kingdoms including Gwynedd, Powys, Dyfed, Brycheiniog, Glywysing and Gwent. Rhodri the Great (844–878) united Gwynedd, Powys and Seisyllwg, and his grandson Hywel Dda (c. 880–950) ruled most of Wales and codified Welsh law (Cyfraith Hywel). Gruffydd ap Llywelyn (1039–1063) came closest to a kingdom of all Wales, briefly conquering his rivals before being defeated and killed in the face of English invasion under Harold Godwinson.

Principality

In the 13th century the princes of Gwynedd, Llywelyn the Great and his grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, asserted leadership over the other Welsh rulers. The Treaty of Montgomery (1267) recognised Llywelyn as Prince of Wales under the English crown. Edward I's conquest of 1277–1283 ended native rule: Llywelyn was killed in 1282, his brother Dafydd executed in 1283, and the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) annexed the principality to England. Owain Glyndŵr's revolt (1400–1415) briefly revived an independent Welsh principality, with parliaments at Machynlleth, but was suppressed.

Later usage

"Kingdom of Wales" is occasionally used retrospectively for these polities, or in modern debate about Welsh nationhood; Wales is today a constituent country of the United Kingdom with its own devolved parliament, the Senedd, but it has never formally been styled a kingdom.
