Republic (Plato)

The Republic (Greek: Πολιτεία, Politeia; Latin: De Re Publica) is a Socratic dialogue, authored by Plato around 375 BC, concerning justice (δικαιοσύνη), the order and character of the just city-state, and the just man. It is Plato's best-known work, and has proven to be one of the world's most influential works of philosophy and political theory, both intellectually and historically.

Content

In the dialogue, Socrates discusses with various Athenians and foreigners the meaning of justice and whether the just man is happier than the unjust man. To examine justice "writ large", the interlocutors construct an ideal city, the Kallipolis, ruled by philosopher-kings and organised into producer, auxiliary (warrior) and guardian classes, with the guardians holding wives, children and property in common. The argument yields Plato's tripartite theory of the soul — reason, spirit and appetite — mirroring the classes of the city.

Central books present the theory of Forms, the allegory of the cave, the analogy of the Sun and the divided line, and the philosopher's arduous education in mathematics and dialectic. The dialogue also contains the Ring of Gyges thought experiment, a typology of degenerate regimes (timocracy, oligarchy, democracy and tyranny), the critique of poetry and imitation, and, in Book X, the Myth of Er, describing the fate of souls after death.

Influence

The Republic shaped Western philosophy from Aristotle's criticisms in the Politics onward. Cicero modelled his own De re publica on it; Augustine and medieval thinkers read it in partial transmission; it influenced Renaissance utopias and modern debates alike. Karl Popper's The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945) famously attacked Plato as a prophet of totalitarianism, a reading many scholars contest.
