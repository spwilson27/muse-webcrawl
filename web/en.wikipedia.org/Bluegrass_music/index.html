Bluegrass music is a genre of American roots music that developed in the 1940s in the Appalachian region of the United States. The genre derives its name from the band Bill Monroe and the Blue Grass Boys. Like mainstream country music, it grew out of old-time string band music, but it is characterised by virtuosic acoustic instrumentation, high harmonies (the "high lonesome sound"), and fast tempos driven by the three-finger banjo style pioneered by Earl Scruggs.

History

Bill Monroe, born in Kentucky — the "Bluegrass State" — formed the Blue Grass Boys in 1939 and joined the Grand Ole Opry. When banjoist Earl Scruggs and guitarist Lester Flatt joined in 1945, the classic bluegrass sound crystallised. Flatt and Scruggs left in 1948 to form the Foggy Mountain Boys, popularising the genre with songs such as "Foggy Mountain Breakdown," later famous from the film Bonnie and Clyde. The Stanley Brothers, Reno and Smiley, and Jimmy Martin were other first-generation pillars.

Characteristics

Bluegrass ensembles are acoustic, built around fiddle, five-string banjo, mandolin, guitar, Dobro, and upright bass. Players take improvisational solo "breaks" in turn, while the mandolin plays percussive "chop" chords on the offbeat. Singing is typically high-pitched and harmonised in trios. Repertoire mixes secular songs, instrumentals (" breakdowns "), and gospel numbers.

Later developments

The folk revival of the 1960s brought bluegrass to new audiences and festivals; the 1970s saw "newgrass" experimentation from groups like the New Grass Revival, and the soundtrack of O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000) sparked a major revival. Contemporary acts such as Alison Krauss and Union Station, Nickel Creek, and Billy Strings have carried the genre forward, and the International Bluegrass Music Association coordinates the professional community.
