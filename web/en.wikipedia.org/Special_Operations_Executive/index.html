Special Operations Executive

The Special Operations Executive (SOE) was a British organisation formed in 1940 to conduct espionage, sabotage and reconnaissance in German-occupied Europe and, later, in Japanese-occupied Southeast Asia. Sometimes referred to as "the Baker Street Irregulars," after Sherlock Holmes's fictional group of spies, or "Churchill's Secret Army," SOE was charged by Winston Churchill to "set Europe ablaze."

Formation

SOE was created in July 1940 by merging Section D of the Secret Intelligence Service, the War Office's MI R, and the propaganda department Electra House, under the Minister of Economic Warfare, Hugh Dalton. Its headquarters were at 64 Baker Street in London. The organisation recruited men and women — many of them émigrés with native language skills — trained them in parachuting, weapons, explosives, codes and survival at country houses across Britain, and inserted them into occupied territory by parachute, boat or submarine.

Operations

SOE supported resistance movements across Europe with weapons, radios and trained organisers. Notable successes include the Norwegian heavy water sabotage at Vemork, which hindered the German nuclear weapons programme, and extensive cooperation with the French Resistance. In the Netherlands, by contrast, German counterintelligence ran captured radios in the Englandspiel ("England game"), leading to the arrest and death of dozens of agents. In Southeast Asia, SOE's Force 136 operated behind Japanese lines in Burma, Malaya and elsewhere.

Agents and legacy

About 13,000 people served in or were associated with SOE, including some 3,200 women; celebrated agents included Violette Szabo, Noor Inayat Khan, Odette Sansom and Forest Yeo-Thomas. SOE was dissolved in January 1946, its remnants absorbed by the Secret Intelligence Service. Its techniques and personnel influenced postwar special forces and intelligence services, including the American OSS and CIA.
