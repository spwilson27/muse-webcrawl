Cafeteria

A cafeteria, sometimes called a canteen outside the United States and Canada, is a type of food service location in which there is little or no waiting staff table service, as opposed to most restaurants. Cafeterias are often found inside a larger institution, such as an office building, school, college, hospital, museum, or place of worship, and may be run by the institution itself or by an outside contractor.

Customers select the food they want as they move through a serving line, putting items on a tray, and pay at a checkout at the end of the line. Many cafeterias have a mixture of hot food, salads, sandwiches, and prepared drinks, and some offer a salad bar or made-to-order stations.

History

Self-service dining emerged in the United States in the late 19th century. The first cafeterias appeared in California during the gold rush era, when miners and workers needed quick, cheap meals. The format spread rapidly in the early 20th century: the Childs restaurant chain and later Horn & Hardart automats popularized inexpensive self-service food in cities, and by the 1940s the cafeteria had become a standard feature of schools, hospitals, and government buildings.

Types

Institutional cafeterias serve a captive clientele such as students or employees. Commercial cafeterias, common in shopping districts and malls, operate as standalone restaurants. School cafeterias are notable for subsidized meal programs, such as the National School Lunch Program in the United States, which provides free or reduced-price meals to eligible children.
