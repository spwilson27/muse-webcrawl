Iron meteorite

CompositionIron–nickel alloy (kamacite, taenite)
Share of findsAbout 5% of observed meteorite falls
OriginCores of differentiated planetesimals
LargestHoba meteorite, Namibia (~60 tonnes)
Diagnostic featureWidmanstätten pattern

Iron meteorites, also known as siderites, are a type of meteorite that consist overwhelmingly of an iron–nickel alloy known as meteoric iron. They are believed to be fragments of the cores of early planetesimals that melted, differentiated, and were later shattered by impacts, liberating core material into space.

Composition and structure

Meteoric iron consists mainly of the low-nickel mineral kamacite and the high-nickel mineral taenite. When a polished slice is etched with acid, interlocking bands of these minerals form the characteristic Widmanstätten pattern, which can only develop through cooling over millions of years — proof that the material originated inside a sizeable parent body.

Classification

Historically iron meteorites were classed by structure as hexahedrites, octahedrites and ataxites, reflecting their nickel content and crystal structure. Modern classification assigns them to chemical groups (such as IAB, IIAB, IIIAB and IVA) based on trace-element abundances, each group interpreted as the core of a distinct parent body. The ungrouped and anomalous irons suggest that dozens of early cores are represented in collections.

History and finds

Before the Iron Age, meteoric iron was worked by several cultures; a dagger in Tutankhamun's tomb was shown to be made of it. Because of their density and resistance to weathering, iron meteorites are over-represented among finds relative to falls. The largest known, the Hoba meteorite in Namibia, weighs roughly 60 tonnes and still lies where it fell. Famous specimens include the Campo del Cielo irons of Argentina and the Willamette Meteorite of Oregon.
