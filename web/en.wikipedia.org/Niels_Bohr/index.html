Niels Bohr

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Niels Henrik David Bohr (7 October 1885 – 18 November 1962) was a Danish physicist who made foundational contributions to understanding atomic structure and quantum theory, for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922. Bohr developed the Bohr model of the atom, in which electrons travel in discrete orbits around the nucleus and chemical properties are determined largely by the number of electrons in the outer orbits.

Early life and the Bohr model

Bohr was born in Copenhagen, the son of physiologist Christian Bohr. After doctoral work at the University of Copenhagen he worked with J. J. Thomson at Cambridge and then with Ernest Rutherford at Manchester. In 1913 he published his trilogy of papers applying Planck's quantum hypothesis to Rutherford's nuclear atom, successfully explaining the spectral lines of hydrogen.

Copenhagen institute and complementarity

In 1920 Bohr founded the Institute for Theoretical Physics in Copenhagen (now the Niels Bohr Institute), which became a world centre of quantum physics. He mentored and collaborated with Heisenberg, Dirac, Pauli and many others, and developed the principle of complementarity — that objects have complementary properties which cannot all be observed at once, as in wave–particle duality. His debates with Albert Einstein over the completeness of quantum mechanics, begun at the 1927 Solvay Conference, became famous in the philosophy of science.

Later life

During the Second World War Bohr escaped occupied Denmark in 1943 and contributed to the Manhattan Project under the alias Nicholas Baker, while advocating international control of atomic weapons. He helped found CERN and received the first Atoms for Peace Award in 1957. The element bohrium is named in his honour. See also Bohr model, Copenhagen interpretation and Aage Bohr.
