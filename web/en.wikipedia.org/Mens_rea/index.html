Mens rea

Mens rea (Law Latin for "guilty mind") is the mental element of a crime: the intention, knowledge or other state of mind that a defendant must have had while committing the act constituting the offence. Together with the actus reus (the guilty act) it is one of the two elements that must generally be proven for criminal liability, expressed in the maxim actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea — "an act does not make one guilty unless the mind is also guilty".

Levels of culpability

The American Model Penal Code organizes mens rea into four levels: purposely (the result is the actor's conscious object), knowingly (the actor is aware the result is practically certain), recklessly (conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk), and negligently (failure to perceive such a risk). English law similarly distinguishes intention — direct intent and oblique intent (foresight of a virtually certain consequence, per R v Woollin) — from recklessness and negligence. Specific crimes require specific intents: murder in English law, for example, requires an intention to kill or cause grievous bodily harm.

Exceptions and related doctrines

Some offences impose strict liability with no mens rea requirement as to one or more elements, typically regulatory offences. Related concepts include transferred malice, corporate mens rea (the identification doctrine), and defences such as insanity and automatism that negate the mental element.
