Brahmavihara

The brahmavihārās (Pali: brahmavihārā; Sanskrit: brahmavihāra, "sublime attitudes", literally "abodes of Brahma") are a series of four Buddhist virtues and the meditation practices made to cultivate them. They are also known as the four immeasurables (Pali: appamaññā) or the four infinite minds (Chinese: 四無量心). The four are:

  • Loving-kindness (mettā) — wishing oneself and others happiness and well-being;
  • Compassion (karuṇā) — the wish that others be free of suffering;
  • Empathetic joy (muditā) — rejoicing in the happiness and success of others;
  • Equanimity (upekkhā) — even-mindedness toward all beings, friend, foe and stranger alike.

Origins and early texts

The term appears in the Tevijja Sutta of the Dīgha Nikāya, where the Buddha teaches two Brahmin youths the way to companionship with Brahmā — not through Vedic sacrifice but by radiating loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity to all quarters of the world. Scholars note that the concept may predate Buddhism and was shared with early Jain and Brahmanical traditions; the Buddhist texts reinterpret "dwelling with Brahmā" ethically, as cultivating a boundless, godlike mind in this life. The Metta Sutta of the Sutta Nipata is the classic short exposition of loving-kindness meditation and is chanted daily in Theravāda countries.

Practice

In meditation, each quality is cultivated by directing well-wishing phrases or feelings toward a sequence of persons: oneself, a benefactor, a dear friend, a neutral person, a difficult person, and finally all beings without exception, "breaking down the barriers" between categories. Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga (5th century CE) gives detailed instructions, including the "near enemies" of each virtue — states that resemble it but are not it (for example, selfish affection masquerades as loving-kindness, and indifference masquerades as equanimity) — and its "far enemy", the opposite quality it overcomes (ill will, cruelty, envy and aversion respectively).

In Mahayana Buddhism

In Mahāyāna Buddhism the four immeasurables are central to the bodhisattva path and are recited in liturgies such as the four-line verse: "May all beings have happiness and the causes of happiness; may they be free from suffering and its causes; may they never be separated from the joy beyond sorrow; may they dwell in equanimity, free from attachment and aversion." In Tibetan Buddhism they precede refuge and bodhicitta prayers in many practice texts (sadhanas).
