Editorial cartoon

An editorial cartoon, also known as a political cartoon, is a cartoon drawing containing a social or political message that usually relates to current events or personalities. Editorial cartoons are typically published on the editorial or op-ed page of newspapers and magazines, and increasingly online. They combine artistic skill, hyperbole, and satire to question authority and draw attention to corruption, political violence, and other social ills.

History

Precursors include the broadsheets and caricatures of the eighteenth century — William Hogarth's engravings in England and the satirical prints of the American and French revolutions. Benjamin Franklin's "Join, or Die" (1754), depicting a snake severed into colonial segments, is often cited as the first American political cartoon. The nineteenth century saw the rise of illustrated satirical magazines such as Britain's Punch (founded 1841). In the United States, Thomas Nast of Harper's Weekly famously attacked the Tweed Ring of Tammany Hall and popularized the elephant and donkey as symbols of the Republican and Democratic parties.

Characteristics

Editorial cartoons distill an argument into a single image using caricature, symbolism, metaphor, labels, and irony. Recurring visual vocabulary includes national personifications such as Uncle Sam and John Bull, the dove and hawk for policies of peace and war, and exaggerated physical features of politicians. Because they assume the reader's familiarity with the news, they comment on events with an immediacy that written editorials rarely match.

Recognition and influence

The Pulitzer Prize for Editorial Cartooning has been awarded since 1922, and the Herblock Prize honors the tradition of Herb Block, whose work during Watergate helped fix the image of Richard Nixon in the public mind. Editorial cartoons have also been flashpoints for controversy and violence, from nineteenth-century sedition prosecutions to the 2005 Jyllands-Posten Muhammad cartoons controversy and the 2015 attack on the French satirical magazine Charlie Hebdo.

Decline of staff positions

The contraction of the newspaper industry in the twenty-first century has eliminated most staff cartoonist positions; many papers rely on syndicated work or publish no cartoons at all, while cartoonists increasingly distribute their work through social media and independent websites.
