New International Economic Order

The New International Economic Order (NIEO) was a set of proposals advanced during the 1970s by developing countries, acting through the Group of 77 in the United Nations, to restructure the global economy in favour of the post-colonial world. Drawing on dependency theory and the precedent of OPEC's success in raising oil prices, the programme was formally adopted by the UN General Assembly on 1 May 1974 in the Declaration on the Establishment of a New International Economic Order and the accompanying Programme of Action, followed in December 1974 by the Charter of Economic Rights and Duties of States.

Proposals

The NIEO demanded, among other things: permanent sovereignty over natural resources, including the right to nationalise foreign assets; stabilisation and indexation of commodity prices, supported by an Integrated Programme for Commodities and a Common Fund under UNCTAD; greater and less conditional development aid, with a target of 0.7 percent of developed countries' GNP; reform of the IMF and World Bank to increase developing-country voting power; regulation of transnational corporations; debt relief; and preferential trade access without reciprocity.

Decline and legacy

Developed countries resisted most of the programme, and the North–South dialogue stalled after the Cancún summit of 1981. The debt crisis of the 1980s, the turn to neoliberal policies ("the Washington Consensus") and the collapse of commodity-price solidarity effectively ended the NIEO as an active project, though a Common Fund agreement was eventually concluded and the 0.7 percent aid target remains a standing UN commitment. Scholars treat the NIEO as the high-water mark of Third World collective bargaining, and some of its themes — sovereign resource rights, reform of global economic governance, and special treatment for developing countries — continue to recur in debates over climate finance and trade.
