The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) is a NASA space telescope designed to search for exoplanets using the transit method. Launched on 18 April 2018 aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9, TESS is an MIT-led Explorer-class mission. Its primary goal is to find planets—especially small, rocky ones—transiting the nearest and brightest stars, so that their masses can be measured from the ground and their atmospheres studied by telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope.

Survey strategy

During its two-year primary mission, TESS divided the sky into 26 observing sectors of 24° × 96°, staring at each for about 27 days. It monitored the brightness of roughly 200,000 preselected stars every two minutes, while full-frame images covered millions more. Because each sector is observed for only about a month, TESS is most sensitive to planets with orbital periods shorter than about 50 days, except near the ecliptic poles, where sectors overlap and coverage extends to a year.

The spacecraft occupies a novel 2:1 lunar-resonant, highly elliptical orbit with a period of 13.7 days, which provides a stable thermal environment, avoids the radiation belts, and permits efficient data downlink at perigee.

Results

TESS has identified thousands of planet candidates (TESS Objects of Interest), with hundreds confirmed as planets, including the first Earth-sized planet in the habitable zone of its star (TOI 700 d) and nearby multi-planet systems such as L 98-59 and TOI 1233 that are prime targets for atmospheric follow-up. The mission has also produced results in stellar astrophysics, supernovae, and solar-system science. After completing its primary mission in July 2020, TESS has continued in a series of extended missions, revisiting both hemispheres with faster full-frame imaging.
