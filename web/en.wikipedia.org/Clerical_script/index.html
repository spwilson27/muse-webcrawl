Clerical script

The clerical script (Chinese: 隸書; pinyin: lìshū) is an archaic style of Chinese calligraphy which evolved from the earlier seal script during the Warring States period and became dominant in the Han dynasty. It was developed to meet the growing demand for fast, efficient record-keeping by government clerks — hence its name — and the transition from seal script to clerical script is regarded as the decisive turning point in the evolution of Chinese characters from pictographic forms toward the modern system of strokes.

Origins

Clerical script began to emerge in the state of Qin during the late Warring States period as an informal, simplified way of writing seal script characters. Tradition credits its invention to the Qin official Cheng Miao, who was said to have systematised the script while in prison, though archaeology shows the style developed gradually among scribes. After the Qin unification of China in 221 BC, the script was used for day-to-day administration alongside the standardised small seal script.

Characteristics

Clerical characters are wider than they are tall, written with straightened, angular strokes rather than the even, curving lines of seal script. Its most distinctive feature is the "silkworm head and swallow tail" (蠶頭燕尾) horizontal stroke, which rises slightly at the beginning and ends with a pronounced upward flick. Characters were written with greater speed and economy, and many components were abbreviated or merged, fixing the basic structure that later regular script inherited.

Development and later use

During the Eastern Han dynasty the script reached its mature, monumental form in engraved stone stelae, which remain models for calligraphers. By the end of the Han, clerical script had in turn given rise to regular script (kaishu), which became the standard. Clerical script survives today mainly in calligraphy, seals, and decorative uses such as shop signs and book titles.
