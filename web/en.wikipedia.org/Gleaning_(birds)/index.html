Gleaning (birds)

Gleaning is a foraging strategy used by many birds in which prey — usually insects and other arthropods — is plucked from a substrate such as leaves, bark, branches, or the ground while the bird is perched or hopping. It is one of the principal feeding techniques of insectivorous songbirds, contrasted with hawking, in which prey is taken on the wing.

Techniques

Gleaners move systematically through vegetation, scanning surfaces and picking off stationary or slow-moving prey. Variants include hover-gleaning, in which the bird briefly hovers to snatch prey from leaf undersides — typical of kinglets and some warblers — and sally-gleaning, combining a short flight from a perch with a glean. Chickadees, tits, nuthatches, treecreepers, wrens, and many Old and New World warblers are specialist gleaners; bark-gleaning species often probe crevices or flake bark to expose hidden insects.

Ecology

Gleaning birds exert significant predation pressure on forest insects and may reduce pest outbreaks. Their dependence on foliage and bark microhabitats makes many species sensitive to forest structure and fragmentation, and mixed-species flocks frequently organise around gleaning species in winter.
