Hannibal

Hannibal (247 – between 183 and 181 BC) was a Carthaginian general and statesman who commanded the forces of Carthage in their battle against the Roman Republic during the Second Punic War. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest military commanders in history. His father, Hamilcar Barca, was a leading Carthaginian commander during the First Punic War. His younger brothers were Mago and Hasdrubal, and he was brother-in-law to Hasdrubal the Fair.

The Second Punic War

Hannibal lived during a period of great tension in the western Mediterranean, as Rome's power grew at Carthage's expense. According to tradition, his father made him swear an oath of eternal enmity toward Rome. Assuming command in Iberia in 221 BC, Hannibal conquered territory south of the Ebro before attacking Saguntum in 219 BC, precipitating war. In 218 BC he led his army — including famous war elephants — from Spain through southern Gaul and across the Alps into Italy, one of the most celebrated feats of military logistics in history, albeit at heavy cost from cold, terrain and hostile tribes.

Campaigns in Italy

In Italy, Hannibal won a string of brilliant victories: at the Ticinus and the Trebia (218 BC), at Lake Trasimene (217 BC), where he destroyed a Roman army in the largest ambush in military history, and above all at Cannae (216 BC), where a double envelopment annihilated an enormous Roman force in a battle still studied in military academies. Yet Hannibal could not take Rome itself; lacking siege equipment, reinforcements and a secure supply base, he campaigned in southern Italy for fifteen years. Rome, refusing terms and avoiding pitched battle under the strategy of Quintus Fabius Maximus, slowly ground down his position, retook his Italian allies' cities, and destroyed his brother Hasdrubal's relief army at the Metaurus in 207 BC.

Defeat and exile

Recalled to Africa in 203 BC to meet Scipio Africanus's invasion, Hannibal was decisively defeated at the Battle of Zama in 202 BC, ending the war. As a peacetime statesman (suffete) he reformed Carthage's finances and oligarchic institutions, but Roman pressure drove him into exile in 195 BC. He served the Seleucid king Antiochus III and later Prusias I of Bithynia. When the Romans demanded his surrender around 183 BC, Hannibal took poison at Libyssa rather than be handed over.

Legacy

Ancient historians, principally Polybius and Livy, preserved his fame; even Roman writers admired his generalship while condemning his supposed cruelty and perfidy. Hannibal's tactics at Cannae remain a byword for the battle of annihilation, and his Alpine crossing continues to fascinate historians and the public alike.
