Brocken spectre

A Brocken spectre (German: Brockengespenst), also called a Brocken bow or mountain spectre, is the magnified (and apparently enormous) shadow of an observer cast upon the surface of clouds or fog below, often surrounded by a rainbow-like halo called a glory. The phenomenon is named after the Brocken, the highest peak of the Harz mountains in Germany, where it was frequently observed and described in the 18th century — notably by Johann Silberschlag in 1780 — and where it became woven into local folklore and literature, including Goethe's Faust.

Explanation

The spectre appears when a low sun behind the observer casts their shadow onto mist or cloud lying below a ridge or summit. Because the shadow falls on water droplets at varying distances, and because the observer lacks nearby reference points, the shadow appears gigantic and can seem to move as the mist moves. The head of the figure is typically encircled by the coloured rings of a glory, formed by the diffraction and back-scattering of light by the droplets — the same optical phenomenon seen around the shadow of an aircraft on cloud. The rings correspond to the antisolar point.

In culture

Brocken spectres fascinated Romantic writers and mountaineers and were recorded by, among others, Charles Wilson (of Wilson cloud chamber fame, who observed one on Ben Nevis, helping inspire his cloud chamber research). The phenomenon is a prize for hill walkers on summits throughout the world whenever conditions — sun behind, mist below — align.
