The Kingdom of Bohemia (Czech: České království; German: Königreich Böhmen; Latin: Regnum Bohemiae), sometimes referenced in English as the Czech Kingdom, was a medieval and early modern monarchy in Central Europe, the predecessor of the modern Czech Republic. It was an Imperial State in the Holy Roman Empire, and the Bohemian king was a prince-elector of the empire. The kingdom was established in 1198 when the dukes of Bohemia of the Přemyslid dynasty obtained hereditary royal title, confirmed by the Golden Bull of Sicily in 1212.

History

Bohemia began as a duchy in the 9th century, Christianised under the Přemyslids and centred on Prague. The kingdom reached heights of power and prosperity under Ottokar II in the 13th century and under the Luxembourg dynasty in the 14th, especially Charles IV, who became Holy Roman Emperor, made Prague the imperial capital, founded Charles University in 1348 and issued the Golden Bull of 1356, which confirmed Bohemia's elector status. After the death of Jan Hus in 1415, the Hussite Wars convulsed the kingdom for two decades.

In 1526 the Bohemian estates elected Ferdinand of Habsburg king, and Bohemia became part of the Habsburg Monarchy. The Bohemian Revolt of 1618 triggered the Thirty Years' War; after the Protestant defeat at the Battle of White Mountain (1620), the kingdom lost much of its autonomy and was recatholicised. Bohemia remained a crown land of the Austrian Empire and then Cisleithanian Austria-Hungary until the monarchy's collapse in 1918, when its lands formed the core of the new state of Czechoslovakia.

Lands of the Bohemian Crown

Together with the Margraviate of Moravia, the duchies of Silesia and the Lusatias, Bohemia formed the Lands of the Bohemian Crown (the "Crown of Saint Wenceslas"). The kingdom's core corresponds to the modern Czech region of Bohemia, roughly the western two-thirds of the Czech Republic.
