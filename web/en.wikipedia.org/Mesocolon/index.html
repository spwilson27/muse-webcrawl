Mesocolon

The mesocolon is the fold of peritoneum — a part of the mesentery — that attaches the colon to the posterior abdominal wall. It carries the blood vessels, nerves and lymphatics supplying the colon between its two peritoneal layers.

Parts

Named regions correspond to the segments of colon they support. The transverse mesocolon suspends the transverse colon from the pancreas and forms the boundary between the supracolic and infracolic compartments of the abdomen; the greater omentum attaches to it. The sigmoid mesocolon is an inverted V-shaped fold anchoring the sigmoid colon in the pelvis, with the left ureter and iliac vessels behind its apex. In the embryo the ascending and descending colon also possess mesocolons, but these normally fuse to the posterior wall (the fusion fascia of Toldt), leaving the ascending and descending colon secondarily retroperitoneal — though a persistent ascending mesocolon occurs as an anatomical variant in a minority of people.

Modern view

Work in the 2010s, notably by J. Calvin Coffey and colleagues, re-characterised the mesentery — including the mesocolon — as a single continuous organ rather than a set of fragmented folds. Surgical practice in colorectal cancer, particularly total mesocolic excision, exploits the embryological planes of the mesocolon to remove the colon with its intact lymphovascular envelope, improving outcomes.

Clinical notes

The mesocolon's vessels — branches of the superior and inferior mesenteric arteries — are central to abdominal surgery; its folds define spaces where fluid collects, and twisting of a mobile segment on its mesocolon causes volvulus, a surgical emergency most often affecting the sigmoid colon.
