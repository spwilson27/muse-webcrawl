Diesel particulate filter

A diesel particulate filter (DPF) is a device designed to remove diesel particulate matter, or soot, from the exhaust gas of a diesel engine. Wall-flow filters usually remove 85% or more of the soot and can approach 100% efficiency under some conditions.

Operation

Most DPFs use a honeycomb substrate of cordierite or silicon carbide with channels blocked at alternate ends, forcing exhaust through porous walls that trap particles. Accumulated soot must periodically be burned off in a process called regeneration, which raises exhaust temperature through engine management or fuel-borne catalysts; ash from lubricant additives accumulates permanently and requires eventual cleaning.

Regulation and issues

DPFs became effectively mandatory on new diesel cars in Europe with Euro 5 standards (2009) and on heavy-duty vehicles under similar rules elsewhere. Short-trip driving can prevent regeneration from completing, causing clogging, while removal of a DPF is illegal in many jurisdictions because it greatly increases particulate emissions.
