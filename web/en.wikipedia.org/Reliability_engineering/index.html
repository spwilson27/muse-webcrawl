Reliability engineering

The bathtub curve: early failures, useful life, then wear-out.

Reliability engineering is a sub-discipline of systems engineering concerned with the ability of a system or component to function under stated conditions for a specified period of time. Reliability is formally defined as the probability that an item will perform a required function without failure under stated conditions for a stated time interval. The field combines probability and statistics with engineering design, testing and maintenance.

Key measures

Central quantities include the failure rate (often modelled by the exponential or Weibull distribution), mean time between failures (MTBF) for repairable systems, and mean time to failure (MTTF) for non-repairable items. The classic bathtub curve describes failure rate over life: high but decreasing "infant mortality" failures, a roughly constant useful-life period, then rising wear-out failures. Reliability of composite systems is computed by reliability block diagrams and fault-tree analysis: components in series reduce reliability; parallel redundancy increases it.

Methods

Reliability engineers use failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), accelerated life testing, reliability growth testing, derating of components, redundancy, and preventive maintenance planning. Standards include the MIL-HDBK-217 tradition for electronic parts and IEC 61508 for functional safety. The discipline grew out of Second World War experience with unreliable military electronics and was institutionalised in aerospace and missile programmes of the 1950s.

Reliability differs from quality (conformance at a point in time) and from availability, which combines reliability with maintainability. Modern applications range from aircraft and medical devices to software reliability engineering.
