Linear perspective

Linear perspective is a system of creating the illusion of depth and three-dimensional space on a flat surface. In linear perspective, parallel lines receding from the viewer appear to converge at one or more vanishing points on the horizon line, and objects appear smaller as their distance from the viewer increases. Together with foreshortening and atmospheric perspective, it is one of the principal depth cues used in Western painting since the Renaissance.

History

The Florentine architect Filippo Brunelleschi is credited with the first systematic demonstration of linear perspective around 1415, using a painted panel of the Baptistery viewed through a peephole and mirror. Leon Battista Alberti codified the method in his treatise De pictura (1435), describing the picture plane as an open window and the visual pyramid converging on a centric point. Masaccio's Holy Trinity (c. 1427) in Santa Maria Novella is among the earliest surviving paintings constructed with rigorous one-point perspective. Piero della Francesca later gave the method mathematical treatment in De prospectiva pingendi.

Types

  • One-point perspective: a single vanishing point on the horizon; used for interiors and views down roads or corridors.
  • Two-point perspective: two vanishing points, for objects viewed at an angle, such as the corner of a building.
  • Three-point perspective: adds a vanishing point above or below for vertical lines, used for extreme views up or down.

Principles

The artist establishes a horizon line at the viewer's eye level and selects vanishing points. Lines parallel in reality and perpendicular to the picture plane (orthogonals) are drawn to the vanishing point; horizontal and vertical lines remain parallel to the picture plane's edges in one-point constructions. The geometric basis corresponds to projective geometry: the size of an object's image varies inversely with its distance from the eye.

Influence

Linear perspective reshaped European art, architecture, stage design, and eventually photography and computer graphics, where perspective projection matrices implement the same geometry. Some twentieth-century movements deliberately rejected or distorted perspective, but it remains the default convention for realistic depiction.
