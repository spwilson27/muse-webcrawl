Devshirme (Ottoman Turkish: دوشيرمه, devşirme, "child levy" or "blood tax") was the Ottoman practice of forcibly recruiting soldiers and administrators from among the children of their Balkan Christian subjects. It is considered a form of slavery in which the recruits were taken from their families, converted to Islam, and trained for service in the sultan's household, the Janissary infantry, or the imperial administration.

History

The system emerged in the late fourteenth century, under Murad I and his successors, as a means of creating a military and administrative elite personally loyal to the sultan and independent of the Turkish aristocracy and the free Muslim population, who could not legally be enslaved. Levies were conducted irregularly — roughly every one to seven years — by Janissary officers touring Christian villages in the Balkans, selecting boys generally between eight and eighteen years old according to quotas.

Life of the recruits

Selected boys (acemi oğlanlar, "novice boys") were taken to Constantinople, circumcised and converted to Islam, and educated in Turkish language, religion and martial skills. The most promising were chosen for the palace schools (Enderun), from which came grand viziers — including Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, a Serb from Bosnia — provincial governors and court officials. Others entered the Janissary corps, the empire's elite standing infantry. Although the levy was traumatic for families, some Muslim and Christian families later sought to place sons in the system because of the advancement it offered.

Decline

From the late sixteenth century the rules eroded: Janissaries won the right to marry and enrol their own sons, and Muslim families bribed their way in. The last recorded levies occurred in the early eighteenth century, and the Janissary corps itself was destroyed in 1826 in the Auspicious Incident.
