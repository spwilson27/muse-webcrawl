Cherokee syllabary

The Cherokee syllabary is a syllabary invented by Sequoyah in the late 1810s and early 1820s to write the Cherokee language. Each symbol represents a syllable rather than a single sound, and the system comprises 85 characters. After witnessing the power of written communication among European settlers, Sequoyah — who was illiterate in English — set out to create a writing system for Cherokee; his achievement is one of the very few instances in history of a member of a pre-literate people independently inventing an effective script.

Invention

Sequoyah began work around 1809 and completed the syllabary by 1821, testing it with his daughter Ayoka. Although some letter shapes were inspired by characters he had seen in English, Latin, and Greek print, the sound values he assigned were entirely his own and bore no relation to the source letters' values. The Cherokee Nation officially adopted the syllabary in 1825, and literacy among the Cherokee rapidly surpassed that of settlers in the United States.

Publishing

In 1828 the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix began publication in New Echota using type cast for the syllabary. Books, religious texts, laws, and newspapers followed. After the forced removal of the Cherokee to Indian Territory (the Trail of Tears, 1838–39), printing resumed with papers such as the Cherokee Advocate.

Modern use

Cherokee is considered endangered, but revitalisation efforts include immersion schools, university courses, and digital support: the syllabary is encoded in Unicode (U+13A0–U+13FF plus a lowercase block) and appears in modern operating systems and smartphones. Sequoyah's syllabary inspired other scripts, notably the Vai syllabary of Liberia.
