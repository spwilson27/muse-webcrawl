Lab-on-a-chip

A lab-on-a-chip (LOC) is a device that integrates one or several laboratory functions on a single integrated circuit — commonly called a "chip" — of only a few square millimetres to square centimetres, so that assays can be performed with extremely small fluid volumes, down to femtolitres. The field combines microfluidics, microfabrication and analytical chemistry, and is central to point-of-care diagnostics, chemical synthesis screening and single-cell analysis.

History

The concept grew out of micro total analysis systems (µTAS) proposed in the 1990s by Andreas Manz and colleagues, building on inkjet-printhead microfluidics and semiconductor-style photolithography. Soft lithography in PDMS, popularised by George Whitesides' group, made rapid prototyping of microfluidic devices widely accessible.

Applications

Applications include portable blood-chemistry and nucleic-acid tests, PCR chips, organ-on-a-chip models for drug testing, environmental sensing and droplet microfluidics for high-throughput biology. Advantages are low sample consumption, fast analysis, parallelisation and disposability; challenges include standardisation, detection sensitivity and commercial integration with readers.
