Diversification (business)

Diversification

Type: Corporate growth strategy

Framework: Ansoff matrix (new products, new markets)

Forms: Related (concentric) and unrelated (conglomerate)

Diversification is a corporate strategy to enter into a new product line or market that one's organization does not previously operate in. In Igor Ansoff's product–market growth matrix, diversification is the most risky of the four growth strategies, because it involves both new products and new markets, taking a firm away from its existing knowledge base.

Types

  • Related (concentric) diversification — entering activities linked to the firm's existing value chain, technology, or customers, so that synergies can be shared. A classic example is a manufacturer adding complementary products sold through the same channels.
  • Unrelated (conglomerate) diversification — entering entirely new industries with no connection to existing operations, producing conglomerates such as Berkshire Hathaway or the diversified groups common in emerging economies.
  • Horizontal diversification — adding new products for the firm's existing customers, unrelated to current products technologically.

Motives

Firms diversify to grow when existing markets saturate, to spread risk across industries, to exploit underused resources and capabilities, and to gain market power. Empirical research finds that moderately diversified firms often outperform both focused and extremely diversified firms, though markets frequently apply a "diversification discount" to conglomerate shares, doubting that managers can allocate capital better than shareholders could themselves.

Risks

Diversification can fail through overpaying for acquisitions, managerial distraction from the core business, and cross-subsidizing weak units. Since the 1980s many Western firms have refocused, divesting unrelated businesses, while diversified business groups remain common where capital and labour markets are less developed.
