Galvanization (or galvanisation in British English) is the process of applying a protective zinc coating to steel or iron, to prevent rusting. The most common method is hot-dip galvanizing, in which parts are submerged in a bath of molten zinc. Galvanizing protects the underlying metal in two ways: the zinc forms a barrier coating, and it acts as a sacrificial anode, corroding preferentially if the coating is scratched — a form of cathodic protection. The term derives from Luigi Galvani, via the nineteenth-century scientific vocabulary of galvanism.

History

An early example is the seventeenth-century Indian armour in the Royal Armouries collection, coated with zinc. In 1742 the French chemist Paul Jacques Malouin described dipping iron in molten zinc to the French Royal Academy. Stanislas Sorel patented a practical process in 1836 — cleaning iron with sulfuric acid and fluxing with ammonium chloride before dipping — and coined the term galvanisation. In Britain, related patents by Henry William Crawford and others led to widespread industrial adoption for corrugated iron, wire, ships' fittings and roofing.

Methods

In batch hot-dip galvanizing, cleaned steel is fluxed and immersed in molten zinc at about 450 °C (842 °F); a series of zinc–iron alloy layers forms, topped by pure zinc, giving the characteristic spangled grey finish. Continuous galvanizing runs sheet steel through the bath at speed for products such as car body panels. Other techniques include electrogalvanizing (electroplating a thin zinc layer, common on fasteners and automotive sheet), sherardizing (diffusing zinc in a heated drum of zinc dust), thermal spraying of zinc, and zinc-rich paints. The term "galvanized" is loosely applied to any zinc coating.

Applications and behaviour

Galvanized steel is used for structural steelwork, guardrails, lamp posts, roofing and cladding, fencing, buckets, nails and ductwork. Over time the bright zinc surface weathers to a matte grey patina of zinc carbonate that slows further corrosion. Galvanized coatings can last 50 years or more in rural atmospheres. Zinc fumes produced when welding or cutting galvanized steel can cause metal fume fever, so ventilation is required.
