Stellar black hole

Type: Black hole
Mass: ~5 to several tens of solar masses
Formation: Gravitational collapse of a massive star
Example: Cygnus X-1

A stellar black hole (or stellar-mass black hole) is a black hole formed by the gravitational collapse of a massive star. They have masses ranging from about 5 to several tens of solar masses. The process of formation is a collapse that may be observed as a supernova or a gamma-ray burst.

Formation

When a star with a mass several times that of the Sun exhausts its nuclear fuel, its core collapses. If the remnant core exceeds the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit (roughly 2–3 solar masses), no known force can halt the collapse, and a black hole forms, often leaving the new black hole in a binary system with a surviving companion star.

Observation

Stellar black holes are detected through their interaction with companion stars: gas stripped from the companion forms a hot accretion disk that emits X-rays, as in the first strong black hole candidate, Cygnus X-1. Since 2015, gravitational-wave observatories such as LIGO and Virgo have detected the mergers of stellar-mass black holes, revealing some heavier than expected from single-star evolution and reshaping understanding of their formation channels.
