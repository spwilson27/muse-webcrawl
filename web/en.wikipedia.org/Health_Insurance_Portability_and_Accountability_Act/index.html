Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (HIPAA) is a United States federal statute enacted on 21 August 1996. Although its original purpose was to improve the portability of health insurance coverage for workers changing jobs, HIPAA is best known today for its national standards protecting the privacy and security of health information, administered by the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) Office for Civil Rights.

Titles

Title I protects health insurance coverage for workers and their families when they change or lose jobs, limiting exclusions for pre-existing conditions (provisions later largely superseded by the Affordable Care Act). Title II, the Administrative Simplification provisions, required HHS to adopt standards for electronic health care transactions and national identifiers, and produced the privacy and security rules for which the act is known. Other titles address tax-related health provisions, group health plans and revenue offsets.

Privacy and Security Rules

The Privacy Rule (compliance required from 2003) governs "protected health information" (PHI) held by covered entities — health plans, health care clearinghouses and most providers — and their business associates. It gives patients rights to access and amend their records, requires authorisation for most non-routine disclosures, and applies the "minimum necessary" standard. The Security Rule sets administrative, physical and technical safeguards for electronic PHI, including access controls, audit logs and encryption. The Breach Notification Rule (2009, under the HITECH Act) requires notification of affected individuals and HHS after breaches of unsecured PHI.

Enforcement

The HHS Office for Civil Rights investigates complaints and conducts audits; civil monetary penalties are tiered by culpability and can reach millions of dollars per violation category per year. Large settlements have been levied against insurers and hospital systems following ransomware attacks and impermissible disclosures. HIPAA provides no private right of action, though state laws may apply. A common misconception is that HIPAA bars anyone from asking about health information; in fact it regulates how covered entities use and disclose PHI.
