Dehesa (pastoral management)

A dehesa (Spanish: [de'esa]) is a multifunctional, agrosylvopastoral system and cultural landscape of southern and central Spain and southern Portugal, where it is known as a montado. Dehesas cover several million hectares of the Iberian Peninsula. They are open woodlands of evergreen oaks — principally holm oak and cork oak — maintained by human management over centuries, in which livestock grazing, forestry, and sometimes cultivation are combined on the same land.

Management and products

The tree layer is pruned and thinned to balance shade, acorn production, and pasture growth. Holm oaks provide acorns (bellotas) that fatten free-ranging Iberian pigs during the autumn and winter montanera, producing the prized jamón ibérico de bellota. Cork oaks are stripped of their bark roughly once a decade for cork production. Sheep, cattle, and fighting bulls are also grazed, and the land may yield firewood, charcoal, honey, and game. Traditional management involves periodic scrub clearing and, in places, rotating strips of cereal cultivation.

Ecology

Dehesas are celebrated as a model of sustainable land use and support high biodiversity. They provide habitat for species such as the Spanish imperial eagle, the Iberian lynx, black vultures, and wintering cranes, and are recognised under European conservation designations. However, the system faces threats: many oak stands suffer from a lack of regeneration because grazing suppresses seedlings, and oak decline associated with the pathogen Phytophthora cinnamomi has damaged large areas. Low profitability and rural depopulation further endanger the active management on which the landscape depends.
