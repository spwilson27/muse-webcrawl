Congregational church

Congregational churches (also called Congregationalism) are Protestant churches in the Reformed tradition practicing congregationalist church governance, in which each congregation independently and autonomously runs its own affairs. Congregationalism arose in England in the late 16th and 17th centuries among Puritan separatists and Independents such as Robert Browne.

Beliefs and practice

Congregational churches hold that each local congregation is a full and independent church under Christ, with authority to call its own ministers, admit members, and manage its property, typically by democratic vote of the members. They generally practice believer's or infant baptism and the Lord's Supper, and they emphasize covenant theology — the gathered church bound by a covenant among its members.

History and spread

Congregationalists were central to the founding of New England — the Pilgrims of Plymouth Colony and the Puritans of Massachusetts Bay were Congregational — and founded institutions such as Harvard and Yale. Many British Congregational churches joined Presbyterian and other churches to form the United Reformed Church in 1972, while in the United States most Congregational churches merged into the United Church of Christ in 1957; independent bodies such as the National Association of Congregational Christian Churches continue the tradition.
