Randomized controlled trial

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) is a type of scientific experiment in which participants are randomly allocated to receive one of several interventions — typically a treatment group and a control group. Because randomization balances both known and unknown confounding factors between groups, the RCT is regarded as the gold standard for establishing causal effects of interventions, especially in medicine.

Design

Key elements include a pre-specified protocol, random sequence generation and allocation concealment, and, where possible, blinding: single-blind (participants), double-blind (participants and investigators), or triple-blind designs reduce performance and detection bias. Outcomes are compared between groups, ideally by intention-to-treat analysis, which preserves the benefits of randomization. Reporting follows the CONSORT guidelines, and trials are commonly registered publicly before enrolment begins.

History

Although earlier experiments used elements of the design, the 1948 Medical Research Council trial of streptomycin for tuberculosis, designed with Austin Bradford Hill, is usually regarded as the first modern RCT. Randomized designs subsequently spread through clinical medicine — where phases of drug trials are a regulated form — and, from the late 20th century, into economics, education, and public policy as "randomized evaluations".

Limitations

RCTs can be expensive and slow, may enroll patients unlike ordinary practice (limiting external validity), and are sometimes unethical or impractical — for example, when withholding a known effective treatment or randomizing harmful exposures. Results can also be distorted by attrition, selective reporting, and publication bias, issues addressed through registration and systematic review.
