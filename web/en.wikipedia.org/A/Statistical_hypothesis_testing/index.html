Statistical hypothesis testing

Statistical hypothesis testing is a method of statistical inference in which a claim about a population (the alternative hypothesis) is evaluated against a default claim (the null hypothesis) using sample data. It is the standard framework for significance testing in science and was assembled in the early 20th century from the work of Ronald Fisher, Jerzy Neyman, and Egon Pearson.

The framework

A test statistic is computed from the data, and its observed value is compared with the distribution it would have if the null hypothesis were true. The p-value is the probability, under the null, of results at least as extreme as those observed; if the p-value falls below a pre-chosen significance level (α, commonly 0.05), the null is rejected. In the Neyman–Pearson approach, the procedure is framed in terms of error rates: a Type I error rejects a true null (probability α), while a Type II error fails to reject a false null (probability β; 1 − β is the test's power).

Criticism and alternatives

Hypothesis testing is widely criticized for encouraging dichotomous thinking, because p-values are routinely misinterpreted as the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and because publication bias toward "significant" results contributes to the replication crisis. Alternatives and supplements include confidence intervals and effect sizes, Bayesian hypothesis testing and Bayes factors, and likelihood-based methods. Many statisticians recommend treating test results as one continuous piece of evidence rather than a verdict.
