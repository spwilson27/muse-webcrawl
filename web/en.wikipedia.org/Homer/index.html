Homer (Ancient Greek: Ὅμηρος, Hómēros) is the legendary author of the Iliad and the Odyssey, the two epic poems that are the foundational works of ancient Greek literature. Homer is a central figure of the ancient Greek literary tradition and one of the most influential authors in history. The Iliad tells of the wrath of Achilles during the Trojan War; the Odyssey of Odysseus's ten-year journey home to Ithaca.

The Homeric Question

Little is known about Homer's life; ancient tradition made him a blind singer from Ionia (Chios and Smyrna both claimed him). Since antiquity — and especially since F. A. Wolf's Prolegomena ad Homerum (1795) — scholars have debated the "Homeric Question": whether the epics were composed by a single poet, and when. In the 1930s Milman Parry and Albert Lord demonstrated that the poems' formulaic language derives from a long tradition of oral composition. Most scholars now date the poems' fixation in writing to the late 8th or early 7th century BC.

Language and style

The poems are written in an artificial literary dialect mixing Ionic and Aeolic forms, in dactylic hexameter. They are characterised by repeated epithets ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn"), extended similes, type scenes, and a vast cast of gods and heroes. In antiquity, the Homeric Hymns and the comic Margites were also attributed to him.

Legacy

Homer stood at the head of Greek education and culture: Plato called him "the educator of Greece". His epics shaped Greek religion, ethics and identity, and through Virgil's Aeneid, Dante, and countless translations they have influenced Western literature ever since. The modern study of Homer spans archaeology (the Troy excavations of Heinrich Schliemann), linguistics, and comparative oral poetry.
