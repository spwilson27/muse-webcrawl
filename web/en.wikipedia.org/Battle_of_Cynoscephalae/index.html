Battle of Cynoscephalae

Date197 BC
LocationCynoscephalae hills, Thessaly, Greece
ResultRoman victory
BelligerentsRoman Republic and allies vs. Kingdom of Macedon

The Battle of Cynoscephalae was fought in 197 BC between the Roman Republic, led by Titus Quinctius Flamininus, and the Antigonid kingdom of Macedon, led by Philip V, near the Cynoscephalae ("Dogs' Heads") hills in Thessaly. It was the decisive battle of the Second Macedonian War and a landmark in military history, demonstrating the superiority of the flexible Roman manipular legion over the Macedonian phalanx on broken ground.

Background

Rome had declared war on Philip V in 200 BC after his aggression against Rome's Greek allies. After two years of inconclusive campaigning, Flamininus, who had cultivated the Greek states with promises of freedom, marched into Thessaly in 197 BC. The two armies, each roughly 25,000–26,000 men — Philip's force including about 16,000 phalangites, Flamininus's including Aetolian cavalry and elephants — stumbled into one another in fog near the twin hills of Cynoscephalae.

Battle

The engagement began as an accidental skirmish between advance guards in the mist. On the Macedonian right, Philip led a charge downhill with half his phalanx fully formed and drove the Roman left back. On his left, however, the phalanx was still struggling over the rough ridges, unformed and in column. Flamininus concentrated against this wing with his right, supported by elephants, and shattered it. The decisive moment came when an unnamed Roman military tribune, on his own initiative, detached twenty maniples from the victorious right wing and led them against the rear of Philip's advancing right phalanx. Caught front and rear on ground unsuited to its long pikes, the phalanx collapsed. Macedonian losses were about 8,000 killed and 5,000 captured, against some 700 Roman dead, according to Polybius and Livy.

Aftermath

Philip sued for peace, accepted Roman terms, abandoned his conquests in Greece, paid an indemnity and surrendered his fleet. At the Isthmian Games of 196 BC, Flamininus dramatically proclaimed the "freedom of the Greeks". Cynoscephalae established Rome as the dominant power in the eastern Mediterranean and became a classic case study — analysed by Polybius — of legion versus phalanx.
