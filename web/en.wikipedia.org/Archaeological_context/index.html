Archaeological context

In archaeology, the word context has two related meanings. In the broad sense it refers to the setting in which an artefact or feature was found—the matrix, location and associations that give it meaning. In the technical sense used in excavation, a context (or stratigraphic unit) is a single distinct deposit, layer, fill or cut that represents one event in the sequence of a site.

Context in excavation

Archaeologists excavate and record context by context, rather than in arbitrary levels, because each context preserves information about the order and circumstances of past activity. A cut (such as a pit or ditch), its fills, and the buried surfaces around them are each separate contexts. The relationships between contexts—what seals, cuts or overlies what—are summarised in the Harris matrix, which diagrams the stratigraphic sequence of the site.

Each context is given a unique number, described on a context record sheet, planned and photographed. Finds are bagged by context so that artefacts can be related to the deposits and to one another. An object removed without recording its context loses most of its archaeological value—a major reason why looting destroys information.

Primary and secondary context

Artefacts in primary context remain where they were originally deposited or used, whereas those in secondary context have been moved by later activity, ploughing, erosion or scavenging. Recognising the difference is essential to interpreting assemblages and dating them.
