Gaston Louis Alfred Leroux (6 May 1868 – 15 April 1927) was a French journalist and writer of detective fiction. He is best known for his novel The Phantom of the Opera (Le Fantôme de l'Opéra, 1910), which has been adapted many times for stage and screen, most famously in Andrew Lloyd Webber's stage musical.

Life and career

Leroux was born in Paris and studied law, but spent much of his inheritance within a year and turned to journalism, working as a court reporter, theatre critic and foreign correspondent for Paris newspapers including L'Écho de Paris and Le Matin. His reporting took him across Europe and to Russia, where he covered the revolution of 1905.

In 1907 he left journalism to write fiction. His detective novel The Mystery of the Yellow Room (1907), featuring the young reporter-detective Joseph Rouletabille, became one of the most celebrated "locked-room" mysteries ever written and a landmark of the genre. Many Rouletabille adventures followed, as well as novels such as The Perfume of the Lady in Black, The Haunted Chair and the Chéri-Bibi stories about an ex-convict.

The Phantom of the Opera

Published in 1910, The Phantom of the Opera tells of Erik, a disfigured musical genius who haunts the Palais Garnier opera house in Paris and falls in love with the singer Christine Daaé. Initially only moderately successful, the novel achieved lasting fame through the 1925 silent film starring Lon Chaney and, above all, the 1986 Lloyd Webber musical, one of the most successful stage productions in history.

Legacy

Leroux also helped found a film company, the Société des Cinéromans, to adapt his serial novels. He died in Nice in 1927. His work, blending journalism, sensation and ingenious plotting, influenced both the French feuilleton tradition and the modern detective novel.
