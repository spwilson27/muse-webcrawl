Machicolation

A machicolation (French: mâchicoulis) is a floor opening between the supporting corbels of a battlement or parapet, through which stones or other material could be dropped on attackers at the base of a defensive wall. Machicolations were common in medieval fortifications and later appeared as a decorative feature on other buildings.

Design

The parapet overhangs the wall face on corbels, and the floor of the projecting gallery has openings set between those corbels. From these slots, defenders could drop stones, boiling water, or quicklime onto enemies below, or shoot arrows at them, while remaining protected. Machicolations are usually found over gateways and along curtain walls and tower tops, and they replaced earlier temporary wooden hoardings that served a similar purpose.

History

Stone machicolations developed in the medieval Middle East and Europe during the Crusades era and spread widely in the 13th and 14th centuries. Examples survive on castles and city walls across France, Britain, Spain, and the Levant. After gunpowder made such defences obsolete, the machicolation persisted as ornament in Gothic Revival and other picturesque architecture, sometimes as a continuous decorative band near a roofline.
