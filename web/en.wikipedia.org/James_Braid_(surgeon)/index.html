James Braid (19 June 1795 – 25 March 1860) was a Scottish surgeon and natural philosopher who is regarded as the founder of modern hypnotism. Practising in Manchester, Braid rejected the theory of animal magnetism on which mesmerism rested and recast the phenomenon as a physiological and psychological state produced by focused attention, coining the terms "hypnotism" and "hypnosis".

From mesmerism to hypnotism

In November 1841 Braid attended a demonstration by the travelling mesmerist Charles Lafontaine, expecting fraud. Observing that a subject could not open his eyelids, Braid concluded the effect was real but had a physical cause: prolonged fixed staring fatigued the eye muscles and induced a sleep-like condition of the nervous system. Reproducing the effect by having subjects gaze fixedly at a bright object held above eye level, he developed his "eye-fixation" method and began using it therapeutically for conditions such as paralysis, rheumatism, and speech disorders.

Neurypnology

Braid set out his theory in Neurypnology; or, the Rationale of Nervous Sleep (1843), deriving the name from the Greek neuron (nerve) and hypnos (sleep). He later recognised that the state was not true sleep and tried, too late, to replace "hypnotism" with "monoideism" (concentration on a single idea); the original term had already stuck. He increasingly explained suggestion itself as the operative mechanism, anticipating later psychological accounts.

Legacy

Braid's work influenced the French schools of Charcot at the Salpêtrière and Bernheim at Nancy, and through them the early development of psychotherapy. Though his name is less famous than Mesmer's, historians of psychology credit Braid with transforming mesmerism from occult doctrine into an object of scientific study.
