Electron diffraction

Electron diffraction refers to the wave nature of electrons and the diffraction patterns formed when a beam of electrons passes through or reflects from a crystalline material. Because electrons have wavelengths comparable to atomic spacings when accelerated through modest voltages, they are widely used to determine crystal structures.

History

The wave nature of matter was proposed by Louis de Broglie in 1924. In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer at Bell Labs observed diffraction of electrons reflected from a nickel crystal, while George Paget Thomson independently observed electrons transmitted through thin films producing ring patterns. Davisson and Thomson shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1937.

Theory

An electron accelerated through a potential difference gains a de Broglie wavelength inversely proportional to the square root of the voltage. Diffraction obeys Bragg's law, as for X-ray diffraction, but electrons interact much more strongly with matter, so only very thin samples or surfaces can be studied.

Techniques

Common methods include transmission electron diffraction in the transmission electron microscope, low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) for surfaces, and reflection high-energy electron diffraction (RHEED) used during thin-film growth.

Applications

Electron diffraction is used in materials science, chemistry and biology to determine atomic arrangements, identify phases, and study surfaces and nanoparticles.
