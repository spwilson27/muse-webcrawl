Aqueduct (water supply)

PurposeConveying water by gravity from source to city or farmland
Famous examplesPont du Gard; Aqueduct of Segovia; Aqua Claudia
Roman system lengthRome's eleven aqueducts totalled over 400 km

An aqueduct is a watercourse constructed to carry water from a source to a distribution point, typically a city or irrigation area. Although the term is popularly associated with the bridge-like arcades of Roman engineering — such as the Pont du Gard in France — most of an aqueduct's length usually runs in channels at or below ground, descending on a very slight, carefully surveyed gradient so that the water flows by gravity alone.

Ancient aqueducts

Early systems include the qanats of Persia, underground channels tapping aquifers, and Assyrian works such as Sennacherib's aqueduct at Jerwan (c. 700 BC), which carried water to Nineveh. Greek cities built tunnels like Eupalinos's on Samos. The Romans perfected the technology: beginning with the Aqua Appia (312 BC), Rome eventually drew on eleven aqueducts totalling more than 400 km, supplying perhaps a billion litres a day to baths, fountains, and homes; the water commissioner Frontinus described the system in De aquaeductu (c. 97 AD). Roman aqueducts used settling tanks, covered conduits, inverted siphons, and monumental bridges where valleys had to be crossed.

Later and modern aqueducts

Medieval and early-modern systems, from Constantinople's Valens aqueduct to the qanats and Persian and Islamic waterworks, kept the principle alive. In the industrial era, gravity aqueducts supplied growing cities — New York's Croton Aqueduct (1842) and Catskill/Delaware system, and Los Angeles's aqueduct (1913) — while modern projects such as California's State Water Project and China's South–North Water Transfer combine gravity channels with pumping. Navigation aqueducts, which carry canals over rivers, are a related but distinct structure.
