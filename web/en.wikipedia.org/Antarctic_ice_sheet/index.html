The Antarctic ice sheet is one of the two great polar ice caps of Earth (the other being the Greenland ice sheet) and the largest single mass of ice in the world. It covers about 98% of the continent of Antarctica — an area of roughly 14 million square kilometres — and contains about 61% of all the world's fresh water. If it melted completely, global sea level would rise by approximately 58 metres.

Structure

The ice sheet is divided by the Transantarctic Mountains into the much larger, high and cold East Antarctic Ice Sheet, resting mainly on land above sea level, and the smaller West Antarctic Ice Sheet, much of which is grounded below sea level and therefore potentially vulnerable to marine ice-sheet instability. The Antarctic Peninsula has its own smaller ice cap. Snow accumulating in the interior compacts into ice that flows slowly outward under its own weight as glaciers and ice streams, feeding floating ice shelves around the coast, which lose mass by calving icebergs and by basal melting.

History

Antarctica began to glaciate around 34 million years ago, at the Eocene–Oligocene transition, as falling atmospheric carbon dioxide and the opening of ocean gateways (which allowed the Antarctic Circumpolar Current to isolate the continent) cooled the continent. The ice sheet has expanded and contracted with glacial cycles; subglacial lakes such as Lake Vostok lie buried kilometres beneath the East Antarctic plateau, and ice cores drilled from the sheet preserve a climate record extending back some 800,000 years.

Mass balance and sea level

Satellite gravimetry and altimetry show that Antarctica has been losing mass in recent decades, contributing measurably to global sea-level rise. Losses are concentrated in West Antarctica — particularly the Thwaites and Pine Island glaciers of the Amundsen Sea sector, whose retreat may be self-sustaining — and in the Antarctic Peninsula, partly offset by gains from increased snowfall in parts of East Antarctica. The rate of loss and its future trajectory under warming are among the largest uncertainties in sea-level projections.
