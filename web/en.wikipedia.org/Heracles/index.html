Heracles

Heracles (Ancient Greek: Ἡρακλῆς, Hēraklês, meaning "glory of Hera") is a divine hero in Greek mythology, the son of Zeus and Alcmene, and the foster son of Amphitryon. He was a descendant of Perseus and half-brother of the mortal hero's twin Iphicles. The Romans adapted him as Hercules. Heracles is famous for his extraordinary strength, his many adventures, and above all for the Twelve Labours imposed on him by King Eurystheus.

Birth and youth

Zeus fathered Heracles on the mortal Alcmene, wife of Amphitryon. Hera, Zeus's wife, persecuted the child from birth: she delayed his delivery so that his cousin Eurystheus would be born first and rule Mycenae, and she sent two serpents to kill the infant in his cradle — which the baby Heracles strangled with his bare hands. Raised in Thebes, he defeated the Minyans, married the princess Megara, but in a fit of madness sent by Hera he killed his wife and children. To atone, the Delphic oracle ordered him to serve Eurystheus for twelve years and perform the tasks the king demanded.

The Twelve Labours

  1. Slay the Nemean lion, whose hide no weapon could pierce; Heracles strangled it and wore its skin ever after.
  2. Slay the many-headed Lernaean Hydra, whose heads regrew; his nephew Iolaus cauterized the stumps.
  3. Capture the Ceryneian Hind, sacred to Artemis, alive and unharmed.
  4. Capture the Erymanthian Boar.
  5. Clean the Augean stables — fouled by thousands of cattle — in a single day, by diverting two rivers.
  6. Drive off the man-eating Stymphalian birds.
  7. Capture the Cretan Bull.
  8. Steal the man-eating Mares of Diomedes.
  9. Obtain the girdle (belt) of Hippolyta, queen of the Amazons.
  10. Steal the cattle of the monster Geryon in the far west.
  11. Steal the golden apples of the Hesperides, briefly holding up the sky for Atlas in the process.
  12. Capture Cerberus, the three-headed hound of Hades, and bring him up from the underworld.

Other adventures and death

Beyond the labours, Heracles sacked Troy in an earlier generation, wrestled Death for Alcestis, fought the giants alongside the gods, served Queen Omphale of Lydia, and journeyed with the Argonauts. His death came through the centaur Nessus's trick: a shirt smeared with Nessus's poisoned blood, sent by his wife Deianira in the false belief it was a love charm, burned Heracles's flesh. On his funeral pyre on Mount Oeta, his mortal part was consumed and he was raised to Olympus, reconciled with Hera and married to the goddess Hebe.

Worship and legacy

Heracles was worshipped throughout the Greek world both as a hero and, uniquely, as a god. The Heracleidae, his descendants, were claimed as ancestors by Dorian kings, including those of Sparta and Macedon. As Hercules he became a Roman national figure, and his image — from the Farnese Hercules of antiquity to modern film and comics — remains the archetype of the strongman hero.
