Low Earth orbit

Low Earth orbit (LEO)
Altitude rangeAbout 180 km to 2,000 km above Earth's surface
Orbital periodAbout 90–128 minutes
Orbital speedAbout 7.8 km/s at 300 km altitude
Notable occupantsInternational Space Station (~400 km), Hubble Space Telescope (~535 km), Starlink constellation

A low Earth orbit (LEO) is an orbit around Earth with a period of 128 minutes or less and an eccentricity less than 0.25; by common convention it covers altitudes from about 180 km — below which atmospheric drag quickly decays an orbit — up to 2,000 km. Most human-made objects in space are in LEO, including the great majority of satellites and both currently operating space stations. Objects in LEO travel at roughly 7.8 km/s (28,000 km/h) and circle the planet about once every 90 minutes at typical station altitudes.

Characteristics

Because LEO is close to Earth, it requires the least energy to reach of any orbital regime and allows high-resolution Earth observation, low communication latency and relatively easy servicing by crewed vehicles. Its drawbacks include rapid orbital decay from residual atmospheric drag at the lowest altitudes, a small field of view of the planet's surface, and swift passage over any ground station — a satellite at 400 km is in view of a given station for only about ten minutes per pass.

Uses

All crewed spaceflight beyond cislunar missions has taken place in LEO, from Yuri Gagarin's first orbit in 1961 to the continuously crewed International Space Station (ISS), which orbits at about 400 km and completes roughly 15.5 orbits per day. LEO hosts Earth-observation satellites (such as the Landsat and Sentinel series), weather satellites in polar orbits, the Hubble Space Telescope, the Iridium and Globalstar communications constellations, and the large broadband "megaconstellations" such as SpaceX's Starlink and OneWeb, which number thousands of satellites each. Many LEO satellites use Sun-synchronous orbits, a near-polar retrograde orbit that keeps lighting conditions constant for imaging.

Debris and congestion

LEO is the most congested orbital region. Tens of thousands of tracked debris objects — spent rocket stages, dead satellites and collision fragments — share the regime with active spacecraft. The 2009 collision of Iridium 33 and Kosmos 2251 and several anti-satellite tests added thousands of fragments. Concerns about a cascading Kessler syndrome have driven debris-mitigation guidelines, passivation and end-of-life disposal rules (typically deorbiting within 25 years, increasingly within 5), and the growth of active debris-tracking and conjunction-warning services.

Orbital decay

Objects below about 600 km altitude experience enough drag to re-enter within years or decades; the ISS requires periodic reboosts by visiting vehicles. Above 800–1,000 km, debris can persist for centuries, making those bands the most sensitive to long-term accumulation.
