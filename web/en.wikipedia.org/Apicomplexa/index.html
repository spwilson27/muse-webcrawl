The Apicomplexa (also called Apicomplexia) are a large phylum of mainly parasitic alveolates. Most possess a unique form of organelle at the apex of the cell, the apical complex, used to enter host cells, and most have a non-photosynthetic plastid called an apicoplast. The group includes the organisms responsible for malaria, toxoplasmosis, and several important diseases of livestock.

Characteristics

Apicomplexans are unicellular and spore-forming; all described species except a few are obligate endoparasites of animals. Their life cycles are complex, typically alternating between asexual replication (schizogony) and sexual reproduction (gametogony), often across different hosts. Infective sporozoites use the apical complex — including rhoptries, micronemes, and a conoid — together with a gliding motility system to invade host cells.

Important parasites

Plasmodium species cause malaria in humans and other vertebrates, transmitted by mosquitoes. Toxoplasma gondii infects a large fraction of humanity and is dangerous in pregnancy and immunosuppression. Cryptosporidium causes waterborne diarrhoeal disease, while Eimeria and Babesia are significant veterinary pathogens. The apicoplast, a relict chloroplast, is a target for antiparasitic drugs.
