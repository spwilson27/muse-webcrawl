Hadith terminology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hadith terminology (Arabic: muṣṭalaḥ al-ḥadīth) is the body of terminology in Islam which specifies the acceptability of the sayings (hadith) attributed to the Islamic prophet Muhammad and other early Islamic figures by other early Islamic figures of significance, such as Muhammad's family and companions. Individual terms distinguish between those hadith considered rightfully attributed to their source or those which are not.

Components of a hadith

Every hadith consists of two parts: the isnad (chain of narrators) and the matn (the text of the report itself). Hadith scholars scrutinise both. The science of narrator evaluation is known as ʿilm al-rijāl ("science of men"), while criticism of the text examines whether the content contradicts the Quran, better-attested reports, or established history.

Classification by authenticity

Sahih

A sahih (sound) hadith is one whose chain of narration is unbroken, whose narrators are all upright (ʿadl) and precise (ḍābiṭ), and whose text contains neither irregularity (shudhūdh) nor a hidden defect (ʿilla). The most esteemed collections are Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim.

Hasan

A hasan (good) hadith meets the criteria of a sahih hadith except that the precision of one or more of its narrators is considered somewhat less than complete. Hasan hadith are nonetheless acceptable as evidence in most matters of law and doctrine.

Da'if

A da'if (weak) hadith fails one or more of the conditions of authenticity — for example, a break in the chain, or a narrator of poor memory or unknown reliability. A fabricated report is termed mawḍūʿ.

Classification by number of narrators

  • Mutawatir — narrated by so many people at every stage that collusion in fabrication is inconceivable; considered to yield certain knowledge.
  • Ahad — narrated by a limited number of chains; subdivided into mashhur (well-known), ʿaziz (rare) and gharib (strange, narrated by a single narrator at some stage).

Classification by the chain's endpoint

  • Marfuʿ — attributed directly to Muhammad.
  • Mawquf — attributed to a companion of Muhammad.
  • Maqtuʿ — attributed to a member of the following generation (the tabiʿun).

A report whose chain omits a narrator is described by terms such as mursal (when a tabiʿi quotes Muhammad directly), munqatiʿ (broken) or muʿdal (perplexing, with two or more consecutive omissions).
