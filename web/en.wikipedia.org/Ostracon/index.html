Ostracon


Material: Pottery shard or limestone flake
Uses: Notes, receipts, votes, sketches
Plural: Ostraca (ostraka)

An ostracon (plural ostraca) is a piece of pottery, usually broken from a vase or other vessel, or occasionally a flake of limestone, bearing writing or drawing. In the ancient world, where potsherds were abundant and free, ostraca served as the cheap everyday writing material equivalent of scrap paper, used for school exercises, receipts, tax records, letters, lists and artists' sketches.

Egypt and the Near East

Thousands of ostraca survive from ancient Egypt, particularly from the workmen's village of Deir el-Medina, written in hieratic and demotic script; they record everything from laundry lists and legal disputes to literary texts and satirical drawings. Hebrew and Aramaic ostraca, such as the Samaria and Lachish ostraca, are important epigraphic sources for the kingdoms of Israel and Judah.

Greece

In classical Athens, ostraca gave their name to ostracism: voters scratched the name of the citizen they wanted exiled onto a shard. Excavations in the Athenian Agora and the Kerameikos have recovered thousands of these inscribed ballots, including a famous cache of ostraca naming Themistocles, many written by a small number of hands, suggesting pre-prepared ballots distributed by political opponents.
