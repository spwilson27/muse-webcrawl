Inversion (music)

In music theory, an inversion is a rearrangement of the elements of a structure — most commonly a chord, an interval, or a melodic line — so that their relative positions are changed. The term has several distinct technical meanings depending on context.

Chords

A chord is in root position when its root is the lowest-sounding note. If the third of the chord is in the bass it is in first inversion; if the fifth is in the bass, second inversion; and a seventh chord with its seventh in the bass is in third inversion. In figured bass, inversions are indicated by figures such as 6 (first inversion) and 6/4 (second inversion). Inversions smooth voice leading and vary harmonic colour without changing a chord's identity.

Intervals

An interval is inverted by moving its lower note up an octave (or the upper note down), producing a complementary interval: a major third (C–E) inverts to a minor sixth (E–C). Interval numbers add to nine, and qualities swap: major ↔ minor, perfect ↔ perfect, augmented ↔ diminished.

Melodic and contrapuntal inversion

In counterpoint and twelve-tone technique, a melody can be inverted by reflecting its intervals around a pitch axis, so rising intervals become falling ones of the same size. Invertible (double) counterpoint allows two voices to swap positions while remaining consonant. Melodic inversion is a standard device in fugue and in serial composition, where the inverted form of a tone row is one of its four basic transformations.
