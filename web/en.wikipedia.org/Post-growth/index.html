Post-growth

Post-growth thinking emphasises well-being and sufficiency over expansion of GDP.

Post-growth is a set of economic and social perspectives that question the centrality of economic growth — conventionally measured as growth of gross domestic product (GDP) — as a policy objective. Post-growth approaches hold that, especially in wealthy countries, continued aggregate growth may be ecologically unsustainable and may no longer improve well-being, and they explore institutions and policies for prosperity without growth.

Key ideas

Post-growth is an umbrella covering several related frameworks, including degrowth, the steady-state economy associated with Herman Daly, doughnut economics, well-being economics, and "agrowth" (agnosticism about GDP). Common themes are the material and energy throughput of economies, the limits described in The Limits to Growth (1972), shortfalls of GDP as a welfare measure, and proposals such as reduced working time, universal basic services, and indicators beyond GDP.

Critiques and debate

Critics argue that growth remains essential for poverty reduction in low-income countries, that decoupling of output from resource use and emissions can be achieved through technology, and that abandoning growth could destabilise employment, public finances, and pension systems. Post-growth scholars respond that absolute decoupling at the required scale and speed has not been demonstrated globally, and that social outcomes can be secured through redistribution and institutional design.
