Growth hormone deficiency

Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) is a medical condition in which the pituitary gland does not produce enough growth hormone (somatotropin). In children it results in short stature and slow growth velocity; in adults it causes reduced lean body mass and bone density, increased adiposity, fatigue and impaired quality of life. GHD may be congenital or acquired, isolated or combined with other pituitary hormone deficiencies.

Causes

Congenital GHD may arise from genetic mutations affecting pituitary development or the GH1 gene, or from structural midline defects such as septo-optic dysplasia. Acquired causes include pituitary adenomas and their treatment (surgery or radiotherapy), craniopharyngioma, head injury, infection, and infiltrative disorders. Many cases remain idiopathic.

Diagnosis

In children, evaluation follows faltering growth: height well below the expected range, slow growth velocity and delayed bone age. Because growth hormone is secreted in pulses, diagnosis relies on measurements of IGF-1 and IGFBP-3 and on provocation (stimulation) tests, in which agents such as insulin, arginine or glucagon are used to provoke GH release; peak values below a cutoff (commonly 7–10 µg/L) support the diagnosis. MRI of the pituitary is performed to seek structural causes.

Treatment

Treatment is replacement with recombinant human growth hormone, given as daily subcutaneous injections, or with long-acting weekly preparations approved in recent years. In children, treatment is most effective when started early and continued until growth is complete; growth velocity typically accelerates markedly in the first year. In adults, replacement can improve body composition, bone density and quality of life. Historically, cadaver-derived pituitary hormone was used until 1985, when its link to Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease led to its withdrawal.
