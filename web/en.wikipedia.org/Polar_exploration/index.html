Polar exploration

A sledging party during the heroic age of polar exploration.

Polar exploration is the process of exploration of the polar regions of Earth — the Arctic north of the Arctic Circle and the Antarctic south of the Antarctic Circle — by ship, sledge, aircraft, and on foot. Driven by trade, science, national prestige, and adventure, it culminated in the early 20th century "heroic age" races to the poles.

Arctic exploration

European interest in the Arctic began with the search for the Northwest and Northeast Passages to Asia. Expeditions by Martin Frobisher, Henry Hudson, and William Barents in the 16th and 17th centuries charted coasts but found no navigable route. The Franklin expedition of 1845 vanished entirely while seeking the Northwest Passage; the passage was first traversed by Roald Amundsen in 1903–1906.

The race for the North Pole ended in controversy: Frederick Cook claimed to have reached it in 1908 and Robert Peary in 1909, though both claims are disputed. Amundsen flew over the pole in the airship Norge in 1926, the first undisputed attainment.

Antarctic exploration

Antarctica was sighted in 1820 by expeditions under Bellingshausen, Bransfield, and Palmer. The heroic age (roughly 1897–1922) saw the great expeditions of Scott, Shackleton, and Amundsen. Amundsen's party reached the South Pole on 14 December 1911, five weeks before Robert Falcon Scott's team, who perished on the return journey. Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914–1917) became famous for the survival of its crew after their ship Endurance was crushed by ice.

Modern era

After the Second World War, exploration gave way to permanent scientific research stations, the International Geophysical Year (1957–58), and the Antarctic Treaty of 1959, which set the continent aside for peaceful scientific purposes.
