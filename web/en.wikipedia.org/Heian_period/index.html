Heian period

Dates794 – 1185
CapitalHeian-kyō (Kyoto)

The Heian period (平安時代, Heian jidai) is the last division of classical Japanese history, running from 794 to 1185. It began when Emperor Kanmu moved the capital to Heian-kyō (present-day Kyoto) and is named after that city. The period is celebrated for the flourishing of Japanese court culture, art and literature.

Politics

Although the emperor remained the nominal ruler, real power for much of the period lay with the Fujiwara clan, whose leaders served as regents (sesshō and kanpaku) and married their daughters into the imperial line. In the later Heian period, retired emperors attempted to rule from behind the scenes in the system known as cloistered rule (insei). Meanwhile the imperial court lost effective control of the provinces, where local warrior families accumulated land and military power.

Culture

Heian court culture produced some of Japan's most famous literature. Around the year 1000, Murasaki Shikibu wrote The Tale of Genji, often called the world's first novel, while Sei Shōnagon composed The Pillow Book. Court women, writing in the new kana phonetic scripts rather than Chinese, were central to this literature. Distinctively Japanese painting (yamato-e), poetry anthologies such as the Kokinshū, and esoteric Buddhist practice also flourished.

Decline

The period ended with the Genpei War (1180–1185) between the rival Taira and Minamoto clans. Minamoto no Yoritomo's victory led to the establishment of the Kamakura shogunate in 1192 (conventionally dated to 1185), shifting power from the court aristocracy to the warrior class.
