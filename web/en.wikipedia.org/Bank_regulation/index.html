Bank regulation

Bank regulation is a form of government regulation which subjects banks to certain requirements, restrictions and guidelines, designed to create market transparency between banking institutions and the individuals and corporations with whom they conduct business, among other things. Because banking is intertwined with the broader economy, an individual bank's failure can spread to others and to the financial system as a whole, so bank regulation generally focuses on ensuring the soundness of individual institutions (microprudential regulation) and the stability of the system (macroprudential regulation).

Objectives

The principal goals of bank regulation commonly include protecting depositors and consumers; maintaining confidence in the banking system; preventing bank runs and systemic crises; reducing financial crime such as money laundering; and allocating credit according to public policy. Regulation is typically carried out by central banks and specialized supervisory agencies — in the United States, for example, the Federal Reserve, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation share oversight.

Instruments

Key regulatory instruments include licensing and chartering requirements; capital adequacy standards, which require banks to hold capital proportional to the riskiness of their assets (internationally framed by the Basel Accords, most recently Basel III, issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision); liquidity requirements; reserve requirements; limits on large exposures and related-party lending; regular examinations and reporting; and deposit insurance schemes, which guarantee depositors' funds up to a limit and reduce the incentive to run. Supervisors may also conduct stress tests to model banks' resilience under adverse scenarios.

History and debates

Modern bank regulation developed in response to repeated banking crises, from the panics of the nineteenth century through the Great Depression — which produced measures such as the US Glass–Steagall Act of 1933 — to the deregulation of the late twentieth century and the global financial crisis of 2007–2008, which prompted wide-ranging reforms including the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and tighter Basel standards internationally. Economists continue to debate the balance between regulation that preserves stability and rules that may burden competition, innovation, or the availability of credit.
