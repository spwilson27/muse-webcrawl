Nitrate film

Nitrate film is motion picture film stock made with a cellulose nitrate (nitrocellulose) base. It was the standard professional film base from the industry's beginnings in the 1890s until the early 1950s, when it was replaced by cellulose acetate "safety film". Nitrate stock produced images of exceptional clarity and tonal richness but was notoriously flammable.

Hazards

Cellulose nitrate is chemically unstable and highly combustible: it contains its own oxygen, burns even when submerged, and decomposing reels give off toxic gases and can ignite spontaneously. Catastrophic vault and cinema fires — including the 1937 Fox vault fire in Little Ferry, New Jersey, and the 1929 Glen Cinema disaster in Scotland — killed archivists and audiences and destroyed large portions of early film heritage. A widely cited estimate holds that some 75% of all silent films are lost, largely through nitrate decomposition, fire, and neglect.

Preservation

Surviving nitrate prints are stored by archives under strictly controlled, cool conditions and copied onto modern polyester or digital media. Because decay is inevitable, preservation is a race against time; occasional discoveries of supposedly lost films in nitrate prints — such as a cache unearthed in Dawson City, Canada, in 1978 — remain major events in film history.
