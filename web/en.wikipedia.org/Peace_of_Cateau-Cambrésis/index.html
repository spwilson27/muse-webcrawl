The Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis, signed in April 1559, ended the Italian War of 1551–1559 and the six-decade series of Italian Wars between the Habsburgs and the Valois. It comprised two treaties: one between Elizabeth I of England and Henry II of France on 2 April, and one between France and Philip II of Spain on 3 April 1559, negotiated at Le Cateau-Cambrésis in northern France. The settlement confirmed Spanish predominance in Italy for the next century and a half.

Terms

France renounced its claims to the Duchy of Milan and the Kingdom of Naples and restored Savoy and Piedmont to Emmanuel Philibert of Savoy (a Spanish ally), while retaining the Three Bishoprics (Metz, Toul and Verdun) and the Pale of Calais, which England formally ceded for a payment of 500,000 crowns — England's last territory on the French mainland, lost the previous year, was thus not recovered. Spain kept Milan, Naples, Sicily, Sardinia and the presidios of Tuscany. The settlement was sealed by marriages: Philip II married Henry II's daughter Elisabeth of Valois, and Emmanuel Philibert married Henry's sister Margaret.

Consequences

With France turning inward on the eve of the French Wars of Religion, Spain emerged as the dominant power in Italy and western Europe. Celebrations of the peace were marred by tragedy: Henry II was fatally wounded in a jousting accident during the tournament held for the wedding festivities in June–July 1559, dying on 10 July. The treaties also freed both crowns to persecute Protestant heresy more vigorously at home.

Significance

Historians regard Cateau-Cambrésis as one of the foundational settlements of the early modern European state system, closing the era of the Italian Wars begun in 1494 and inaugurating a long period of Spanish hegemony in Italy.
