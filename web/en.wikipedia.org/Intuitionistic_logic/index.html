Intuitionistic logic, sometimes more generally called constructive logic, refers to systems of symbolic logic that differ from the systems used for classical logic by more closely mirroring the notion of constructive proof. In particular, systems of intuitionistic logic do not assume the law of excluded middle and the double negation elimination, which are fundamental inference rules in classical logic.

History

Intuitionistic logic was developed by the Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer as part of his philosophy of intuitionism, which held that mathematics is a creation of the mind and that a statement is true only when a mental construction proves it. Arend Heyting formalized the logic in 1930, and Andrey Kolmogorov gave a parallel interpretation of the calculus as a calculus of problems.

Constructive interpretation

Under the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov (BHK) interpretation, a proof of a disjunction "A or B" must provide a proof of A or a proof of B together with a method indicating which; a proof of an existential statement must construct a witness. Consequently, a proof by contradiction that merely derives a contradiction from the negation of a statement does not, in general, establish the statement itself.

Excluded middle and double negation

In intuitionistic logic, from the double negation of a proposition one cannot in general infer the proposition. The law of excluded middle, "A or not A", is not valid for arbitrary A, although its double negation is provable. This reflects the constructive stance that a claim of existence requires an explicit construction.

Semantics

Intuitionistic logic can be given algebraic semantics using Heyting algebras, in which the operations corresponding to implication and negation differ from their Boolean counterparts. Saul Kripke developed a relational semantics (Kripke models) in which truth is monotone along possible worlds: once a statement becomes true at a stage of knowledge, it remains true thereafter.

Relation to classical logic

Classical logic can be embedded into intuitionistic logic via double-negation translations, such as the Gödel–Gentzen translation. Intuitionistic logic is therefore weaker in its theorems but richer in its distinctions: statements that are classically equivalent may be intuitionistically distinct.

Applications

Through the Curry–Howard correspondence, intuitionistic logic underlies typed lambda calculi and proof assistants such as Coq and Agda, in which proofs are programs and propositions are types. It is also connected to topos theory and constructive analysis.
