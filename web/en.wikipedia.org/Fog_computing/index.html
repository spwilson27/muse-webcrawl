Fog computing or fog networking, also known as fogging, is an architecture that uses edge devices to carry out a substantial amount of computation, storage, and communication locally and routes the remainder over the Internet backbone. The term was introduced by Cisco to describe extending cloud computing capabilities to the edge of the network, closer to the Internet of things (IoT) devices that produce and act on data.

Rationale

Cloud-only architectures can struggle with the volume of data produced by billions of sensors and with applications that require very low latency, such as industrial control, autonomous vehicles, and smart grids. Sending every reading to a distant data centre wastes bandwidth and introduces delay. Fog computing inserts an intermediate layer of "fog nodes" — gateways, routers, or local servers — that filter, aggregate and analyse data near its source, sending only summaries or exceptions to the cloud. This reduces latency and backhaul traffic, supports mobility and location awareness, and can keep sensitive data local for privacy and regulatory reasons.

Fog versus edge computing

The terms are often used interchangeably. A common distinction is that edge computing processes data on or very near the device itself, while fog computing describes a hierarchical architecture that includes the edge plus the network layers and infrastructure connecting edge nodes to the cloud, with standardised interfaces and orchestration. The OpenFog Consortium, founded in 2015 by Cisco, Intel, Microsoft, and universities including Princeton and Purdue, published a reference architecture for fog computing before merging into the Industrial Internet Consortium in 2019.

Applications

Typical applications include smart cities (traffic management from camera feeds), industrial IoT (predictive maintenance and closed-loop control in factories), connected vehicles, content delivery, and healthcare monitoring, where rapid local responses matter more than global analytics. Challenges include security and trust across many distributed nodes, resource management and orchestration, and interoperability between vendors.
