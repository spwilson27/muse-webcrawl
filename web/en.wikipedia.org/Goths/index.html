Goths

The Goths (Gothic: 𐌲𐌿𐍄𐌸𐌹𐌿𐌳𐌰, Gutþiuda; Latin: Gothi) were an East Germanic people who played a major role in the fall of the Western Roman Empire and the emergence of medieval Europe. Originating, according to their own traditions preserved by the historian Jordanes, in Scandinavia, they migrated southeast to the Black Sea region by the 3rd century AD. In the 4th and 5th centuries they divided into the Visigoths and Ostrogoths, who established kingdoms in Hispania and Italy respectively.

History

Roman sources place Goths near the Vistula by the 1st–2nd centuries AD. Migrating to the Pontic steppe, they raided the Roman Balkans in the 3rd century, killing the emperor Decius at the Battle of Abritus (251). Converted largely to Arian Christianity through the mission of Ulfilas, who translated the Bible into Gothic — the earliest substantial text in any Germanic language — many Goths sought refuge inside the Empire in 376. Mistreatment provoked revolt, culminating in the Visigothic victory at the Battle of Adrianople (378), where the emperor Valens was killed.

Under Alaric I, the Visigoths sacked Rome in 410 and eventually settled in southern Gaul and Spain, where their kingdom endured until the Muslim conquest of 711. The Ostrogoths under Theodoric the Great conquered Italy in 493 and ruled from Ravenna until Justinian's Gothic War (535–554) destroyed their kingdom. A remnant people, the Crimean Goths, preserved their language into the early modern period.

Culture and legacy

The Goths spoke Gothic, an East Germanic language now extinct but documented in Ulfilas's Bible translation and the Codex Argenteus. Their kingdoms preserved much Roman law and administration — the Visigothic Code influenced later Spanish law — and their name survives in place names, the historical term "Gothic" for medieval art and architecture (applied by Renaissance critics as a slur for "barbarian"), and, through a long chain of association, in Gothic fiction and the modern goth subculture.
