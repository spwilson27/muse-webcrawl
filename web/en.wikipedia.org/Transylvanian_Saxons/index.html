The Transylvanian Saxons (German: Siebenbürger Sachsen) are a people of mainly German ethnicity who settled in Transylvania (now in Romania) from the mid-12th century onwards. They were invited by King Géza II of Hungary to colonise and defend the south-eastern borders of the Kingdom of Hungary, receiving extensive autonomy under the Diploma Andreanum of 1224.

History

Despite the name "Saxons", most settlers came from the Rhine–Moselle region, Flanders, and Luxembourg, and spoke a Moselle Franconian dialect. They founded the "Seven Seats" (Sieben Stühle) around Hermannstadt (Sibiu) and built fortified churches — many now UNESCO World Heritage sites — against Ottoman and Tatar invasions. After the Reformation they largely adopted Lutheranism. In 1918 Transylvania united with Romania, and the Saxons initially thrived, but the Second World War, deportations to the Soviet Union, and communist expropriation devastated the community.

Emigration and present day

Most Transylvanian Saxons emigrated to Germany, especially after 1989; from around 250,000 in Romania in the 1930s (and some 170,000–200,000 in the 1970s), only roughly 13,000–15,000 remain today. Klaus Iohannis, president of Romania from 2014 to 2025, is a Transylvanian Saxon and former mayor of Sibiu.
