The Suez Crisis, also known as the Second Arab–Israeli War and, in Egypt, as the Tripartite Aggression, was an invasion of Egypt in late 1956 by Israel, followed by the United Kingdom and France. It followed Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser's nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company on 26 July 1956, after the US and Britain withdrew funding for the Aswan High Dam.

Background

The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, was the vital artery for oil shipments from the Persian Gulf to Europe and was owned by an Anglo-French company. After the 1952 Egyptian revolution, Nasser sought to end British influence; Britain agreed in 1954 to withdraw its troops from the Canal Zone. The nationalisation of the canal company in July 1956 — following the Aswan Dam financing dispute and Nasser's arms deal with Czechoslovakia — convinced London and Paris that he must be removed.

The invasion

Under the secret Protocol of Sèvres, Israel invaded the Sinai on 29 October 1956; Britain and France then issued an ultimatum to both sides and, on 31 October, began bombing Egyptian airfields. Anglo-French paratroops landed at Port Said on 5 November. Egyptian forces resisted, and Egypt blocked the canal by sinking ships.

International response and aftermath

The United States under President Eisenhower, furious at the collusion and fearing Soviet involvement, applied intense financial and diplomatic pressure — including a run on sterling — while the USSR threatened intervention. A UN ceasefire took effect on 7 November, and the first UN Emergency Force was deployed to the Sinai. Anglo-French forces withdrew by December 1956 and Israel by March 1957. The crisis humiliated Britain and France, ended Anthony Eden's premiership, confirmed the decline of British great-power status, and hugely enhanced Nasser's prestige in the Arab world.
