Thule people

The Thule people (pronounced /ˈtuːliː/) were an ancestral Inuit culture that spread across the Arctic from Alaska to Greenland beginning around 1000 CE. They are considered the ancestors of all modern Inuit. The culture is named after the Thule settlement in northwestern Greenland, near the modern town of Qaanaaq, where the first archaeological evidences of this people were excavated by Danish archaeologist Therkel Mathiassen following the 1921–1924 Danish Fifth Thule Expedition led by Knud Rasmussen.

Migration

The Thule originated in coastal Alaska, around the Bering Strait, developing out of earlier cultures such as the Birnirk culture. Around 1000 CE, during a period of relative warming known as the Medieval Warm Period, Thule groups migrated eastward across the Canadian Arctic, reaching Greenland by about 1200–1300 CE. The migration may have been driven by the pursuit of bowhead whales, changes in sea ice conditions, or interest in sources of meteoric iron in northwestern Greenland.

Genetic studies, including a major 2014 study of ancient and modern DNA, confirm that the Thule largely replaced the earlier Paleo-Eskimo Dorset culture with little interbreeding, and that modern Inuit descend primarily from the Thule.

Culture and technology

The Thule were accomplished marine mammal hunters. Their technology included large skin boats (umiaks) for hunting bowhead whales, smaller kayaks, toggling harpoons, and dog sleds. For winter housing they built semi-subterranean houses of stone and turf with frames of whalebone where wood was unavailable; in summer they lived in skin tents. They also used soapstone lamps (qulliq) burning seal or whale oil.

Decline and transition

With the cooling climate of the Little Ice Age from about 1400 CE onward, bowhead whale migration patterns changed and Thule society adapted, placing greater emphasis on smaller sea mammals, fish, and caribou. Over several centuries the Thule culture developed into the historic Inuit cultures encountered by Europeans.
