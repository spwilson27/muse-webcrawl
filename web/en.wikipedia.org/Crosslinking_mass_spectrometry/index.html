Crosslinking mass spectrometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crosslinking mass spectrometry (XL-MS or CLMS) is a technique in structural biology and proteomics in which chemical crosslinkers are used to covalently connect amino acid residues that are close together in space within a protein or between interacting proteins. The crosslinked sample is then digested into peptides and analyzed by mass spectrometry, and the identified crosslinks provide distance restraints that reveal information about protein structure and interaction networks.

Method

A bifunctional crosslinking reagent, such as the lysine-reactive NHS-ester crosslinker BS3 or the MS-cleavable DSSO, reacts with two functional groups separated by a spacer of known length. After proteolytic digestion, mass spectrometry identifies pairs of peptides joined by the linker. Because the spacer length is known, each identified crosslink indicates that the two residues lie within a maximum distance of one another in the folded structure.

Applications

XL-MS is used to map protein–protein interactions, determine the architecture of large macromolecular complexes, and validate or refine structural models. It complements crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, and NMR spectroscopy, and can be applied to complexes in near-native conditions, including in cells. The distance restraints it provides are widely used in integrative structural modelling.
