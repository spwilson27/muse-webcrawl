Hopi

The Hopi are a Native American people who primarily live on the Hopi Reservation in northeastern Arizona, an enclave within the Navajo Nation. They are one of the Pueblo peoples, descendants of the Ancestral Puebloans, and their name for themselves, Hopituh Shi-nu-mu, is often rendered "the Peaceful People."

History

Hopi villages on three high desert mesas — First, Second, and Third Mesa — include Oraibi, founded around 1100 and among the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in North America. The Hopi participated in the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, which expelled the Spanish from New Mexico for twelve years, and thereafter maintained relative independence. Relations with the neighbouring Navajo over land produced a long-running dispute formally addressed by federal legislation in the twentieth century.

Culture and religion

Hopi society is traditionally matrilineal and clan-based. Religious life centres on kachina (katsina) spirits, ceremonial cycles tied to the agricultural calendar, and dryland farming of corn, beans, and squash in an arid environment — water stewardship is a core cultural value. The Hopi language, a Uto-Aztecan language, became widely known through debates over Benjamin Lee Whorf's claims about its treatment of time.

Present day

The federally recognised Hopi Tribe governs the reservation from Kykotsmovi. Hopi artists are renowned for overlay silver jewellery, kachina carvings, pottery, and woven textiles.
