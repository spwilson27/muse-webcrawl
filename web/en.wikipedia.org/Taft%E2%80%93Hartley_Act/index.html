Taft–Hartley Act

Labor Management Relations Act of 1947
EnactedJune 23, 1947
Named forSen. Robert A. Taft; Rep. Fred A. Hartley Jr.
AmendsNational Labor Relations Act of 1935

The Labor Management Relations Act of 1947, commonly known as the Taft–Hartley Act, is a United States federal law that restricts the activities and power of labor unions. Congress enacted it over President Harry S. Truman's veto.

Provisions

The act prohibited jurisdictional strikes, wildcat strikes, solidarity or political strikes, secondary boycotts, and secondary picketing. It outlawed the closed shop, permitted states to pass "right-to-work" laws banning union shops, and required union leaders to sign affidavits that they were not communists.

Background

After a wave of major strikes in 1946, the Republican-controlled 80th Congress moved to curb union power established by the 1935 Wagner Act. Truman vetoed the bill, calling it a "slave-labor bill," but Congress overrode him.

Legacy

Taft–Hartley remains a cornerstone of U.S. labor law and a perennial target of repeal efforts by organized labor. Its Section 14(b) enabled state right-to-work laws that spread widely in later decades.
