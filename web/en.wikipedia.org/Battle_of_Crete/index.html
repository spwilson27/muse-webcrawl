Battle of Crete

The Battle of Crete (German: Luftlandeschlacht um Kreta, Greek: Μάχη της Κρήτης) was fought during the Second World War on the Greek island of Crete. It began on the morning of 20 May 1941, when Nazi Germany launched an airborne invasion of Crete, code-named Unternehmen Merkur (Operation Mercury), under the command of General der Flieger Kurt Student. Greek and other Allied forces, along with Cretan civilians, defended the island.

Background

After the German invasion of mainland Greece in April 1941 (Operation Marita), Allied forces were evacuated to Crete, which became a key position controlling sea lanes in the eastern Mediterranean and guarding the approaches to the Suez Canal and the Romanian oil fields at Ploiești. The island was defended by some 40,000 British, Commonwealth and Greek troops under Major General Bernard Freyberg, a New Zealand officer.

The airborne assault

On 20 May 1941, German Fallschirmjäger (paratroops) and glider-borne troops descended on the airfields at Maleme, Rethymno and Heraklion. The initial landings were costly: German paratroops suffered heavy casualties, and the attack stalled. However, the capture of Maleme airfield on 21 May—after Allied troops withdrew from Hill 107 overlooking it—allowed German transport aircraft to land mountain troops of the 5th Mountain Division, turning the tide.

Evacuation and aftermath

By 27 May the Allied position was untenable. The British decided to evacuate; thousands of troops withdrew across the mountains to Sfakia on the south coast, where Royal Navy ships took off about 16,500 men to Egypt. Some 12,000 Allied troops were left behind and captured. German losses were also severe—over 4,000 killed or missing according to some estimates—which led Hitler to forbid further large-scale airborne operations for the rest of the war.

Cretan civilians engaged in fierce resistance, and German reprisals against villages such as Kandanos were brutal. The battle remains a landmark in the history of airborne warfare, demonstrating both its potential and its vulnerability.
