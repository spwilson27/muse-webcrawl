Marie Skłodowska Curie (born Maria Salomea Skłodowska; 7 November 1867 – 4 July 1934) was a Polish and naturalised-French physicist and chemist who conducted pioneering research on radioactivity. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields (Physics in 1903 and Chemistry in 1911), and the first woman to become a professor at the University of Paris.

Early life and education

Born in Warsaw, then part of the Russian Empire, Skłodowska could not attend a regular university as a woman and studied at the clandestine Flying University. In 1891 she moved to Paris and studied at the Sorbonne, earning degrees in physics and mathematics. There she met Pierre Curie, whom she married in 1895.

Research

Building on Henri Becquerel's discovery of uranium rays, Marie Curie developed the theory of radioactivity — a term she coined — and techniques for isolating radioactive isotopes. Working with Pierre, she discovered two new elements: polonium (named after her native Poland) and radium, extracted from pitchblende through years of laborious chemical processing in a draughty shed. The Curies shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with Becquerel. After Pierre's death in a street accident in 1906, Marie succeeded him as professor. In 1911 she received a second Nobel Prize, in Chemistry, for the discovery of radium and polonium and the isolation of radium.

World War I and later life

During the First World War she organised mobile radiography units — the famous petites Curies — that brought X-ray equipment to field hospitals, and trained women, including her daughter Irène, as operators. She founded the Institut du Radium in Paris and an institute in Warsaw.

Death and legacy

Curie died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia caused by decades of exposure to radiation, the dangers of which were not yet understood. Her papers and even her cookbooks remain radioactive and are stored in lead-lined boxes. In 1995 she became the first woman interred in the Panthéon in Paris on her own merits. The radioactive element curium and the unit of radioactivity, the curie, are named in her and Pierre's honour.
