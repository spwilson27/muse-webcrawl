Gallium arsenide

Gallium arsenide (GaAs) is a III–V direct band gap semiconductor with a zinc blende crystal structure. It is an important material for microwave frequency integrated circuits, infrared light-emitting diodes, laser diodes, solar cells, and optical windows. Its chemical formula is GaAs and its molar mass is 144.645 g/mol.

Properties

GaAs has a band gap of about 1.424 eV at room temperature and an electron mobility substantially higher than that of silicon, which makes it attractive for high-frequency devices. Unlike silicon, it has a direct band gap, so it can emit light efficiently. Its density is 5.32 g/cm³ and it melts at 1238 °C. The material is brittle and its dust is toxic because of the arsenic content.

Preparation

Single crystals of gallium arsenide are grown from a melt by methods such as the Czochralski process and the Bridgman–Stockbarger technique; liquid encapsulated Czochralski growth is widely used commercially. Thin films are deposited by metalorganic vapour phase epitaxy (MOVPE) or molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), often on GaAs substrates, to build layered device structures including aluminium gallium arsenide (AlGaAs) heterostructures.

Applications

GaAs devices include MESFETs, HEMTs and HBTs used in mobile phone power amplifiers and satellite communications, and Gunn diodes for microwave sources. In optoelectronics, GaAs and AlGaAs form the basis of many laser diodes and LEDs, and multi-junction GaAs solar cells achieve some of the highest photovoltaic efficiencies, widely used on spacecraft.

Comparison with silicon

GaAs offers higher electron mobility and radiation resistance but is more expensive, more fragile, and lacks a high-quality native oxide comparable to silicon dioxide, which has limited its use in mainstream logic circuits.
