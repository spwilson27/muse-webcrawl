Esophageal speech

The pharyngoesophageal segment serves as the vibratory source in esophageal speech

Esophageal speech, also known as esophageal voice, is a method of speech production used after a laryngectomy, in which the surgical removal of the larynx deprives a person of the vocal folds as a sound source. In esophageal speech, the speaker swallows or injects air into the upper esophagus and releases it in a controlled manner; the vibration of the pharyngoesophageal segment produces a low-pitched voice that is then shaped into speech by the articulators.

Technique

Two basic methods are used to charge the esophagus with air: the inhalation method, in which air is drawn in during a quick intake of breath against a lowered intrathoracic pressure, and the injection method, in which the tongue pumps air down into the esophagus. Only a small volume of air is stored — far less than the lungs supply for normal speech — so phrases tend to be short and the fundamental frequency is low, around 60–80 Hz, with limited loudness.

Comparison with other methods

Esophageal speech requires no equipment and leaves the hands free, but it is difficult to learn; only a proportion of laryngectomees achieve fluent esophageal speech. Alternatives include the electrolarynx, an external vibrating device held against the neck or cheek, and tracheoesophageal speech, in which a one-way prosthetic valve diverts pulmonary air into the esophagus, generally producing more fluent and sustained voice.
