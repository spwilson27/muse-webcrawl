Pygmalion (mythology)

Pygmalion is a legendary figure of Cyprus in Greek mythology, most commonly known from Ovid's narrative poem Metamorphoses. A sculptor, Pygmalion carved a statue of a woman so beautiful that he fell in love with it. The goddess Venus (Aphrodite) took pity on him and brought the statue to life; the two married and had a daughter, Paphos, from whom the Cypriot city of Paphos took its name.

Pygmalion
Mythology: Greek
Abode: Cyprus
Consort: Galatea (the statue)
Children: Paphos

The myth

In Ovid's telling, Pygmalion, disgusted by the behaviour of the Propoetides, resolved to live unmarried and devoted himself to his art. He sculpted a figure of a woman in ivory, surpassing any living woman in beauty. He dressed the statue, adorned it with jewels, and laid it upon a couch. At the festival of Venus he prayed for a bride like his ivory maiden; Venus understood his true wish and animated the statue. The maiden awoke beneath his touch, blushing, and the two were wed with Venus herself blessing the union.

Later influence

The name of the statue, Galatea, is a later addition not found in Ovid. The story has inspired countless works: George Bernard Shaw's play Pygmalion (1913), adapted as the musical My Fair Lady, transfers the theme to a professor transforming a flower seller. The "Pygmalion effect" in psychology, named after the myth, describes how higher expectations placed on a person can improve their performance.
