Race to the Pole

Roald Amundsen's team at the South Pole, 14 December 1911.

The race to the Pole refers to the competition between the Norwegian expedition led by Roald Amundsen and the British Terra Nova Expedition led by Robert Falcon Scott to become the first to reach the geographic South Pole, during the austral summer of 1911–1912. Amundsen's party reached the Pole on 14 December 1911; Scott's party arrived on 17 January 1912 to find the Norwegian flag already planted, and all five members of Scott's polar party died on the return journey.

Background

By 1910 the South Pole was the last great unclaimed prize of terrestrial exploration. Scott had previously led the Discovery expedition (1901–1904) and Ernest Shackleton had come within 97 nautical miles of the Pole in 1909. Amundsen, an experienced polar traveller who had completed the Northwest Passage and wintered in Antarctica with the Belgica, originally planned an attempt on the North Pole; when he learned that the Pole had been claimed by Cook and Peary, he secretly redirected his expedition south.

The expeditions

Amundsen established his base, Framheim, at the Bay of Whales on the Ross Ice Shelf, some 60 nautical miles closer to the Pole than Scott's base at Cape Evans. His team of expert skiers used Greenland dogs and depots laid in advance, reaching the Pole after a fast, disciplined march. Scott's expedition relied on a combination of motor sledges, Manchurian ponies and ultimately man-hauling; dogged by bad weather, equipment failures and illness — including the death of Edgar Evans and the self-sacrifice of Lawrence Oates — the polar party died in their tent about 11 miles from a supply depot in March 1912.

Legacy

The race became one of the defining stories of the Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration. Amundsen was celebrated for meticulous planning, while Scott's death made him a national hero in Britain; modern reassessments have debated the relative roles of preparation, logistics and luck in the outcome.
