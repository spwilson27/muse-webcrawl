Free-culture movement

The free-culture movement is a social movement that promotes the freedom to distribute and modify the creative works of others in the form of free content or open content, without compensation to, or the consent of, the work's original creators, by using the Internet and other forms of media. The movement objects to what it considers over-restrictive copyright laws. Many members of the movement argue that such laws hinder creativity and create a "permission culture".

The movement emerged from and was strongly influenced by the earlier free software movement, and it produced important licensing frameworks and institutions, most notably Creative Commons, founded in 2001 by law professor Lawrence Lessig and others.

History

The free-culture movement grew out of the free and open-source software communities of the 1980s and 1990s, whose licenses — such as the GNU General Public License — guaranteed users the freedom to use, study, share, and modify software. Activists and scholars argued that similar freedoms should apply to texts, images, music, and other cultural works, especially as digital networks made copying and remixing inexpensive.

Lawrence Lessig's books Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace (1999) and Free Culture (2004) became foundational texts, arguing that increasingly long and broad copyright terms, together with automated enforcement technologies, threatened the balance between creators' rights and the public domain. The launch of Wikipedia in 2001 demonstrated the power of freely licensed collaborative production, and organizations such as the Free Culture Forum and Students for Free Culture spread the movement's ideas internationally.

Licenses and definitions

The movement is organized around licenses that grant some or all of the freedoms defined in the "Definition of Free Cultural Works": the freedom to use the work, to study it, to redistribute copies, and to distribute derivative works. Creative Commons licenses range from the permissive Attribution license to copyleft-style ShareAlike licenses that require derivatives to be shared under the same terms. Works dedicated to the public domain, such as through CC0, occupy the most permissive end of the spectrum.

Criticism

Critics, including some creators and collecting societies, argue that free licensing can undermine creators' ability to earn a living from their work, and free software founder Richard Stallman warned against applying licenses that permit commercial restriction or non-derivative terms under the "free culture" banner. Others question whether voluntary licensing can reform a copyright system they regard as structurally unbalanced.
