Edict of Fontainebleau

The Edict of Fontainebleau, issued by King Louis XIV of France in October 1685, revoked the Edict of Nantes of 1598 and declared Protestantism illegal in France. Also known as the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes, it ended nearly a century of limited religious toleration for the Huguenots, France's Calvinist minority.

Background

The Edict of Nantes, granted by Henry IV, had given Huguenots freedom of worship and civil rights after the French Wars of Religion. Louis XIV, committed to the principle of "one king, one law, one faith" (une foi, une loi, un roi), progressively eroded these rights. From 1681 the dragonnades — the billeting of unruly dragoons in Protestant households — were used to coerce conversions, and thousands of Huguenots abjured under pressure before the edict was issued.

Provisions

The edict ordered the demolition of Huguenot churches, the closing of Protestant schools, the baptism of all children as Catholics, and the exile of pastors who refused to convert. Lay Protestants were forbidden to leave the kingdom, on pain of the galleys for men and imprisonment for women, precisely to prevent emigration.

Consequences

Despite the ban, an estimated 200,000 Huguenots fled France for England, the Dutch Republic, Brandenburg-Prussia, Switzerland, and North America. The exodus deprived France of many skilled artisans, merchants, and soldiers, while enriching its rivals; Frederick William of Brandenburg responded with the Edict of Potsdam inviting refugees to settle. Within France, Protestant worship survived clandestinely in the "Church of the Desert," particularly in the Cévennes, where persecution later provoked the Camisard revolt. The edict remained in force until the Edict of Versailles of 1787 restored civil status to Protestants.
