Kingdom of Ends

The Kingdom of Ends (German: Reich der Zwecke) is a thought experiment and central concept in the moral philosophy of Immanuel Kant, introduced in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785). It describes an ideal community of rational beings, each of whom treats every person always as an end in themselves and never merely as a means.

Context

The kingdom of ends grows out of Kant's categorical imperative, in particular the "humanity formula": act so that you use humanity, in your own person or another's, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means. In the kingdom of ends, every rational agent is simultaneously legislator and subject of the universal moral law, and each person has an innate dignity (Würde) rather than a price.

Interpretation

Philosophers debate whether the kingdom of ends is a practical ideal to approximate, a way of representing the collective standpoint of moral judgement, or a model of a just political community; it has been read as an ancestor of Rawls's original position and of discourse ethics in Habermas. A "kingdom" for Kant is a systematic union of rational beings under common laws — as opposed to the amoral "kingdom of nature", where everything happens by causal necessity alone.

Related ideas

The concept connects closely to Kant's ideas of autonomy — giving oneself the moral law — and to his later vision of perpetual peace and a cosmopolitan federation, in which persons and states alike are treated as ends.
