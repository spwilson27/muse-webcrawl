Swahili coast

The Swahili coast is the coastal area of Southeast Africa inhabited by the Swahili people, stretching from southern Somalia through Kenya and Tanzania to northern Mozambique, together with offshore islands such as Zanzibar, Lamu, Mombasa and Kilwa. From roughly the 8th century onwards it was home to a chain of prosperous city-states that traded across the Indian Ocean with Arabia, Persia, India and China.

History

Swahili settlements grew as local Bantu-speaking communities engaged in maritime trade carried by the monsoon winds. By the medieval period towns such as Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi and Lamu had become wealthy entrepôts exporting gold, ivory, mangrove poles and enslaved people, and importing ceramics, glassware, cloth and beads. Islam spread along the coast from an early date and became central to Swahili culture, expressed in distinctive stone architecture of coral rag and lime mortar, mosques and carved tombs.

Portuguese and Omani periods

The arrival of Vasco da Gama in 1498 began a period of Portuguese intervention; the Portuguese built Fort Jesus at Mombasa and attempted to control the trade, provoking repeated resistance. In the late 17th century the Omanis expelled the Portuguese from most of the coast, and in the 19th century Sultan Seyyid Said moved his court to Zanzibar, making it the hub of a commercial empire based on cloves and the caravan trade into the African interior.

Culture

The coast gave its name to the Swahili language (Kiswahili), a Bantu language with extensive Arabic loanwords that became the lingua franca of East Africa and is today an official language of several states and of the African Union. Historic stone towns, notably Stone Town of Zanzibar and Lamu Old Town, are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
