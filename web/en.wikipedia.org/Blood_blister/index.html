Blood blister

A blood blister is a type of blister that forms when subdermal tissues and blood vessels are damaged without the skin being pierced. It consists of a pool of lymph, blood, and other body fluids trapped beneath the epidermis. Because the trapped fluid contains blood, the blister appears red, purple, or nearly black, distinguishing it from an ordinary friction blister filled with clear serum.

Causes

Blood blisters most commonly result from pinching or crushing injuries — for example, catching skin in a door, pliers, or a hinge — which rupture small blood vessels while leaving the surface of the skin intact. They can also arise from intense friction, as in prolonged rowing, shovelling, or playing stringed instruments, and may occur in the mouth from biting the cheek or from thermal injury.

Treatment

Most blood blisters heal on their own within one to two weeks as the body reabsorbs the fluid. Leaving the blister intact protects the underlying tissue from infection. Applying a cold compress soon after the injury can limit bleeding under the skin. Medical attention is advisable if a blister shows signs of infection, recurs without an obvious cause, or appears together with easy bruising elsewhere.
