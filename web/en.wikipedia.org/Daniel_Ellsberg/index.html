Daniel Ellsberg

Daniel Ellsberg (April 7, 1931 – June 16, 2023) was an American political activist, economist, and United States military analyst. While employed by the RAND Corporation, he precipitated a national political controversy in 1971 when he released the Pentagon Papers, a top-secret Pentagon study of the U.S. government's decision-making in relation to the Vietnam War, to The New York Times, The Washington Post, and other newspapers.

Early life and career

Ellsberg studied economics at Harvard, earned a PhD with a dissertation on decision-making under uncertainty — the source of the "Ellsberg paradox" in decision theory — and served as a U.S. Marine Corps officer. He joined the RAND Corporation in 1959 and worked on nuclear strategy and command and control. From 1964 he served in South Vietnam with the State Department, an experience that turned him against the war.

The Pentagon Papers

As a contributor to the classified McNamara study of Vietnam decision-making, Ellsberg concluded the war was unwinnable and that successive administrations had misled the public. In 1969–1971, with Anthony Russo, he photocopied the 7,000-page study and leaked it. Publication by the New York Times began on June 13, 1971; the Nixon administration sought an injunction, but the Supreme Court ruled for the newspapers in New York Times Co. v. United States. Ellsberg was charged under the Espionage Act, facing up to 115 years in prison, but in 1973 the charges were dismissed due to governmental misconduct, including the White House "Plumbers" break-in at his psychiatrist's office — an episode that fed into the Watergate scandal.

Later activism

Ellsberg became a prominent anti-war and anti-nuclear activist and lecturer, was arrested many times in protests, and published the memoirs Secrets: A Memoir of Vietnam and the Pentagon Papers (2002) and The Doomsday Machine: Confessions of a Nuclear War Planner (2017). He supported later whistleblowers including Chelsea Manning and Edward Snowden. He died of pancreatic cancer in 2023.
