Unergative verb

An unergative verb is an intransitive verb whose single argument is an agent-like external argument, as in English run, laugh, work or talk: in "She laughed", the subject initiates and controls the event. Unergative verbs contrast with unaccusative verbs such as arrive, fall or melt (intransitive), whose subject behaves syntactically like an underlying object — a patient or theme rather than an agent.

The Unaccusative Hypothesis

The distinction was systematized in David Perlmutter's 1978 formulation of the Unaccusative Hypothesis within Relational Grammar: some surface intransitive subjects originate as direct objects, others as subjects. Unergatives belong to the second class. The split is taken as evidence that argument structure is not a simple mapping from thematic roles to surface positions.

Diagnostics

Languages offer various tests separating the classes. In Italian and Dutch, unergatives select the auxiliary avere/hebben ("have") in compound tenses while unaccusatives select essere/zijn ("be"): compare Italian ha lavorato ("he has worked") with è arrivato ("he has arrived"). The partitive clitic ne in Italian can extract from the postverbal subject of an unaccusative but not of an unergative. In English, resultative constructions and certain adjectival participles ("a recently arrived train" versus the impossible "*a laughed man" in the relevant sense) provide partial diagnostics.

Semantic basis

Unergatives typically denote volitional, agentive activities, while unaccusatives denote changes of state or location and directed motion. Sorace's auxiliary selection hierarchy places verbs of controlled non-motional activity at the unergative extreme. Borderline classes — verbs of manner of motion, for example — can behave as either class depending on whether a telic result is added: "She swam" is unergative, but "She swam to the shore" patterns with unaccusatives in several languages.
