Lake Lahontan

Lake Lahontan was a large endorheic Pleistocene lake that occupied much of modern northwestern Nevada in the United States during the ice ages, roughly between 40,000 and 12,000 years ago.

At its maximum extent, about 12,700 years ago, Lake Lahontan covered approximately 22,000 km², reaching depths of up to 270 m in places — rivalling modern Lake Erie in size. It filled a number of separate basins in the Great Basin, including the Carson, Humboldt, Truckee and Walker river valleys.

Remnants

As the climate dried after the last glacial maximum, the lake shrank and divided. Its modern remnants include Pyramid Lake, Walker Lake, Winnemucca Lake (now dry) and the Carson Sink. Shoreline terraces and tufa deposits high on desert hillsides remain visible evidence of its former level.

Name and study

The lake was named by the geologist Clarence King of the Fortieth Parallel Survey, after Baron de Lahontan, a French explorer of North America. Israel Cook Russell's 1885 monograph on the lake's geological history remains a classic of Great Basin geology. The modern reservoir created by Lahontan Dam on the Carson River (1915) is also called Lake Lahontan.
