Dublin Castle (Irish: Caisleán Bhaile Átha Cliath) is a major Irish government complex, conference centre and visitor attraction in the centre of Dublin. Until 1922 it was the seat of the British government's administration in Ireland, and the phrase "the Castle" became shorthand for that administration.

History

The castle was first founded as a major defensive work by Meiler Fitzhenry on the orders of King John in 1204, shortly after the Norman invasion of Ireland, on the site of an earlier Viking settlement at the junction of the River Liffey and its tributary the Poddle — the "dark pool" (Dubh Linn) that gave Dublin its name. Much of the present complex dates from the 18th century, after fire destroyed most of the medieval castle in 1684.

State Apartments

The State Apartments, built as the residence of the Lord Lieutenant, include St Patrick's Hall, with its ceiling painted by Vincenzo Valdre, and are used for presidential inaugurations and state receptions. The Chapel Royal, a Gothic Revival church completed in 1814, stands adjacent.

Modern use

On 16 January 1922 the last Viceroy formally handed the castle over to Michael Collins and the Provisional Government. Today it hosts government offices, the Garda Museum, the Chester Beatty Library and Revenue Commissioners' facilities, and has served as a venue for European Union presidencies and state visits.
