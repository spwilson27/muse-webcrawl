Toyota Production System

The TPS "house" diagram

The Toyota Production System (TPS) is an integrated socio-technical system, developed by the Toyota Motor Corporation, that comprises its management philosophy and practices. The TPS is a management system that organises manufacturing and logistics for the automobile manufacturer, including interaction with suppliers and customers. It was developed between 1948 and 1975 by Taiichi Ohno, Eiji Toyoda and Shigeo Shingo, drawing on earlier work by Sakichi and Kiichiro Toyoda.

Principles

TPS is commonly represented as a "house" resting on a foundation of stability and standardised work, with two pillars: just-in-time (JIT), the production of only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed, coordinated through kanban signalling; and jidoka, automation with a human touch, by which machines and operators stop production when a defect is detected (andon). The roof of the house is the goal of highest quality, lowest cost and shortest lead time. Underpinning the system are practices such as kaizen (continuous improvement), genchi genbutsu ("go and see"), heijunka (production levelling) and the systematic elimination of waste (muda), together with its companions mura (unevenness) and muri (overburden).

Influence

TPS is the origin of lean manufacturing (later lean production and lean management), a term coined by researcher John Krafcik in 1988 and popularised by the MIT study and the 1990 book The Machine That Changed the World. Its principles have been adapted far beyond the automotive industry, in sectors from aerospace and electronics to healthcare, software development and services, and it remains a benchmark against which production systems worldwide are compared.
