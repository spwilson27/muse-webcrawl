Service animal


A service dog wearing a harness and vest.

Service animals are working animals that have been trained to perform tasks that assist people with disabilities. The best-known examples are guide dogs for people who are blind or have low vision, but service animals may also be trained to alert deaf handlers to sounds, pull a wheelchair, retrieve dropped items, remind a person with mental illness to take medication, provide balance support, or intervene during psychiatric episodes. Service animals are distinguished from emotional support animals and therapy animals, which provide comfort or therapeutic benefit without task training.

Legal status in the United States

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), a service animal is defined as a dog (with a limited provision for miniature horses) that has been individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. Places of public accommodation must generally permit service animals, and staff may ask only two questions: whether the animal is required because of a disability, and what task it has been trained to perform. No registration, certification or identifying vest is required by federal law. Other jurisdictions differ: many countries recognise "assistance animals" under distinct statutory schemes, and airline rules were tightened in the United States in 2021 to limit cabin access to trained service dogs.

Training and behaviour

Service animals may be trained by organisations — often over one to two years, at a cost of tens of thousands of dollars — or by their handlers themselves, which the ADA permits. They are expected to be under the handler's control, housebroken, and focused on their work in public; a business may exclude an animal that is out of control or not housebroken. Fraudulent representation of pets as service animals, facilitated by online sales of vests and certificates, has prompted many US states to enact penalties.

Types of work

Beyond guide work, service dogs perform mobility assistance, hearing alerting, medical response and alert (such as seizure or blood-sugar alerts), and psychiatric service tasks for conditions including PTSD. Miniature horses are occasionally used as guide animals, favoured by some handlers for their long working lives and calm temperaments.
