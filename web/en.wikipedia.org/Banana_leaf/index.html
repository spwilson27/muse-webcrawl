Banana leaf

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The banana leaf is the leaf of the banana plant (Musa species), which may produce up to 40 leaves over a plant's lifetime. Large, flexible, and waterproof, banana leaves are used across the tropics as disposable plates, food wrappers for cooking and serving, thatch, and ceremonial decoration. They are especially important in South and Southeast Asian cuisines and in Hindu and Buddhist ritual practice. Although the leaf itself is not eaten, it imparts a subtle grassy aroma to food cooked or served on it.

Culinary uses

In South India, meals — notably the festive sadhya — are traditionally served on banana leaves, and food is commonly eaten from them in parts of Southeast Asia as well. Leaves are used to wrap foods for steaming, grilling, and roasting: Indonesian dishes such as pepes and lemper, Filipino suman and the leaf-lined presentation of boodle fights, Mexican tamales in the south, Caribbean pasteles, and South Indian ada are examples. Wrapping keeps food moist, protects it from direct flame, and perfumes it. Briefly passing the leaf over a flame or hot water makes it pliable and prevents tearing.

Other uses

Banana leaves serve as temporary plates and placemats, umbrellas, thatch for roofing, packaging material, and writing surfaces historically (as in some Southeast Asian manuscripts). In Hindu ritual they are auspicious decorations for festivals and weddings, and plates for offerings; in parts of Indonesia and the Philippines they figure in folk and religious ceremonies. Because they are biodegradable, banana leaves have drawn renewed interest as a sustainable alternative to plastic tableware and packaging.
