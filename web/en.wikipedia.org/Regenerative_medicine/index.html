Regenerative medicine

Regenerative medicine is the branch of medicine that develops methods to regrow, repair or replace damaged or diseased cells, organs or tissues. It includes the generation and use of therapeutic stem cells, tissue engineering and the production of artificial organs. Rather than merely treating symptoms, regenerative medicine aims to restore normal function.

FieldMedicine / biotechnology
ApproachesStem cell therapy, tissue engineering, biomaterials, gene therapy
ApplicationsBurns, cartilage repair, diabetes, heart disease

Approaches

Cell-based therapies transplant living cells — often stem cells — into damaged tissue. Tissue engineering combines cells with biodegradable scaffolds and growth factors to grow replacement tissue in the laboratory. Biomaterials and medical devices can stimulate the body's own repair mechanisms, while gene therapies deliver corrected genetic material to restore function.

History

The field grew out of transplant medicine and developmental biology. Key milestones include the first successful bone marrow transplants in the 1960s, the isolation of human embryonic stem cells in 1998, and Shinya Yamanaka's 2006 demonstration of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), which earned the Nobel Prize in 2012.

Clinical status

Established regenerative treatments include skin grafts for burns, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for blood disorders, and certain cartilage and corneal therapies. Many other applications — engineered organs, stem cell treatments for neurodegenerative disease — remain experimental, and regulators warn against unproven commercial stem cell clinics.
