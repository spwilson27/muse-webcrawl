Primary sclerosing cholangitis

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a long-term progressive disease of the liver and gallbladder characterised by inflammation and scarring of the bile ducts, which normally allow bile to drain from the gallbladder. Affected individuals may have no symptoms or may experience signs and symptoms of liver disease such as yellow discoloration of the skin and eyes, itching, and abdominal pain.

Signs and symptoms

Many people with PSC do not receive a diagnosis until routine blood tests show abnormal liver enzymes; the disease may remain asymptomatic for years. When symptoms occur they include fatigue, pruritus (itching), jaundice, right upper quadrant abdominal pain, and episodes of bacterial cholangitis (fever, chills, and pain from infection of the bile ducts). Complications of advanced disease include cirrhosis, portal hypertension, fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, and an increased risk of cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer).

Cause

The exact cause is unknown. PSC is considered an immune-mediated disease and has a strong association with inflammatory bowel disease, particularly ulcerative colitis: roughly 70% of people with PSC also have ulcerative colitis, whereas only a small minority of people with ulcerative colitis develop PSC. Genetic susceptibility and abnormal immune responses to gut bacteria are thought to contribute.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made by cholangiography — imaging of the bile ducts by magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) or endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) — which characteristically shows multifocal strictures with intervening dilation, giving a "beaded" appearance. Blood tests typically show a cholestatic pattern with elevated alkaline phosphatase. Liver biopsy is rarely required.

Treatment

No medical therapy has been proven to slow progression. Management focuses on treating complications: endoscopic dilation of dominant strictures, antibiotics for bacterial cholangitis, fat-soluble vitamin supplementation, and surveillance for cholangiocarcinoma and colorectal cancer. Ursodeoxycholic acid is sometimes used but has not shown a clear survival benefit. Liver transplantation is the only definitive treatment for advanced disease, though PSC can recur in the transplanted liver.

Prognosis

PSC is progressive in most patients, leading eventually to cirrhosis and liver failure over a timescale of roughly 10–20 years from diagnosis, though the course varies widely. Patients face a substantially increased lifetime risk of cholangiocarcinoma and, when ulcerative colitis coexists, colorectal cancer.
