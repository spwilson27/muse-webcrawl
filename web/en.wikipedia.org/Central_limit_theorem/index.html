Central limit theorem

Overview
FieldProbability theory, statistics
Key figuresPierre-Simon Laplace, Aleksandr Lyapunov, Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg
RelatedLaw of large numbers, normal distribution

In probability theory, the central limit theorem (CLT) states that, under appropriate conditions, the distribution of a normalized version of the sample mean of a random sample converges to a standard normal distribution. This holds even if the original variables themselves are not normally distributed. The theorem is a cornerstone of probability theory and statistics because it explains why many distributions tend to be close to the normal distribution and justifies many statistical procedures.

Statement

Suppose X1, …, Xn are independent and identically distributed random variables with expected value μ and finite variance σ². Then as n grows, the distribution of the standardized sample mean approaches the standard normal distribution:

√n ( X̄n − μ ) / σ → N(0, 1) in distribution

The earliest version of the theorem was proved by Abraham de Moivre in 1738 for the binomial case; Pierre-Simon Laplace generalized it in 1810, and rigorous modern proofs were given by Aleksandr Lyapunov (1901) and under weaker conditions by Jarl Waldemar Lindeberg (1922). The name "central limit theorem" comes from George Pólya's 1920 paper, where he emphasized its central importance in probability.

Applications

The CLT underpins confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, and error analysis: sample averages of measurements, polling results, and aggregate random effects tend to be approximately normally distributed, with standard error shrinking as σ/√n.
