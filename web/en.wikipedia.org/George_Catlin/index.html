George Catlin (July 26, 1796 – December 23, 1872) was an American lawyer, painter, author, and traveller, who specialised in portraits of Native Americans in the American West. Travelling to the American frontier five times in the 1830s, Catlin wrote about and painted portraits that depicted the customs and cultures of Indigenous peoples, amassing a collection of over 500 paintings — his famous "Indian Gallery".

Career

Trained as a lawyer in Philadelphia, Catlin gave up the law after encountering a delegation of Native American leaders and resolving to record what he feared was a vanishing way of life. Between 1830 and 1836 he travelled thousands of miles, visiting some fifty tribes including the Mandan, Crow, Sioux, Pawnee, and Comanche, often accompanying expeditions up the Missouri River. His accounts describe the Mandan O-kee-pa ceremony and rituals that disappeared when smallpox devastated the tribe in 1837.

Exhibitions and writings

Catlin exhibited his Indian Gallery in the United States and from 1840 in London and Paris, sometimes travelling with Native American performers. His illustrated Letters and Notes on the Manners, Customs, and Conditions of the North American Indians (1841) became a classic of frontier literature. Plagued by debt, he lost much of his collection to creditors; the bulk of the surviving works was eventually donated to the Smithsonian Institution, where they form a cornerstone of the Smithsonian American Art Museum.

Legacy

Catlin was among the first to propose preserving the Great Plains as a "nation's park" with its peoples and wildlife intact — an idea that anticipated the national park movement.
