Measure theory

FieldMathematical analysis
Key conceptsMeasure, σ-algebra, measurable function, Lebesgue integral
FoundersHenri Lebesgue, Émile Borel, Johann Radon, Maurice Fréchet

Measure theory is the branch of real analysis that investigates σ-algebras, measures, measurable functions and integrals. It provides a rigorous way to assign a size — length, area, volume, or probability — to sets more general than intervals or rectangles, and it underlies modern probability theory, functional analysis and ergodic theory.

Basic definitions

A measure on a set X is a function μ defined on a σ-algebra of subsets of X, taking values in the non-negative extended reals, such that μ(∅) = 0 and μ is countably additive: the measure of a countable disjoint union is the sum of the measures. The pair (X, Σ) is a measurable space; adding μ gives a measure space. The standard example is Lebesgue measure on the real line, which extends the notion of length from intervals to a vast class of sets.

Development

Henri Lebesgue created the theory in his 1902 thesis, constructing the Lebesgue integral, which integrates far more functions than the Riemann integral and behaves well under limits through the monotone and dominated convergence theorems. Later work by Radon, Fréchet, Carathéodory and others generalized measures to abstract spaces. Not every set can be measured: assuming the axiom of choice, there exist non-measurable sets, such as Vitali sets, and paradoxical decompositions such as the Banach–Tarski paradox.

Applications

Andrey Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatization made probability a branch of measure theory, with probability spaces as measure spaces of total measure 1. Measure theory also supports the theory of Hausdorff dimension and fractals, Haar measure on groups, and the Riesz representation theorem linking measures to linear functionals.
