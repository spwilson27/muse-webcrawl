Cumulant

In probability theory and statistics, the cumulants κn of a probability distribution are a set of quantities that provide an alternative to the moments of the distribution. Any two probability distributions whose moments are identical will have identical cumulants as well, and vice versa.

The first cumulant is the mean; the second and third cumulants are, respectively, the variance and the third central moment. Higher cumulants, however, are not the central moments themselves but polynomial combinations of them. The cumulants of a normal distribution beyond the second are all zero, which makes cumulants especially useful for measuring departures from normality.

Definition

The cumulants are defined through the cumulant generating function, the natural logarithm of the moment generating function:

K(t) = log E[exp(tX)] = Σ κn tn / n!

Equivalently, they can be obtained from the logarithm of the characteristic function. This definition is due to the Danish statistician Thorvald N. Thiele, who introduced cumulants (as "half-invariants") in 1889.

Properties

  • Additivity: the cumulants of a sum of independent random variables are the sums of the individual cumulants — a property not shared by central moments.
  • Homogeneity and translation: cumulants shift appropriately under changes of scale and, except for the first, are unchanged by translations.
  • Joint cumulants generalize the notion to several random variables and vanish when the variables can be split into independent groups, making them a natural tool in multivariate analysis and in the study of stochastic processes.

Applications

Cumulants appear in Edgeworth expansions of distributions, in statistical physics (where they arise as connected correlation functions), in signal processing (higher-order statistics), and in free probability theory, where the analogous quantities are called free cumulants.
