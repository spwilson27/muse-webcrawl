Atra-Hasis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Cuneiform tablet of the epic

Atra-Hasis (Akkadian: "exceedingly wise") is the protagonist of an 18th-century BCE Akkadian epic recorded on clay tablets. The epic, sometimes called the Epic of Atra-Hasis, combines a creation myth with one of the earliest surviving flood narratives, and is a principal source for Mesopotamian mythology's account of the origin and early history of humankind.

Summary

In the epic, the lesser gods (the Igigi) grow weary of their labour and rebel, so the god Enki proposes the creation of humans to bear the workload. Humans multiply and become noisy, disturbing the sleep of the chief god Enlil, who sends plague, famine, and drought to reduce their numbers. When these fail, Enlil resolves on a great flood. Enki warns Atra-Hasis, who builds a boat and preserves life. After the flood, the gods institute mortality, infertility, and other limits on human population.

Parallels

The flood story in Atra-Hasis is closely related to the flood episode in the Epic of Gilgamesh (where the hero is Utnapishtim) and shows notable parallels with the later biblical account of Noah in Genesis. The surviving copies are fragmentary; the best-known recension was copied by the scribe Ipiq-Aya during the reign of Ammi-Saduqa in the 17th century BCE.
