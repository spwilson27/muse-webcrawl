Metonymy

Metonymy is a figure of speech in which a thing or concept is referred to by the name of something closely associated with it. The word derives from the Greek metōnymia, meaning "change of name." Common examples include "the Crown" for the monarchy, "the White House" for the President of the United States or his administration, and "Hollywood" for the American film industry.

Types of metonymy

Metonymic relations include cause and effect, container and contained, producer and product, place and institution, and part and whole. The last of these overlaps with synecdoche, which some rhetoricians treat as a subtype of metonymy and others classify separately.

Metonymy and metaphor

Metonymy is often contrasted with metaphor: metaphor works by similarity across different domains, while metonymy works by contiguity or association within a single domain. The linguist Roman Jakobson famously placed metaphor and metonymy at opposite poles of language use, associating them with the two axes of selection and combination.

Examples

  • "The pen is mightier than the sword" — pen for writing/diplomacy, sword for military force.
  • "Suits" for business executives.
  • "The kettle is boiling" — container for contents.
  • "Wall Street" for the American financial sector.
