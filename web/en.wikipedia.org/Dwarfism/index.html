Dwarfism is a condition of short stature, conventionally defined as an adult height of 4 feet 10 inches (147 cm) or less resulting from a genetic or medical condition. It is generally classified as either disproportionate, in which some body parts are of average size while others are short, or proportionate, in which the body is uniformly small.

Causes

More than 300 conditions can cause dwarfism. The most common is achondroplasia, a disproportionate form caused by a gain-of-function variant in the FGFR3 gene, affecting roughly 1 in 15,000–40,000 births; about 80 percent of cases arise from new mutations. Other causes include spondyloepiphyseal dysplasia, diastrophic dysplasia, Turner syndrome, and growth hormone deficiency (pituitary dwarfism), the principal cause of proportionate short stature, which can be treated with recombinant human growth hormone in childhood.

Health considerations

Health issues vary by condition. In achondroplasia, spinal stenosis, sleep apnea, obesity and recurrent ear infections are common concerns; life expectancy is typically near normal. Treatments may include hormone therapy, limb-lengthening surgery (which remains controversial), and management of orthopedic complications.

Society and terminology

Advocacy organizations such as Little People of America note that many people with dwarfism prefer the terms "short-statured" or "little person", while others identify simply as people with dwarfism; the word "midget" is widely considered offensive. Historically, people with dwarfism were employed as court dwarfs and entertainers; today, notable performers with achondroplasia work across film, television and sport.
