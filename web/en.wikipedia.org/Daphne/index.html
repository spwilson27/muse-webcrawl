Daphne (Greek: Δάφνη, "laurel") is a figure in Greek mythology, a naiad associated with fountains, wells, springs, streams, and other bodies of fresh water. She is best known for the myth in which the god Apollo, inflamed with desire by a golden arrow of Eros, pursued her relentlessly; praying to her father, the river god Peneus, for deliverance, she was transformed into a laurel tree (Laurus nobilis) at the moment Apollo reached her.

The myth

The fullest account is in Book 1 of Ovid's Metamorphoses. Apollo, having mocked Eros's archery, was struck by a gold-tipped arrow causing irresistible love, while Daphne received a lead-tipped arrow causing aversion. Fleeing through the wilderness, she begged her father to change her form; bark enclosed her body, her hair became leaves, her arms branches, and her feet roots. Apollo embraced the tree and declared the laurel sacred to him, to be worn as a crown by victors and poets.

Other versions

In an Arcadian variant, Daphne was a huntress daughter of Amyclas who rejected all suitors; the mortal Leucippus disguised himself as a girl to join her band and was killed when Apollo engineered his exposure. Other traditions make her mother Gaia, the earth, into whom she simply sank and from whom the laurel sprang.

In art and literature

The myth has inspired countless works, including Bernini's marble sculpture Apollo and Daphne (1622–1625) in the Galleria Borghese, paintings by Tiepolo and Waterhouse, Handel's cantata Apollo e Dafne, and Richard Strauss's opera Daphne (1938).
