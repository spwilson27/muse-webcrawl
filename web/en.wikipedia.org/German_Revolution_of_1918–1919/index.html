German Revolution of 1918–1919

The German Revolution of 1918–1919 (German: Novemberrevolution, "November Revolution") was the series of events at the end of the First World War that overthrew the German Empire, forced the abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II, and established a parliamentary republic — the Weimar Republic. Beginning with a sailors' mutiny at Kiel in late October 1918, the revolution spread across Germany within days, toppling the federal monarchies, and continued through armed conflicts between the new government and radical left-wing movements into the spring of 1919.

Background

By autumn 1918 Germany faced military collapse. After the failure of the spring offensives and the Allied Hundred Days Offensive, the Supreme Army Command under Ludendorff privately demanded an armistice and the "parliamentarisation" of the government, partly to shift blame for defeat onto civilian politicians. Prince Maximilian of Baden became Chancellor in October and began constitutional reforms, but popular exhaustion with war, hunger from the Allied blockade, and the example of the Russian Revolution fuelled demands for deeper change.

The Kiel mutiny and the spread of the revolution

When naval commanders planned a final, suicidal sortie against the Royal Navy, sailors of the High Seas Fleet mutinied at Wilhelmshaven and Kiel (3 November 1918). Workers' and soldiers' councils sprang up across the country on the Russian model, and the monarchy collapsed: on 9 November Chancellor Max von Baden announced the Kaiser's abdication and handed power to Friedrich Ebert, leader of the Majority Social Democrats (SPD). That afternoon Philipp Scheidemann proclaimed a republic from a Reichstag window, while Karl Liebknecht proclaimed a rival "free socialist republic" from the Berlin Palace. Wilhelm II fled to the Netherlands the next day, and the Armistice was signed on 11 November.

Council government and conflict with the radical left

Power passed to a Council of the People's Deputies, a coalition of the SPD and the Independent Social Democrats (USPD) under Ebert. The Ebert–Groener pact secured army support for the government against further revolution. A national congress of councils in December voted for elections to a National Assembly rather than a council system, and the USPD ministers resigned after troops fired on revolutionary sailors in the Christmas crisis. The founding of the Communist Party (KPD) and the January 1919 Spartacist uprising in Berlin were crushed by Freikorps units; KPD leaders Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht were murdered on 15 January 1919. Elections on 19 January — the first in which German women could vote — produced a National Assembly that met at Weimar and adopted the Weimar Constitution in August 1919.

Aftermath

Sporadic fighting — the Bavarian Soviet Republic, the Ruhr uprising of 1920 — continued into 1919 and beyond. The revolution ended Germany's monarchies and created a democratic republic with universal suffrage and extensive social reforms, but its reliance on the old officer corps and judiciary, and the "stab-in-the-back" myth it spawned, left the Weimar Republic burdened from birth.
