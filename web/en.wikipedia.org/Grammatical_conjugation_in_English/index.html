English verbs: conjugation

Grammatical conjugation in English is comparatively simple by Indo-European standards. Modern English verbs inflect for few categories: most regular verbs have only four distinct forms (for example walk, walks, walked, walking), and even the most irregular verb, be, has only eight. Person and number agreement survives mainly in the third-person singular present ending -s and in the verb be.

Regular and irregular verbs

Regular ("weak") verbs form the past tense and past participle with the suffix -ed (with spelling changes such as stop → stopped). Irregular ("strong" and anomalous) verbs — around 200 in common use — use vowel changes (sing, sang, sung), suppletion (go, went), or no change at all (cut, cut, cut). Historically, English has steadily levelled irregular forms; many once-strong verbs are now weak.

Auxiliaries and periphrasis

Because inflection is so limited, English expresses most tense, aspect, mood, and voice distinctions periphrastically with auxiliary verbs: have (perfect), be (progressive and passive), do (questions, negation, emphasis), and the modal auxiliaries can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, which are defective and do not inflect for person.

The verb "be"

Be is uniquely irregular, with forms be, am, is, are, was, were, been, being — the only English verb still distinguishing person and number in both present and past.
