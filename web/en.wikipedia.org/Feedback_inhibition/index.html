Feedback inhibition

In feedback inhibition, the end product of a pathway binds an allosteric site on an early enzyme and shuts the pathway down.

Feedback inhibition, also called end-product inhibition, is a form of cellular regulation in which the final product of a metabolic pathway inhibits an enzyme that acts earlier in the pathway, thereby preventing overproduction of the product and conserving the cell's resources. It is one of the principal mechanisms of allosteric regulation and of metabolic homeostasis, operating on a timescale of seconds — much faster than regulation of gene expression.

Mechanism

Typically, the end product binds not to the active site of the first committed-step enzyme but to a separate regulatory (allosteric) site. Binding changes the enzyme's shape and reduces its activity. Because the inhibition is non-competitive and reversible, the pathway switches back on as soon as the product is consumed and its concentration falls. In branched pathways the control is more elaborate: several end products may each partially inhibit a common enzyme (concerted or cumulative feedback inhibition), or each branch may control its own first enzyme, as in the multiple forms of aspartate transcarbamoylase regulation in bacteria.

Examples

The classic example is the synthesis of isoleucine from threonine in bacteria: isoleucine allosterically inhibits threonine deaminase, the first enzyme of the pathway — an effect demonstrated in the 1950s by Edwin Umbarger, one of the founding observations of feedback control. In mammals, cholesterol synthesis is feedback-inhibited at the enzyme HMG-CoA reductase — the target of statin drugs — and ATP, the end product of energy metabolism, inhibits key enzymes of glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, while CTP inhibits aspartate transcarbamoylase in pyrimidine synthesis.

Significance

Feedback inhibition allows cells to respond instantly to changing needs, avoid wasting energy and raw materials on unneeded products, and keep internal metabolite concentrations stable. Its discovery, along with the operon model of gene regulation, established the modern picture of the cell as a network of self-regulating circuits, and the same principle is exploited industrially: fermentation processes for amino acids use mutant microbes whose feedback controls have been disabled so that they overproduce the desired compound.
