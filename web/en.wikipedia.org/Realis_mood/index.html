Realis mood

The realis mood marks propositions the speaker presents as known or directly verifiable fact.

A realis mood (from Latin realis, "relating to reality") is a grammatical mood which is used principally to indicate that something is a statement of fact; in other words, to express what the speaker considers to be a known state of affairs, as in declarative sentences. Most languages have a single realis mood called the indicative mood, although some languages have additional realis moods, for example to express differing certainty in the speaker's view of what is being said.

Contrast with irrealis

Realis moods are contrasted with irrealis moods, which indicate that an event or situation is not known to have happened or not to be the case. Irrealis categories include the subjunctive, conditional, optative, jussive and potential moods, which cover wishes, hypotheses, commands, future contingencies and events that the speaker presents as uncertain or contrary to fact. The boundary varies by language: in many Australian Aboriginal and Papuan languages, for instance, future events are obligatorily marked as irrealis because they are not yet factual.

Indicative as realis

In languages such as English and the Romance languages, the indicative is the default realis mood used for factual assertions — "The cat is on the mat" — while the subjunctive or conditional handles non-factual meanings. Some languages mark realis status by special verb forms or particles rather than the indicative/subjunctive opposition familiar from European languages; in several languages of the Pacific, for example, distinct realis and irrealis prefixes attach to verbs and the realis form is used for past and present actualized events.

Further nuances

Some languages subdivide the realis domain to encode the speaker's source of evidence, using evidential markers alongside or instead of mood. Typologists note that "realis" and "irrealis" are umbrella categories whose precise contents differ across languages, and their usefulness as cross-linguistic categories has been debated, notably by Joan Bybee and others.
