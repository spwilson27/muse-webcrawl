Sense and reference

In the philosophy of language, sense and reference (German: Sinn und Bedeutung) are two distinct aspects of a linguistic expression's meaning, introduced by the German philosopher and logician Gottlob Frege in his influential 1892 paper "Über Sinn und Bedeutung" ("On Sense and Reference").

The reference of an expression is the object it refers to or denotes, while its sense is the "mode of presentation" of that object — the way in which the expression presents its referent to a thinker.

Frege's puzzle

Frege introduced the distinction to explain how an identity statement such as "the Morning Star is the Evening Star" can be informative, while "the Morning Star is the Morning Star" is trivial. Both names refer to the same object, the planet Venus, but they present it in different ways, and thus have different senses.

Application to sentences

Frege held that the reference of a declarative sentence is its truth value (the True or the False), while its sense is the thought (German: Gedanke) it expresses. In indirect contexts such as reported speech, expressions take on their customary senses as their references.

Influence

The distinction became foundational for analytic philosophy and modern semantics, influencing the descriptivist theory of names associated with Bertrand Russell and later challenged by direct reference theorists such as Saul Kripke.
