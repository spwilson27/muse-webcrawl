Lava

Lava is molten or partially molten rock (magma) that has been expelled from the interior of a terrestrial planet or moon onto its surface. Lava, when molten and underground, is called magma; once erupted it may flow as a liquid at temperatures typically between 800 and 1,200 °C (1,470–2,190 °F), eventually cooling and solidifying into igneous rock.

Composition and viscosity

Lava composition is dominated by silicate minerals. Felsic lavas, rich in silica, are cooler and extremely viscous, tending to form thick, blocky flows or domes; mafic (basaltic) lavas are hotter, poorer in silica and much more fluid, producing the great shield volcanoes of Hawaii and Iceland. Viscosity and gas content govern whether an eruption is effusive (flowing) or explosive.

Types of flows

Hawaiian terms are used worldwide for basaltic flow surfaces. Pāhoehoe is smooth, billowy or ropy lava that forms from very fluid flows; ʻaʻā is rough, clinkery lava with a spiny, fragmented surface. Underwater eruptions produce pillow lavas, bulbous sacks formed as lava quenches in water. Block lavas, from more viscous melts, move as jumbled masses of large angular blocks.

Landforms

Lava creates lava tubes, which can carry flows long distances; lava channels and levees; lava lakes that pond in craters, as at Kīlauea and Erta Ale; and extensive flood-basalt provinces such as the Deccan Traps and Siberian Traps, produced by some of the largest eruptions in Earth's history and linked to mass extinctions.
