Iron deficiency

Iron deficiency progresses from depleted stores to iron-deficiency anaemia.
SpecialtyHaematology, nutrition
SymptomsFatigue, pallor, weakness; often none initially
ComplicationsIron-deficiency anaemia
DiagnosisLow serum ferritin, blood tests
TreatmentIron supplements, dietary change, treating the cause

Iron deficiency is a state in which the body has insufficient iron to supply its needs. It is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide and the leading cause of anaemia. Iron is essential for the production of haemoglobin in red blood cells, myoglobin in muscle, and numerous enzymes involved in energy metabolism and DNA synthesis.

Stages

Iron deficiency develops in stages. First, storage iron (measured by serum ferritin) falls. Next, iron supply to developing red blood cells becomes inadequate, though haemoglobin may remain normal. Finally, haemoglobin production drops enough to cause iron-deficiency anaemia, characterised by small (microcytic), pale (hypochromic) red blood cells.

Causes

Causes include inadequate dietary intake, impaired absorption (as in coeliac disease or after gastric surgery), increased requirements during pregnancy and growth, and blood loss—most importantly menstrual loss in women of reproductive age and gastrointestinal blood loss in adults, which may signal underlying disease requiring investigation.

Signs and symptoms

Early deficiency may be asymptomatic. Symptoms can include fatigue, pallor, breathlessness on exertion, brittle nails, hair loss, restless legs, and pica (craving non-food substances such as ice or clay). In children, deficiency is associated with impaired cognitive development.

Treatment

Management involves identifying and treating the underlying cause, increasing dietary iron (haem iron from meat is absorbed best; vitamin C enhances absorption of non-haem iron), and iron supplementation, usually oral ferrous salts or, when these fail or are not tolerated, intravenous iron.
