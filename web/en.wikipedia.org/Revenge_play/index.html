Revenge play

The revenge play, or revenge tragedy, is a dramatic genre in which the protagonist seeks revenge for a real or imagined injury. The term was coined by A. H. Thorndike in 1900 to describe a class of plays that were most popular in the late Elizabethan and Jacobean period of English theatre.

Hamlet, the most famous revenge play

Origins

The genre drew heavily on the Roman tragedies of Seneca, whose plays featured ghosts, violence and moral disintegration. Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) established the form in England and is one of the most influential plays of the period; it survives as the type specimen of the genre, and its popularity spawned many imitations.

Conventions

Revenge plays typically share a set of conventions:

  • A murder (often of a ruler or family member) that has gone unpunished by the state, compelling private vengeance.
  • The appearance of a ghost demanding revenge.
  • The revenger's hesitation, feigned or real madness, and elaborate scheming.
  • Soliloquies in which the revenger debates his course of action.
  • A play within the play used to expose or trap the villain.
  • Escalating violence culminating in a final scene of carnage in which the revenger, the villain and many others die.

Major examples

  • The Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) by Thomas Kyd
  • Hamlet (c. 1600) by William Shakespeare
  • The Revenger's Tragedy (1606), attributed to Thomas Middleton
  • The Duchess of Malfi (1613) by John Webster
  • 'Tis Pity She's a Whore (c. 1629) by John Ford
  • Antonio's Revenge by John Marston
  • The Changeling (1622) by Thomas Middleton and William Rowley

Legacy

Shakespeare's Hamlet both epitomises and complicates the genre: its hero's delay and moral reflection turn the revenge plot into a study of conscience. The conventions of the revenge tragedy have influenced later drama, film and literature, from Jacobean tragedy to modern revenge films.
