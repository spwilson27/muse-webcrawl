Skald

A skald (Old Norse skáld) was a poet in Viking Age and medieval Scandinavia, typically attached to a royal or noble court. More than 300 skalds are known by name, and their verse, composed in elaborate metres such as dróttkvætt, is a major source for Norse mythology and early Scandinavian history.

Role and poetry

Skalds composed praise poetry (drápur) celebrating kings' deeds, memorial poems, and occasional verses (lausavísur) responding to particular events. Their verse is characterized by complex metre, interlaced word order, and heavy use of kennings — compressed metaphorical phrases such as “whale-road” for the sea. Because skaldic stanzas were quoted in the Icelandic sagas and in Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda as historical evidence, they have an outsized importance in reconstructing Viking Age history.

Famous skalds

Among the most celebrated skalds are Bragi Boddason, often considered the first skald; Egill Skallagrímsson, hero of Egils saga; Sigvatr Þórðarson, court poet of St Olaf; and Einarr Skálaglamm. Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda was itself written partly as a handbook to preserve skaldic technique after the conversion to Christianity.
