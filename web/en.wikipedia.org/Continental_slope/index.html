Continental slope

The continental slope is the relatively steep slope between the outer edge of the continental shelf and the deep ocean floor. It is the boundary between the continental crust and the oceanic crust, and marks the seaward edge of the continent itself. The slope begins at the shelf break, typically at a depth of about 130 metres, and descends to depths of roughly 2,000 to 3,000 metres, where it gives way to the gentler continental rise.

Topography

The average gradient of the continental slope is about 4 degrees, although it varies considerably: it may exceed 25 degrees off some coasts and is much gentler elsewhere. Worldwide the slope totals roughly 300,000 km in length. Submarine canyons — steep-walled valleys such as the Hudson Canyon off New York — cut into many slopes and act as conduits that carry sediment from the shelf to the deep sea, where turbidity currents spread it out to form the continental rise and abyssal fans.

Formation and sediments

Slopes form at both passive margins, where they are built up by sediment accumulation and subsidence, and active margins, where tectonic deformation dominates. Sediments on the slope are typically finer than those of the shelf — muds and oozes — punctuated by coarser deposits emplaced by slumps, slides and turbidity currents. Slope failures can generate submarine landslides and, occasionally, tsunamis.

Life on the slope

The bathyal zone of the slope, between roughly 200 and 2,000 m depth, supports diverse communities of fishes, squids, corals and other invertebrates, sustained largely by organic matter sinking from sunlit surface waters. Deep-water coral mounds and cold seep communities occur on many slopes, and several important commercial fisheries operate along the upper slope.
