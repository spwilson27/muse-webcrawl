Conflict of laws

Conflict of laws, also called private international law in civil law jurisdictions, is the set of rules or laws a jurisdiction applies to a case, transaction or other occurrence with connections to more than one jurisdiction. It answers three questions: which court has jurisdiction to hear the case, which jurisdiction's law should be applied to resolve it (choice of law), and whether and how a foreign judgment will be recognised and enforced.

Private international law mediates between legal systems.

Scope

The field is "private" international law because it concerns disputes between private parties — contracts, marriages, inheritance, torts, property — rather than relations between states. Despite its name it is largely national law, varying from country to country, though international conventions, notably those of the Hague Conference on Private International Law (founded 1893), and regional instruments such as the European Union's Brussels and Rome Regulations, have harmonised significant areas.

Choice of law

A court first characterises the issue (for example as contractual or matrimonial), then applies its choice-of-law rules, which point to a legal system through connecting factors such as domicile, nationality, the place of contracting (lex loci contractus), the place of the tort (lex loci delicti), or the location of property (lex situs). Parties to a contract may often choose the governing law themselves. Doctrines such as renvoi — under which the forum accepts a reference back from the foreign system's own conflict rules — and the public policy exception, which lets a court refuse foreign law repugnant to fundamental local values, complicate the analysis.

History

Conflict rules emerged in medieval Italian city-states, whose jurists such as Bartolus of Saxoferrato developed the statutist theory distinguishing personal, real and mixed statutes. Dutch scholars of the seventeenth century, above all Ulric Huber, grounded the doctrine in comity between sovereigns, an idea transmitted into English and American law through Joseph Story's Commentaries on the Conflict of Laws (1834). In the twentieth century American scholarship, led by critics of the traditional territorial rules, produced approaches such as Brainerd Currie's governmental interest analysis.
