The Bronze Horseman (Russian: Медный всадник, Medny vsadnik) is an equestrian statue of Peter the Great in Senate Square in Saint Petersburg, Russia. Commissioned by Catherine the Great, it is one of the city's most famous symbols.

Creation

The French sculptor Étienne Maurice Falconet, recommended by Denis Diderot, arrived in Russia in 1766 and spent twelve years on the statue. Peter is shown heroically astride a rearing horse, his outstretched arm gesturing toward the Neva River, the horse trampling a serpent representing treason and evil. The head was modelled by Falconet's student (and later wife) Marie-Anne Collot.

The Thunder Stone

The pedestal is the Thunder Stone, a glacial boulder of roughly 1,500 tonnes found at Lakhta in 1768 — the largest stone known to have been moved by humans. Without modern machinery, hundreds of men hauled it on a sledge over a track of bronze spheres to the Gulf of Finland, whence it was floated to the city in 1770, to great international acclaim.

Cultural legacy

The statue takes its popular name from Alexander Pushkin's 1833 narrative poem The Bronze Horseman, a masterpiece of Russian literature, in which the statue comes alive and pursues the clerk Evgeny through the flooded city after the great flood of 1824. The poem's tension between the imperial state and the ordinary individual has shaped Russian debates about power ever since.
