The Callanish Stones (Scottish Gaelic: Clachan Chalanais) are an arrangement of standing stones placed in a cruciform pattern with a central stone circle, erected in the late Neolithic era near the village of Callanish on the west coast of the Isle of Lewis in the Outer Hebrides, Scotland. They are among the most spectacular megalithic monuments in Britain.

Description

The main site, Callanish I, consists of a circle of thirteen stones with a tall central monolith about 4.8 metres high, and a small chambered cairn between the centre and the eastern stones. Rows of stones radiate to the north — forming a long avenue — and to the east, south and west, giving the monument a cross shape. The stones are of local Lewisian gneiss, notable for its swirling bands of colour. Numerous smaller stone settings, numbered Callanish II, III and so on, stand in the surrounding landscape.

History and interpretation

The stones were erected between roughly 2900 and 2600 BC, earlier than the main phases of Stonehenge. Ritual activity continued into the Bronze Age, when the cairn was built, before the site was abandoned and gradually buried in peat, which had covered the stones to a depth of over a metre before it was cleared in 1857. Theories about the site's purpose often invoke astronomical alignments, especially with the moon: every 18.6 years the moon appears to skim along the southern horizon as seen from the circle. Folklore calls the stones Fir Bhreig, "the false men", petrified giants who refused to convert to Christianity.
