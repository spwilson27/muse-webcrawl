Subprime lending

Subprime lending is the provision of loans to borrowers who do not qualify for conventional, "prime" loans, usually because of a poor or limited credit history. Because the borrower's creditworthiness is lower, subprime loans carry higher interest rates, less favourable terms, or both, to compensate the lender for the greater risk of default.

Subprime mortgages

The term became widely known through subprime mortgage lending in the United States. Subprime mortgage borrowers typically had credit scores below about 640, high debt-to-income ratios, or incomplete documentation. Many subprime mortgages of the mid-2000s were hybrid adjustable-rate loans ("2/28" and "3/27" ARMs) with low introductory "teaser" rates that reset sharply higher after two or three years.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

The rapid growth of subprime lending, the securitisation of subprime mortgages into mortgage-backed securities and collateralised debt obligations, and the collapse of US house prices after 2006 produced a wave of defaults and foreclosures. The resulting losses were a central trigger of the 2007–2008 financial crisis and the Great Recession. In response, the United States enacted reforms including the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010, which imposed ability-to-repay requirements on mortgage lenders.
