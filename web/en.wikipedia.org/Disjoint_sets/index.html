Disjoint sets

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In set theory, two sets are said to be disjoint sets (or simply disjoint) if they have no element in common. Equivalently, two disjoint sets are sets whose intersection is the empty set. For example, the sets {1, 2, 3} and {4, 5, 6} are disjoint, while {1, 2, 3} and {3, 4, 5} are not.

Generalizations

A collection of more than two sets is called disjoint if any two distinct sets in the collection are disjoint; such a collection is also called a pairwise disjoint family or mutually disjoint family. A partition of a set is a family of nonempty, pairwise disjoint subsets whose union is the whole set. In probability theory, disjoint events (mutually exclusive events) cannot occur simultaneously, so the probability of their union equals the sum of their probabilities.

Helly family and applications

Disjointness is a fundamental notion in measure theory, combinatorics and topology. In computer science, the disjoint-set data structure (also called a union-find structure) maintains a collection of disjoint sets under operations that merge two sets and determine whether two elements belong to the same set; it underlies Kruskal's algorithm for minimum spanning trees and algorithms for image segmentation and percolation.
