Capsicum chinense is a species of chili pepper native to the Amazon basin and widely cultivated in the Caribbean, Central America and the Yucatán Peninsula. It is famous for containing the world's hottest naturally occurring peppers, many of which combine extreme pungency with a distinctive fruity, apricot-like aroma prized in hot sauces and regional cuisines.

The name is a historical misnomer: the botanist Nikolaus Joseph von Jacquin, who described the species in 1776, mistakenly believed the plant came from China; like all Capsicum species, it is in fact of American origin.

Characteristics

Plants are typically compact bushes with crinkled leaves and two or more flowers per node whose corollas are usually greenish-white. The fruit are often lantern- or bonnet-shaped with wrinkled, thin flesh. Pungency varies widely among cultivars, from nearly sweet types such as the Trinidad perfume pepper to the super-hot Indian Bhut jolokia ("ghost pepper"), which in 2007 was certified by Guinness World Records as the world's hottest chili at over one million Scoville heat units.

Cultivars

The best-known cultivars include the habanero of the Yucatán; the Scotch bonnet of the Caribbean, essential to Jamaican jerk seasoning; the aji dulce and related seasoning peppers of Venezuela and Puerto Rico; the datil of St. Augustine, Florida; and the Bhut jolokia and its relative the Naga Morich of north-eastern India and Bangladesh. Several record-holding super-hot peppers, including the Carolina Reaper and Pepper X, are hybrids with substantial C. chinense parentage.

Cultivation and use

The species requires a long, hot growing season and germinates slowly; in temperate climates seed is started indoors well before the last frost. The fruit is used fresh, dried, fermented into hot sauces, and in marinades; in the Caribbean it defines the heat of jerk and pepperpot dishes, and in Yucatán habanero salsas accompany cochinita pibil and other regional foods. As with other super-hot peppers, handling requires care, since the capsaicin in C. chinense fruit can reach concentrations of several percent of dry weight.
