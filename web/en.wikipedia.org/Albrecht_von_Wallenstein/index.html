Albrecht von Wallenstein

Albrecht Wenzel Eusebius von Wallenstein (24 September 1583 – 25 February 1634) was a Bohemian military leader and politician who offered his services, and an army of 40,000–100,000 men, to Emperor Ferdinand II during the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648). He became the supreme commander of the Imperial forces and a key figure of the conflict.

Rise

Born into a minor Protestant noble family in Bohemia, Wallenstein converted to Catholicism and, through marriages and shrewd purchases of confiscated estates after the Battle of White Mountain, amassed one of the largest fortunes in the Empire. In 1625 Ferdinand made him commander of a new Imperial army, raised and financed largely by Wallenstein himself on the principle that war should feed war.

Commands and fall

Wallenstein defeated Mansfeld at Dessau Bridge and pursued Danish forces out of northern Germany, earning the dukedom of Mecklenburg. Dismissed in 1630 under pressure from the electors, he was recalled after Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden invaded; he fought the Swedes at Alte Veste and at Lützen in 1632, where Gustavus was killed. Suspected of negotiating independently with the enemy, Wallenstein was secretly condemned by the Emperor and assassinated by officers of his own army at Eger (Cheb) in February 1634.

Legacy

Wallenstein's career inspired Schiller's dramatic trilogy Wallenstein and remains a byword for the ambitions of the military enterprisers of the seventeenth century.
