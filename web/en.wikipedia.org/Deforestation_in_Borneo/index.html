Deforestation in Borneo has taken place on an industrial scale since the 1960s. Borneo, the world's third-largest island, was once covered almost entirely by rich tropical rainforest, but much of the forest has been cleared for timber extraction and conversion to oil palm and pulpwood plantations. The island is divided among Malaysia (Sabah and Sarawak), Indonesia (Kalimantan), and Brunei.

History

Before 1950 much of Borneo remained densely forested. Large-scale logging began in the 1960s and 1970s as Malaysia and Indonesia pursued economic development. By the 1980s, Borneo's timber exports were a major source of revenue. From the 1990s onward, conversion to oil palm plantations became the dominant driver of forest loss, as global demand for palm oil grew.

Environmental impact

Borneo's rainforests are among the most biodiverse on Earth, home to species such as the Bornean orangutan, pygmy elephant, and thousands of endemic plants. Deforestation threatens these species with extinction, destroys peatland carbon stores, and contributes to regional haze from fires used to clear land. Fires in 1997–98 and 2015 produced severe transboundary air pollution crises in Southeast Asia.

Conservation efforts

Initiatives such as the Heart of Borneo agreement (2007) between Brunei, Indonesia, and Malaysia aim to conserve a large transboundary forest area. Rates of primary forest loss in Malaysian and Indonesian Borneo declined in the late 2010s, though pressure from plantation expansion continues.
