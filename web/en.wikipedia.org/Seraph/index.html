A seraph (plural seraphim) is a type of celestial or heavenly being originating in Ancient Judaism. The term derives from the Hebrew saraph, associated with burning — hence "the burning ones." Seraphim are mentioned in the Book of Isaiah, where they attend the throne of God, each with six wings: with two they cover their faces, with two their feet, and with two they fly, crying "Holy, holy, holy" (Qadosh).

In Judaism

In Isaiah's vision (Isaiah 6), a seraph touches the prophet's lips with a live coal from the altar, purifying him for his mission. Later Jewish literature incorporated seraphim into elaborate angelologies, and Maimonides ranked them fifth among ten orders of angels.

In Christianity

In the celestial hierarchy of Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, seraphim form the highest of the nine orders of angels, closest to God and defined by burning love and purification. Saint Francis of Assisi was said to have received the stigmata following a vision of a crucified seraph, giving rise to his title "the Seraphic Father."

Iconography

Seraphim are commonly depicted as faces surrounded by three pairs of wings, often coloured red to suggest fire and love, distinguishing them from the blue-eyed, four-winged cherubim.
