Bologna

Bologna is the capital and largest city of the Emilia-Romagna region in northern Italy, with a population of around 390,000 in the city and over a million in its metropolitan area. It is home to the University of Bologna, founded in 1088 and recognised as the oldest university in continuous operation in the world. The city's nicknames capture its character: la dotta ("the learned"), la grassa ("the fat", for its food), and la rossa ("the red", for its terracotta rooftops and its traditionally left-wing politics).

History

The site was the Etruscan city of Felsina before becoming the Roman colony of Bononia in 189 BC, on the Via Aemilia. In the Middle Ages Bologna was a free commune famous for its scholars of Roman law — the jurist Irnerius revived the study of Justinian's code — and for its towers, of which some twenty survive from perhaps a hundred; the leaning Asinelli and Garisenda towers remain the city's emblem. Bologna became part of the Papal States in 1506, apart from a brief Napoleonic period, and joined the Kingdom of Italy in 1860.

In 1980 a bomb planted by neo-fascists at Bologna Centrale railway station killed 85 people, the deadliest terrorist attack in postwar Italian history.

The city

Bologna is celebrated for its porticoes — nearly 40 kilometres of arcades line the streets of the historic centre, and in 2021 they were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. Landmarks include the Piazza Maggiore, the Basilica of San Petronio, the Sanctuary of the Madonna di San Luca, reached by a portico of 666 arches, and the Archiginnasio, the historic seat of the university.

Cuisine

Bologna gives its name to ragù alla bolognese, the slow-cooked meat sauce served with tagliatelle (not, locally, spaghetti). Other specialities include tortellini, lasagne, mortadella and the wines of the surrounding hills. The wine and food trade fairs, together with the motor industry of nearby Modena and Maranello, anchor the regional economy.
