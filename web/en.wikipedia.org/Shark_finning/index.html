Shark finning

Shark fins drying in Hong Kong, long the centre of the fin trade

Shark finning is the practice of removing fins from sharks and discarding the rest of the shark, typically while the vessel is still at sea. The finned shark is often thrown back into the ocean alive, where, unable to swim effectively, it dies of suffocation, blood loss, or predation. Finning is driven by the high value of fins for shark fin soup, a prestige dish in Chinese cuisine, compared with the low value of bulky shark meat; fins, at only 1–5% of a shark's weight, maximize hold space for the most valuable product. The practice has been widely condemned as wasteful and cruel and is a central target of shark conservation campaigns.

The fin trade

Dried shark fins, graded by size and quality, were historically traded principally through Hong Kong, which handled around half of the world trade at its height, with major markets in mainland China, Singapore, and Taiwan. Fin prices could reach hundreds of dollars per kilogram before demand fell in the 2010s. The fins themselves are mostly cartilage; in soup they provide a gelatinous, stringy texture while the flavour comes from the (usually chicken and ham) broth. Fin soup dates to the Song and Ming dynasties and became a status symbol at banquets and weddings.

Scale and impact

Analyses of trade data have estimated that fins from 26 to 73 million sharks entered the trade annually in the early 2000s, and total shark mortality from all fishing has been estimated at around 100 million sharks per year. Because sharks reproduce slowly, this level of mortality has driven steep declines in many species, particularly oceanic pelagic sharks such as the oceanic whitetip and scalloped hammerhead, both now critically endangered.

Regulation

Most major fishing nations and all the tuna regional fisheries management organizations now prohibit finning, increasingly through "fins naturally attached" policies requiring sharks to be landed whole — a rule the European Union adopted in 2013 and which avoids the loopholes of fin-to-carcass weight ratios (typically 5%) used in earlier regulations such as the US Shark Finning Prohibition Act of 2000. The United States strengthened its law with the Shark Conservation Act (2010) and, in December 2022, enacted a federal ban on the domestic trade in shark fins. Many US states, beginning with Hawaii in 2010, had already banned fin sales, as have Canada (2019) and the United Kingdom (Shark Fins Act 2023). China's 2012–2013 austerity rules banned shark fin at official functions, and consumption surveys recorded large declines in consumption during the 2010s.

Debate

Supporters of regulation argue finning bans reduce mortality and waste and that demand reduction is essential given sharks' life history. Critics in some fisheries argue that well-managed fisheries using the whole shark are sustainable and that blanket trade bans can punish legitimate fishers; conservationists counter that species identification of loose fins is difficult, enforcement at sea is weak, and the trade's structure inherently incentivizes overfishing.
