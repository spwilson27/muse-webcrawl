A town is a type of human settlement, generally larger than a village but smaller than a city. The precise criteria for what constitutes a town vary considerably between countries and legal traditions; they may be based on population, administrative status, historical charters, or economic function such as hosting a regular market.

Origins

Towns grew out of agricultural villages that acquired a market, a defensive wall, or a charter granting self-government. In medieval Europe, a market town held a legal right to host markets on set days, distinguishing it from surrounding villages. Industrialization greatly expanded many towns into cities, while others remained modest local centres.

By country

In the United Kingdom, town status historically followed from a market charter or borough status, and is today often declared by the local council. In the United States, "town" is a specific legal form of municipality in several states — in New England, towns are the primary unit of local government, while in other states the term is informal. Many countries use statistical population thresholds that differ widely, from a few hundred residents to tens of thousands.

Functions

Towns typically serve as commercial and service centres for a surrounding rural hinterland, providing shops, schools, healthcare, and local government offices. Commuter or "dormitory" towns house residents who work in nearby cities.
