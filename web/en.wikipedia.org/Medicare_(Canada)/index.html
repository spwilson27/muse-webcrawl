Medicare (Canada)

TypePublicly funded, single-payer health insurance system
Governing lawCanada Health Act (1984)
AdministrationProvincial and territorial insurance plans
CoversMedically necessary hospital and physician services
Key figureTommy Douglas, premier of Saskatchewan

Medicare is the unofficial name for Canada's publicly funded health care system, under which all Canadian residents receive coverage for medically necessary hospital and physician services without direct charges at the point of care. Rather than a single national program, it consists of thirteen interlocking provincial and territorial health insurance plans operating under the standards of the federal Canada Health Act.

History

Saskatchewan pioneered public hospital insurance in 1947 under Premier Tommy Douglas, and in 1962 became the first province to insure physician services — an innovation that survived a doctors' strike. Following the Hall Commission's recommendations, the federal Medical Care Act of 1966 (in force 1968) offered to share the costs with provinces meeting its principles; by 1972 all provinces participated. The Canada Health Act of 1984 consolidated the system around five principles: public administration, comprehensiveness, universality, portability and accessibility, and banned extra-billing and user charges on pain of losing federal transfer payments.

Coverage and limitations

Medicare covers hospital and doctor services, but not most prescription drugs outside hospital, dental care, vision care or many other services, which are covered by private insurance or provincial programs for some groups. Delivery is largely by private practitioners paid fee-for-service by the provincial plans. Wait times for elective procedures, physician shortages and the system's cost are perennial subjects of debate, though Canadians consistently rank medicare among the country's defining institutions, and Douglas was voted "The Greatest Canadian" in a 2004 CBC poll.
