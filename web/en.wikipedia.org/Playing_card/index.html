Playing card

French-suited playing cards

A playing card is a piece of specially prepared card stock, heavy paper, thin cardboard, plastic-coated paper, cotton-paper blend, or thin plastic that is marked with distinguishing motifs. Each card has a face and a back; the backs of the cards in a deck are identical, while the faces carry suits and ranks that identify each card. A complete set of playing cards is called a pack, or deck, and the subset held by one player is a hand.

Playing cards are used for playing card games, for cardistry, for magic tricks, and in activities such as fortune-telling with tarot cards, which originated as a card game pack before acquiring occult associations.

History

Playing cards first appeared in China during the Tang dynasty, around the 9th century, in connection with printed "leaf" games. Cards spread westward through the Islamic world: Mamluk Egypt produced ornate hand-painted packs with suits of cups, coins, swords and polo-sticks. Cards arrived in Europe in the late 14th century, where they were initially luxury hand-painted objects. The invention of woodblock printing made cards cheap to produce, and regional suit systems developed: Latin suits (cups, coins, swords, batons) in Spain and Italy, German suits (hearts, bells, acorns, leaves), and the French suits — trèfles (clubs), carreaux (diamonds), cœurs (hearts) and piques (spades) — whose simple stencil-friendly shapes made them the most economical to manufacture and ultimately the international standard.

The standard 52-card deck

The most common pack today is the French-suited 52-card deck: four suits of thirteen ranks each — ace through ten, jack, queen and king. English-speaking countries added the joker in the 19th century for the game of euchre. Court cards acquired their familiar double-ended (reversible) designs in the 19th century so that players need not orient them upright. Standardised indices in the corners, attributed to American manufacturers, made it possible to fan a hand and read all the cards at once.

Manufacture

Modern cards are typically made from laminated paper or cellulose acetate plastic, printed by offset lithography and cut with rounded corners. Casino-grade cards are often 100% plastic for durability and are replaced frequently to prevent marking. Card sizes vary: the "poker" size (2.5 × 3.5 in) is common in North America, while the narrower "bridge" size (2.25 × 3.5 in) is standard in much of Europe.
