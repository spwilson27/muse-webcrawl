The Quirang (Scottish Gaelic: A' Chuith-raing) is a landslip on the eastern face of Meall na Suiramach, the northernmost summit of the Trotternish ridge on the Isle of Skye in Scotland. Part of the Trotternish landslip — the largest in Britain — it is a dramatic landscape of pinnacles, cliffs, and hidden plateaus, and remains geologically active, shifting gradually year by year.

The Quirang on the Trotternish peninsula, Skye

Formation

The Trotternish ridge is composed of thick layers of basalt lava, erupted around 60 million years ago during the opening of the North Atlantic, resting on weaker Jurassic sedimentary rocks. The immense weight of the lava caused the underlying strata to give way along the escarpment, producing a series of landslips, of which the Quirang and the Old Man of Storr further south are the best known. The Quirang's slip is still moving: the road at Flodigarry requires periodic repair as the ground creeps.

Features

The landslip contains a series of distinctive named formations. The Needle is a jagged pinnacle about 37 metres (120 ft) high. Behind and above it lies the Table, a flat grassy plateau tilted from the summit plateau, said to have been used for shinty games. The Prison is a pyramidal rock mass that resembles a medieval fortress from certain angles. A popular walking route of about 7 kilometres circuits the formations from the car park on the Staffin–Uig road.

Name and folklore

The name is said to derive from Gaelic cuith-raing, a "hidden fold," because cattle were concealed there from Viking raiders. The landscape has featured in films and television, and is one of Skye's most visited natural attractions.
