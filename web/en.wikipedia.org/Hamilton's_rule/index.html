Hamilton's rule

Hamilton's rule is a central theorem of sociobiology and kin selection theory, formulated by British evolutionary biologist W. D. Hamilton in 1964. It states that an altruistic trait or behaviour will spread in a population when the benefit to the recipient, devalued by the genetic relatedness between actor and recipient, exceeds the cost to the actor.

Formulation

The rule is expressed as rB > C, where r is the coefficient of genetic relatedness between the actor and the recipient, B is the reproductive benefit to the recipient, and C is the reproductive cost to the actor. Hamilton derived the rule in his two 1964 papers on the genetical evolution of social behaviour.

Explanation

Relatedness is the probability that a gene in one individual is identical by descent to a gene in another. For full siblings and for parent and offspring, r is typically 0.5; for cousins, 0.125. Because relatives share genes, helping them reproduce can propagate copies of the helper's own genes — a process called inclusive fitness. The rule predicts, for example, that workers in eusocial insects can evolve sterility when they are highly related to the queen's offspring they rear.

Applications and criticism

Hamilton's rule underlies explanations of alarm calls, cooperative breeding, eusociality in Hymenoptera, and senescence. Its generality has been debated: some formulations treat it as an accounting identity rather than a predictive law, and empirical tests require careful measurement of costs, benefits and relatedness in natural populations.
