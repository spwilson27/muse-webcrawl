Genetic rescue

Genetic rescue is a conservation strategy in which new genetic material is introduced into a small, inbred population — usually by translocating individuals from another population — in order to increase genetic diversity, mask deleterious recessive alleles, reduce inbreeding depression, and improve the population's fitness and chances of persistence.

Mechanism

Small isolated populations lose genetic diversity through drift and accumulate inbreeding, exposing harmful recessive alleles and reducing fertility, survival, and disease resistance. Immigrants bring new alleles; their offspring have higher heterozygosity, which often produces an immediate boost in fitness known as heterosis. Over time, the introduced variation also restores the population's capacity to adapt to environmental change.

Examples

The best-known case is the Florida panther: by the early 1990s the population had fallen to a few dozen animals showing kinked tails, heart defects, and poor sperm quality. In 1995, eight female pumas from Texas were released in Florida; the resulting admixture increased heterozygosity, reduced defects, and the population subsequently grew severalfold. Genetic rescue has also benefited the greater prairie chicken in Illinois, the Scandinavian wolf, and the mountain pygmy possum in Australia.

Risks and debate

Managers must weigh rescue against the risk of outbreeding depression, in which crossing populations adapted to different environments, or with incompatible gene complexes, reduces fitness in later generations. Other concerns include disease transmission and the genetic swamping of locally adapted populations. Guidelines generally favour donors from historically connected populations in similar habitats.
