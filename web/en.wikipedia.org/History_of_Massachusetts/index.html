History of Massachusetts

The history of Massachusetts reaches from the Indigenous peoples who inhabited the region for thousands of years, through English colonisation in the 17th century, a leading role in the American Revolution, and industrial and intellectual prominence in the centuries since.

Colonial period

Plymouth Colony was founded by the Pilgrims in 1620, and the larger Massachusetts Bay Colony followed in 1630 under John Winthrop, whose "city upon a hill" sermon expressed the Puritan ambition of the settlement. Relations with Native peoples deteriorated into devastating conflicts such as King Philip's War (1675–1676). The colonies merged under a royal charter in 1691, and the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 became a lasting symbol of colonial religious fervour.

Revolution to the present

Massachusetts was the cradle of the American Revolution: the Boston Massacre (1770), the Boston Tea Party (1773), and the battles of Lexington and Concord (1775) all occurred there. The state constitution of 1780, largely drafted by John Adams, is the oldest written constitution still in use and abolished slavery in the Commonwealth through court interpretation. In the 19th century Massachusetts led in textile manufacturing, whaling, abolitionism, and public education; in the 20th and 21st it became a centre of higher education, medicine, finance, and technology around Boston and Cambridge.
