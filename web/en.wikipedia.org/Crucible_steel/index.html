Crucible steel

Material typeHigh-carbon steel

Crucible steel is steel made by melting iron and other materials in a crucible. In ancient times steel-making was rare because pure iron's melting point (1,538 °C) exceeded what most furnaces could sustain; crucible techniques circumvented this by prolonged heating of iron with carbon-rich materials in sealed clay vessels. The resulting high-carbon steel was of far higher quality than most contemporary ironwork.

History

Crucible steel was produced in South India and Sri Lanka from around the mid-1st millennium BCE. Indian wootz steel — the name a corruption of ukku, the Kannada word for steel — was exported as ingots ("cakes") across Asia and the Middle East, where blades forged from it became famous as Damascus steel, prized for their strength and watered, banded surface pattern.

Methods

Processes varied by region. In the South Indian method, wrought iron was sealed in crucibles with wood, leaves and other carbon sources and heated for many hours, absorbing carbon until it partially melted. Other methods melted iron together with cast iron, or decarburised cast iron. The ingots were then forged carefully at relatively low temperatures to preserve their carbide structure.

Modern revival

In 1740s Sheffield, Benjamin Huntsman reinvented crucible melting to produce homogeneous steel for watch springs and cutlery, and crucible steel remained the premium tool steel until displaced by electric arc furnace production in the 20th century. Archaeometallurgists continue to study historic crucible steels, which some analyses have found to contain carbon nanotubes and nanowires formed during production.
