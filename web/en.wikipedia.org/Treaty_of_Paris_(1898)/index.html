The Treaty of Paris of 1898 was an agreement made in 1898 that resulted in the Spanish ceding control of Cuba and in Spain conveying control of Puerto Rico, Guam, and — for US$20 million — the Philippines to the United States. It brought an end to the Spanish–American War and is generally regarded as marking the emergence of the United States as a world power with overseas colonial possessions.

Negotiations

The peace commission met in Paris from October 1898, following an armistice signed in August. The American delegation, led by Secretary of State William R. Day, initially demanded only Luzon, but President William McKinley instructed it to obtain the entire Philippine archipelago. Filipino representatives were not admitted to the negotiations.

Terms

  • Spain renounced all claim to Cuba, which came under United States occupation and then independence under American oversight.
  • Spain ceded Puerto Rico and Guam outright to the United States.
  • Spain ceded the Philippine Islands to the United States in return for a payment of $20 million.

Ratification and aftermath

The treaty was narrowly ratified by the US Senate on 6 February 1899, by a single vote above the two-thirds required. Fighting between American and Filipino forces had already broken out two days earlier, beginning the Philippine–American War. Spain's loss of its last major colonies — remembered there as el Desastre del 98 — provoked a period of national introspection associated with the Generation of '98.
