Cis–trans isomerism

Cis–trans isomerism, also known as geometric isomerism, E–Z isomerism or configurational isomerism, is a type of stereoisomerism describing the relative orientation of functional groups within a molecule. In the cis isomer (Latin: "on this side"), the groups lie on the same side of a double bond or ring; in the trans isomer (Latin: "across"), they lie on opposite sides.

Organic chemistry

The phenomenon occurs where rotation about a bond is restricted, classically at carbon–carbon double bonds. Cis-2-butene and trans-2-butene have identical connectivity but different boiling points, melting points and dipole moments. Cis isomers of alkenes are generally less stable than trans because of steric crowding, though cis geometry is essential in biology — for instance in natural unsaturated fats and in the retinal of vision pigments, whose light-driven cis–trans isomerisation initiates sight.

E–Z notation

When the simple cis/trans labels are ambiguous (for tri- and tetrasubstituted double bonds), the E–Z system assigns priorities to substituents by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules: Z (zusammen) if the two higher-priority groups are on the same side, E (entgegen) if on opposite sides.

Inorganic chemistry

Cis and trans forms also occur in coordination compounds, e.g. the anticancer drug cisplatin, whose cis isomer is therapeutically active while transplatin is not.
