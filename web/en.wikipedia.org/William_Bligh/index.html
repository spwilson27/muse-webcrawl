William Bligh

William Bligh (9 September 1754 – 7 December 1817) was an English Royal Navy officer and colonial administrator, best known as the commanding officer of HMS Bounty, whose crew mutinied in 1789. Despite that notoriety, Bligh was an accomplished navigator and hydrographer who had served as sailing master under Captain James Cook.

The mutiny on the Bounty

After the Bounty's breadfruit voyage to Tahiti, master's mate Fletcher Christian and part of the crew seized the ship on 28 April 1789 and set Bligh and 18 loyalists adrift in an open launch. In one of the greatest feats of navigation on record, Bligh sailed the launch some 3,600 nautical miles to Timor without charts, losing only one man. A subsequent voyage successfully delivered breadfruit to the West Indies.

Later career

Bligh distinguished himself in the battles of Camperdown (1797) and Copenhagen (1801), where Nelson praised his conduct. As the fourth governor of New South Wales from 1806, his campaign against the corrupt rum trade provoked the Rum Rebellion of 1808, in which he was deposed by the New South Wales Corps — the only successful armed takeover of an Australian government. He was nevertheless promoted to rear-admiral and then vice-admiral, and died in London in 1817.
