J. Frank Duryea

Born: 8 October 1869, Washburn, Illinois, U.S.
Died: 15 February 1967, Saybrook, Connecticut, U.S.
Known for: Co-building the first successful American gasoline automobile; winning the first American car race (1895)

James Frank Duryea (1869–1967) was an American inventor and automotive pioneer who, with his brother Charles Edgar Duryea, built the first successful gasoline-powered automobile in the United States and won America's first automobile race.

The first Duryea automobile

The Duryea brothers, bicycle mechanics from Illinois, set up in Springfield, Massachusetts, financed by local investors. Charles produced designs and promotion, while Frank — a skilled machinist — did most of the practical engineering and construction. Their single-cylinder "motor wagon", based on a used horse buggy fitted with a 4-horsepower gasoline engine, made its first successful road run in Springfield in September 1893; a report in the Springfield newspaper The Republican marks it as the first working American gasoline car. Frank subsequently redesigned the machine substantially, including its ignition and transmission.

Company and the Chicago race

In 1895 the brothers founded the Duryea Motor Wagon Company in Springfield, the first American firm formed to manufacture automobiles, and built a small series of cars in 1896. On Thanksgiving Day 1895, Frank drove a Duryea in the Chicago Times-Herald race — the first automobile race held in the United States — covering some 54 miles through snow from Chicago to Evanston and back in about ten hours, and winning the prize against a field of mostly European Benz cars, most of which failed to finish.

Later career

The brothers quarrelled over credit and business strategy and separated. Frank went on to design the Stevens-Duryea cars, high-quality automobiles built by the J. Stevens Arms and Tool Company of Chicopee Falls from 1901 into the 1910s, favoured for their craftsmanship. He continued engineering work for decades, lived to see the automobile age he had helped begin, and died in Connecticut in 1967 at the age of 97. The brothers' respective contributions were disputed in their lifetimes, but historians generally credit Frank with the principal hands-on engineering of the 1893–95 cars.
