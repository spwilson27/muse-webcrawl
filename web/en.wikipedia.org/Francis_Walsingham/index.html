Francis Walsingham

Sir Francis Walsingham (c. 1532–1590), principal secretary and spymaster of Elizabeth I.

Sir Francis Walsingham (c. 1532 – 6 April 1590) was principal secretary to Queen Elizabeth I of England from 1573 until his death, and is remembered as her "spymaster". He built one of the most effective intelligence networks in early modern Europe and uncovered several Catholic plots against the queen.

Early life

Walsingham was educated at King's College, Cambridge, and studied law and languages in Italy. A committed Protestant, he went into exile during the reign of Mary I. Under Elizabeth he entered Parliament, served as ambassador to France (1570–1573), where he witnessed the St Bartholomew's Day massacre, and in 1573 became a privy councillor and secretary of state, sharing duties with William Cecil, Lord Burghley.

Intelligence network

Walsingham ran agents across Catholic Europe, intercepted and deciphered correspondence (employing the cryptanalyst Thomas Phelippes), used double agents, and ran a network of informants known to historians as one of the first recognisably modern espionage services. His agents exposed the Ridolfi plot's aftermath, the Throckmorton plot (1583) and, most famously, the Babington Plot of 1586: intercepted letters between Anthony Babington and Mary, Queen of Scots, deciphered by Phelippes, provided the evidence that led to Mary's trial and execution in 1587.

Walsingham also promoted English maritime expansion and colonisation, supporting figures such as Sir Francis Drake and Sir Humphrey Gilbert, and his intelligence on Spanish preparations contributed to England's readiness for the Armada of 1588. Despite his services he died in debt, having spent much of his own fortune on intelligence work.
