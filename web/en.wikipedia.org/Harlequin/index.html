Harlequin

Harlequin (Italian: Arlecchino; French: Arlequin) is the best-known of the zanni or comic servant characters from the Italian commedia dell'arte, whose introduction into Italy is traditionally credited to the actor-manager Zan Ganassa in the 1570s. The role is traditionally played by an actor wearing a black half-mask and a costume covered in bright, irregular patches, later regularised into a pattern of diamond-shaped lozenges. Harlequin is traditionally performed with a wooden stick or "slapstick" and a magic bat.

Characteristics

Harlequin's primary aspect was that of a light-hearted, nimble and serving character — a servant, often to the Innamorato (the lover) or to Pantalone. He is characterized by his quick wit, agility and acrobatic movement; his physical agility and wit were used to circumvent the obstacles placed in the way of his master. Harlequin is often in love with the Innamorata or with the soubrette Columbina, and his lust is countered by his appetite for food. Though originally a rustic simpleton (a zanni of low intelligence), in later French harlequinades his role was expanded — he became a more complex character, often acting as the voice of reason or as a bandit-like trickster.

History

The character is thought to derive in part from earlier comic figures: the medieval French "Herlequin" or "Hellequin", a leader of a troop of demons in popular legend, and the rustic Bergamo servant of Italian comedy. Tristano Martinelli is credited with popularising Harlequin in France in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, making the character a staple of the Comédie-Italienne in Paris. In England, Harlequin was adapted into the harlequinade, the dumbshow comic portion of the pantomime, in which he became a magical, romantic lead figure, famously played in the 19th century by Joseph Grimaldi's successors in the role of Clown. The name "harlequin" derives from Italian Arlecchino, whose origins are disputed.

In later culture

Harlequin has remained a durable figure in art, literature and popular culture. Pablo Picasso repeatedly depicted Harlequin as a melancholy alter-ego, and the character's diamond-patterned costume has become a generic visual shorthand for jesters and carnival figures. The term has also been borrowed for other uses, including the Harlequin duck and harlequin colour patterns in animals.
