Symphony

A symphony is an extended musical composition in Western classical music, most often for orchestra. Although the term has had many meanings from its origins in the ancient Greek era, by the late 18th century the word had taken on the meaning common today: a work usually consisting of multiple distinct sections or movements, often four, with the first movement in sonata form.

Form

The classical four-movement plan comprises a fast opening movement in sonata form, a slow movement, a minuet or scherzo, and a lively finale, often in sonata or rondo form. Composers have varied this scheme freely — Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique has five movements, Mahler's symphonies range widely in number and scale, and many 20th-century symphonies are in a single movement.

History

The symphony developed out of the Italian opera overture (sinfonia) in the early 18th century. The Mannheim school and, above all, Joseph Haydn — who wrote 104 numbered symphonies — established it as the central orchestral genre. Mozart's late symphonies and Beethoven's nine symphonies, culminating in the choral Ninth (1824), made the symphony the most prestigious form of instrumental music. Romantic composers including Schubert, Schumann, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, and Dvořák, and later Bruckner, Mahler, Sibelius, and Shostakovich, expanded its scale and expressive range.
