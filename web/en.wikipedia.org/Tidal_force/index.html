Tidal force

Tidal force
TypeDifferential gravitational effect
ScalingProportional to mass / distance³
EffectsOcean tides, tidal locking, Roche limit, spaghettification
Explained byNewtonian gravitation; general relativity
The Moon's gravity stretches Earth along the Earth–Moon line

The tidal force is the difference in gravitational pull exerted by one body on different parts of another body. Because gravity weakens with distance, the side of an object nearer a massive body is pulled more strongly than its centre, and the far side less strongly, producing a stretching effect along the line joining the two bodies.

Explanation

For a body of mass m at distance R acting on an object of radius r, the tidal acceleration is approximately 2Gmr/R³ — it falls with the cube of distance, which is why the nearby Moon raises stronger tides on Earth than the far more massive Sun. The effect produces two bulges, on the near and far sides, accounting for the roughly twice-daily cycle of ocean tides.

Effects

Tidal forces cause tidal locking, by which the Moon rotates once per orbit and always shows the same face to Earth, and they drive the Moon's gradual recession (about 3.8 cm per year) as Earth's rotation slows. Within the Roche limit, tides can tear a body apart; Saturn's rings are thought to lie within it. Strong tidal heating flexes Jupiter's moon Io — the most volcanically active body in the Solar System — and keeps subsurface oceans liquid on Europa and Enceladus. Near a black hole, extreme tidal gradients produce "spaghettification" of infalling matter.

In general relativity

In Einstein's theory, tidal effects correspond to the curvature of spacetime and are described mathematically by the geodesic deviation equation.
