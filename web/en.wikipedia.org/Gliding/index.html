Gliding

Gliding is a recreational activity and competitive air sport in which pilots fly unpowered aircraft known as gliders or sailplanes, using naturally occurring currents of rising air — principally thermals, ridge lift, and lee waves — to remain airborne and cover long distances. Skilled pilots can fly for many hours and hundreds of kilometres, and world championship competitions are held under the auspices of the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale.

Soaring

Because a glider continuously descends through the air mass around it, sustained flight requires finding air that rises faster than the glider sinks. Pilots climb in thermals — columns of warm rising air often marked by cumulus clouds — then glide toward the next thermal. Ridge lift forms where wind is deflected upward by hills, and mountain waves can carry gliders to altitudes above 15,000 metres (49,000 ft). Launches are made by aerotow behind a powered aircraft or by winch.

History

The sport developed in Germany in the 1920s after the First World War, when the Treaty of Versailles restricted powered aviation. Otto Lilienthal's 19th-century experiments had pioneered controlled gliding flight. Modern sailplanes are sleek composite aircraft with glide ratios exceeding 50:1. Distance records exceed 3,000 kilometres in a single flight.
