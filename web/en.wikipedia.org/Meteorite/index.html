Meteorite

A meteorite is a solid piece of debris from an object such as an asteroid, comet or meteoroid that originates in outer space and survives its passage through the atmosphere to reach the surface of a planet or moon. While travelling through the atmosphere the object is called a meteor (a "shooting star"); before that, a meteoroid.

Classification

Meteorites are traditionally divided into three broad types. Stony meteorites, by far the most common falls, include chondrites — primitive material from the early solar system containing small round grains called chondrules — and achondrites, which come from differentiated bodies. Iron meteorites consist mainly of iron–nickel alloy from the cores of shattered planetesimals and show striking Widmanstätten patterns when etched. Stony-iron meteorites, such as the pallasites with olivine crystals set in metal, are the rarest.

Falls and finds

An observed fall that is recovered is called a "fall"; a meteorite discovered later is a "find". More than 60,000 meteorites are catalogued, with Antarctica and the deserts of North Africa and Oman especially productive because dark stones stand out on ice or sand. Famous examples include the Hoba meteorite in Namibia, the largest known at about 60 tonnes, still lying where it fell; the meteorite that formed Meteor Crater in Arizona; and the 2013 Chelyabinsk airburst, which injured some 1,500 people, mostly from flying glass.

Scientific importance

Meteorites are the oldest objects available for direct study — calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions in chondrites date to about 4.567 billion years ago, the age of the solar system. Some contain organic compounds and amino acids, feeding speculation about the delivery of life's ingredients to Earth. A small number are identified as pieces of the Moon and Mars, blasted off by impacts. The unusual iridium layer deposited worldwide 66 million years ago records a giant meteorite impact implicated in the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs.
