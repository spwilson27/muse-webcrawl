Bus rapid transit

A BRT station with off-board fare collection and level boarding, in Bogotá's TransMilenio system.

Bus rapid transit (BRT), also called a busway or transitway, is a bus-based public transport system designed to have much more capacity and reliability than a conventional bus system. Typically, a BRT system includes roadways that are dedicated to buses, gives priority to buses at intersections, uses off-board fare collection and station platforms level with the bus floor for fast, accessible boarding, and operates frequent, branded services — features intended to combine the capacity and speed of a metro with the lower cost and flexibility of buses.

History and major systems

The first comprehensive BRT system is generally considered to be the Rede Integrada de Transporte in Curitiba, Brazil, developed from the 1970s under mayor Jaime Lerner, which paired dedicated bus lanes with land-use planning along transport axes. Bogotá's TransMilenio, opened in 2000, scaled BRT to metro-like capacities with dual carriageways and express services, carrying over a million passengers a day at its peak. Other notable systems include the Guangzhou BRT, Istanbul's Metrobüs, Mexico City's Metrobús, the Orange Line in Los Angeles and numerous Chinese and Indian systems. The Institute for Transportation and Development Policy publishes a BRT Standard scoring corridors from basic to gold.

Characteristics

Key elements, in the BRT Standard's definition, include a dedicated right-of-way, busway alignment (usually median), off-board fare collection, intersection treatments such as prohibiting turns across the busway and signal priority, and platform-level boarding. Systems may range from full BRT with all elements to "BRT lite" with only some. Criticism of diluted implementations has led to the term "BRT creep" for systems that gradually lose defining features.

Advantages and criticism

BRT can be built in a few years at a fraction of the per-kilometre cost of rail, can be extended incrementally, and buses can leave the busway to serve suburbs directly. Critics note that capacity is generally lower than heavy rail, that buses produce more local emissions (mitigated by electric buses), and that permanence for transit-oriented development is weaker than with fixed rail. Capacity claims vary widely by design: multi-bay stations with passing lanes and bi-articulated buses allow the highest throughputs.
