Decline in amphibian populations

Amphibian declines
First recognised1989, First World Congress of Herpetology, Canterbury
Species threatened~41% of assessed amphibian species (IUCN)
ExtinctionsAt least 200 species lost or suspected lost since the 1970s; dozens more possibly extinct
Key driversHabitat loss, chytridiomycosis, climate change, pollution, invasive species

Since the 1980s, a worldwide decline in amphibian populations has been documented, including population crashes and mass localised extinctions of frogs, toads, and salamanders. Amphibians are the most threatened class of vertebrates: global assessments by the IUCN find roughly two-fifths of species threatened with extinction, and declines have been reported on every continent where amphibians occur, often in pristine, protected habitats.

History of the observation

Reports of disappearing frogs accumulated through the 1980s — the golden toad of Monteverde, Costa Rica, was last seen in 1989 — and at the First World Congress of Herpetology that year herpetologists realised the pattern was global. Some early disappearances were later attributed to natural fluctuation, but long-term monitoring and the Global Amphibian Assessment (2004) confirmed genuine, accelerating declines, with the second global assessment (2023) finding the situation had worsened.

Causes

The leading cause of decline overall is habitat loss and degradation through agriculture, logging, and urbanisation. The most striking driver of sudden collapses in remote areas is chytridiomycosis, an infectious disease caused by the fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis and B. salamandrivorans, which attack amphibians' skin and have driven declines in more than 500 species and roughly 90 presumed extinctions. Other contributing factors include climate change altering breeding phenology and moisture regimes, pesticide and pollutant exposure (notably endocrine-disrupting chemicals), ultraviolet-B radiation, introduced predators and competitors such as trout and cane toads, and collection for food, medicine, and the pet trade.

Conservation responses

Responses include habitat protection, captive-breeding "assurance colonies" coordinated through programmes such as the Amphibian Ark, reintroductions (sometimes aided by treatments that reduce chytrid loads), and research into probiotic skin bacteria and selective breeding for disease resistance. Because amphibians are sensitive indicators of environmental health, their decline is widely treated as a warning signal for ecosystems generally.
