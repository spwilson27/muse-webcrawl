Mitotic spindle

The mitotic spindle, also called the spindle apparatus, is a dynamic structure of microtubules and associated proteins that forms during mitosis in eukaryotic cells. Its function is to separate duplicated chromosomes equally into two daughter cells, ensuring that each receives a complete set of genetic material.

Structure

The spindle is organised by two centrosomes, the cell's main microtubule-organising centres, which migrate to opposite poles of the cell. Three classes of microtubules compose the apparatus:

  • Kinetochore microtubules, which attach to the kinetochores of chromosomes and pull sister chromatids apart.
  • Polar microtubules, which interdigitate from opposite poles and push the poles apart.
  • Astral microtubules, which radiate toward the cell cortex and position the spindle within the cell.

Assembly and the spindle checkpoint

Spindle assembly begins in prophase and is driven by microtubule dynamic instability and motor proteins such as kinesins and dynein. Before the cell proceeds to anaphase, the spindle assembly checkpoint verifies that every chromosome is properly attached to microtubules from both poles (amphitelic attachment) and under tension. Failure of this checkpoint can lead to aneuploidy, a hallmark of many cancers.

Clinical relevance

Because rapidly dividing cancer cells depend on the spindle, it is a target of chemotherapy drugs. Taxanes such as paclitaxel stabilise microtubules and prevent their disassembly, while vinca alkaloids such as vincristine prevent microtubule polymerisation; both arrest cells in mitosis and trigger cell death.
