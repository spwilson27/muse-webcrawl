A ketubah (Hebrew: כְּתוּבָּה, "written thing"; plural ketubot) is a Jewish marriage contract, traditionally written in Aramaic, that outlines the rights and responsibilities of the groom in relation to the bride. It is considered an integral part of a traditional Jewish marriage and is signed by two witnesses before the wedding ceremony.

History

The ketubah is one of the oldest known documents protecting women's financial rights in marriage. The Babylonian Talmud's tractate Ketubot records its standard provisions; tradition attributes its institution to Simeon ben Shetach in the 1st century BCE so that a husband could not divorce his wife casually, since divorce triggered a substantial payment to her. Ancient ketubot have been found among the documents of the Judaean Desert, including the Babatha archive from the early 2nd century CE.

Contents

The traditional text records the date and place of the wedding, the names of the bride and groom, the bride's dowry, and the groom's undertaking to "work for, honour, provide for and support" his wife. Its core financial clause obliges the husband (or his estate) to pay the wife a fixed sum — 200 zuz for a virgin bride, 100 for a widow or divorcee — in the event of divorce or his death. The ketubah is traditionally read aloud under the wedding canopy (chuppah) and given to the bride for safekeeping.

Modern practice

Orthodox and Conservative weddings use the traditional Aramaic text, while Reform and other liberal movements often use egalitarian versions written in Hebrew and/or the local language, with mutual commitments. Elaborately illuminated ketubot have a long artistic history, especially in Italy and the Ottoman world, and contemporary ketubah art is a popular Jewish ceremonial art form. In Israel, the ketubah's monetary clause can be enforced in rabbinical courts.
