Radiation fog

Radiation fog is a type of fog that forms at night or in the early morning under clear skies and calm conditions, when the ground loses heat by longwave radiation and cools the air immediately above it to its dew point, causing water vapour to condense into droplets. It is common in autumn and winter in temperate climates, particularly in valleys and low-lying areas where cold air pools — a pattern also called a temperature inversion.

Formation

Favourable conditions are clear skies (allowing maximum radiative cooling), light winds (enough to mix a shallow layer but not to disperse it), and moist air near the surface, often after rain or near water. Radiation fog typically forms after sunset, thickens overnight, and is most dense around dawn. It usually "burns off" as the sun warms the ground in the morning, dissipating from the top down or lifting into low stratus cloud.

Effects

Radiation fog is a major hazard for road transport and aviation because visibility can fall below 100 metres in patches. It differs from advection fog, which forms when warm moist air moves over a cold surface, and from valley fog and freezing fog, which are related variants. Dense, polluted radiation fog contributed to historical smog episodes in industrial cities.
