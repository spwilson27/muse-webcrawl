Classical conditioning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Classical conditioning (also known as Pavlovian or respondent conditioning) is a type of learning that occurs when a conditioned stimulus (CS) is paired with an unconditioned stimulus (US). After pairing is repeated, the animal or organism learns to associate the two stimuli and will respond to the conditioned stimulus alone with a conditioned response (CR) similar to the unconditioned response (UR). Usually, the conditioned stimulus is a neutral stimulus (e.g., the sound of a tuning fork), the unconditioned stimulus is biologically potent (e.g., the taste of food), and the conditioned response is the same as the unconditioned response (e.g., salivation).

Pavlov's experiments

The phenomenon was first described by Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov, who, while studying digestion in dogs around the turn of the 20th century, noticed that the animals salivated not only at the food itself but at stimuli that preceded it, such as the sight of the attendant or the sound of a bell rung before feeding. Pavlov systematically studied how such "psychical secretions" were acquired, extinguished, and generalized to similar stimuli.

Phenomena

Key phenomena of classical conditioning include acquisition (the formation of the association), extinction (the decline of the conditioned response when the CS is presented alone), spontaneous recovery (the return of an extinguished response after a rest interval), stimulus generalization (responding to stimuli similar to the CS), and discrimination (learning to respond only to a specific CS). Conditioning is strongest when the CS slightly precedes the US. John B. Watson's "Little Albert" experiment (1920) demonstrated emotional conditioning in a human infant, and later research — such as Kamin's blocking effect and the Rescorla–Wagner model — showed that conditioning depends on the informativeness of the CS, not mere temporal pairing.

Applications and biology

Classical conditioning underlies taste aversion learning, fear responses and phobias, and aspects of drug tolerance and craving. It is applied in therapies such as systematic desensitization and in advertising, where products are paired with pleasant imagery. Neural studies implicate the amygdala in fear conditioning and the cerebellum in eyeblink conditioning.
