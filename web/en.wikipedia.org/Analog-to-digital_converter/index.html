Analog-to-digital converter

An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) is a system that converts an analog signal, such as a sound picked up by a microphone or light entering a digital camera, into a digital signal. The conversion involves sampling the signal at discrete time intervals and quantizing its amplitude to a finite number of levels.

Resolution and sampling

The resolution of an ADC, expressed in bits, determines the number of discrete values it can produce: an 8-bit ADC encodes 256 levels, a 16-bit ADC 65,536. By the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, a signal can be reconstructed if it is sampled at more than twice its highest frequency.

Architectures

Common designs include flash ADCs (very fast, low resolution), successive-approximation (SAR) converters, and sigma-delta (ΔΣ) converters, which oversample and noise-shape to achieve high resolution in audio and instrumentation.

Applications

ADCs are embedded in microcontrollers, audio interfaces, medical imaging, software-defined radio, and virtually every device that digitizes real-world signals. The reverse process is performed by a digital-to-analog converter (DAC).
