W. A. J. "Alan" Watson (1933–2018) was a Scottish legal scholar known as one of the world's foremost authorities on Roman law, comparative law and legal history. He is best known for developing the theory of legal transplants — the idea that most legal change occurs through the borrowing of rules and doctrines from other legal systems, often with little regard to the fit with local social conditions.

Career

Watson was educated at the University of Glasgow and Oxford. He held chairs at Oxford, where he was a fellow of Oriel College, and at the University of Edinburgh, where he held the Salvesen Chair of European Institutions. He later emigrated to the United States, becoming Ernest P. Rogers Professor of Law at the University of Georgia School of Law, where he continued to write for several decades.

Legal transplants thesis

In Legal Transplants: An Approach to Comparative Law (1974), Watson argued that borrowing is the most fertile source of legal development, and that transplanted rules often survive for centuries despite changed social circumstances — a thesis he illustrated from Roman law's reception across Europe and beyond. The thesis proved highly influential and also controversial: critics such as Pierre Legrand argued that legal rules cannot truly be separated from their cultural context, while others, like Otto Kahn-Freund, urged caution about the ease of transplantation. Watson replied in later editions and essays, and the debate remains central to comparative law scholarship.

Other work

Watson wrote extensively on Roman law, including work on the law of persons, property and obligations, and translated the Digest of Justinian into English (1985). He also wrote on law and society in the ancient world, slavery, and the Bible and law, including The Trial of Jesus and studies of ancient law.
