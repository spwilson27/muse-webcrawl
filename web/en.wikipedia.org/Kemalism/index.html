Kemalism (Turkish: Kemalizm), also known as Atatürkism (Atatürkçülük), is the founding ideology of the Republic of Turkey, based on the principles and reforms of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk. It is symbolised by the "Six Arrows" adopted by the Republican People's Party and written into the Turkish constitution in 1937.

The Six Arrows

The six principles are: republicanism (sovereignty of the nation expressed through a republic); populism (social equality and rule in the people's interest, rejecting class or group privilege); nationalism (a civic-territorial Turkish nation and its unity); secularism (laiklik — separation of religion and state, with state oversight of religious affairs); statism (devletçilik — a leading state role in the economy); and reformism or revolutionism (continuous modernisation and defence of the reforms against reaction).

Reforms

Kemalism was implemented through the "Atatürk reforms": abolition of the sultanate (1922) and caliphate (1924), a secular civil code and legal system modelled on European codes, adoption of the Latin alphabet (1928), Turkish-language reform, full political rights for women (suffrage in 1934), state-led industrialisation, and secular national education. Atatürk's speeches, especially the Nutuk (1927), and his maxim "Peace at home, peace in the world" remain canonical references.

Legacy and debate

For much of the 20th century the Turkish military presented itself as guardian of Kemalism, intervening in politics in its name. Since the early 2000s, governments led by the AKP have contested aspects of the Kemalist settlement, particularly assertive secularism, sparking continuing debate about the ideology's place in Turkish democracy. Kemalism remains the official ideology of the CHP and an influential framework in Turkish political discourse.
