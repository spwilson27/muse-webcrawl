Joachim Murat

[Image placeholder: portrait of Joachim Murat]

Joachim Murat (born Joachim Murat; Italian: Gioacchino Murat; 25 March 1767 – 13 October 1815) was a French military commander and statesman who served during the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. Under the French Empire he received the military titles of Marshal of the Empire and Admiral of France. He was the first Prince Murat, Grand Duke of Berg from 1806 to 1808, and King of Naples from 1808 to 1815.

Born in Labastide-Fortunière (now Labastide-Murat) in southwestern France to an innkeeper's family, Murat studied briefly for the priesthood before joining a cavalry regiment in 1787. He rose rapidly during the Revolution, attracting the attention of Napoleon Bonaparte, whom he served as aide-de-camp during the Italian campaign of 1796–97. In 1800 he married Caroline Bonaparte, Napoleon's youngest sister.

Cavalry commander

Murat became famous as one of the finest cavalry commanders of his age, noted for his flamboyant uniforms, personal bravery, and dramatic charges. He played important roles at Marengo, Austerlitz, Jena, Eylau, and Borodino, where he led massed cavalry attacks. At the Battle of Eylau in 1807 he led a massive charge of some 10,000 horsemen that helped save Napoleon's army.

King of Naples

In 1808 Murat was made King of Naples after Joseph Bonaparte was sent to Spain. As king he introduced reforms, abolished feudal privileges in parts of the kingdom, and tried to win the loyalty of his subjects. During the Hundred Days in 1815 he issued the Rimini Proclamation calling for Italian unification, but his forces were defeated by the Austrians at Tolentino in May 1815, and he fled the kingdom.

Death

Attempting to regain his throne, Murat landed in Calabria with a small force in October 1815. He was captured, tried by court-martial, and executed by firing squad at Pizzo on 13 October 1815. Famously, he is said to have faced the firing squad unblindfolded, telling the soldiers, "Soldiers, do your duty. Aim at the heart, but spare the face."
