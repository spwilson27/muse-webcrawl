Exact solutions in general relativity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In general relativity, an exact solution is a solution of the Einstein field equations whose physical interpretation can be worked out with sufficient precision to be useful in modeling gravitational phenomena. Because the field equations are a system of coupled nonlinear partial differential equations, exact solutions are difficult to find, and most known examples exploit symmetries such as spherical symmetry, axial symmetry, or stationarity to reduce the equations to a tractable form.

Notable examples

The first exact solution, found by Karl Schwarzschild in 1916 only months after Einstein published the theory, describes the gravitational field outside a spherically symmetric, non-rotating mass and the exterior of a non-rotating black hole. The Kerr solution (1963) generalizes this to a rotating mass and describes the spacetime around a rotating black hole; the Kerr–Newman solution adds electric charge. The Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) metrics describe homogeneous, isotropic expanding or contracting universes and underpin modern cosmology.

Other well-known exact solutions include the Reissner–Nordström metric for a charged spherical mass, the Gödel universe, which contains closed timelike curves, the Taub–NUT solution, pp-wave and plane-wave solutions describing gravitational radiation, and the interiors of stars modeled as perfect fluids, such as the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff solutions.

Difficulties of interpretation

Even when a metric is known in closed form, extracting its physical meaning can be subtle: coordinate effects can masquerade as physical singularities, and global properties of the spacetime may only emerge from maximal analytic extensions, as with the Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates for the Schwarzschild solution. Exact solutions are often idealized — describing isolated systems or perfectly symmetric cosmologies — whereas astrophysical situations are usually handled with approximation schemes such as post-Newtonian expansions or numerical relativity.
