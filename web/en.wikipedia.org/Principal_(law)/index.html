Principal (law)

In commercial law, a principal is a person, legal or natural, who authorizes an agent to act to create one or more legal relations with a third party. This branch of law is called agency law and relies on the common law proposition qui facit per alium, facit per se ("one who acts through another, acts oneself").

The principal–agent relationship

The principal manifests assent that the agent shall act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, and the agent assents to act in that way. Within the authority granted, contracts made by the agent bind the principal as if the principal had made them personally. Principals may be disclosed (named to the third party), partially disclosed, or undisclosed, and the rules governing liability differ accordingly.

Types of authority

An agent may act with actual authority (express or implied) conferred by the principal, or with apparent (ostensible) authority, where the principal's representations lead a third party reasonably to believe the agent is authorised. A principal may also ratify an unauthorised act after the fact, adopting it as if originally authorised.

Duties and liability

Principals generally owe their agents duties of compensation, reimbursement and indemnification, while agents owe the principal fiduciary duties of loyalty, obedience and care. A principal may be liable in tort for acts of an agent or employee under doctrines such as respondeat superior. In criminal law, "principal" also has a distinct sense — the main perpetrator of an offence, as distinguished from an accessory.
