Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics (from Ancient Greek pharmakon "drug" and kinetikos "moving, putting in motion"; see chemical kinetics), sometimes abbreviated PK, is a branch of pharmacology dedicated to describing how the body affects a specific substance after administration — often summarised as "what the body does to the drug". The substances of interest include pharmaceutical drugs, hormones, nutrients, and toxins. It is complemented by pharmacodynamics.

ADME

Pharmacokinetics is commonly described by the acronym LADME or ADME: liberation of the drug from its formulation, absorption into the circulation, distribution throughout the body's fluids and tissues, metabolism (chemical modification, largely in the liver by enzymes such as the cytochrome P450 family), and excretion, mainly via the kidneys and bile. Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged; drugs given intravenously have 100% bioavailability by definition, whereas oral drugs may be reduced by incomplete absorption and first-pass metabolism.

Modelling

The time course of drug concentration is analysed with compartmental models (one- or multi-compartment) or non-compartmental methods. Important parameters include the volume of distribution, clearance, and the elimination half-life, which together determine dosing intervals and the time needed to reach steady state (about four to five half-lives). Most drugs follow first-order (linear) kinetics, in which a constant fraction is eliminated per unit time, but drugs such as phenytoin and ethanol display saturable, zero-order elimination.

Clinical relevance

Pharmacokinetic variability arising from age, genetics, organ impairment, and drug interactions is a major determinant of individual dosing, and therapeutic drug monitoring is used for agents with a narrow therapeutic index, such as aminoglycosides, vancomycin, and tacrolimus.
