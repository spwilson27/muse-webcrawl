Hegra (Ancient Greek: Ἕγρα), known in Arabic as Mada'in Salih (مدائن صالح, "the cities of Salih"), is an archaeological site located in the area of AlUla within the Al Madinah Region of the Hejaz, Saudi Arabia. A majority of the remains date from the Nabataean kingdom (1st century AD). The site constitutes the kingdom's southernmost and second-largest city after Petra, its capital in present-day Jordan.

History

Hegra was originally settled by earlier peoples including the Lihyanites before the Nabataeans developed it into a major caravan city on the incense trade route linking southern Arabia with the Mediterranean world. The Nabataeans' mastery of water engineering — wells, cisterns and dams — allowed agriculture and a permanent settlement to flourish in the arid environment. After Rome annexed the Nabataean kingdom in 106 AD, the frontier shifted and Hegra gradually declined, though inscriptions show continued use of the site for centuries.

Archaeology

The site is famous for its 111 monumental tombs, 94 of them with decorated facades carved into sandstone outcrops, many bearing detailed inscriptions that record ownership and curses against tomb violators. Among its most iconic monuments is Qasr al-Farid, a large isolated unfinished tomb carved from a single rock. The residential area, surrounded by a rammed-earth wall, contains houses, wells and the remains of a city centre; excavated remains of the Hijaz railway station, built by the Ottomans in the early twentieth century, also lie nearby.

In 2008 UNESCO proclaimed Mada'in Salih a World Heritage Site — Saudi Arabia's first — citing it as an outstanding example of Nabataean architecture and hydraulic technology.
