Acadians

The Acadians (French: Acadiens) are an ethnic group descended from the French settlers who colonized Acadia, in present-day Canada's Maritime Provinces, beginning in the early 17th century. Between 1755 and 1764, during the French and Indian War, the British deported more than 10,000 Acadians in the Expulsion of the Acadians (le Grand Dérangement). Many eventually returned to the Maritimes, while others settled in Louisiana, where their descendants became known as Cajuns.

History

French colonization began at Port-Royal in 1605. Acadian farmers developed a distinctive society, reclaiming tidal marshland with dykes (aboiteaux) and maintaining neutrality between French and British authorities. After Britain gained permanent control of peninsular Nova Scotia in 1713, pressure grew for an unconditional oath of allegiance. In 1755, Governor Charles Lawrence ordered the deportation; families were scattered among British colonies, England and France, and thousands died of disease and shipwreck.

Renaissance

From the 1880s, an Acadian national renaissance produced the first national conventions, the adoption of the flag and the anthem Ave Maris Stella, and later French-language institutions including the Université de Moncton (1963). In 2003, the Canadian government formally acknowledged the deportation, and December 13 is observed as Acadian Remembrance Day.

Culture

Acadian culture is celebrated at the quinquennial Congrès mondial acadien. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem Evangeline (1847) popularized the story of the expulsion, and the Grand-Pré National Historic Site in Nova Scotia, a UNESCO World Heritage landscape, commemorates the Acadian homeland.
