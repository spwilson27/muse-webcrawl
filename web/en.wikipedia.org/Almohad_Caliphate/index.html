Almohad Caliphate

The Almohad Caliphate (from Arabic al-Muwaḥḥidūn, "the monotheists" or "the Unitarians") was a North African Berber Muslim empire founded in the twelfth century. At its height it controlled most of the Maghreb and Islamic Iberia (al-Andalus), from the Atlantic to present-day Libya.

Origins

The movement began with Ibn Tumart, a Masmuda Berber religious reformer from the High Atlas who preached a strict doctrine of divine unity (tawḥīd) and proclaimed himself the Mahdi around 1121. After his death, his successor Abd al-Mu'min built the movement into a state, capturing Marrakesh in 1147 and destroying the ruling Almoravid dynasty. Abd al-Mu'min declared himself caliph — the first Berber and non-Qurayshi ruler to take the title — and extended Almohad power across North Africa and into Iberia.

Height and decline

Under Abu Yusuf Yaqub al-Mansur (1184–1199) the empire reached its zenith, defeating Castile at Alarcos in 1195 and commissioning monuments including the Koutoubia Mosque in Marrakesh, the Giralda in Seville, and the Hassan Tower in Rabat. The court patronized scholars such as Ibn Rushd (Averroes) and Ibn Tufayl. Decline set in after the caliph Muhammad al-Nasir's defeat at Las Navas de Tolosa in 1212; Christian kingdoms conquered most of al-Andalus over the following decades, while the Maghreb fragmented among the Hafsids, Zayyanids, and Marinids, the last of whom took Marrakesh in 1269.
