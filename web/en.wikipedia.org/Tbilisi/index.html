Tbilisi

Tbilisi (Georgian: თბილისი), in some languages still known by its pre-1936 name Tiflis, is the capital and the largest city of Georgia, lying on the banks of the Mtkvari (Kura) River with a population of around 1.2 million. Founded in the 5th century AD by Vakhtang I of Iberia, Tbilisi has served, with interruptions, as the capital of various Georgian kingdoms and republics for almost fifteen centuries.

History

According to legend, Vakhtang's falcon fell into the area's hot sulphur springs while hunting, and the king, impressed by the warm waters — tbili means "warm" in Georgian — founded a city on the site. Because of its position on crossroads between Europe and Asia and on the historic Silk Road, Tbilisi was contested and periodically ruled by Persians, Byzantines, Arabs, Khazars, Seljuks, and Mongols. It flourished under Queen Tamar in Georgia's golden age, was annexed by the Russian Empire in 1801, served as capital of the short-lived Democratic Republic of Georgia (1918–1921) and of the Georgian SSR, and became capital of independent Georgia in 1991.

Geography and landmarks

The city stretches along the Mtkvari gorge between mountain ridges. Its Old Town is noted for narrow streets, carved wooden balconies, and the sulphur bathhouses of Abanotubani, overlooked by the ancient Narikala Fortress. Major landmarks include the Sioni and Sameba (Holy Trinity) cathedrals, the Metekhi church, Rustaveli Avenue, the Mtatsminda funicular, and the modern Bridge of Peace. Tbilisi's architecture reflects its layered history, mixing medieval, neoclassical, Art Nouveau, and Soviet styles.

Culture

Tbilisi is Georgia's cultural center, home to national opera, ballet, and theatre companies, major museums, and universities. The city is known for its polyphonic singing traditions, supra feasts, and wine culture, and hosted the Eurovision events and numerous international festivals.
