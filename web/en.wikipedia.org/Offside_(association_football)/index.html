Offside is a law of association football, codified as Law 11 of the Laws of the Game maintained by the IFAB. It is among the most discussed and frequently misunderstood rules in the sport, designed to prevent players from simply waiting near the opponents' goal for the ball.

The law

A player is in an offside position if any part of the head, body or feet is in the opponents' half and nearer to the opponents' goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent (usually, but not always, the goalkeeper and one defender). Being in an offside position is not an offence in itself. A player commits an offside offence only if, at the moment the ball is played by a team-mate, they become involved in active play — by playing the ball, interfering with an opponent, or gaining an advantage from a rebound. The sanction is an indirect free kick from where the offence occurred.

Exceptions and details

There is no offside offence if a player receives the ball directly from a goal kick, a throw-in or a corner kick, nor can a player be offside in their own half. Level with the second-last opponent is onside. Assistant referees judge offside from the touchline, and since the late 2010s top competitions have used video assistant referees (VAR) and semi-automated offside technology to review close decisions.

History

The rule descends from the stricter offside laws of nineteenth-century English football, which originally resembled rugby's prohibition on forward passing. The requirement was relaxed from three defenders (1866–1925) to two in 1925, a change that sharply increased scoring, and in 1990 being level with the second-last opponent was made onside. Teams exploit the law tactically through the "offside trap", while attackers time runs to "beat the trap".
