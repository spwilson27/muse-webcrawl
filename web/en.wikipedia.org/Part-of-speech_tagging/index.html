Part-of-speech tagging

In corpus linguistics, part-of-speech tagging (POS tagging or PoS tagging, also called grammatical tagging) is the process of marking up a word in a text (corpus) as corresponding to a particular part of speech — noun, verb, adjective, and so on — based on both its definition and its context. It is a foundational step in natural language processing, feeding downstream tasks such as parsing, named-entity recognition, word-sense disambiguation, and information extraction. For example, in "The quick brown fox jumps," the tags are roughly DET ADJ ADJ NOUN VERB.

Tagssets and corpora

Tagging presupposes a tagset: an inventory of labels and guidelines for their use. The Brown Corpus (1960s) used 87 tags; the Penn Treebank tagset of 45 tags became the de-facto standard for English; the Universal Dependencies project defines 17 universal categories used across many languages. The difficulty of the task depends on the language: morphologically rich languages may need tags encoding case, number, tense and other features.

Approaches

Rule-based tagging

Early systems, such as the TAGGIT programme for the Brown Corpus and the ENGTWOL tagger, used dictionaries plus hand-written rules about context — for instance, that a word following a determiner and an adjective is likely a noun.

Stochastic tagging

From the late 1980s, statistical methods came to dominate: hidden Markov models choose the tag sequence maximising the product of word-likelihood and tag-transition probabilities, decoded with the Viterbi algorithm. Later discriminative models — maximum-entropy taggers, conditional random fields, and transformation-based learning (Brill's tagger) — improved accuracy to around 97% on Penn Treebank English, close to human agreement.

Neural tagging

Modern taggers use bidirectional LSTMs, often combined with character-level models and CRF output layers, and more recently pre-trained transformer language models, achieving state-of-the-art results and robust handling of out-of-vocabulary words.

Difficulties

Ambiguity is the central problem: many words belong to several classes ("book" as noun or verb; "that" as determiner, pronoun, or complementiser), and correct choice depends on context. Other difficulties include unknown words, multiword expressions, disagreements between tagsets, and the fact that some decisions require world knowledge. Errors in tagging propagate to later stages of an NLP pipeline.

Applications

Beyond linguistics research, POS tagging underlies text-to-speech pronunciation, search indexing, machine translation pipelines, grammar checking, and preprocessing for large language corpora used in lexicography.
