Aqueduct (bridge)

Aqueducts are bridges constructed to convey watercourses across gaps such as valleys or ravines. In modern engineering, the term aqueduct may also describe any system of pipes, ditches, canals, tunnels, and supporting structures used to convey water from a distant source. The word derives from the Latin aqua ("water") and ducere ("to lead"). Aqueduct bridges were a hallmark of Roman engineering, built throughout the Roman Empire to supply cities and towns with water; many survive today.

The Pont du Gard in southern France, a Roman aqueduct bridge

Roman aqueduct bridges

Roman aqueduct systems totaled over 415 km (258 mi) for the city of Rome alone, mostly underground, but where valleys had to be crossed, engineers built monumental arched bridges to preserve the gentle gradient of the water channel (specus). Famous examples include the Pont du Gard in France (which carried water to Nîmes and later also served as a toll bridge), the Aqueduct of Segovia in Spain, the Pont de les Ferreres in Spain, and the Valens Aqueduct in Constantinople (Istanbul).

Modern aqueduct bridges

Navigable aqueducts carry canals — and boats — over rivers and valleys; notable examples include the Pontcysyllte Aqueduct in Wales (1805), the Magdeburg Water Bridge in Germany (2003), and the Barton Swing Aqueduct in England. Municipal water-supply aqueducts, such as the Croton Aqueduct and the Los Angeles Aqueduct, bridge terrain as part of long-distance conveyance systems, although modern practice favors tunnels and pipelines.

Materials and design

Roman bridges used stone and concrete with brick facing, arranged in superimposed tiers of arches for the tallest crossings. Later structures used cast iron, steel, and reinforced concrete. The governing design constraint is gradient: the channel must fall steadily and slowly along its entire course, dictating the height and route of the bridge.
