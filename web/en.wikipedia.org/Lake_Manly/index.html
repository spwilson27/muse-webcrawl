Lake Manly

Lake Manly was a large prehistoric lake in Death Valley, California, that existed during the Pleistocene epoch. Fed by increased precipitation and runoff during the last glacial period, the lake once filled much of the valley floor and is regarded as one of the major pluvial lakes of the American Southwest.

Ancient shorelines of Lake Manly are visible on the slopes above Badwater Basin.

LocationDeath Valley, California, United States
TypePluvial lake (prehistoric)
Primary inflowsAmargosa River, Mojave River, Owens River system
Max. depth~180 m (600 ft)

Formation

During cooler and wetter periods of the Pleistocene, roughly between 186,000 and 128,000 years ago and again around 35,000 to 10,000 years ago, runoff from the Amargosa River, the Mojave River, and overflow from the Owens River chain of lakes filled Death Valley with a body of water some 160 km (100 miles) long and up to about 180 m (600 ft) deep. At its maximum the lake covered an area of roughly 1,600 km².

Evidence

Wave-cut terraces and tufa deposits along the valley walls record former shorelines of Lake Manly. The highest prominent shoreline lies about 90 m above sea level, far above the modern valley floor at Badwater Basin, which lies 86 m below sea level. Sediment cores from the valley floor show alternating salt and mud layers corresponding to deep-lake and dry playa phases.

Aftermath

As the climate became hotter and drier at the end of the Pleistocene, the lake evaporated, leaving behind the salt pans and playas seen today. The lake is named after William Lewis Manly, who in 1849 helped rescue a party of emigrants stranded in Death Valley during the California Gold Rush. Occasionally, after exceptionally wet winters, shallow ephemeral lakes form on the valley floor and are popularly nicknamed "Lake Manly" in reference to the ancient lake.
