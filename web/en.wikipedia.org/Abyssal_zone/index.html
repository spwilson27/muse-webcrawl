The abyssal zone or abyssopelagic zone is a layer of the pelagic zone of the ocean. The word abyss comes from the Greek ἄβυσσος (abyssos), meaning "bottomless". At depths of 4,000–6,000 m (13,000–20,000 ft), this zone remains in perpetual darkness. It covers 83% of the total area of the ocean and 60% of Earth's surface, making it the largest environment for life on Earth.

Conditions

The abyssal zone has a temperature of around 2 to 3 °C (35 to 37 °F), and pressures range from 40 to 60 megapascals — hundreds of times greater than at the surface. Because no sunlight penetrates, there is no photosynthesis; ecosystems depend on organic material raining down from above, known as marine snow, supplemented in places by chemosynthesis around hydrothermal vents.

Life

Despite the extreme conditions, many organisms live in the abyssal zone, including anglerfish, gulper eels, giant squid, tripod fish, sea cucumbers, xenophyophores and numerous invertebrates. Adaptations include slow metabolisms, bioluminescence, large mouths and expandable stomachs, and in many species gigantism or, conversely, dwarfism. Food is scarce, so many animals are scavengers or predators that feed infrequently.

Abyssal plains

The ocean floor within this depth range consists largely of abyssal plains — vast, flat sediment-covered areas that are among the flattest regions on Earth. They are dotted in places with abyssal hills, seamounts, polymetallic (manganese) nodules and cold seeps.
