Theme (music)

In music, a theme is a melodic, rhythmic, or harmonic idea that serves as the principal material of a composition or of a section of one, and to which other parts of the work stand in a developmental or contrasting relationship. Themes are the building blocks from which movements are constructed: a theme is stated, then repeated, varied, fragmented, sequenced, or opposed by other themes, generating the form of the piece.

Characteristics

A theme is usually a phrase or period with a clear melodic identity, complete enough to be remembered and recognized when it returns, yet open enough to permit development. In sonata form, the exposition presents a first theme (or theme group) in the tonic and a contrasting second theme in the dominant or relative major, and the drama of the movement arises from their interplay in the development and recapitulation. In theme and variations form, a single theme is presented plainly and then passes through a series of characterful alterations.

Related concepts

The theme is related to but distinct from the motif, a shorter fragment — often just a few notes — that may combine with others to build a theme, and from the subject of a fugue, the main melodic idea announced at the start and imitated in other voices. In film, television, and programme music, a recurring theme associated with a character, place, or idea is a leitmotif. A "theme song" or "signature tune" is the characteristic music identifying a programme, performer, or production.

Examples

Famous themes include the four-note opening of Beethoven's Fifth Symphony, the theme of Paganini's Caprice No. 24 that inspired variations by Rachmaninoff and others, and the folk-like themes of Dvořák's New World Symphony. Analysts from Heinrich Schenker onward have debated whether entire movements grow from a single underlying thematic idea.
