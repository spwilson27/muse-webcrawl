Dinosaurs are a diverse group of reptiles of the clade Dinosauria. They first appeared during the Triassic period, between 243 and 233.23 million years ago, although the exact origin and timing of the evolution of dinosaurs is a subject of active research. They became the dominant terrestrial vertebrates after the Triassic–Jurassic extinction event 201.3 million years ago and remained so until the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago.

The fossil record shows that birds are feathered dinosaurs, having evolved from earlier theropods during the Late Jurassic epoch, and are the only dinosaur lineage known to have survived the mass extinction. Dinosaurs can therefore be divided into avian dinosaurs — birds — and the extinct non-avian dinosaurs, which are all dinosaurs other than birds.

Characteristics

Dinosaurs are united by anatomical features including a hole in the hip socket (perforate acetabulum) and an upright stance with legs positioned directly beneath the body, unlike the sprawling posture of most other reptiles. They ranged enormously in size: the smallest species, such as the bee hummingbird alive today, weigh a few grams, while the largest sauropods exceeded 30 metres in length and tens of tonnes in mass. Many dinosaurs were bipedal, others quadrupedal; some could switch between stances. Most laid eggs, many built nests and cared for their young, and a wide variety of species bore feathers, horns, crests, armour or spikes.

History of discovery

Giant fossil bones were known for centuries but were attributed to mythical creatures or biblical giants. In the 1820s, William Buckland and Gideon Mantell described the first dinosaurs recognised scientifically — Megalosaurus and Iguanodon. The term Dinosauria ("terrible lizards") was coined by Richard Owen in 1842. The "Bone Wars" of the late 19th century, spectacular mounted skeletons in museums, and the dinosaur renaissance beginning in the 1960s — which recognised dinosaurs as active, warm-blooded animals closely related to birds — transformed scientific and public understanding.

Extinction

About 66 million years ago, at the end of the Cretaceous, all non-avian dinosaurs became extinct, along with roughly three-quarters of all species on Earth. Evidence points to a 10-kilometre asteroid impact at Chicxulub, Mexico, as the principal cause, possibly aggravated by massive volcanism in the Deccan Traps of India. Birds, together with mammals, crocodilians and others, survived into the Cenozoic.

Cultural significance

Dinosaurs are among the most popular subjects in museums, books and film, from Jurassic Park to countless children's stories. More than 1,000 genera of non-avian dinosaurs have been named, and new species are described every week.
