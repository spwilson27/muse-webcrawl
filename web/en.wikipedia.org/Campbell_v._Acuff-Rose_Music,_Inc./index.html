Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), was a United States Supreme Court case concerning copyright law and the fair use doctrine. The Court held that the commercial nature of a parody does not render it presumptively unfair, and that a parody's taking from the "heart" of an original work does not disqualify it from fair use protection. The case arose from the rap group 2 Live Crew's parody of Roy Orbison's song "Oh, Pretty Woman."

Background

In 1989, the rap group 2 Live Crew, led by Luther Campbell, released a parody version of "Oh, Pretty Woman" on their album As Clean As They Wanna Be. The original song, written by Roy Orbison and William Dees, was published by Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. Before releasing the parody, 2 Live Crew's manager had written to Acuff-Rose seeking a license, but Acuff-Rose refused. The group released the song anyway, crediting Orbison and Dees as authors and listing Acuff-Rose as publisher.

Acuff-Rose sued for copyright infringement. The District Court granted summary judgment to Campbell, finding fair use. The Sixth Circuit reversed, reasoning that the commercial purpose of the parody and the amount taken from the original weighed against fair use.

Opinion of the Court

Justice David Souter, writing for a unanimous Court, reversed the Sixth Circuit. The Court emphasized that parody, like other comment or criticism, may claim fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. Central to the analysis was the concept of a "transformative" use: the more transformative the new work, the less significance factors such as commercialism carry.

The Court rejected the presumption, drawn from Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios, that every commercial use is presumptively unfair, holding that such a presumption is inappropriate in the parody context. It also held that although 2 Live Crew had taken the "heart" of the original—the opening bass riff and first line—parody by its nature must conjure up enough of the original to make its target recognizable. The Court remanded for further proceedings on whether the parody harmed the market for derivative works such as a rap version of the song.

Significance

Campbell is regarded as a landmark decision that revitalized the fair use doctrine by centering the "transformative use" inquiry that had been introduced in Judge Pierre Leval's scholarship and the Court's earlier opinion in Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service. The case remains the leading authority on parody and fair use.
