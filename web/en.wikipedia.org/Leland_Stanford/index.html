Leland Stanford

Amasa Leland Stanford (9 March 1824 – 21 June 1893) was an American industrialist, politician and philanthropist. A leading figure among the "Big Four" who built the western portion of the first transcontinental railroad, he served as president of the Central Pacific and Southern Pacific railroads, as Governor of California (1862–1863) and as a United States Senator (1885–1893). With his wife Jane he founded Stanford University in memory of their only child.

Leland Stanford, railroad magnate and founder of Stanford University.

Early life and business

Born in New York, Stanford studied law and moved to California during the Gold Rush, prospering as a merchant in Sacramento. With Collis P. Huntington, Mark Hopkins and Charles Crocker — the "Big Four" — he incorporated the Central Pacific Railroad in 1861. As its president he drove the line eastward over the Sierra Nevada, largely with Chinese immigrant labour, and on 10 May 1869 he famously drove the ceremonial golden spike at Promontory Summit, Utah, completing the transcontinental railroad.

Politics

A Republican, Stanford was elected Governor of California in 1861, serving during the Civil War, and entered the U.S. Senate in 1885, where he served until his death. His railroad empire made him one of the richest men in America, though critics attacked the Pacific railroads' monopoly power and political influence.

Stanford University

After their son, Leland Stanford Jr., died of typhoid fever in 1884, just before his sixteenth birthday, the Stanfords resolved that "the children of California shall be our children" and founded Leland Stanford Junior University on their Palo Alto stock farm. The university opened in 1891. Stanford also commissioned Eadweard Muybridge's pioneering motion studies of a galloping horse on his estate, helping settle the question of whether all four of a horse's hooves leave the ground.
