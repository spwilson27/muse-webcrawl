Digital signal processing (DSP) is the use of digital processing — by computers or specialised digital signal processors — to perform a wide variety of operations on signals that have been converted from analogue form into sequences of numbers. Its goals include filtering, spectral analysis, compression, error correction, and the measurement and control of physical systems. DSP underlies modern telecommunications, digital audio and video, radar and sonar, medical imaging and much of consumer electronics.

Foundations

A continuous signal is sampled at discrete instants and quantised to finite precision by an analogue-to-digital converter. The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a band-limited signal can be perfectly reconstructed if sampled at more than twice its highest frequency; sampling more slowly causes aliasing. Analysis uses the discrete Fourier transform — computed efficiently by the fast Fourier transform (FFT), popularised by Cooley and Tukey in 1965 — and the z-transform, which plays the role for discrete systems that the Laplace transform plays for continuous ones.

Filters and systems

The workhorse of DSP is the digital filter, implemented as finite impulse response (FIR) filters — inherently stable and capable of exactly linear phase — or infinite impulse response (IIR) filters, which achieve sharp responses with fewer coefficients but may be unstable. Filters are designed in the frequency domain (window methods, Parks–McClellan equiripple design, Butterworth/Chebyshev/elliptic prototypes) and embedded in structures for convolution, correlation, adaptive filtering (LMS, Kalman filters) and multi-rate processing such as decimation and interpolation.

Applications

  • Audio and speech: MP3/AAC compression, echo and noise cancellation, synthesis and recognition
  • Communications: modulation/demodulation, channel equalisation, error-correcting codes, software-defined radio
  • Images and video: JPEG/H.264 compression, enhancement, computer vision front-ends
  • Biomedical: ECG/EEG analysis, hearing aids, ultrasound and MRI reconstruction
  • Geophysics and radar: seismic deconvolution, synthetic-aperture radar, sonar

Implementation

DSP algorithms run in real time on dedicated DSP chips (a lineage begun by the Texas Instruments TMS32010 in 1983), on FPGAs, or as software on general processors; fixed-point arithmetic and single-instruction multiple-data pipelines are used where cost and power matter. The field grew from 1960s mainframe research and the availability of the FFT into a standard discipline of electrical engineering, with its theory of discrete-time systems developed by researchers such as Alan Oppenheim and Ronald Schafer, whose textbooks defined the subject.
