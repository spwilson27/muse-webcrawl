Efficiency

Efficiency is the often measurable ability to avoid wasting materials, energy, efforts, money, and time in doing something or in producing a desired result. In general, it is the ratio of useful output to total input in a system, process, or machine.

In physics and engineering

In thermodynamics and engineering, efficiency (often denoted by the Greek letter η, eta) is the fraction of energy supplied that performs useful work: η = useful energy output / total energy input. No real machine is perfectly efficient; heat engines are bounded by the Carnot efficiency determined by their operating temperatures, and electrical and mechanical devices lose energy to friction, resistance, and sound. Efficiencies are commonly expressed as percentages — a modern electric motor may exceed 90%, while an incandescent light bulb converts only about 5% of its energy into light.

In economics

In economics, efficiency describes how well resources are allocated. Allocative efficiency occurs when goods are produced in quantities matching consumer demand; productive efficiency when they are produced at the lowest possible cost; and Pareto efficiency when no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. Economic efficiency is a central, and contested, criterion for evaluating markets and policies.

Other uses

The term is also used for algorithmic efficiency in computer science (use of time and memory), efficiency in statistics (the precision of an estimator), ecological efficiency (energy transfer between trophic levels, typically around 10%), and everyday organisational productivity. In all senses, efficiency must be distinguished from effectiveness: efficiency concerns doing things with minimal waste, effectiveness concerns doing the right things.
