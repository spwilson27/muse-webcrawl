The Manhattan Project was a research and development undertaking during World War II that produced the first nuclear weapons. It was led by the United States with the support of the United Kingdom and Canada. From 1942 to 1946, the project was under the direction of Major General Leslie Groves of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Nuclear physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer was the director of the Los Alamos Laboratory that designed the bombs. The project name derives from the Manhattan Engineer District of the Army Corps of Engineers, which administered it and had its early offices in New York City.

Prompted by fears that Nazi Germany was developing nuclear weapons, and by the 1939 Einstein–Szilárd letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt, the project employed more than 130,000 people at its peak and cost nearly US$2 billion (equivalent to over $25 billion today). Over 80% of the cost went to building and operating plants that produced fissile material — uranium-235 and plutonium-239.

Sites and work

Three secret cities anchored the effort. At Oak Ridge, Tennessee, enormous electromagnetic separation (Y-12 calutrons) and gaseous diffusion (K-25) plants enriched uranium. At Hanford, Washington, the B Reactor and sister reactors bred plutonium from natural uranium, which was chemically separated on an industrial scale. At Los Alamos, New Mexico, scientists and engineers under Oppenheimer designed the weapons themselves: the uranium gun-type device "Little Boy" and the plutonium implosion device "Fat Man", whose design required the breakthrough of explosive lenses to compress a plutonium core symmetrically. Supporting work took place at universities and sites across North America and Britain, including the Chicago pile (CP-1), where Enrico Fermi's team achieved the first self-sustaining chain reaction in December 1942.

Trinity and combat use

The plutonium device was tested at the Trinity site in the New Mexico desert on 16 July 1945, yielding an explosion of roughly 20 kilotons. On 6 August 1945 the uranium bomb Little Boy was dropped on Hiroshima, and on 9 August the plutonium bomb Fat Man on Nagasaki, killing well over one hundred thousand people, most of them civilians, and contributing to Japan's decision to surrender.

Legacy

The Manhattan Project was dissolved in 1946 and its functions transferred to the civilian Atomic Energy Commission in 1947. It inaugurated the nuclear age: the Cold War arms race, nuclear power, and enduring ethical debates about scientists' responsibility for weapons of mass destruction. Many project scientists, including Oppenheimer, advocated international control afterwards; others, led by Edward Teller, pressed on to the hydrogen bomb.
