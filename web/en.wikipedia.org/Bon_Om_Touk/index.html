Bon Om Touk

Bon Om Touk (Khmer: បុណ្យអុំទូក), the Cambodian Water Festival, is celebrated annually in November over three days, marking the end of the rainy season and the reversal of the flow of the Tonlé Sap River. It is one of the largest festivals in Cambodia, drawing millions of people to Phnom Penh, where the royal palace riverfront hosts competitive boat racing.

The river reversal

The Tonlé Sap is unique among the world's rivers: during the monsoon, the Mekong rises so high that water flows "uphill" into the Tonlé Sap lake, which swells to several times its dry-season size and stores huge quantities of fish. When the Mekong falls in November, the river reverses and drains back, carrying fish to the waiting nets. The festival gives thanks for this natural bounty.

Celebrations

The centrepiece is the boat racing: long, narrow "dragon boats", brightly painted and crewed by dozens of paddlers rowing in unison, race in pairs before the King. Villages and pagodas from all over Cambodia send boats, some carrying more than 70 rowers. The festival also includes:

  • Loy pratip — a flotilla of illuminated floats on the river at night.
  • Sampeah preah khae — salutations to the moon, with offerings including ak ambok, flattened rice eaten with banana and coconut.
  • Ork ambok — the ceremonial eating of flattened rice at midnight.

The festival's origins are traced to the 12th century and King Jayavarman VII, whose naval victories are depicted in bas-reliefs at the Bayon and Banteay Chhmar temples. In 2010 the festival was marred by tragedy when a stampede on a bridge to Koh Pich (Diamond Island) killed 347 people; the festival was cancelled in the following years on several occasions, including for mourning and drought, before being revived.
