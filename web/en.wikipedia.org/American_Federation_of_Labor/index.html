American Federation of Labor

The American Federation of Labor (AFL) was a national federation of labour unions in the United States founded in Columbus, Ohio, in December 1886 by an alliance of craft unions disaffected from the Knights of Labor. Under its longtime president Samuel Gompers, it became the largest and most influential labour federation in the country for decades.

American Federation of Labor
Founded: 8 Dec 1886, Columbus, Ohio
President: Samuel Gompers (1886–1894, 1895–1924)
Merged: 1955, into AFL–CIO

Philosophy

The AFL practised "pure and simple" business unionism: organising skilled workers by craft, pursuing higher wages, shorter hours, and better conditions through collective bargaining and strikes rather than political transformation. It generally opposed organising unskilled industrial workers — a gap that led dissident unions to found the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) in 1935.

History

The federation survived the anti-union campaigns of the 1920s, shaped New Deal labour law (supporting the Wagner Act while guarding its autonomy), and grew during the Second World War. After decades of rivalry and raiding, the AFL and CIO merged in 1955 under George Meany to form the AFL–CIO, uniting some 15 million members.

Criticism

Historians note the AFL's craft exclusivity often excluded Black workers, immigrants, and women, though some affiliates organised inclusively, and the federation's record on immigration restriction and race remains contested.
