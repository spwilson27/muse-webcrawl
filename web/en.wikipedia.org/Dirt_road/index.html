Dirt road

A dirt road is an unpaved road made from the native material of the land surface, as opposed to a gravel road, which is surfaced with imported aggregate. Dirt roads range from simple tracks passable only in dry weather to well-formed, regularly graded roads of clay or sand.

Characteristics

Because they lack a weatherproof surface, dirt roads can become impassable when wet, developing deep mud and ruts; in dry conditions they generate large quantities of dust and often develop corrugations ("washboard" surfaces) from vehicle traffic. They are usually maintained by blading or grading rather than resurfacing.

Usage

Dirt roads predominate in rural and remote areas and in low-income regions where paving is unaffordable. In the United States, a substantial fraction of rural public roads remain unpaved. Terminology varies: similar roads are called tote roads, forest roads, farm tracks, or (in parts of Africa) earth roads.

Safety and environment

Loose surfaces reduce traction and lengthen braking distances. Dust from dirt roads can damage crops and air quality, and eroding road surfaces contribute sediment to waterways; mitigation includes speed limits, surface treatments, and drainage works.
