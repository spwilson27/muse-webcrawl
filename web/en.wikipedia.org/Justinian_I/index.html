Justinian I (Latin: Flavius Petrus Sabbatius Iustinianus; c. 482 – 14 November 565), also known as Justinian the Great, was the Byzantine emperor from 527 to 565. His reign is marked by the ambitious renovatio imperii, the attempt to restore the Roman Empire's lost western territories, the codification of Roman law in the Corpus Juris Civilis, and a vast building programme crowned by the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople.

Early life

Justinian was born Petrus Sabbatius at Tauresium in the Balkans, probably to a peasant family. His uncle Justin, a soldier who rose through the ranks, adopted him and brought him to Constantinople, where he received a good education. When Justin became emperor in 518, Justinian became his close adviser and was made co-emperor in April 527, succeeding sole on Justin's death that August. His wife Theodora, a former actress, was crowned alongside him and proved a formidable political partner.

Reconquests

Justinian's generals, above all Belisarius, reconquered large parts of the former Western Empire: the Vandal kingdom in North Africa fell in 533–534, followed by a long, devastating war against the Ostrogothic kingdom of Italy (535–554) and the seizure of a coastal strip of southern Spain from the Visigoths. The wars, combined with the outbreak of the Plague of Justinian in 541–542, severely strained the empire's resources.

Law and administration

Justinian's commission under Tribonian produced the Corpus Juris Civilis: the Code, the Digest (a compilation of juristic writings), the Institutes (a textbook), and later the Novels (new laws). This codification preserved Roman law and became foundational for the legal systems of much of continental Europe.

Domestic affairs and building

The reign nearly ended in the Nika riots of 532, when chariot-racing factions united against the government and burned much of Constantinople; Theodora's refusal to flee stiffened Justinian's resolve, and the revolt was crushed with great loss of life. The rebuilt city saw the construction of Hagia Sophia (537), whose dome was an engineering marvel, and of churches, cisterns, and fortifications across the empire.

Later reign and legacy

In his last decades Justinian faced Persian wars, Slav and Avar incursions in the Balkans, and theological controversies, including the Second Council of Constantinople in 553. He died childless in 565 and was succeeded by his nephew Justin II. Justinian is venerated as a saint in the Eastern Orthodox Church, and his reign is conventionally seen as the last great flowering of the late Roman Empire.
