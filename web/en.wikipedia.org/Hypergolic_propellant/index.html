A hypergolic propellant combination consists of a fuel and an oxidizer that ignite spontaneously when they come into contact with each other, without any ignition source. The term was coined during the Second World War from the Greek hyper (over), ergon (work), and the Latin oleum (oil). Because they ignite reliably and are storable as liquids at room temperature, hypergolic propellants are widely used in spacecraft propulsion and in missile systems that must remain ready to launch for long periods.

Common combinations

The most common hypergolic pairs combine a hydrazine-family fuel—hydrazine, monomethylhydrazine (MMH), or unsymmetrical dimethylhydrazine (UDMH), sometimes blended as Aerozine 50—with the oxidizer dinitrogen tetroxide (NTO). Earlier systems used nitric acid oxidizers, such as red fuming nitric acid with aniline or UDMH fuels. These combinations powered the Apollo Lunar Module descent and ascent engines, the Space Shuttle's orbital manoeuvring system, many satellite thrusters, and intercontinental ballistic missiles such as the Titan II.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hypergolic engines are simple and highly reliable: propellant valves alone control ignition, restarts are easy, and the liquids can be stored for years without the insulation cryogenic propellants require. Their performance (specific impulse around 280–320 seconds in vacuum) is lower than that of liquid hydrogen/oxygen systems, however, and the propellants are extremely toxic and corrosive—hydrazines are carcinogenic, and NTO produces poisonous nitrogen dioxide fumes—demanding elaborate ground-handling precautions. These hazards have motivated research into less toxic "green" propellants.
