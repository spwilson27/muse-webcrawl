In accountancy, depreciation is a term that refers to two aspects of the same concept: first, the actual decrease in fair value of an asset, such as the decrease in value of factory equipment each year as it is used and wears; and secondly, the allocation in accounting statements of the original cost of such assets over their useful life, so that the cost is matched against the revenue the asset helps generate.

Accounting treatment

A business depreciates long-lived tangible assets — buildings, machinery, vehicles, furniture — for both tax and accounting purposes. The depreciation expense reduces reported income (and usually taxable income) without any cash outlay; the accumulated depreciation is a contra-asset that reduces the asset's book (carrying) value. Land is generally not depreciated because it is considered to have an unlimited useful life. Allocating the cost requires estimates of the asset's useful life and residual (salvage) value.

Methods

  • Straight-line: equal expense each year — (cost − residual value) / useful life.
  • Declining balance: a fixed percentage of the opening book value each year, front-loading the expense.
  • Units of production: expense proportional to actual output or usage.
  • Sum-of-the-years'-digits: an accelerated method using a declining fraction.

Economic depreciation

In economics, depreciation is the gradual decrease in the economic value of the capital stock of a firm, nation, or other entity, either through physical depreciation, obsolescence, or changes in demand for the services of the capital in question. It enters national accounts as the consumption of fixed capital, the difference between gross and net investment and between GDP and NDP.

Related concepts

Amortisation is the equivalent allocation for intangible assets such as patents; depletion applies to wasting assets such as mines and oil wells. Distinct from depreciation, impairment is an unexpected, significant write-down of an asset's value.
