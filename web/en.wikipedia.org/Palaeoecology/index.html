Palaeoecology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Palaeoecology (or paleoecology) is the study of interactions between organisms and/or interactions between organisms and their environments across geologic timescales. As a discipline, it uses data from fossils and sedimentary environments to reconstruct past ecosystems, communities, and biodiversity through deep time. It sits at the intersection of palaeontology, ecology, and sedimentology.

Overview

Palaeoecologists ask the same questions as modern ecologists — who ate whom, how species were distributed, how communities responded to environmental change — but must answer them from the incomplete record preserved in rocks. Their evidence includes the composition of fossil assemblages, the functional morphology of extinct organisms, trace fossils such as burrows and tracks, stable-isotope geochemistry, and the sedimentary context in which remains were buried.

A central concern of the field is taphonomy, the study of how assemblages of dead organisms are altered between death and discovery. Transport, scavenging, dissolution, and time-averaging — the mixing of remains from many generations into a single bed — all distort the signal of the living community, and palaeoecological inference depends on recognising and correcting for those distortions.

Methods

Quantitative methods are widely used: ordination and cluster analysis of species-abundance data identify recurring communities; functional-trait and ecomorphological analyses infer diet, locomotion, and habitat use from anatomy; and comparisons with modern analogue ecosystems calibrate interpretations of fossil ones. Stable carbon and oxygen isotopes in shells, teeth, and sediments record temperature, productivity, and diet. In Quaternary studies, fossil pollen, diatoms, and chironomids from lake and bog cores give high-resolution records of recent ecological change.

Applications

Palaeoecology informs understanding of mass extinctions and recoveries, the assembly of modern biotas, long-term responses of ecosystems to climate change, and conservation baselines — for example, showing what coastal and reef communities looked like before industrial fishing. Marine palaeoecology has been especially influential, reconstructing trophic structure in ancient seas from predation traces such as drill holes in shells.
