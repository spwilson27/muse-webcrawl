The National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) is a program created by the Congress of the United States in 1968 through the National Flood Insurance Act of 1968 (P.L. 90-448). The program enables property owners in participating communities to purchase insurance protection, administered by the government, against losses from flooding.

History

The NFIP was created in response to the rising cost of taxpayer-funded disaster relief for flood victims and the increasing amount of damage done by floods. Before the program, flood insurance was generally unavailable from private insurers, who regarded flood risk as uninsurable because of adverse selection — only those at greatest risk would buy it. The Flood Disaster Protection Act of 1973 required the purchase of flood insurance for properties in high-risk areas receiving federal financial assistance.

How it works

The program is administered by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). Communities that adopt and enforce floodplain management ordinances meeting NFIP requirements may participate; in return, residents can buy federally backed flood insurance. Policies are sold and serviced by private insurance companies and agents, while the risk itself is borne by the federal government. FEMA produces Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRMs) that delineate Special Flood Hazard Areas.

Financial issues and reform

The NFIP has periodically fallen deeply into debt, particularly after Hurricane Katrina in 2005 and Hurricane Sandy in 2012, when claims far exceeded premium income. The Biggert–Waters Flood Insurance Reform Act of 2012 sought to move premiums toward actuarially sound rates, though parts of it were softened by the Homeowner Flood Insurance Affordability Act of 2014. FEMA later introduced Risk Rating 2.0, a pricing methodology that sets premiums based on individual property characteristics rather than map zones alone.
