Metabolite

Metabolites are the small molecules that are the intermediates and products of metabolism. The term usually refers to molecules under about 1,500 daltons — sugars, amino acids, nucleotides, lipids, organic acids and vitamins — as distinct from macromolecules such as proteins and nucleic acids. The complete set of metabolites in an organism, tissue or cell at a given moment is its metabolome, and its systematic study is metabolomics.

Primary and secondary metabolites

Primary metabolites are directly involved in normal growth, development and reproduction: amino acids, nucleotides, fermentation products such as ethanol and lactic acid, and the intermediates of central carbon metabolism. Secondary metabolites are not essential for basic growth but confer ecological advantages — antibiotics such as penicillin, pigments, toxins, alkaloids like morphine and caffeine, and plant signalling compounds. Many medicines derive from secondary metabolites, and industry exploits microbial production of both classes.

Drug metabolites

In pharmacology, a metabolite is what the body turns a drug into, chiefly through liver enzymes such as the cytochrome P450 family (phase I) and conjugation reactions (phase II). Metabolites may be inactive, toxic, or pharmacologically active — sometimes deliberately so, as in prodrugs such as codeine, which is converted to morphine. Drug and doping tests typically detect metabolites, which persist longer than the parent compound.

Analysis

Metabolites are measured by mass spectrometry and NMR spectroscopy in fields from clinical diagnostics (newborn screening, biomarkers) to environmental monitoring and food science.
