Royal Game of Ur

The Royal Game of Ur, also known as the Game of Twenty Squares, is a two-player strategy race board game played in ancient Mesopotamia. Boards dating to roughly 2600–2400 BCE were excavated from the Royal Cemetery at Ur by Leonard Woolley in the 1920s; one of the finest examples is held by the British Museum.

Description

The game is played on a board of twenty squares arranged in a distinctive shape — two blocks of twelve and four squares joined by a bridge of two. Two players each race a set of seven pieces along a track, moving according to throws of tetrahedral (four-sided) dice. Certain squares, marked with rosettes, appear to have been safe or granted an extra throw.

Rules

The exact rules were reconstructed by Irving Finkel from a Babylonian cuneiform tablet written by the scribe Itti-Marduk-balāṭu around 177 BCE, the oldest known rule set for any board game. The tablet describes the game's connection to fortune-telling: the squares were associated with omens, and the game appears to have had a divinatory dimension alongside gambling.

Legacy

Game of Twenty Squares boards have been found across the ancient Near East and eastern Mediterranean, from Egypt to Crete and Sri Lanka, and versions of the game survived in parts of the Middle East into modern times. The game is considered a possible ancestor of later race games such as backgammon.
