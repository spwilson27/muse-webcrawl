The equatorial coordinate system is a celestial coordinate system widely used to specify the positions of celestial objects. It may be implemented in spherical or rectangular coordinates, both defined by an origin at the centre of Earth, a fundamental plane consisting of the projection of Earth's equator onto the celestial sphere (forming the celestial equator), and a primary direction towards the March equinox.

Unlike the horizontal coordinate system, equatorial coordinates do not change with the time of night or the observer's location in a way that moves the objects: the celestial sphere appears to rotate around the poles, so an object's equatorial coordinates stay (nearly) fixed while its altitude and azimuth change continuously. This makes the system ideal for star catalogues and for telescope mounts.

Coordinates

Declination (δ) measures angular distance north or south of the celestial equator, from +90° at the north celestial pole to −90° at the south celestial pole; it is analogous to latitude. Right ascension (α) is measured eastward along the celestial equator from the March equinox, but expressed in hours, minutes, and seconds of time (24 hours = 360°), because of its close relationship to sidereal time and the hour angle used at the telescope. An older alternative to right ascension is the hour angle itself, measured westward from the local meridian.

Epoch and precession

Because Earth's axis precesses with a period of about 26,000 years, the celestial equator and equinox drift slowly, so coordinates must be quoted for a particular epoch. Modern catalogues use epoch J2000.0 (1 January 2000); earlier catalogues used B1950.0. Smaller effects — nutation, aberration, and proper motion of the stars themselves — must also be accounted for in precise work. The International Celestial Reference System, realised by distant quasars, has superseded the dynamical equinox as the fundamental frame, though equatorial coordinates remain in everyday use.
