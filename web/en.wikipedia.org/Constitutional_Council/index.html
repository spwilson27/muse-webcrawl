Constitutional Council

The Palais Royal in Paris, seat of France's Constitutional Council.

A Constitutional Council is a public body, found in a number of countries, charged with reviewing the conformity of laws and other state acts with the constitution. The best-known example is the Constitutional Council of France (Conseil constitutionnel), created by the Constitution of the Fifth Republic in 1958. Similar bodies exist in several francophone and other states, sometimes under names translated as Constitutional Council or Constitutional Court.

France

The French Constitutional Council consists of nine members serving single nine-year terms, one third appointed every three years. Three members are appointed by the President of the Republic, three by the President of the National Assembly, and three by the President of the Senate. Former Presidents of the Republic are also entitled to sit as ex officio members, though most have chosen not to do so.

Originally conceived mainly as a guardian of the boundary between statute law and executive regulation, the Council's role expanded dramatically with its 1971 Freedom of Association decision, which incorporated the preamble of the constitution, and with it the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789, into the standards of review. A 1974 reform allowed sixty deputies or senators to refer a bill for review before promulgation. In 2008 the introduction of the question prioritaire de constitutionnalité (QPC) allowed litigants in ordinary court cases to challenge the constitutionality of statutes already in force.

The Council also rules on the regularity of presidential and parliamentary elections and on referendums, and its decisions are binding on all public authorities and are not subject to appeal.

Other countries

Bodies bearing the name Constitutional Council have existed in countries including Algeria, Cambodia, Kazakhstan (until its replacement by a Constitutional Court in 2023), Lebanon, Morocco, Senegal, and Sri Lanka. Their composition and powers vary, but they typically combine constitutional review with electoral supervision.
