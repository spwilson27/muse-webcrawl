Fluid balance

Fluid balance is an aspect of the homeostasis of organisms in which the amount of water in the organism is controlled, via osmoregulation and behaviour, such that the concentrations of electrolytes (salts in solution) in the various body fluids are kept within healthy ranges. The core principle of fluid balance is that the amount of water lost from the body must equal the amount taken in.

Physiology

Water makes up roughly 50–60% of an adult human's body mass, distributed between intracellular fluid (about two-thirds) and extracellular fluid, which includes plasma and interstitial fluid. Daily intake comes from drinks, food and metabolic water, while losses occur in urine, faeces, sweat and insensible losses through the lungs and skin. The kidneys regulate water excretion under the control of antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin), released in response to osmolality changes detected by hypothalamic osmoreceptors; the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system regulates sodium, and therefore water, reabsorption. Thirst is the main behavioural mechanism maintaining intake.

Clinical significance

In medicine, fluid balance charts record a patient's intake and output to detect imbalance. Excess loss leads to dehydration and, if severe, hypovolaemic shock; excess retention causes oedema and, in heart failure or kidney failure, dangerous fluid overload. Disturbances of fluid balance accompany many illnesses, from diarrhoeal disease and burns to syndrome of inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH). Intravenous fluid therapy is used to replace losses and maintain balance in patients who cannot drink.
