Lignin

Lignin is a class of complex organic polymers that form key structural materials in the support tissues of most plants. It is the second most abundant biopolymer on Earth after cellulose.

Structure

Lignin is built mainly from three monolignols — p-coumaryl, coniferyl and sinapyl alcohols — cross-linked in a three-dimensional network. The proportions differ between softwoods, hardwoods and grasses.

Biological function

Deposited mainly in cell walls, lignin makes wood rigid, waterproofs the xylem vessels that conduct water, and resists microbial attack. Lignification was a key innovation in the evolution of land plants and tall trees.

Uses

Lignin is a by-product of pulp and paper manufacture, most of it burned for energy. It is also used in adhesives, dispersants, vanillin production and increasingly researched for bioplastics, carbon fibre and aromatic chemicals.

Degradation

White-rot fungi are the principal organisms able to mineralise lignin, using enzymes such as lignin peroxidase.
