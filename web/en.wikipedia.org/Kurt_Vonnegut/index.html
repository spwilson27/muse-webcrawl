Kurt Vonnegut

Kurt Vonnegut Jr. (November 11, 1922 – April 11, 2007) was an American writer and humorist known for his satirical novels blending science fiction, black comedy, and humanist morality. His best-known work, Slaughterhouse-Five (1969), drew on his experience as a prisoner of war who survived the bombing of Dresden. Over a fifty-year career he published fourteen novels, three short-story collections, five plays, and five nonfiction works.

Early life and war

Vonnegut was born in Indianapolis to a prominent German-American family hurt by Prohibition and the Depression. He enlisted in the U.S. Army in 1943, was captured during the Battle of the Bulge, and as a POW survived the firebombing of Dresden (February 1945) while sheltered in a slaughterhouse meat locker. The experience haunted his writing; his mother's suicide in 1944 also marked him deeply.

Career

After the war Vonnegut worked as a police reporter in Chicago, in public relations for General Electric, and taught at the Iowa Writers' Workshop. Early novels such as Player Piano (1952), The Sirens of Titan (1959), Mother Night (1961), and Cat's Cradle (1963) built a cult following. Slaughterhouse-Five — with its refrain "So it goes," the Tralfamadorians, and its anti-war stance embraced by the Vietnam generation — made him famous. Later works include Breakfast of Champions (1973), Jailbird (1979), Galápagos (1985), and Timequake (1997).

Views and later life

A lifelong humanist and honorary president of the American Humanist Association, Vonnegut campaigned against censorship (his books were frequently challenged in schools) and for arms control and environmental protection. He died in New York in 2007 from head injuries after a fall. His hometown of Indianapolis maintains the Kurt Vonnegut Museum and Library.
