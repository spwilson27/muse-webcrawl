Shaitan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shaitan (Arabic: شيطان, shayṭān, plural shayāṭīn) is an Arabic term for evil spirits in Islamic belief. In the Quran, the word is used both for a class of beings — devils who whisper temptations to humans and jinn — and, pre-eminently, for Iblis, the chief devil, who refused to bow before Adam and was cast out of paradise. The word is cognate with Satan in Hebrew and shares the root sense of "adversary" or "accuser."

In the Quran and tradition

The shayatin are described as created from smokeless fire, like the jinn, and as descendants or followers of Iblis. They incite sin by "whispering" (waswasa) into human hearts, a theme developed in the final chapter of the Quran, Surah An-Nas. Islamic tradition elaborates that devils are chained during Ramadan, that they flee from the call to prayer and from recitations of the Quran, and that individual humans are accompanied by a devil-companion (qarīn). Protection is sought by the formula aʿūdhu bi-llāhi min ash-shayṭāni r-rajīm ("I seek refuge in God from the accursed Satan").

In theology and folklore

Theologians debated the nature of the shayatin — whether they are fallen angels, a kind of jinn, or symbols of human inclination to evil (nafs). In folklore across the Islamic world, shaitans appear as tricksters and tempters in tales and manuscripts such as the Persian Book of Wonders. The term also entered other languages of the region and, in plural form, figures in the hadith lore of the "devils among men and jinn."
