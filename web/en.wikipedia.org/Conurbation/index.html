Conurbation

A conurbation is a region comprising a number of metropolises, cities, large towns and other urban areas which, through population growth and physical expansion, have merged to form one continuous built-up area. The term was coined by the Scottish planner Patrick Geddes in his book Cities in Evolution (1915) to describe the new urban regions then emerging in industrial Britain.

Characteristics

A conurbation is typically polycentric: it contains several historically distinct centres that retain separate identities, local governments and names, even though the built fabric between them is continuous. This distinguishes it from a single city that has simply grown outward from one centre, and from a metropolitan area, which is defined by commuting and economic ties and may include countryside between settlements.

Examples

Classic examples include the West Midlands conurbation around Birmingham, Greater Manchester and the other industrial regions of northern England; the Ruhr district in Germany, a polycentric region of many cities; the Dutch Randstad ring of Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht; and, at the largest scale, urban corridors such as the Boston–Washington "BosWash" megalopolis in the United States or Japan's Taiheiyō Belt from Tokyo to Fukuoka.

Planning implications

Conurbations pose distinctive governance problems, since transport, housing and environmental planning must be coordinated across many municipalities. Green belts were introduced around British conurbations from the mid-twentieth century to contain their outward spread and preserve open land between settlements.
