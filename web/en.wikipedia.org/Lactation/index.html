Lactation

Lactation is the secretion of milk from the mammary glands and the period of time that a mother lactates to feed her young. The process naturally occurs with all sexually mature female mammals and many other animals.

Lactation is a defining characteristic of the class Mammalia. In humans the process is also commonly called breastfeeding or nursing when it refers to a mother feeding her infant.

Physiology

Milk is produced in the alveoli of the mammary glands under the influence of the hormone prolactin, released from the anterior pituitary gland. Suckling stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, triggering prolactin release and also the release of oxytocin, which causes contraction of myoepithelial cells around the alveoli and ejection of milk — the let-down reflex.

Stages of milk

The first milk produced after birth is colostrum, a thick, yellowish fluid rich in antibodies and proteins. It is followed by transitional milk and then mature milk, which is thinner and higher in fat and lactose. Milk production is regulated by supply and demand: the more frequently milk is removed, the more is produced.

Evolution

Lactation is thought to have evolved in early synapsid ancestors of mammals, possibly beginning as a secretion that kept eggs moist and provided antimicrobial protection, before becoming a primary source of nutrition for offspring.
