Inertial navigation system

An inertial navigation system tracks position by dead reckoning from a known starting point.

Inertial navigation system
ComponentsAccelerometers, gyroscopes, computer
MethodDead reckoning via integration of acceleration and rotation
ApplicationsAircraft, spacecraft, missiles, submarines, ships
Key limitationDrift — position error grows over time

An inertial navigation system (INS) is a navigation device that uses motion sensors (accelerometers), rotation sensors (gyroscopes) and a computer to continuously calculate by dead reckoning the position, orientation, and velocity of a moving object without the need for external references. It is used on vehicles such as ships, aircraft, submarines, guided missiles, and spacecraft.

Principle

Once initialised with a known position and velocity, the INS measures acceleration along and rotation about three axes. Integrating acceleration once yields velocity; integrating again yields displacement, from which the new position is computed. Because errors in the sensors are also integrated, position error grows with time — a phenomenon called drift. High-grade systems drift as little as a fraction of a nautical mile per hour, while low-cost micro-electromechanical (MEMS) units may drift much faster and are usually aided by GPS or other references.

History

Inertial guidance was pioneered for rocketry in Germany during the Second World War (the V-2 rocket used gyroscopes and accelerometers for guidance) and developed extensively during the Cold War for ballistic missiles and nuclear submarines, which needed navigation that worked without surfacing or radio contact. Charles Stark Draper's laboratory at MIT was central to turning the theory into practical systems. Commercial airliners adopted INS in the late 1960s and 1970s before satellite navigation became common.

Types

Early systems mounted the sensors on a gimballed, gyro-stabilised platform kept aligned with a fixed frame of reference. Modern systems are usually "strapdown": sensors are fixed to the vehicle and their outputs are resolved computationally, which is cheaper, smaller and more reliable. Ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyroscopes have largely replaced spinning-mass gyros in high-performance systems.
