German dialects

German dialects are the regional and local varieties of the German language spoken across Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, Belgium, Italy (South Tyrol) and neighbouring areas. They form a dialect continuum with gradual transitions between neighbouring varieties, and they differ from Standard German (Hochdeutsch) in pronunciation, vocabulary and grammar — in many cases enough that speakers of distant dialects are not mutually intelligible without recourse to the standard language.

Classification

The traditional division rests on the High German consonant shift, whose isoglosses (notably the Benrath line, separating maken from machen) divide the language area into:

  • Low German (Plattdeutsch) in the north, unaffected by the consonant shift, including Low Saxon and Low Franconian varieties — the latter shading into Dutch.
  • Central German dialects in the middle of the country — including Ripuarian (around Cologne), Moselle Franconian, Hessian, Thuringian and Upper Saxon — partly affected by the shift.
  • Upper German dialects in the south — Alemannic (including Swiss German and Swabian), Bavarian (in Bavaria and Austria) and East Franconian — fully affected by the shift.

Finer classification uses further isoglosses, such as the Speyer line, and regional vocabulary recorded in dialect atlases descending from Georg Wenker's nineteenth-century surveys.

History and the standard language

Standard German emerged from Early New High German chancery languages and Martin Luther's Bible translation, and was spread by printing, schooling and, in the twentieth century, broadcasting, but it long coexisted with dialects used at home and locally. Yiddish developed from medieval High German dialects, and German emigration carried dialects overseas, producing varieties such as Pennsylvania Dutch and Texas German.

Present status

Since the mid-twentieth century, dialect use has receded under the influence of standard-language media, mobility and urbanisation; many northern and central dialects survive mainly among older speakers, while southern varieties, especially in Switzerland — where dialect dominates everyday speech alongside a standard used in writing — and parts of Austria and Bavaria, remain vigorous. Low German is recognised as a regional language under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages. Dialect literature, theatre, comedy and popular music, as well as regional identity politics, continue to sustain interest in the dialects.
