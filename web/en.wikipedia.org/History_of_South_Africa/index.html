The history of South Africa stretches from some of the earliest human fossils on earth, through Khoisan hunter-gatherers and herders and Bantu-speaking farming kingdoms such as Mapungubwe and Great Zimbabwe's trading world, to Dutch and British colonisation, the mineral revolution, apartheid and the democratic republic of 1994.

Colonisation and expansion

The Dutch East India Company founded a refreshment station at the Cape in 1652 under Jan van Riebeeck. Settlers (Boers) expanded inland, dispossessing Khoisan and clashing with Xhosa chiefdoms in a century of frontier wars. Britain took the Cape in 1795/1806; discontent with British rule — including the abolition of slavery in 1834 — drove the Great Trek, founding Boer republics in the interior. Diamonds (1867) and gold on the Witwatersrand (1886) transformed the economy and the stakes, culminating in the South African (Boer) War of 1899–1902, won by Britain at great civilian cost.

Union and apartheid

The Union of South Africa (1910) united the four colonies under white minority rule; the 1913 Natives Land Act reserved most land for whites. The National Party's 1948 victory institutionalised apartheid — racial classification, pass laws, forced removals and Bantustans. Resistance, led by the ANC (founded 1912) and figures such as Nelson Mandela, survived Sharpeville (1960), the Rivonia Trial, Soweto (1976) and states of emergency in the 1980s.

Democracy

F. W. de Klerk unbanned the ANC and freed Mandela in 1990. Negotiations produced the April 1994 election, won by the ANC; Mandela became president, and a Truth and Reconciliation Commission examined apartheid-era abuses. The post-1994 republic has grappled with inequality, unemployment, corruption (notably the Zuma-era "state capture") and service delivery, while remaining Africa's most industrialised economy.
