Animal nutrition

Animal nutrition
FieldNutritional science; animal science
FocusDietary needs of domestic and wild animals, especially in agriculture

Animal nutrition is the study of the dietary needs of animals, principally those in agriculture and food production, but also in zoos, aquaculture and wildlife management. It concerns the chemical composition of feeds, the digestion and metabolism of nutrients, and the formulation of diets that meet an animal's requirements for maintenance, growth, reproduction, lactation and work without excess or deficiency.

Nutrients

Animals require energy and about forty essential nutrients:

  • Water — the most critical nutrient, needed in the largest quantity.
  • Energy — supplied by carbohydrates, fats and protein; expressed as digestible, metabolisable or net energy.
  • Protein — providing amino acids, ten of which are essential for most species; ruminants obtain much of their protein from rumen microbes.
  • Fats and essential fatty acids — concentrated energy sources and carriers of fat-soluble vitamins.
  • Minerals — macrominerals (calcium, phosphorus, sodium, potassium, magnesium) and trace elements (iron, zinc, copper, selenium, iodine).
  • Vitamins — fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) and water-soluble (B-complex, C) groups.

Digestive strategies

Species differ greatly in digestive anatomy. Ruminants (cattle, sheep, goats) ferment fibrous feeds in the rumen with the help of microorganisms, enabling them to use cellulose and non-protein nitrogen. Hindgut fermenters such as horses use the caecum and colon. Pigs and poultry are monogastric omnivores fed largely on cereals and oilseed meals, while dogs and cats have shorter digestive tracts suited to more concentrated diets, cats being obligate carnivores with specific requirements such as taurine and arachidonic acid.

Feed evaluation and formulation

Feeds are assessed by proximate analysis (moisture, crude protein, ether extract, crude fibre, ash and nitrogen-free extract), digestibility trials and energy systems. Rations are formulated — commonly by least-cost computer programs — to meet nutrient requirements from ingredients such as grasses, silages, cereal grains, soybean meal, fish meal and mineral/vitamin premixes. Poor nutrition causes deficiency diseases (for example rickets from vitamin D or calcium imbalance) and production losses, while excess nutrients, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus, cause environmental pollution.
