Rotary kiln

A cement rotary kiln

A rotary kiln is a pyroprocessing device used to raise materials to a high temperature (calcination) in a continuous process. It consists of a long cylindrical steel shell, lined inside with refractory brick, mounted at a slight inclination (typically 1–4 degrees from horizontal) and rotated slowly about its axis on support rollers (tyres and trunnions). Material fed into the upper end gradually travels toward the lower end as the kiln rotates, while hot gases—usually from a burner firing into the lower end—flow counter-currently, driving off moisture and volatile matter and inducing chemical reactions.

Applications

The rotary kiln is indispensable to the cement industry, where raw meal of limestone and clay is calcined and sintered into clinker at material temperatures around 1,450 °C; cement kilns are among the largest pieces of moving industrial equipment in the world, up to about 6 metres in diameter and over 200 metres long. Rotary kilns are also used to produce lime and dolomite, calcine bauxite and alumina, roast ores, regenerate activated carbon, calcine petroleum coke, make lightweight aggregate, dry and heat aggregates, and incinerate hazardous and municipal wastes.

Operation

Residence time and temperature profile are controlled through rotation speed, slope, feed rate and burner output. The rolling bed of solids provides good mixing and gas–solid contact. Kilns are often fitted with preheater towers (cyclone stages) and precalciner vessels in modern cement plants to improve fuel efficiency, and with a rotary or grate cooler at the discharge end to recuperate heat from the product. Operation requires careful control of refractory condition, shell ovality and alignment, and of the formation of coating and rings inside the kiln.

History

The rotary kiln was patented for cement making in the 1880s (notably by Frederick Ransome in England) and commercialised around the turn of the 20th century, especially in the United States, where Thomas Edison's works developed very long kilns. It displaced batch kilns by enabling continuous, large-scale, uniform production.
