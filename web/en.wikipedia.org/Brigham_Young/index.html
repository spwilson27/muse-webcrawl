Brigham Young

Brigham Young (June 1, 1801 – August 29, 1877) was an American religious leader and politician. He was the second president of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS Church) from 1847 until his death, succeeding the church's founder, Joseph Smith. Young led the Mormon pioneers west across the Great Plains and founded Salt Lake City and the provisional State of Deseret, which later became the Utah Territory, where he served as its first governor.

The westward migration

After Joseph Smith's killing in 1844, Young prevailed in the succession crisis as president of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles. Facing violent expulsion from Nauvoo, Illinois, he organized the largest forced migration in American history, leading thousands of Latter-day Saints across the plains. In July 1847 his vanguard company reached the Salt Lake Valley, where, according to tradition, Young declared "this is the right place." Over the following decades he directed the founding of hundreds of settlements across the Intermountain West, from Idaho to Arizona and into Mexico and Canada, earning the epithet "the American Moses" and, from scholars, "a colonizer of extraordinary reach."

Governor and later years

Young served as governor of Utah Territory from 1851 to 1858, clashing with the federal government in the bloodless "Utah War" of 1857–58, during which the Mountain Meadows Massacre occurred. He championed plural marriage, which he practiced — he married dozens of wives and fathered 56 children — and institutional policies of his era, including a priesthood and temple ban on Black members, remain subjects of historical debate. He founded academies that became Brigham Young University and the University of Utah, promoted railroads and telegraph lines, and dominated Utah's political and economic life until his death in 1877.
