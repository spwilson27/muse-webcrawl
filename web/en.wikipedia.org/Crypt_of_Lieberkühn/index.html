Crypt of Lieberkühn

The crypts of Lieberkühn, also called intestinal crypts or intestinal glands, are pit-like tubular glands found in the mucosal lining of the small intestine and large intestine (colon). They are named after the German anatomist Johann Nathanael Lieberkühn, who described them in 1745. The crypts sit between the villi in the small intestine and extend down into the lamina propria toward the muscularis mucosae.

Structure

Each crypt is a test-tube-shaped invagination of the intestinal epithelium, roughly 0.3 to 0.5 millimetres deep in the small intestine. In the colon, where there are no villi, the crypts are longer and more densely packed, opening onto a flat luminal surface. The crypts enormously increase the secretory and regenerative surface area of the gut lining.

Cell types

The crypt epithelium contains several specialized cell types. Stem cells at the crypt base continually divide to renew the entire intestinal lining every few days. Paneth cells, also at the base of small-intestinal crypts, secrete antimicrobial substances such as lysozyme and defensins. Transit-amplifying cells, goblet cells, enteroendocrine cells, and absorptive enterocytes are found along the crypt walls as cells migrate upward toward the villus tip.

Function

The crypts secrete intestinal juice containing enzymes and mucus, provide the stem-cell niche from which the epithelium is constantly regenerated, and defend the gut through antimicrobial secretions. Disturbances of crypt architecture are important in pathology: crypt abscesses are a hallmark of ulcerative colitis, and mutations in crypt stem cells are implicated in the development of colorectal cancer.
