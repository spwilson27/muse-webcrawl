Honorific

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An honorific is a title that conveys esteem, courtesy, or respect for position or rank when used in addressing or referring to a person. In many languages and cultures, honorifics take the form of prefixes or suffixes added to a name (Mr., Dr., -san, -ji), distinct styles of address (Your Majesty, Your Honour, The Right Honourable), or entire grammatical systems of honorific speech that alter pronouns, verb forms, and vocabulary according to the relative social status of speaker, addressee, and referent.

Honorific speech systems

Some languages grammaticalise respect extensively. Japanese keigo distinguishes respectful (sonkeigo), humble (kenjōgo), and polite (teineigo) registers, with dedicated verb forms used according to hierarchy and in-group/out-group relations; Korean has comparable speech levels encoded in sentence endings. Javanese has stratified vocabularies (ngoko, madya, krama). Classical Nahuatl and many other languages likewise developed reverential registers. Even in languages without such systems, speakers signal deference through titles, forms of address, and the T–V distinction between familiar and polite pronouns found in French (tu/vous), German (du/Sie), and many other languages, historically including English (thou/you).

Types and examples

Common English honorifics include Mr., Mrs., Miss, and the gender-neutral Mx.; professional and academic titles such as Dr. and Professor; ecclesiastical styles (The Most Reverend, His Holiness); judicial and parliamentary styles (Your Honour, The Honourable); and royal styles (His Majesty, Her Royal Highness). Post-nominal letters (OBE, FRS, PhD), regnal numbers, and honorific nicknames such as "the Great" are related phenomena. Misusing an honorific — omitting it, applying the wrong rank, or using one sarcastically — can constitute a serious social affront, and style guides and protocol offices exist to govern their correct use.

Historical and social notes

Honorifics reflect and reinforce social hierarchy, so their use is often contested: egalitarian movements have promoted plain address (as among Quakers historically) or universal titles such as "citizen" during the French Revolution and "comrade" in socialist states. Feminist critique of Miss/Mrs. led to the adoption of Ms. in the twentieth century, and debates continue over gendered and professional titles in several languages.
