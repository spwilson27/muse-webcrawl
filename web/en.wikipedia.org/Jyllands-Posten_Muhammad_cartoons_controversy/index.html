Jyllands-Posten Muhammad cartoons controversy
Publication: September 30, 2005, Jyllands-Posten (Denmark)
Cartoons: 12 editorial cartoons of Muhammad
Consequences: Protests, boycotts, diplomatic crisis, riots

The Jyllands-Posten Muhammad cartoons controversy began after the Danish newspaper Jyllands-Posten published twelve editorial cartoons, most of which depicted the Islamic prophet Muhammad, on September 30, 2005. Culture editor Flemming Rose had commissioned the drawings to test what he described as growing self-censorship regarding Islam, following difficulties a children's-book author had finding illustrators willing to depict Muhammad.

Escalation

Danish Muslim organizations protested, and in late 2005 and early 2006 a delegation of Danish imams toured the Middle East presenting dossiers that included the cartoons alongside additional, more offensive images not published by the paper. The controversy then became an international crisis: ambassadors sought meetings with the Danish government, Danish goods were boycotted across the Muslim world, and protests — some violent — erupted in many countries in February 2006. Danish and Norwegian embassies in Damascus and Beirut were attacked and burned, churches and Christians were targeted in some countries, and well over one hundred deaths were reported in related unrest.

Aftermath and debate

The affair sparked a global debate on freedom of speech, blasphemy, and religious sensitivity. Cartoonist Kurt Westergaard, whose drawing of Muhammad with a bomb in his turban became the most reproduced image, survived an axe attack by an intruder at his home in 2010. Newspapers around the world variously reprinted the cartoons in solidarity or declined to do so. The controversy is frequently cited alongside later events, including the 2015 Charlie Hebdo shooting, in discussions of depictions of Muhammad and the limits of free expression.
