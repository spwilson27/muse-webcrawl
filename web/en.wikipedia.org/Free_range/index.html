Free range

Free-range hens with outdoor access

Free range denotes a method of farming husbandry where the animals, for at least part of the day, can roam freely outdoors, rather than being confined in an enclosure for 24 hours each day. On many farms, the outdoors ranging area is fenced, making it technically an enclosure; however, free-range systems usually offer the opportunity for extensive locomotion and sunlight that otherwise would be prevented by indoor housing systems. In practice, the outdoor ranging area may be as small as a fenced yard or as large as open rangeland or pasture.

Free range is a popular marketing claim for eggs and poultry, and is also applied to meat, milk, and other livestock products. The term is regulated differently — or loosely — in different jurisdictions, which leads to wide variation in what the label guarantees in practice.

Eggs and poultry

In the European Union, egg farming is classified as barn, free-range, and organic, in addition to caged systems; free-range hens must have continuous daytime access to open-air runs mainly covered with vegetation, with a maximum stocking density for the outdoor area, and the eggs carry the farming-method code "1". In the United States, the USDA requires only that free-range poultry have access to the outdoors; the duration, quality, and size of that access are not specified, so the label can apply even where the "access" is a small door to a limited porch area. Consumer and animal-welfare organisations therefore distinguish regulated free-range production from supplementary certifications (such as "pasture-raised" or certified humane labels) that demand more outdoor space per bird.

Advantages and disadvantages

Free-range systems allow animals to perform natural behaviours such as scratching, dust-bathing, foraging, and perching, and are generally considered to offer better animal welfare than intensive indoor or cage systems. Free-range eggs and meat may command a premium price. Disadvantages include greater exposure to predators, parasites, and diseases such as avian influenza, more variable production, higher land and labour costs, and — in laying hens — higher rates of keel-bone fractures and injurious pecking in some systems. Environmental impacts are mixed: more land is needed per animal, but outdoor systems avoid some costs of intensive housing.
