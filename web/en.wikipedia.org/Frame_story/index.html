Frame story

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A frame story (also called a frame tale or framing device) is a literary technique in which an introductory or enclosing narrative is used to set the stage for a main story or a series of stories. The frame provides a context — often a character telling a story to other characters — within which the embedded narratives are presented.

History and examples

The device is ancient: the Indian Panchatantra and the Sanskrit epics embed tales within tales, and the Mahabharata itself is presented as a recitation. The most famous frame story in world literature is One Thousand and One Nights, in which Scheherazade tells a new story each night to King Shahryar to postpone her execution, with many tales containing further framed stories inside them.

In European literature, Giovanni Boccaccio's Decameron (c. 1353) frames one hundred tales told by ten young Florentines fleeing the plague, and Geoffrey Chaucer's Canterbury Tales presents stories told by pilgrims on a journey. Later examples include Mary Shelley's Frankenstein, whose letters frame nested narrations, Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness, narrated by Marlow aboard a boat on the Thames, and Emily Brontë's Wuthering Heights, told largely through the housekeeper Nelly Dean to the visitor Lockwood.

Functions

Frame stories can unify otherwise unrelated tales, lend plausibility or distance to fantastic material, comment on the main narrative, and connect storytelling itself to the work's themes. Frames may be closed (the narrative returns to the frame at the end) or left open. The technique extends to film and television, where flashbacks and narrators' accounts commonly serve as frames.
