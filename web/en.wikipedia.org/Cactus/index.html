A cactus (plural cacti, cactuses, or cactus) is a member of the plant family Cactaceae, comprising about 127 genera with some 1,750 known species of the order Caryophyllales. The word "cactus" derives, through Latin, from the Ancient Greek káktos, a name originally used by Theophrastus for a spiny plant whose identity is now uncertain.

Nearly all cacti are native to the Americas, ranging from Patagonia in the south to parts of western Canada in the north; the sole exception is Rhipsalis baccifera, which also grows in Africa and Sri Lanka. Most cacti inhabit arid and semi-arid environments, though some are epiphytes of tropical rainforests.

Adaptations

Cacti show many adaptations to conserve water. Almost all are stem succulents, storing water in their thickened, fleshy stems, while their true leaves are typically reduced to spines. The spines grow from specialized structures called areoles, a defining feature of the family; they deter herbivores, provide shade, and trap moist air. Photosynthesis is carried out in the stems using crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM), in which stomata open at night to absorb carbon dioxide and close during the day to limit water loss — an adaptation shared with other desert plants.

Growth forms range from the towering columnar saguaro of the Sonoran Desert to tiny globular species only a centimetre across. Flowers are often large, showy, and short-lived; the night-blooming species are pollinated by bats and large moths.

Uses

Humans have used cacti for food, drink, medicine, and ornament for millennia. The prickly pear (Opuntia) produces edible fruits (tunas) and pads (nopales) that are staples of Mexican cuisine. Dragon fruit comes from species of Selenicereus (formerly Hylocereus). The peyote cactus (Lophophora williamsii) contains the psychoactive alkaloid mescaline and has been used in Indigenous religious ceremonies for thousands of years. Many species are grown worldwide as houseplants and in xeriscaping.

Conservation

Wild populations of many cacti are threatened by habitat loss and illegal collection for the horticultural trade. All cacti are included in the appendices of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES), and trade in most species requires permits.
