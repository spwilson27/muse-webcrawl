Pyroptosis is a highly inflammatory form of lytic programmed cell death that occurs most frequently upon infection with intracellular pathogens and is likely part of the antimicrobial response. The term was coined in 2001 by microbiologists Brad Cookson and Molly Brennan, from the Greek pyro (fire/fever) and ptosis (falling), to describe pro-inflammatory programmed cell death distinct from non-inflammatory apoptosis.

Mechanism

Pyroptosis is triggered when cytosolic pattern-recognition receptors assemble inflammasome complexes in response to pathogen-associated or damage-associated molecular patterns. Inflammasome activation recruits and activates caspase-1, which (i) processes the cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature, secreted forms, and (ii) cleaves gasdermin D. The N-terminal fragment of gasdermin D oligomerises in the plasma membrane to form large pores, causing cell swelling, membrane rupture and release of inflammatory contents. In the non-canonical pathway, human caspase-4 and caspase-5 (mouse caspase-11) sense intracellular lipopolysaccharide directly and cleave gasdermin D.

Biological role

By destroying the intracellular niche of bacteria such as Salmonella, Shigella and Legionella and by releasing potent cytokines, pyroptosis recruits neutrophils and promotes pathogen clearance. However, excessive pyroptosis drives pathology: it contributes to sepsis, inflammatory bowel disease, gout (via the NLRP3 inflammasome), atherosclerosis and neurodegenerative diseases, making inflammasome and gasdermin inhibitors targets of drug development. Despite superficial similarities, pyroptosis is mechanistically and morphologically distinct from apoptosis and from necroptosis.
