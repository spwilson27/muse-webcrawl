Weaving is a method of textile production in which two distinct sets of yarns or threads are interlaced at right angles to form a fabric or cloth. Other methods are knitting, crocheting, felting, and braiding or plaiting. The longitudinal threads are called the warp and the lateral threads are the weft (an old English word meaning "that which is woven"). The way the warp and filling threads interlace with each other is called the weave. The majority of woven products are created with one of three basic weaves: plain weave, satin weave, or twill.

Process

Weaving is done on a loom, which holds the warp threads under tension while the weft is passed through. The principal operations are shedding (raising selected warp threads to open a gap, the shed), picking (passing the weft, traditionally in a shuttle, through the shed), beating up (pushing the weft into place with a reed), and taking up and letting off (winding on the finished cloth and releasing more warp). Patterns are produced by varying which warp threads are lifted — controlled in hand looms by shafts and treadles, in Jacquard looms by punched cards, and in modern looms by electronic dobby or jacquard heads.

History

Weaving is among the oldest human crafts: woven textiles survive from Neolithic sites, and warp-weighted looms were used across ancient Europe, while Egypt, China, India and the Andes developed sophisticated weaving traditions in linen, silk, cotton and camelid fibres. Medieval guilds regulated cloth production in European towns; the putting-out system employed rural handloom weavers. The Industrial Revolution began in textiles: John Kay's flying shuttle (1733), the spinning machinery that fed the looms, and Edmund Cartwright's power loom (patented 1785) mechanised weaving in the mills of Lancashire and New England. The Jacquard loom (1804), controlled by punched cards, inspired later computing. Modern shuttleless looms — projectile, rapier, air-jet and water-jet — weave at speeds exceeding 1,000 weft insertions per minute, yet hand weaving persists in craft, art textiles and the production of goods such as Harris Tweed, which by law must be handwoven.
