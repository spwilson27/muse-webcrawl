Construction of the real numbers

The real numbers can be visualised as points on an infinitely long line.

In mathematics, there are several ways of defining the real numbers rigorously from simpler systems such as the rational numbers. These constructions of the real numbers were developed in the nineteenth century, when the need for a firm logical foundation for calculus became pressing. Each construction produces a complete ordered field, and all such fields are isomorphic, so the choice among them is a matter of convenience.

Dedekind cuts

Richard Dedekind, in 1872, defined a real number as a cut in the rational numbers: a partition of the rationals into two non-empty sets A and B such that every element of A is less than every element of B, and A contains no greatest element. Rational numbers themselves correspond to cuts in which A has a largest rational boundary, while numbers such as the square root of 2 correspond to cuts with no rational boundary. Arithmetic operations and order can be defined directly on cuts, and the crucial completeness property, that every bounded non-empty set has a least upper bound, follows from the construction.

Cauchy sequences

The approach associated with Georg Cantor defines a real number as an equivalence class of Cauchy sequences of rational numbers, where two sequences are equivalent if their difference tends to zero. Addition and multiplication are defined term by term. Charles Méray and Karl Weierstrass developed similar constructions independently in the same period.

Other approaches

Further constructions include infinite decimal expansions, nested intervals, and axiomatic treatments that simply postulate the real numbers as the unique complete ordered field. Modern set-theoretic developments have also produced alternative number systems, such as the hyperreal and surreal numbers, built on the same foundational impulse.
