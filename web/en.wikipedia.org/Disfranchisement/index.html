Disfranchisement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Disfranchisement, also called disenfranchisement, is the restriction of suffrage (the right to vote) of a person or group of people, or the revocation of rights more broadly. Disfranchisement may be accomplished explicitly by law, or implicitly through requirements applied in a discriminatory fashion, through intimidation, or by placing unreasonable requirements on voters seeking to cast a ballot.

Historical examples

Throughout history, suffrage was commonly restricted by sex, race, property ownership, literacy, religion and criminal conviction. In the United States, African Americans in the Southern states were effectively disfranchised after Reconstruction by means of poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses and racially exclusive primaries, a system largely dismantled by the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Women were disfranchised in most democracies until the suffragette and suffragist movements of the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Modern forms

Modern debates concern felon disfranchisement, which in many jurisdictions removes voting rights from people convicted of crimes, sometimes permanently; voter identification laws, which critics argue disproportionately burden minority and low-income voters; and the exclusion of non-citizen residents and younger citizens from the franchise. In some countries, persons under guardianship for mental incapacity also lose the vote.

The opposite process, extending voting rights to previously excluded groups, is known as enfranchisement.
