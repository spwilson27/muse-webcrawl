Women's suffrage in the United Kingdom

The movement for women's suffrage in the United Kingdom campaigned from the mid-19th century until women won the right to vote on equal terms with men in 1928. It combined constitutional campaigning by the suffragists of the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies (NUWSS), led by Millicent Fawcett, with the militant direct action of the suffragettes of the Women's Social and Political Union (WSPU), led by Emmeline Pankhurst and her daughters.

Early campaigns

Calls for women's votes went back to reform debates of the 1830s and were raised in Parliament by John Stuart Mill in 1867. Local suffrage societies merged into the NUWSS in 1897 and pursued petitions, lobbying, and election campaigning. Frustrated by decades of failure, the Pankhursts founded the WSPU in 1903 under the motto "Deeds, not words". From 1905 its members heckled ministers, chained themselves to railings, smashed windows, and committed arson; many were imprisoned, went on hunger strike, and were forcibly fed. Emily Davison died after running onto the track at the 1913 Derby.

Victory

During the First World War the suffrage organisations suspended militancy and women took on war work on a large scale. The Representation of the People Act 1918 enfranchised women over 30 who met a property qualification — about 8.4 million women — while abolishing most property qualifications for men. Women could sit in Parliament from the same year. The Representation of the People (Equal Franchise) Act 1928 finally gave women the vote on the same terms as men, at age 21.
