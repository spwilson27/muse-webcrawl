Gender neutrality in languages with gendered third-person pronouns

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

When a person is referred to with a third-person pronoun, in languages where pronouns encode gender (such as English he and she), a decision arises when the referent's gender is unknown, irrelevant, or non-binary, or where a generic statement is intended. Various solutions have been proposed and adopted, and the topic is related to gender-neutral language more broadly.

English

Historically, the masculine pronoun was prescribed as the default for mixed or unknown gender ("generic he"), a convention that came under criticism from the 1970s onward. Singular they — the use of they, them and their for a singular referent — is attested in English from the 14th century and is used by writers such as Shakespeare. It has become increasingly standard in style guides, dictionaries and everyday usage, and major dictionaries and publishers now accept it both for indefinite referents and for specific individuals who use they/them pronouns. Other approaches include alternating or pairing forms (he or she), rewriting to avoid pronouns altogether, and, historically, invented neologistic pronouns such as thon or the Spivak pronouns.

Other languages

Many languages have no gendered third-person pronouns at all (for example Turkish, Finnish, Hungarian, spoken Chinese and Persian), so no equivalent issue arises. In languages with strong gender systems, innovations have been debated: Swedish formally added the gender-neutral pronoun hen alongside han (he) and hon (she), and it entered the Swedish Academy's word list in 2015. In Romance languages such as Spanish, proposals involving the endings -e or the @ and x forms (latinx) have been promoted by some speakers and rejected by language academies such as the Real Academia Española. German-language debates concern the use of forms such as the gender star (Student*innen).

Debate

Supporters of gender-neutral pronouns argue that they avoid presuming gender, are more inclusive of women and non-binary people, and can be clearer than the generic masculine. Critics raise objections on grounds of grammatical tradition, clarity (number ambiguity with they), or opposition to mandated language change. Usage continues to evolve, and acceptability varies by register, region and institution.
