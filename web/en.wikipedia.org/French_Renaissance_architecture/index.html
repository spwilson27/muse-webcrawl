French Renaissance architecture

French Renaissance architecture is the name given to the French architecture of the period between roughly 1495 and the early 17th century, when Italian Renaissance forms introduced during the Italian Wars were adapted to French traditions, producing the celebrated châteaux of the Loire Valley.

Development

Charles VIII's invasion of Italy in 1494 brought French nobles and craftsmen into contact with Italian art, and Italian artists were invited to the French court. Early works such as the Château d'Amboise and the Château de Blois combined Gothic plans and steep roofs with Italian ornament. Under Francis I the style matured at Chambord (begun 1519), with its double-helix staircase, and at Fontainebleau, where Italian artists Rosso Fiorentino and Francesco Primaticcio formed the First School of Fontainebleau.

Later Renaissance

In the later 16th century, native architects such as Pierre Lescot (Louvre Cour Carrée), Philibert de l'Orme, Jean Bullant, and Jacques Androuet du Cerceau gave French classicism a more rigorous, national character. Henry IV's urban projects in Paris, including the Place des Vosges and the Pont Neuf, marked the transition toward Baroque and classical architecture.

Characteristics

French Renaissance buildings typically retain high pitched roofs, dormers, and tall chimneys from the Gothic tradition, while applying classical orders, symmetrical façades, pilasters, and Italianate decorative detail.
