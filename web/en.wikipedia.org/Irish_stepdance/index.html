Irish stepdance

Irish stepdance is a style of performance dance whose traditions developed in Ireland. It is characterized by a stiff upper body and quick, precise movements of the feet, performed either solo or in groups. Stepdance can be divided broadly into the highly formalized competitive style taught in dance schools worldwide and the older, improvisational sean-nós ("old style") tradition.

History

Irish dance masters travelled the countryside from the 18th century, teaching in villages and competing in showmanship. In 1893 the Gaelic League was founded to promote Irish culture, and it codified competitions called feiseanna (singular feis). The Irish Dancing Commission (CLRG) was established in 1927 to regulate teaching, adjudication and championships.

Technique and costume

Dancers keep the torso erect and arms held still at the sides, concentrating all movement in the legs and feet. Reels, slip jigs and light jigs are danced in soft leather shoes called ghillies or pumps; hornpipes, heavy jigs and modern treble reels use hard shoes fitted with fibreglass tips and heels that produce percussive rhythms. Competitive solo dresses are famously elaborate, with Celtic embroidery and, traditionally, curled wigs.

Competitions

Dancers progress through grades at feiseanna toward championship level. Major events include the World Irish Dancing Championships (Oireachtas Rince na Cruinne), first held in 1970, which attract thousands of competitors from Ireland and the global diaspora.

Global popularity

The 1994 Eurovision interval act Riverdance, choreographed by Michael Flatley and Jean Butler, expanded into a worldwide stage phenomenon with Lord of the Dance, bringing Irish stepdance to a mass global audience and swelling enrolments in dance schools far beyond Ireland.
