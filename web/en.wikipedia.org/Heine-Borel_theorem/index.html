Heine–Borel theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In real analysis, the Heine–Borel theorem, named after Eduard Heine and Émile Borel, states that a subset of Euclidean space is compact if and only if it is both closed and bounded. Equivalently, for a set of real numbers, compactness means that every open cover of the set has a finite subcover.

A subset S of Rn is compact  ⟺  S is closed and bounded.

Statement and history

For a subset of the real line, the theorem says that a set is compact precisely when it is a union of intervals that includes its endpoints and is contained within some finite bound. Heine first proved a version of the result in 1872 in the context of uniform continuity; Borel gave a proof for countable covers of an interval in 1895, and the general theorem took shape in the work of Cousin, Lebesgue and others around the turn of the twentieth century.

Significance

The theorem connects the topological notion of compactness with elementary geometric properties, and it underpins many central results of analysis: a continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains its maximum and minimum (the extreme value theorem) and is uniformly continuous. The equivalence fails in general metric and topological spaces, where compactness need not coincide with closedness and boundedness — one reason the covering definition of compactness is preferred in general topology.
