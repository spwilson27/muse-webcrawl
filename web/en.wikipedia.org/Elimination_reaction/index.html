Elimination reaction

An elimination reaction is a type of organic reaction in which two substituents are removed from a molecule in either a one-step or a two-step mechanism, typically forming a multiple bond such as the carbon–carbon double bond of an alkene. Elimination reactions are the reverse of addition reactions and often compete with substitution reactions.

Mechanisms

Three main mechanisms are recognised. In the E1 mechanism, ionisation first forms a carbocation and a leaving group; a base then removes a β-proton, and the reaction rate depends only on the concentration of substrate. In the E2 mechanism, bond breaking and bond forming occur in a single concerted step: a strong base abstracts the β-proton at the same time as the leaving group departs, giving second-order kinetics. The E1cB mechanism proceeds through a carbanion intermediate, the conjugate base of the substrate, formed by deprotonation before the leaving group is expelled.

Regioselectivity

When a choice of β-hydrogens exists, elimination may give several alkene products. According to Zaitsev's rule, the more substituted (more stable) alkene usually predominates under thermodynamic control; bulky bases or particular substrates instead favour the less substituted Hofmann product. The stereochemistry of E2 elimination generally requires the departing groups to be anti-periplanar.

Applications

Elimination reactions are widely used to prepare alkenes and alkynes, for example by dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides with alcoholic potassium hydroxide, dehydration of alcohols with concentrated acid, and in industrial cracking processes.
