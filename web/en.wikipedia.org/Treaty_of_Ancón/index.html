The Treaty of Ancón was a peace treaty signed by Chile and Peru on 20 October 1883 in the district of Ancón, near Lima. It formally ended the state of war between the two countries in the War of the Pacific (1879–1884) and restored relations, at the cost of major Peruvian territorial losses to Chile.

Background

By 1881 Chilean forces had occupied Lima after victories at Chorrillos and Miraflores, while Peru fragmented among rival governments. After the Peruvian defeat at Huamachuco in July 1883, the government of General Miguel Iglesias, recognised by Chile, opened negotiations. The treaty was signed for Peru by José Antonio de Lavalle and others, and for Chile by Jovino Novoa. Peru's Congress ratified it in 1884 amid continuing controversy, and the settlement contributed to the Peruvian civil war of 1884–1885 between Iglesias and Andrés Avelino Cáceres, who had rejected the territorial clauses.

Terms

Under the treaty, Peru ceded the littoral province of Tarapacá to Chile in perpetuity — a nitrate-rich territory whose loss stripped Peru of its principal source of export revenue. The provinces of Tacna and Arica were to remain under Chilean administration for ten years, after which a plebiscite would decide their sovereignty; Chile paid Peru an indemnity of 10 million pesos in connection with the arrangement. The plebiscite was never held, and the Tacna–Arica question poisoned relations between the two countries for nearly half a century.

Resolution and legacy

The dispute was finally settled by the Treaty of Lima in 1929, mediated under United States auspices: Tacna returned to Peru and Arica remained with Chile, with financial compensation and port facilities for Peru at Arica. The Treaty of Ancón remains a sensitive subject in Peruvian national memory, emblematic of the catastrophic cost of the War of the Pacific, which also cost Bolivia its coastline under the separate Treaty of Valparaíso (1884) and the Treaty of Peace and Friendship of 1904.
