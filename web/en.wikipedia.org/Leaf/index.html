Leaf

A leaf is a principal appendage of the stem of a vascular plant, usually borne laterally above ground and specialised for photosynthesis. Leaves are collectively called foliage, and together with the stem they form the shoot system. Leaves develop from leaf primordia in the shoot apical meristem and typically have a determinate growth pattern, a flattened shape that maximises light capture, and internal tissues arranged between upper and lower epidermal layers.

Morphology

A typical leaf consists of a broad, flat lamina (blade), often attached to the stem by a stalk called a petiole; in many plants small paired outgrowths called stipules occur at the petiole base. Veins containing xylem and phloem run through the blade and distribute water and sugars. Leaves are described as simple, with a single undivided blade, or compound, in which the blade is divided into leaflets. Their arrangement on the stem (phyllotaxis) may be alternate, opposite, or whorled.

Anatomy and function

Inside the leaf, the mesophyll contains chloroplast-rich cells where photosynthesis occurs: an upper palisade layer of columnar cells and a lower spongy layer with air spaces facilitating gas exchange. The epidermis is covered by a waxy cuticle and pierced by stomata—pores flanked by guard cells that regulate the uptake of carbon dioxide and the loss of water vapour in transpiration.

Adaptations and variation

Leaf form varies enormously with environment: needles and scales in conifers reduce water loss; succulent leaves store water; tendrils, spines, bracts, and the traps of carnivorous plants are all modified leaves. Many temperate trees shed their leaves seasonally (deciduous habit), and autumn colour results from the breakdown of chlorophyll revealing carotenoid and anthocyanin pigments.
