Defamiliarization

Defamiliarization or ostranenie (Russian: остранение) is an artistic technique of presenting familiar things in an unfamiliar or strange way so that the audience experiences them freshly rather than perceiving them automatically. The concept was introduced in 1917 by the Russian formalist Viktor Shklovsky in his essay "Art as Technique".

Shklovsky's theory

Shklovsky argued that habitual perception makes objects "disappear" — we recognise rather than see them. The purpose of art is to restore the sensation of things, "to make the stone stony", by impeding and prolonging perception through unusual description, shifts of perspective, and strange language. He illustrated the technique with examples from Tolstoy, who narrates from a horse's point of view in "Kholstomer" and describes ordinary actions as if seen for the first time.

Influence

Defamiliarization became a central concept of Russian formalism and influenced later literary theory, as well as Bertolt Brecht's "alienation effect" (Verfremdungseffekt) in theatre, which similarly sought to prevent passive identification and provoke critical reflection.
