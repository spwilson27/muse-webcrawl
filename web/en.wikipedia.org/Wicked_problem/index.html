Wicked problem

In planning and policy, a wicked problem is a problem that is difficult or impossible to solve because of incomplete, contradictory, and changing requirements that are often difficult to recognize.

Origin of the term

The term was introduced by design theorists Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber in 1973, contrasting "wicked" problems in social policy planning with the "tame" problems of mathematics and engineering, which have clear formulations and testable solutions.

The ten properties

Rittel and Webber listed ten characteristic properties, including: there is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem; wicked problems have no stopping rule; solutions are not true-or-false but good-or-bad; every solution attempt counts significantly; and every wicked problem is a symptom of another problem.

Examples

Climate change, poverty, crime, healthcare and education policy are frequently cited as wicked problems, because interventions interact with economic, political and cultural systems in unpredictable ways.

Approaches

Suggested strategies include involving stakeholders in framing the problem, iterative prototyping, systems thinking, and accepting provisional, revisable interventions rather than final solutions.
