Scrum (software development)

Scrum is an agile team collaboration framework commonly used in software development, although it has also been applied to other fields including research, sales, marketing and advanced technologies. It is designed for teams of ten or fewer members who break their work into goals that can be completed within time-boxed iterations, called sprints — most commonly two weeks, and no longer than one month.

The term "scrum" comes from rugby, where it denotes a formation used to restart play. Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka introduced the analogy in a 1986 Harvard Business Review article comparing high-performing product development teams to rugby squads. Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber developed the framework in the early 1990s and co-authored the Agile Manifesto in 2001.

How it works

A Product Owner orders the work in a product backlog. The Scrum team turns a selection of that work into an increment of value during a sprint. The team and its stakeholders inspect the results in the sprint review and adjust for the next sprint in the retrospective. A Scrum Master helps the team follow the framework and removes impediments. The brief daily scrum meeting synchronizes the team and surfaces obstacles.

Values and theory

Scrum is founded on empiricism — knowledge comes from experience and decisions from what is observed — and lean thinking. Its five values are commitment, focus, openness, respect, and courage. The framework's three pillars are transparency, inspection, and adaptation.

Usage and criticism

Scrum is the most widely used agile framework in industry surveys. Critics note that it is frequently implemented as rigid ritual ("cargo cult agile"), that estimation practices can become counterproductive, and that the framework says little about technical engineering practices, which are often supplied by complementary methods such as extreme programming.
