Welsh language

Welsh (Welsh: Cymraeg) is a Celtic language of the Brittonic subgroup spoken natively in Wales, by some in England, and in Y Wladfa, the Welsh settlement in Patagonia, Argentina. It is the most widely spoken Celtic language and the only one not classified as endangered by UNESCO. Historically it has also been known in English as "British" and "Cambrian."

Status

Welsh declined for centuries under legal and social pressure — the Laws in Wales Acts of 1535 and 1542 required English in administration — but revived after a long campaign. The Welsh Language Act 1993 and the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011 gave Welsh official status in Wales. The 2021 census recorded about 538,000 speakers, roughly 18% of the population, concentrated in the north and west. The Welsh Government's Cymraeg 2050 strategy aims for a million speakers.

Features

Welsh is written in a Latin alphabet of 28 letters including the digraphs ch, dd, ff, ng, ll, ph, rh and th; the letter ll represents a voiceless lateral fricative. The language is known for its initial consonant mutations, verb-initial (VSO) word order, and inflected prepositions.

Media and education

Welsh-medium education is widespread, the television channel S4C broadcasts in Welsh, and the National Eisteddfod celebrates Welsh-language culture each year.
