The hippocampus (from Greek ἱππόκαμπος, "seahorse", after its curved shape) is a major component of the brain of humans and other vertebrates. Humans have two hippocampi, one in each cerebral hemisphere, embedded in the medial temporal lobe. It belongs to the limbic system and plays essential roles in the consolidation of information from short-term memory to long-term memory and in spatial memory that enables navigation.

Role in memory

The importance of the hippocampus was established by the famous case of patient H.M. (Henry Molaison), who after bilateral medial temporal lobe resection in 1953 lost the ability to form new declarative memories while retaining older ones and normal procedural learning. Subsequent research showed the hippocampus is critical for forming episodic and declarative memories and for binding the elements of an experience into a coherent representation. During sleep, hippocampal "replay" of waking activity is thought to drive systems consolidation into the neocortex.

Spatial navigation

Place cells in the hippocampus, discovered by John O'Keefe in 1971, fire when an animal occupies a specific location; together with grid cells in the entorhinal cortex (characterised by May-Britt and Edvard Moser) they form the brain's positioning system, recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Enlargement of the posterior hippocampus in London taxi drivers is a well-known example of experience-dependent plasticity.

Anatomy

The hippocampal formation includes the dentate gyrus, the cornu ammonis fields CA1–CA4, and the subiculum. Information flows largely in a circuit: entorhinal cortex → dentate gyrus → CA3 (via mossy fibres) → CA1 (via Schaffer collaterals) → subiculum → entorhinal cortex. The CA3–CA1 synapse is a classic site for studying long-term potentiation (LTP), a cellular model of learning.

Clinical significance

The hippocampus is among the first regions damaged in Alzheimer's disease, producing early memory loss; hippocampal volume measured by MRI is a biomarker in the disease. It is also affected in temporal lobe epilepsy (hippocampal sclerosis), depression, PTSD and schizophrenia, and is unusually vulnerable to stress hormones (glucocorticoids) and to oxygen deprivation.
