Battle of Brunanburh
Date: 937
Location: Northern England (site debated)
Result: Anglo-Saxon victory

The Battle of Brunanburh was fought in 937 between the army of Æthelstan, king of the English, and an alliance of Olaf Guthfrithson, king of Dublin; Constantine II, king of Alba; and the kings of Strathclyde. The English victory confirmed Æthelstan's rule over a unified England and is celebrated in a famous Old English poem preserved in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle.

Background

Æthelstan had conquered Viking York in 927 and received the submission of the northern kings at Eamont in the same year, but after his overlordship was challenged, Olaf Guthfrithson invaded in alliance with Constantine and the Strathclyde Britons in 937, sailing into the Humber according to some accounts, or landing on the north-west coast according to others. The location of "Brunanburh" remains unidentified; the leading modern candidate is Bromborough on the Wirral.

The battle

The Chronicle poem describes a day-long slaughter: the West Saxons and Mercians fought from dawn to dusk, killing five young kings and seven of Olaf's earls; Constantine lost a son and fled north, while Olaf escaped by ship to Dublin with a remnant. Æthelstan and his brother Edmund returned in triumph to Wessex.

Legacy

Historians often call Brunanburh one of the most important battles in English history, forestalling the break-up of the new English kingdom. The poem is among the best-known pieces of Old English verse, and the battle features in later literature, including a translation of the poem by Tennyson and a story by Jorge Luis Borges.
