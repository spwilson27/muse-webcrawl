SMAD (protein)

SMADs are a family of intracellular proteins that transduce signals from transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) superfamily receptors at the cell surface to the nucleus, where they regulate gene expression. They are the principal signal transducers of TGF-β, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) and activin pathways, controlling cell proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis and embryonic development.

Name and discovery

The name SMAD is a portmanteau of the founding members: the Caenorhabditis elegans gene sma ("small" body size) and the Drosophila gene Mad ("Mothers against decapentaplegic"), whose vertebrate homologues were identified in the mid-1990s.

Classes

Eight SMAD proteins are known in humans, in three functional classes:

Receptor-regulated SMADs (R-SMADs) — SMAD1, SMAD2, SMAD3, SMAD5 and SMAD8/9 — are phosphorylated directly by activated type I receptors. SMAD2 and SMAD3 mediate TGF-β/activin signals; SMAD1, 5 and 8/9 mediate BMP signals.

Common-mediator SMAD (Co-SMAD) — SMAD4 — partners with phosphorylated R-SMADs; the complex accumulates in the nucleus and regulates target genes. Loss of SMAD4 (also called DPC4, "deleted in pancreatic cancer") is a frequent event in pancreatic and colorectal cancers.

Inhibitory SMADs (I-SMADs) — SMAD6 and SMAD7 — antagonise signalling, competing for receptor binding and targeting receptors for degradation.

Structure and mechanism

R-SMADs and SMAD4 contain conserved MH1 (DNA-binding) and MH2 (protein-interaction) domains joined by a linker region. Receptor phosphorylation of the C-terminal SSXS motif of an R-SMAD triggers complex formation with SMAD4, nuclear translocation, and cooperative binding with other transcription factors at regulatory elements. Mutations in SMAD genes cause developmental disorders and hereditary cancer syndromes, such as juvenile polyposis syndrome.
