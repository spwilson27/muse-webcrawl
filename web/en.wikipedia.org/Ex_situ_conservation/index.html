Ex situ conservation

Ex situ conservation (Latin for "off-site conservation") is the process of protecting an endangered species, variety, or strain of organism outside its natural habitat — for example, by removing part of the population from a threatened habitat and placing it in a new location, such as a zoo, botanical garden, seed bank, or captive breeding facility. It complements in situ conservation, the protection of species in their natural surroundings.

Methods

  • Seed banks store plant seeds under cold, dry conditions; the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway holds backup copies of collections from around the world, and the Millennium Seed Bank in England aims to bank seed from a large share of the world's flora.
  • Botanical gardens and arboreta maintain living plant collections and tissue cultures.
  • Zoos, aquariums, and captive breeding programs maintain animal populations for potential reintroduction, as with the Arabian oryx, California condor, and golden lion tamarin.
  • Gene banks and cryopreservation preserve sperm, eggs, embryos, and DNA; "frozen zoos" store genetic material from endangered species.

Advantages and limitations

Ex situ methods provide insurance against extinction in the wild, allow controlled breeding that manages genetic diversity, and support research and public education. However, captive populations can suffer inbreeding and adaptation to captivity, collections are expensive to maintain, and stored crop varieties may lose associated traditional knowledge. Conservationists generally treat ex situ conservation as a backup to — not a substitute for — habitat protection.
