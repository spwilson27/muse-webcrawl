Slavery in the Ottoman Empire

Slavery in the Ottoman Empire was a major institution and a significant part of the Empire's economy and society from the fourteenth century into the early twentieth century. Slaves were drawn from warfare, raids, and trade networks reaching into sub-Saharan Africa, the Caucasus, the Black Sea steppe and Europe. Under Islamic law, free Muslims could not be enslaved, so most slaves were non-Muslims captured or purchased abroad; manumission was religiously encouraged, and the children of slaves by free fathers were generally born free.

Sources of slaves

For centuries the Crimean Khanate supplied the empire with slaves from the steppe through raids into Poland-Lithuania, Muscovy and the Caucasus, shipped through Caffa and sold in Istanbul. Corsairs based in North Africa captured Europeans in the Mediterranean and raided coastal settlements as far as Iceland. In the south, slaves came across the Sahara and up the Nile from Sudan and Ethiopia, and through Red Sea ports. Circassian and Georgian slaves from the Caucasus were prized for military households and harems, while African slaves were heavily used in domestic service, agriculture in some regions, and as eunuchs.

The devşirme and the kul system

A distinctive institution was the devşirme, a periodic levy of Christian boys from the Balkans, who were converted to Islam, educated, and recruited into the sultan's household service — the kul system — supplying the Janissary infantry and much of the imperial administration. Although formally the sultan's slaves, kul officials could rise to the rank of grand vizier and acquire great wealth and power, which makes Ottoman "slavery" of this type very different from chattel slavery in the Americas.

Harem and domestic slavery

Female slaves, obtained chiefly from the Caucasus and Africa, staffed imperial and elite harems as servants and concubines; the most powerful, such as the valide sultan (the sultan's mother), could exercise considerable political influence. Eunuchs — castrated male slaves, white eunuchs generally of European or Caucasian origin and black eunuchs of African origin — guarded harems and administered palace and religious institutions, including the chief black eunuch's supervision of the holy cities' endowments.

Abolition

The trade was restricted in stages under European pressure and Tanzimat reforms: the Istanbul slave market was closed in 1847, the African slave trade was prohibited by firman in 1857, and trade in Circassians was banned in the 1880s, though enforcement was uneven and domestic slavery persisted into the twentieth century. The Young Turk government abolished slavery in practice after 1908, and the institution faded with the empire's dissolution.
