Stream of consciousness

Stream of consciousness is a narrative mode or method that attempts to depict the multitudinous thoughts and feelings that pass through the mind of a narrator or character. The term was coined by the philosopher and psychologist William James in his The Principles of Psychology (1890), where he wrote that consciousness "does not appear to itself as chopped up in bits... it flows," like a river or a stream. The literary critic May Sinclair first applied the phrase to literature in 1918, reviewing the novels of Dorothy Richardson.

In literature

Stream of consciousness became a hallmark of literary modernism. James Joyce's Ulysses (1922) is its most celebrated monument, following the inner lives of Leopold Bloom and Stephen Dedalus through a single Dublin day and culminating in Molly Bloom's unpunctuated soliloquy. Virginia Woolf developed the technique in Mrs Dalloway (1925) and To the Lighthouse (1927), dissolving external plot into the drift of perception and memory. William Faulkner's The Sound and the Fury (1929) opens with the fractured consciousness of Benjy Compson. Earlier antecedents include the interior monologues of Édouard Dujardin's Les Lauriers sont coupés (1888), which Joyce acknowledged as an influence, and passages of Tolstoy and Dostoevsky.

Techniques

Writers employ devices such as interior monologue, free indirect discourse, fragmented syntax, associative leaps, repetition, and the suppression of punctuation or narrative framing to simulate the mind's non-linear movement. The technique conveys sensory impressions, half-formed thoughts and memories in the order in which they occur, rather than in a logical sequence.

In psychology

In psychology, the stream of consciousness refers to the continuous flow of subjective experience — sensations, thoughts, feelings and memories — studied by introspectionist schools and, more recently, by research on mind-wandering and default-mode brain activity.
