Mataram Kingdom

The Mataram Kingdom (also called Medang or the Ancient Mataram Kingdom) was a Javanese Hindu-Buddhist kingdom flourishing between the 8th and 11th centuries in what is now Central Java and East Java, Indonesia. Founded by King Sanjaya around 732 CE, Mataram is renowned for building some of the greatest monuments of Southeast Asia, including the Mahayana Buddhist temple of Borobudur and the Shaivite temple complex of Prambanan. The kingdom's history unfolded as a rivalry and interplay between the Buddhist Sailendra dynasty and the Shaivite Sanjaya dynasty.

History

Foundation and the two dynasties

The Canggal inscription of 732 CE records Sanjaya erecting a lingam for Shiva on the hill of Gunung Wukir. In the late 8th century the Buddhist Sailendra family rose to prominence, building Borobudur (c. 780–825) and dominating maritime trade through links with Srivijaya. The Sanjaya line reasserted itself under Rakai Pikatan, whose marriage to the Sailendra princess Pramodhawardhani symbolised reconciliation; his reign produced Prambanan (c. 850), among the largest Hindu temple complexes in Southeast Asia.

The move east and decline

Around 929 CE King Mpu Sindok shifted the court to East Java — possibly prompted by a catastrophic eruption of Mount Meru (Merapi) that buried temples in Central Java. The eastern kingdom, ruled by the Isyana dynasty, became a trading power, but in 1016 the capital was destroyed in an attack by the vassal state of Wurawari, allied with Srivijaya, and King Dharmawangsa was killed. The survivor Airlangga later reunified the realm as Kahuripan.

Culture and architecture

Mataram's courts produced masterpieces of stone architecture and sculpture and the earliest flowering of Old Javanese literature: the Kakawin Ramayana, composed in the 9th century, adapted the Indian epic to Javanese kingship. Wet-rice agriculture on Java's volcanic soils, organised around the royal court and temple estates, underpinned the kingdom's wealth.

Legacy

Later Javanese states invoked Mataram's name for legitimacy — most notably the Islamic Mataram Sultanate of the 16th–18th centuries, an unrelated state that ruled from the same heartland near modern Yogyakarta and Surakarta. Borobudur and Prambanan, restored in the 20th century, are now UNESCO World Heritage Sites and symbols of Indonesia.
