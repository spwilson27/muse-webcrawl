Peer review

Peer review is the evaluation of work by one or more people with similar competences as the producers of the work (peers). It functions as a form of self-regulation by qualified members of a profession within the relevant field. In academic publishing, peer review is used by journals to assess a manuscript's suitability for publication and to improve its quality.

Process

An author submits a manuscript to a journal editor, who sends it to (usually two or three) anonymous expert referees. Referees report on originality, validity, and significance and recommend acceptance, revision, or rejection. Review may be single-blind (referees know the authors), double-blind (neither side knows the other), or open (identities and reports are public).

History

Forms of editorial review date to the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society in the 17th century, but systematic external peer review became standard in scientific journals mainly after the Second World War, spreading with the growth of research funding and grant review by agencies such as the US National Institutes of Health.

Criticism and reform

Peer review has been criticised for slowness, conservatism, bias, inconsistency between referees, and failure to detect fraud or serious error. Because reviewers are unpaid and the volume of submissions has grown, journals report increasing difficulty in recruiting referees. Reform proposals include open review, post-publication review, registered reports, and preprint servers such as arXiv, which decouple dissemination from journal certification.

Other uses

Peer review is also used in medicine (review of clinical practice), engineering, software development (code review), and professional accounting.
