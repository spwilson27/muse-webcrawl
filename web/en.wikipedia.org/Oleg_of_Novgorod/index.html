Oleg (Old East Slavic: Ѡлегъ; Old Norse: Helgi), known as Oleg the Prophet, was a Varangian prince who ruled the Rus' from Novgorod and later Kiev in the late 9th and early 10th centuries. According to the Primary Chronicle, he was a relative and successor of Rurik, acting as regent for Rurik's young son Igor.

Conquest of Kiev

In 882, according to the chronicle, Oleg led a force down the Dnieper, capturing Smolensk and Lyubech, and then seized Kiev by trickery: he lured its rulers Askold and Dir out of the city and had them killed, declaring Kiev "the mother of Rus' cities." This act is traditionally taken to mark the founding of the unified Kievan Rus' state, linking the northern trade route from Novgorod to the Black Sea.

Campaign against Constantinople

In 907 Oleg is said to have led a great naval expedition against Constantinople, the Byzantine capital. The chronicle relates that the Greeks chained shut the Golden Horn, so Oleg's men put their boats on wheels and sailed overland to the city walls; the terrified Byzantines sued for peace. The resulting treaty of 907, expanded in 911, granted the Rus' favorable trading rights in the empire. Some historians doubt the 907 campaign occurred as described, but the 911 treaty text preserved in the chronicle is generally regarded as authentic.

Death and legend

The chronicle gives Oleg the epithet "the Prophet" from a famous legend: he was told by pagan priests that his favorite horse would cause his death, so he had the horse sent away. Years later, learning the horse had died, he visited its bones and mocked the prophecy — whereupon a snake slithered from the skull, bit him, and he died. The tale was immortalized in Alexander Pushkin's ballad "The Song of the Prophet Oleg" (1822).
