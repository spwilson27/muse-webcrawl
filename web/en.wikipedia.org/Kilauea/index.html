Kilauea

Kīlauea (Hawaiian: "spewing" or "much spreading") is an active shield volcano in the Hawaiian Islands, located on the southeastern coast of the island of Hawai'i within Hawai'i Volcanoes National Park. Rising 1,247 metres above sea level, it is one of the most active volcanoes on Earth and has been erupting frequently for decades.

Geology

Kilauea is the second-youngest product of the Hawaiian hotspot and the current eruptive centre of the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain. Its summit features a large caldera, about 4 by 3.2 kilometres, containing the Halema'uma'u crater, in Hawaiian tradition the home of the volcano goddess Pele.

Eruptive history

The volcano's longest modern eruption, from Pu'u 'Ō'ō on its east rift zone, lasted from 1983 to 2018 and covered vast areas with lava, destroyed the communities of Kalapana and Kapoho-area settlements, and added new land to the island. In 2018 a major lower East Rift Zone eruption destroyed more than 700 homes and was followed by a dramatic collapse of the summit caldera. Subsequent summit eruptions in 2020–2021, 2021–2022, and 2023 refilled Halema'uma'u with lava lakes.

Monitoring

Kilauea is monitored continuously by the United States Geological Survey's Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, founded in 1912 atop the caldera rim. Scientists track earthquakes, ground deformation, and gas emissions to forecast eruptive activity that threatens nearby communities in the Puna and Ka'ū districts.
