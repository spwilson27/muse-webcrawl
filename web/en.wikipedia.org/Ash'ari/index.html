Ash'ari

Ash'arism (Arabic: الأشعرية, al-Ashʿariyya) is a school of theology in Sunni Islam named after its founder, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (c. 874–936 CE). It became, alongside Maturidism, one of the two principal orthodox schools of Sunni theology (kalam).

Ash'ari theology sought a middle path between pure rationalism and strict literalism.

History

Al-Ash'ari was originally a follower of the Mu'tazilite school but broke with his teacher al-Jubba'i around the age of forty, after a famous disputation concerning God's justice toward three brothers who died in different states. He then developed a theology that affirmed the attributes of God "without asking how" (bila kayf) while still using rational argumentation to defend orthodox doctrine.

Doctrine

Ash'ari theology holds that human reason cannot on its own determine good and evil, which are known through revelation. It defends the doctrine of kasb (acquisition), according to which God creates human acts while humans "acquire" them, reconciling divine omnipotence with moral responsibility. The school also affirms that the Qur'an is God's uncreated speech and that God's attributes are real yet unlike those of creatures.

Influence

Developed further by thinkers such as al-Baqillani, al-Juwayni, and al-Ghazali, Ash'arism became the dominant theological orientation of the Shafi'i and Maliki legal schools and remains influential in much of the Sunni world today.
