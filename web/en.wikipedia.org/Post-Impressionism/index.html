Post-Impressionism

Post-Impressionism (also spelled Postimpressionism) is a predominantly French art movement that developed roughly between 1886 and 1905, from the last Impressionist exhibition to the birth of Fauvism. Post-Impressionism emerged as a reaction against Impressionists' concern for the naturalistic depiction of light and colour. Its broad aims were to extend Impressionism while rejecting its limitations: the artists continued using vivid colours and real-life subject matter, but were more inclined to emphasize geometric forms, distort form for expressive effect, and use unnatural or high-key arbitrary colours.

Term and origin

The term was coined by the British artist and critic Roger Fry for the 1910 exhibition "Manet and the Post-Impressionists" at the Grafton Galleries in London, which introduced the work of Cézanne, Van Gogh, and Gauguin to the British public, causing a sensation.

Styles within the movement

Post-Impressionism was not a single style. Georges Seurat and Paul Signac developed Pointillism (Neo-Impressionism), building images from distinct dots of pure colour. Cézanne pursued a structured, almost architectural reconstruction of nature; Gauguin moved towards Symbolism and Synthetism; Van Gogh developed his expressive, swirling brushwork saturated with emotion.

Legacy

The Post-Impressionists laid the foundations of much of 20th-century modern art, opening the way to Fauvism, Cubism, and abstraction.
