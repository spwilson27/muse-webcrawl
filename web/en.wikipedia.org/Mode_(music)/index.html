Mode (music)


Image placeholder: the seven diatonic modes

In the theory of Western music, a mode is a type of musical scale coupled with a set of characteristic melodic behaviours. Since the 18th century the term has most often been used for the seven diatonic modes, the rotations of the major scale, each beginning on a different degree: Ionian (the major scale itself), Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian (the natural minor scale), and Locrian.

The diatonic modes

On white keys of the piano, starting from C gives Ionian (C–C), from D Dorian (D–D), from E Phrygian, from F Lydian, from G Mixolydian, from A Aeolian, and from B Locrian. Each mode has a distinctive character: Dorian is a minor mode with a raised sixth; Lydian is a bright major mode with a sharpened fourth; Mixolydian resembles the major scale with a flattened seventh and dominates much rock and folk music; Locrian, with its diminished tonic triad, is rarely used as a tonal centre. Jazz theory assigns modes to chord-scales for improvisation, an approach codified in George Russell's Lydian Chromatic Concept (1953) and heard on Miles Davis's modal-jazz landmark Kind of Blue (1959).

Earlier and wider meanings

In medieval music, the eight church modes (four authentic and four plagal) governed Gregorian chant classification, codified by theorists such as Guido d'Arezzo. Heinrich Glarean's Dodecachordon (1547) expanded the system to twelve modes. In other traditions, concepts analogous to mode include the Indian raga, the Arabic maqam, and the Persian dastgah, each of which specifies both a scale and rules of melodic development.
