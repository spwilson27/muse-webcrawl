Wiener–Khinchin theorem

The Wiener–Khinchin theorem (also known as the Wiener–Khintchine theorem and sometimes the Wiener–Khinchin–Einstein theorem) states that the autocorrelation function of a wide-sense stationary random process has a spectral decomposition given by the Fourier transform of its power spectral density. In other words, the power spectrum and the autocorrelation function form a Fourier transform pair.

Statement

For a wide-sense stationary process x(t) with autocorrelation function Rxx(τ) and power spectral density Sxx(f), the theorem states that Sxx(f) is the Fourier transform of Rxx(τ), and conversely the autocorrelation is the inverse Fourier transform of the power spectral density. A practical consequence is that the total power of the process equals the integral of its power spectrum.

History

Norbert Wiener proved the result for deterministic functions in 1930, and Aleksandr Khinchin extended it to stationary stochastic processes in 1934. Albert Einstein had sketched an informal version of the idea in a short 1914 paper, which went largely unnoticed until later.

Applications

The theorem underlies modern spectral estimation in signal processing, since power spectra can be estimated by Fourier-transforming measured autocorrelation functions (the correlogram method). It is also fundamental in statistical physics, where it connects time correlations of fluctuating quantities to measurable spectra, as in the analysis of Brownian motion and noise in electronic devices.
