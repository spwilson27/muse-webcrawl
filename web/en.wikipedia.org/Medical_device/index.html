Medical device

A medical device is any device intended to be used for medical purposes. Significant potential for hazards are inherent when using a medical device for medical purposes and, thus, medical devices must be proved safe and effective with reasonable assurance before regulating governments allow marketing of the device in their country. As a general rule, as the associated risk of the device increases, the amount of testing required to establish safety and efficacy also increases.

Medical devices range from simple tongue depressors to implantable pacemakers.

Definition and scope

Medical devices range from simple low-risk items such as tongue depressors, medical thermometers, and examination gloves, to complex implantable devices such as pacemakers, artificial joints, and heart valves, as well as diagnostic equipment such as MRI scanners and in vitro diagnostic tests. Unlike pharmaceuticals, devices generally achieve their primary purpose by physical, mechanical, or software means rather than by chemical action within the body.

Regulation

In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) classifies devices into Class I (low risk, general controls), Class II (moderate risk, usually requiring a 510(k) premarket notification), and Class III (high risk, usually requiring premarket approval with clinical evidence). In the European Union, devices are regulated under the Medical Devices Regulation (MDR 2017/745), with classes I, IIa, IIb, and III and assessment by notified bodies. International harmonization is promoted by the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF).

Industry

The global medical device market is worth several hundred billion dollars annually. Major manufacturers include Medtronic, Johnson & Johnson, Abbott, Siemens Healthineers, GE HealthCare, and Philips. The sector is characterized by rapid innovation in imaging, minimally invasive surgery, wearable monitoring, and software as a medical device (SaMD).
