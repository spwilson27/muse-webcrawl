The Oghuz Turks (also spelled Oguz or Ghuzz) were a confederation of Turkic pastoral tribes of the Central Asian steppe whose descendants include the modern Turks of Turkey, Azerbaijanis, Turkmens and related peoples. Their language group, the Oghuz or Southwestern branch of the Turkic family, is spoken today by well over a hundred million people.

Origins and migration

The Oghuz emerged by the eighth century in the steppe between the Aral and Caspian seas, with a tribal union described in Turkic inscriptions and in Muslim sources such as the works of al-Masudi and Ibn Hawqal. From the tenth century many Oghuz — known in Islamic lands as Turkmens after their conversion to Islam — pressed south and west. Under the Seljuk family, Oghuz forces conquered Persia, Iraq and, after the Battle of Manzikert (1071), much of Anatolia, transforming the ethnic and linguistic map of the Middle East.

Later history

Oghuz groups founded or staffed many of the great states of the medieval and early modern Islamic world, including the Seljuk Empire, the Anatolian beyliks and the Ottoman Empire, the Qara Qoyunlu and Aq Qoyunlu confederations, and the Afsharid and Qajar dynasties of Persia. The Book of Dede Korkut, a cycle of heroic tales, preserves the legendary history of the Oghuz and remains a shared literary heritage in Turkey, Azerbaijan and Turkmenistan.

Peoples and languages today

Modern Oghuz-speaking peoples include the Turks of Turkey and Cyprus, Azerbaijanis of Azerbaijan and northwestern Iran, Turkmens of Turkmenistan, the Qashqai and other Turkic groups of Iran, the Gagauz of Moldova, and Iraqi and Syrian Turkmens. Their languages — Turkish, Azerbaijani and Turkmen being the largest — share the characteristic Oghuz features that distinguish them from Kipchak and Karluk Turkic languages.
