Customary law

Customary law is a body of law based on the established patterns of behaviour and customs of a particular community, rather than on written legislation enacted by a central authority. Customs become legally binding when they are long-established, consistently observed, and accepted by the community as obligatory. Customary law remains an important source of law in many countries, particularly in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific.

Characteristics

Customary law is typically unwritten and passed down orally, though some systems have been partly codified. It often deals with land tenure, marriage, inheritance, and compensation for wrongs, and enforcement may be handled by chiefs, elders, or community assemblies rather than state courts. Restorative and compensatory outcomes are often preferred over punishment.

In modern legal systems

Many constitutions recognise customary law subject to limits: it must not conflict with statute law or constitutional rights. In England, the common law itself grew out of the customs of the royal courts, and local customs can still be legally recognised if they are ancient, continuous, and reasonable. Canon law, customary international law, and the law merchant (lex mercatoria) are other historical systems with customary foundations.

Criticism and reform

Customary systems are praised for accessibility and cultural legitimacy, but criticised where practices disadvantage women or younger people in matters of property and marriage. Courts in countries such as South Africa have developed customary law in line with constitutional values while respecting its living, evolving character.
