Bridge (nautical)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia — the ship's bridge


The bridge of a modern ship

The bridge of a ship is the room or platform from which the vessel is commanded. Located high in the superstructure, traditionally at the top and spanning the full width of large ships, it gives the officer of the watch an unobstructed view for navigation and manoeuvring. The term derives from the early paddle steamers, whose captain walked on a literal bridge spanning the paddle boxes to move between the paddle wheels.

Equipment

A modern bridge integrates radar, electronic chart systems (ECDIS), GPS navigation, automatic identification systems, gyrocompasses, autopilot, echo sounders, engine and rudder controls, and radio communications including GMDSS distress equipment. On large vessels the bridge wings extend to the ship's sides so pilots can see alongside when docking. At night the bridge is kept dark and instruments dimmed to red lighting to preserve night vision. Regulations of the International Maritime Organization set standards for bridge layout, visibility and equipment, and conventions govern watchkeeping so that a qualified officer is in charge at all times at sea.

Personnel

The bridge is manned by the officer of the watch, a helmsman or autopilot, and lookouts as conditions require. The master (captain) has overall command and typically takes direct control, called "the conn", during arrivals, departures and emergencies, while a harbour pilot may advise on local navigation. In naval usage the bridge hosts the commanding officer's station in action, alongside a separate combat information centre.
