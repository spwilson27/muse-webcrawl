Procedural drama

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A typical crime-scene investigation scene from a police procedural

A procedural drama is a cross-genre type of literature, film, or television program involving a sequence of technical detail. A fictional work in this genre may feature a number of related subgenres, the best known of which are the police procedural, the legal drama, and the medical drama. Episodes of a procedural drama typically resolve their mysteries or cases within the episode itself, as opposed to serialised dramas whose story arcs span multiple episodes or whole seasons.

Characteristics

Procedurals focus on the process of an institution — a police department, hospital, law firm, forensic laboratory or military unit — and on how its professionals solve problems step by step. The episodic, self-contained structure ("case of the week") makes episodes largely order-independent, which suits syndication and casual viewing. Character development and personal subplots usually take second place to the investigation or case, though long-running series often build an ensemble cast with continuing relationships.

Examples

Successful procedurals include Dragnet, Law & Order and its spin-offs, the CSI franchise, NCIS, Bones, The Mentalist and Elementary in the police and forensic fields; ER, House and Grey's Anatomy among medical dramas (the latter with strong serial elements); and legal series such as Perry Mason and The Practice. The format originated on radio and in detective fiction, where the police procedural novel was developed by writers such as Ed McBain in his 87th Precinct series.
