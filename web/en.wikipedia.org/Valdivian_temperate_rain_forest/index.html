The Valdivian temperate rain forest (Spanish: Bosque templado valdiviano) is an ecoregion on the west coast of southern South America, in Chile and extending into adjacent Argentina. It is part of the Neotropical realm and one of only a handful of temperate rainforests in the world. The forest is renowned for its high endemism — a legacy of its long isolation as a forested refuge since the time of Gondwana, separated from other forests by the Andes, the Pacific Ocean and the arid Atacama Desert to the north.

Geography and climate

The ecoregion stretches roughly from 35° to 47° south latitude along the Chilean coast and the western slopes of the Andes, including the Chilean Coastal Range and the island of Chiloé. Westerly winds bring heavy rainfall, from about 1,000 mm annually in the north to over 4,000–6,000 mm in favoured coastal and Andean locations, with a mild oceanic temperature regime and infrequent frost at low elevations.

Flora

The forest is evergreen and multi-layered. Characteristic trees include the southern beeches (Nothofagus dombeyi, N. nitida, N. betuloides), laurels and myrtles (such as Laureliopsis philippiana, Aextoxicon punctatum and arrayán, Luma apiculata), the conifers alerce (Fitzroya cupressoides) — one of the longest-lived trees on Earth, with individuals over 3,600 years old — Guaitecas cypress (Pilgerodendron uviferum), and the monkey puzzle tree (Araucaria araucana) at higher elevations. Dense understoreys feature bamboo (Chusquea), tree ferns, climbing plants and a profusion of epiphytes and mosses. Many lineages, including the family Aextoxicaceae and numerous genera, are endemic Gondwanan relicts more closely related to the floras of New Zealand and Australia than to the rest of South America.

Fauna

Endemic and distinctive animals include the pudú, the world's smallest deer; the kodkod (güiña), one of the smallest wild cats; Darwin's frog; the monito del monte, a marsupial that is the sole living representative of the order Microbiotheria and more closely related to Australian marsupials than to other South American ones; and birds such as the chucao tapaculo and the Magellanic woodpecker. The region's streams and coasts support the southern river otter and marine species including blue whales in the Gulf of Corcovado.

Conservation

Historically the forest was cleared for agriculture, burned during colonisation, and logged for alerce and other timbers; in the late twentieth century large areas of native forest were converted to plantations of Monterey pine and eucalyptus. Protected areas include Pumalín Douglas Tompkins National Park, Alerce Andino, Chiloé and Nahuelbuta national parks in Chile and Nahuel Huapi and Los Alerces national parks in Argentina. Conservation International classifies the Chilean Winter Rainfall–Valdivian Forests as a global biodiversity hotspot, and conservationists have expanded protection through large private reserves assembled by Douglas and Kristine Tompkins and donated to the Chilean state.
