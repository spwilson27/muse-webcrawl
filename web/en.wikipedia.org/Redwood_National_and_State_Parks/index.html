The Redwood National and State Parks (RNSP) are a complex of one national park and three California state parks, cooperatively managed, located along the coast of northern California. Together they protect about 139,000 acres of temperate rainforest, including roughly half of all the remaining old-growth coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens) forest in the world.

Old-growth redwoods in the park can exceed 100 metres in height.

Composition

The complex comprises Redwood National Park (established 1968 and expanded in 1978) and the earlier California state parks: Prairie Creek Redwoods, Del Norte Coast Redwoods, and Jedediah Smith Redwoods, founded in the 1920s largely through the efforts of the Save the Redwoods League. Since 1994 the National Park Service and California State Parks have managed them jointly. The parks were designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980.

Ecology

Coast redwoods are the tallest trees on Earth; the park contains some of the tallest known specimens, including (in a location kept secret) trees over 115 metres tall. The ecosystem also includes Sitka spruce, Douglas-fir, and tanoak, 40 miles of coastline, prairies, and rivers supporting Roosevelt elk, black bear, banana slugs, and endangered species such as the marbled murrelet and northern spotted owl.

History

The region was home to Native American peoples including the Yurok, Tolowa, and Chilula for millennia. Logging from the 1850s onward removed about 96% of the original old-growth redwood forest before park protections took effect. The parks' creation, and especially the 1978 expansion, was fiercely contested by the timber industry and local communities.
