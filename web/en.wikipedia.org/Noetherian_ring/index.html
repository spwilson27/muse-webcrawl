Noetherian ring

In mathematics, a Noetherian ring is a ring that satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals: every increasing sequence of ideals I₁ ⊆ I₂ ⊆ I₃ ⊆ … eventually stabilizes. Equivalently, every ideal of the ring is finitely generated, and every non-empty set of ideals has a maximal element. The concept, named after Emmy Noether, who developed its theory in the 1920s, is fundamental in commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, because it guarantees that algebraic varieties can be decomposed into finitely many irreducible components.

An ascending chain of ideals must stabilize in a Noetherian ring

Definitions

A ring R is left Noetherian if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on left ideals, and right Noetherian analogously; for commutative rings the two notions coincide. A module is Noetherian if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on submodules, and a ring is Noetherian exactly when it is Noetherian as a module over itself. The equivalence between the chain condition and finite generation of ideals follows from a classic union argument.

Examples and theorems

Fields, principal ideal domains such as the integers, and finite rings are Noetherian. The Hilbert basis theorem states that if R is Noetherian, then the polynomial ring R[x] is Noetherian, so polynomial rings in finitely many variables over a field or over ℤ are Noetherian — the algebraic basis for the finiteness results of classical algebraic geometry. Quotients and localizations of Noetherian rings are Noetherian. A standard non-Noetherian example is the ring of polynomials in infinitely many variables, where the chain (x₁) ⊂ (x₁, x₂) ⊂ … never stabilizes.

Significance

Noether's framework recast ideal theory, enabling the Lasker–Noether theorem on primary decomposition, which generalizes the fundamental theorem of arithmetic to ideals in Noetherian rings. In algebraic geometry, coordinate rings of varieties are Noetherian, so every algebraic set is a finite union of irreducible varieties and descending chains of varieties terminate.
