Isocrates

Isocrates (Greek: Ἰσοκράτης; 436–338 BC) was an ancient Greek rhetorician, one of the ten Attic orators. In his time he was among the most influential teachers in Greece, and his school at Athens attracted students from across the Greek world. A shy man with a weak voice, he published his speeches rather than delivering them, and used published discourses to advocate Panhellenic unity against Persia.

Life

Isocrates studied under Prodicus, Gorgias, and Theramenes, and was reportedly associated with Socrates. After losing his family fortune in the Peloponnesian War, he worked as a logographer (speechwriter) before founding his school of rhetoric about 393 BC, which he ran for over fifty years. Pupils included the historians Ephorus and Theopompus and the orators Hyperides and Lycurgus.

Ideas

In Against the Sophists and the Antidosis (his long self-defense, written at age 82), Isocrates defined education as the cultivation of practical judgment through discourse (logos), contrasting his "philosophy" with both eristic sophistry and Plato's abstract dialectic. His Panegyricus (380 BC) urged the Greek states to unite under joint Athenian–Spartan leadership for a crusade against Persia; late in life he addressed similar appeals to Philip II of Macedon.

Legacy

Isocrates shaped the ideal of liberal education transmitted through Cicero and Quintilian to Renaissance humanism; his smooth, periodic prose style became a model of Greek literary style. He died in 338 BC, the year of Philip's victory at Chaeronea.
