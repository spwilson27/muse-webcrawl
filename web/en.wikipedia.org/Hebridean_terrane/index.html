Hebridean terrane

The Hebridean terrane is one of the terranes that form part of the Caledonian orogenic belt in northwest Scotland. It comprises the foreland to the Caledonian mountain belt — the ancient continental basement of the Outer Hebrides, Tiree, Coll and the far northwest mainland coast from Cape Wrath to the Kyle of Lochalsh — and the sedimentary successions laid down on it. Its eastern boundary is the Moine Thrust Belt, along which the Northern Highlands terrane was pushed over it during the Caledonian orogeny.

Basement: the Lewisian complex

The foreland basement is the Lewisian complex, among the oldest rocks in Europe, formed mainly between about 3.0 and 1.7 billion years ago. It consists largely of banded gneisses — the Scourian complex of the central region — flanked by the Laxfordian belts reworked during the Palaeoproterozoic, and cut by the Scourie dyke swarm. These rocks represent a fragment of the North Atlantic craton, whose counterparts occur in Greenland and Labrador; they were never significantly deformed by the Caledonian orogeny west of the thrust belt.

Sedimentary cover

Unconformably overlying the Lewisian is the Torridonian succession — thick red sandstones and conglomerates of the Stoer Group (c. 1.2 billion years) and Torridon Group (c. 1.0–0.95 billion years), deposited by rivers on the ancient continent of Laurentia; the Stoer Group also preserves an impact ejecta layer from a meteorite strike. Above another unconformity lie Cambrian to Ordovician shelf sediments — the basal Cambrian quartzite with its famous Skolithos "Pipe Rock", the Fucoid Beds and Salterella Grit, and the Durness Group limestones and dolomites, which form the distinctive karst country around Assynt and Durness.

Structure and significance

During the Silurian collision of Laurentia with Baltica and Avalonia, the Moine Thrust carried metamorphic rocks of the Moine Supergroup westward over the foreland; the imbricated Cambrian slices in the thrust belt at Assynt became a classic area for understanding thrust tectonics in the work of Peach, Horne and their Geological Survey colleagues. Mesozoic sediments and Palaeogene igneous rocks of the Hebrides locally overlie the terrane offshore and on the islands.
