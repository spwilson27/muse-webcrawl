Acropolis

An acropolis was the settlement of an upper part of an ancient Greek city, especially a citadel, and frequently a hill with precipitous sides, chosen for purposes of defence. The word comes from the Greek akros ("highest point") and polis ("city"). Acropoleis became the nuclei of large cities of classical antiquity and often housed important temples and civic buildings.

Acropolis of Athens

The best-known example is the Acropolis of Athens, a rocky outcrop about 150 metres above sea level. Habitation dates back to Neolithic times; in the Mycenaean period it was fortified and held a royal palace. After the Persian destruction of 480 BCE, Pericles directed a great rebuilding programme in the mid-5th century BCE that produced the Parthenon, dedicated to Athena Parthenos; the Propylaea gateway; the Erechtheion; and the Temple of Athena Nike. The site is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the most visited archaeological sites in the world.

Other acropoleis

Many Greek cities had acropoleis, including Argos, Corinth (Acrocorinth), Thebes, and Sparta. The concept has parallels in other cultures, such as Rome's Capitoline Hill.
