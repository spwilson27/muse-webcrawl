Northumbrian smallpipes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Northumbrian smallpipes (also known as the Northumbrian pipes) are a melodious, bellows-blown bagpipe developed in North East England, in the counties of Northumberland and Durham. They are noted for their sweet, quiet tone, a closed-ended chanter with a unique tight-fingering technique, and a staccato style of playing that contrasts with the louder Highland bagpipes of Scotland.

Description

The instrument consists of a bag inflated by bellows worked by the player's elbow, one chanter, and typically four drones. Air passes to the reeds via the bag, which the player squeezes under the other arm. The chanter is entirely closed at the end, and the finger holes are kept covered except for the one sounding the note, producing a crisp, detached articulation. The chanter characteristically has a number of keys — most commonly seven — which extend its range and allow chromatic notes.

Most sets are pitched in F, G, or D, and the drones are tuned to provide both tonic and dominant harmony. Because the bellows supply "cold" dry air, the cane reeds are more stable than in mouth-blown pipes.

History

The smallpipes developed during the late 17th and 18th centuries from earlier pastoral pipes. By the early 19th century makers in Newcastle upon Tyne, such as John Dunn and the Reid family, had added keys and additional drones, creating the modern instrument. The pipes were played by miners, farmers, and gentry alike; the Duke of Northumberland maintains an official piper to this day.

The Northumbrian Pipers' Society, founded in 1928, helped revive interest in the instrument and its traditional repertoire of marches, hornpipes, airs, and variation sets. Notable players include Tom Clough, Billy Pigg, and Kathryn Tickell, who brought the pipes to a wide international audience.

Repertoire

The traditional repertoire is strongly tied to Northumbrian folk music and features dance tunes in hornpipe rhythm, slow airs, and elaborate variation sets in which a simple melody is progressively ornamented. Collections such as Peacock's collection of tunes (c. 1800) preserve some of the earliest written smallpipe music.
