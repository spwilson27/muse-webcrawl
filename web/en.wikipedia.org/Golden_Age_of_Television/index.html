Golden Age of Television

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Golden Age of Television is the era of live television production in the United States, roughly from the late 1940s to the late 1950s. It is remembered for prestigious live dramatic anthology series broadcast from New York, including Kraft Television Theatre, Studio One, The Philco Television Playhouse and Playhouse 90, which presented original teleplays by writers such as Paddy Chayefsky, Rod Serling, Reginald Rose and Gore Vidal.

Characteristics

Productions were performed live before studio cameras and transmitted directly to viewers, giving them a theatrical immediacy. Many dramas dealt seriously with contemporary American life: Chayefsky's Marty (1953), Rose's Twelve Angry Men (1954) and Serling's Requiem for a Heavyweight (1956) were later adapted as celebrated films. The period also produced landmark variety, comedy and news programming, from Milton Berle and Sid Caesar to Edward R. Murrow's See It Now.

Decline

By the end of the 1950s the industry's centre shifted to Hollywood. Filmed series — cheaper to rerun and easier to control than live drama — replaced the anthology format, sponsor influence waned in favour of network control, and quiz-show scandals damaged the medium's prestige. The term "Golden Age" was coined in retrospect by critics contrasting the era's ambitions with the formulaic programming that followed; the term has also been applied to later periods, including a "Second Golden Age" of American drama series beginning in the late 1990s.
