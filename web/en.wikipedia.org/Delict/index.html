Delict

In civil law jurisdictions, delict (from Latin delictum, a wrong) is a wrongful act or omission that causes harm to another person or their property and gives rise to a civil obligation to compensate the victim. It occupies broadly the field of tort in common law systems. Delict forms, with contract and quasi-contract, one of the principal sources of obligations in civil law codes, descending from the treatment of delicts in Roman law and Justinian's Institutes.

Roman origins

Classical Roman law recognised specific delicts, among them furtum (theft), rapina (robbery), damnum iniuria datum (wrongful damage to property, governed by the Lex Aquilia), and iniuria (outrage or insult to the person). The Aquilian action's requirement of iniuria — wrongfulness — and the later development of general principles from these specific actions laid the foundations of modern delictual liability. Roman delicts were penal in character: the wrongdoer owed a penalty to the victim, not merely compensation.

Modern law

Modern civil codes differ in structure. French law states a general principle (articles 1240–1241 of the Civil Code, formerly 1382–1383): any act that causes damage to another obliges the person by whose fault it occurred to repair it, with liability resting on fault, whether by act or negligence. German law, by contrast, lists protected rights and specific wrongs in §§ 823–853 of the BGB. Scots law and South African law, as mixed systems, use the term delict for the same body of doctrine, drawing on Roman-Dutch and civilian sources.

Elements and boundaries

Typical elements of delictual liability are conduct (an act or omission), wrongfulness, fault (intention or negligence), causation, and harm. Delict is distinguished from crime — prosecuted by the state and punished — although the same conduct may ground both a criminal prosecution and a delictual claim. It is also distinguished from breach of contract, though the boundary is contested where contractual and delictual claims overlap.
