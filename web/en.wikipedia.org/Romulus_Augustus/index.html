Romulus Augustus (c. 465 – after 511), nicknamed Augustulus, was Roman emperor of the West from 31 October 475 until his deposition on 4 September 476. His deposition by the Germanic ruler Odoacer is traditionally taken to mark the end of the Western Roman Empire and the beginning of the Middle Ages in Western Europe.

Romulus was the son of Orestes, a Roman officer of Pannonian origin who had once served as secretary to Attila the Hun and who, in 475, led a revolt against the western emperor Julius Nepos. Rather than taking the throne himself, Orestes proclaimed his young son emperor at Ravenna. The Eastern emperor Zeno never recognised the appointment, continuing to regard Nepos as the legitimate western ruler.

Reign

The boy emperor was a figurehead for his father, who governed in his name. The reign was brief and dominated by conflict with the empire's Germanic foederati, who demanded a third of the land of Italy for themselves. When Orestes refused, their leader Odoacer proclaimed himself king and marched on Ravenna. Orestes was captured at Pavia and executed in August 476.

Deposition

On 4 September 476 Romulus was forced to abdicate. In a famous gesture, he surrendered the imperial regalia — the diadem, sceptre, and purple cloak — which Odoacer sent to Constantinople, symbolically uniting the empire under the Eastern emperor. Romulus was pensioned with an annual allowance of 6,000 solidi and retired to the Castel dell'Ovo in Naples, where he may have lived as late as 511.

Legacy

Romulus Augustus's reign is more significant for what it ended than for anything accomplished within it. His name, joining Romulus, the mythical founder of Rome, with Augustus, its first emperor, has often been noted as an ironic capstone to Roman history in the West. Historians remain divided over whether 476 truly marks the "fall" of Rome, but the date remains the conventional turning point.
