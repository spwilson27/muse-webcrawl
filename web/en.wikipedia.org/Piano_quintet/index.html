Piano quintet
InstrumentationPiano plus string quartet (2 violins, viola, cello)

A piano quintet is a chamber ensemble of five musicians — most commonly a piano together with a string quartet (two violins, viola, and cello) — or a work composed for such a group. It is one of the grandest of chamber combinations, bridging the intimacy of chamber music and the scale of a piano concerto. A related but distinct ensemble is the piano quartet (piano, violin, viola, cello).

History and repertoire

Although earlier experiments exist, the genre was firmly established by Robert Schumann's Quintet in E-flat major, Op. 44 (1842), composed rapidly and dedicated to his wife Clara, which made the piano-and-strings quintet a central Romantic form. Schubert's beloved "Trout" Quintet, D. 667 (1819), uses a variant scoring with double bass instead of a second violin.

Brahms's Quintet in F minor, Op. 34 (1864) — which passed through versions as a string quintet and as a two-piano sonata before reaching its final form — is often considered the genre's summit. Other landmarks include the quintets of César Franck (1879), Dvořák (Op. 81, 1887), Elgar (Op. 84, 1918), Fauré, Webern, and Shostakovich, whose Op. 57 (1940) won the Stalin Prize and remains among his most performed chamber works. Amy Beach, Florence Price, and Amy Cheney Beach's contemporaries expanded the repertoire in the 20th century, and the form continues to attract composers today.

Characteristics

The combination offers composers the full string quartet's blend plus the piano's percussive attack and wide range, but poses balance problems: a modern concert grand can easily overwhelm four string players. Performances typically unite an established string quartet with a guest pianist.
