Rapid antigen test

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rapid antigen test (RAT), sometimes called a rapid antigen detection test or lateral flow test, is a rapid diagnostic test suitable for point-of-care and home use that directly detects the presence of a target antigen, such as a viral surface protein. It is a type of lateral flow test that returns a result, usually within 5–30 minutes, without laboratory equipment. Rapid antigen tests are widely used to detect respiratory infections including influenza, respiratory syncytial virus and SARS-CoV-2, the virus causing COVID-19, and also include the long-established rapid strep test for group A Streptococcus.

How it works

A sample — typically a nasal or throat swab eluted in buffer — is applied to a test strip. If the target antigen is present it binds to labelled antibodies (often conjugated to coloured gold or latex particles) and the complex migrates along the strip by capillary action until captured by a second set of antibodies fixed at the test line, producing a visible coloured line. A control line captures excess labelled antibody and confirms the test has run correctly. The format is a sandwich immunoassay; the absence of a test line indicates a negative result.

Performance

Rapid antigen tests are generally highly specific (few false positives, typically over 98–99%) but less sensitive than nucleic acid amplification tests such as PCR: they detect most infections when viral load is high — roughly the period when a person is most infectious — but miss a proportion of early, late or low-level infections. Sensitivity depends on the target, sample type and technique; repeat (serial) testing over consecutive days substantially improves detection, a strategy recommended by public-health agencies during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Uses

During the COVID-19 pandemic, rapid antigen tests were manufactured in the billions and used for mass screening in schools, workplaces, travel and household self-testing, with many governments distributing them free of charge. Other long-standing applications include influenza A/B testing, rapid strep testing in pharyngitis, malaria antigen tests, and tests for RSV, HIV (antibody/antigen combos) and certain gastrointestinal pathogens.
