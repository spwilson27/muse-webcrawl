Sampling rate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sampling rate

Symbol: fs

Unit: hertz (Hz), samples per second

CD audio: 44,100 Hz

Video / professional audio: 48,000 Hz

The sampling rate or sampling frequency defines the number of samples per unit of time (usually seconds) taken from a continuous signal to make a discrete signal. For a time-domain signal it is measured in hertz (Hz), the reciprocal of the sampling interval. Sampling rate and bit depth together determine the data rate and fidelity of a digital representation: an uncompressed stereo CD stream, for example, carries 44,100 × 16 × 2 = about 1.41 million bits per second.

Nyquist criterion

By the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, a sampling rate must exceed twice the highest frequency present in the signal — the Nyquist rate — for exact reconstruction to be possible. Half the sampling rate is the Nyquist frequency; content above it folds back as aliasing. Human hearing extends to about 20 kHz, which is why audio sampling rates begin a little above 40 kHz.

Common rates

  • 8,000 Hz — traditional telephony (narrowband speech).
  • 22,050 Hz — low-quality / legacy computer audio.
  • 44,100 Hz — Compact Disc Digital Audio, chosen to cover 20 kHz with margin for anti-alias filters in early equipment.
  • 48,000 Hz — professional audio, digital television, DVD and most video production.
  • 96,000 and 192,000 Hz — high-resolution audio production and archiving.
  • 1 Hz to GHz — data acquisition ranges from slow environmental logging to oscilloscopes sampling billions of times per second.

Audio and video

In audio, the sampling rate fixes the highest reproducible frequency, while bit depth fixes dynamic range; debates continue over whether rates above 48 kHz confer audible benefits. In video, the analogous quantity is the frame rate (for whole images) and the pixel clock (for scanned rasters). Sample-rate conversion — resampling a signal from one rate to another — is a standard, mathematically delicate operation in digital signal processing.
