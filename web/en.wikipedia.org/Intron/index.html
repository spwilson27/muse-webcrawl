An intron is any nucleotide sequence within a gene that is not expressed, or suitable for translation, in the final RNA product. Introns are transcribed into the primary RNA transcript but are removed during RNA splicing, in which the remaining sequences — the exons — are joined together to form mature messenger RNA (mRNA). The word intron is derived from the term intragenic region, i.e. a region inside a gene.

Discovery

Introns were discovered in 1977 during work on adenovirus genes, when researchers including Phillip Sharp and Richard Roberts found that viral genes were "split": the mRNA did not map contiguously onto the DNA template. The discovery of split genes was recognized with the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1993.

Classification

Introns are commonly classified into four groups. Group I introns are self-splicing ribozymes found in some rRNA genes. Group II introns are also self-splicing and are thought to be ancestors of spliceosomal introns. Spliceosomal introns are removed by the spliceosome, a large ribonucleoprotein complex, and are the dominant type in eukaryotes. Transfer RNA introns occur in some tRNA genes and are removed by a separate protein-based mechanism.

Splicing

Spliceosomal introns begin with the dinucleotide GU (in RNA terms) at the 5′ end and end with AG at the 3′ end. The spliceosome excises the intron as a lariat-shaped structure. Errors in splicing are implicated in many human genetic diseases.

Biological functions

Although once dismissed as "junk DNA", introns are now known to host small nuclear RNAs, microRNAs and regulatory elements. Alternative splicing of the exons surrounding introns allows a single gene to produce multiple protein isoforms, greatly expanding the proteome. Introns may also enhance gene expression through intron-mediated enhancement.

Evolution

The origin of introns is debated between "introns-early" theories, which hold that introns were present in the earliest genes, and "introns-late" theories, which propose they invaded eukaryotic genes later. Prokaryotic genomes are nearly devoid of spliceosomal introns, whereas some eukaryotes have intron-rich genes — in humans, introns make up roughly a quarter of the genome.
