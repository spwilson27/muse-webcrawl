Communist party

A communist party is a political party that seeks to realize the socio-economic goals of communism. The term was popularized by the title of the 1848 pamphlet The Communist Manifesto by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. As a vanguard party, a communist party guides the political education and development of the working class (proletariat).

History

The first communist parties arose after the Russian Revolution of 1917, when the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin broke from social democratic parties and formed the Communist International (Comintern) in 1919. Communist parties aligned with Moscow spread across Europe, Asia, and the Americas. After the Second World War, communist governments came to power in Eastern Europe, China (1949), North Korea, Vietnam, and Cuba (1959).

Organization

Historically, ruling communist parties were organized on the Leninist principle of democratic centralism: free debate until a decision is taken, then strict unity in carrying it out. The party is typically led by a general secretary (or first secretary), a politburo, and a central committee, with a party congress as the nominally highest body. In one-party states such as China, Cuba, Vietnam, Laos, and North Korea, the communist party's leading role is often enshrined in the constitution.

Communist parties today

The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 led many communist parties to dissolve, rename themselves, or adopt social democratic programs. Communist parties nevertheless remain in government in China, Cuba, Laos, Vietnam, and North Korea, and participate in electoral politics in many other countries, including India, Nepal, South Africa, Japan, and several European states.
