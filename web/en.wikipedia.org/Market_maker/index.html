Market maker

A market maker or liquidity provider is a company or individual that quotes both a buy price (bid) and a sell price (ask) in a tradable asset held in inventory, hoping to make a profit on the difference between the two prices, known as the bid–ask spread. By standing ready to buy and sell at publicly quoted prices, market makers provide liquidity and immediacy to a market.

Function

Market makers accept the risk of holding a certain number of shares of a particular security to facilitate trading in it. Each market maker competes for customer order flow by displaying buy and sell quotations for a guaranteed number of shares. When an order arrives, the market maker may sell from its own inventory or buy for inventory in the market.

Profit and risk

The market maker's profit comes from the spread: buying at the bid price and selling at the slightly higher ask price. The main risks are inventory risk — the price of a held position moving against the maker — and adverse selection, trading against better-informed counterparties.

Designated market makers

Many exchanges appoint official market makers with obligations to maintain fair and orderly markets. On the New York Stock Exchange these are called designated market makers (formerly specialists); on NASDAQ, numerous firms act as registered market makers in each listed stock.
