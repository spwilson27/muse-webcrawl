Coronation sword

A coronation sword is a ceremonial sword used as part of the regalia in the coronation of a monarch, symbolising the sovereign's authority, the defence of the realm and of the faith, and the duty to render justice. Unlike weapons made for combat, coronation swords are often richly decorated with gold, enamel and precious stones, and many are centuries old, carried before the monarch in procession rather than wielded.

Examples

The British regalia include several swords: the Sword of State, carried before the sovereign; the Jewelled Sword of Offering, made for George IV in 1820 and presented during the service, its hilt encrusted with diamonds, rubies and emeralds forming rose, thistle and shamrock motifs; and the Swords of Justice and Mercy — the pointed Curtana, traditionally called the Sword of Mercy, whose blade is intentionally broken off near the tip. The Scottish regalia include a Sword of State presented by Pope Julius II in 1507.

In France, Joyeuse, the sword attributed to Charlemagne, was used at coronations from the medieval period and is now in the Louvre. Poland's coronation sword, Szczerbiec, survives in Kraków; Sweden, Denmark, Norway, the Netherlands and other monarchies maintain their own coronation or state swords.

Symbolism

The presentation of the sword during the rite typically represents the monarch's obligation to protect the church, punish wickedness, and defend the innocent — duties expressed in prayers accompanying the girding or offering of the sword in several coronation liturgies.
