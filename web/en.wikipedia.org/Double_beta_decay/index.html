Double beta decay

In nuclear physics, double beta decay is a type of radioactive decay in which two neutrons are transformed into two protons, or vice versa, inside an atomic nucleus. A second type — neutrinoless double beta decay — is hypothesised but has never been observed; its discovery would demonstrate that the neutrino is its own antiparticle (a Majorana particle) and would have major implications for physics beyond the Standard Model.

Two-neutrino double beta decay

In ordinary (two-neutrino) double beta decay, two neutrons in a nucleus are simultaneously converted into two protons, and two electrons and two electron antineutrinos are emitted. It occurs in nuclei for which single beta decay is energetically forbidden or strongly suppressed, but where the double decay is allowed. The process has been observed in about a dozen isotopes, including 82Se, 100Mo, 130Te, 136Xe and 150Nd, with extremely long half-lives of the order of 1018–1021 years — among the rarest processes ever measured.

Neutrinoless double beta decay

If the neutrino is a Majorana fermion, the antineutrino emitted at one vertex can be absorbed as a neutrino at the second, so only two electrons emerge and no neutrinos. This would violate conservation of lepton number by two units. The rate of the process is related to an "effective Majorana mass" of the neutrino. Large underground experiments — including GERDA and its successor LEGEND (using 76Ge), CUORE (130Te), KamLAND-Zen and EXO/nEXO (136Xe) — have searched for the decay, setting lower limits on its half-life exceeding 1026 years in several isotopes. Protons can similarly undergo double proton-conversion modes in double electron capture.

History

The process was first discussed by Maria Goeppert Mayer in 1935; the neutrinoless mode was proposed by Ettore Majorana and developed by Wendell Furry in the late 1930s. Two-neutrino double beta decay was first observed in the laboratory in 1987 in 82Se, after earlier geochemical detections.
