The Polabian Slavs (Lower Sorbian: Połobske Słowjany; Polish: Słowianie połabscy) were a group of West Slavic tribes who lived along the Elbe (Slavic Laba, hence "Polabian") in what is now northeastern Germany and northwestern Poland. Settling the region from about the 6th century AD, they formed the westernmost extent of the Slavic world, bordering the Frankish and later German realms.

Tribes and society

The principal tribal confederations were the Obotrites in the northwest (Mecklenburg and Holstein), the Veleti, later called Lutici, in the northeast, and the various Sorbian tribes in the south along the upper Elbe and Saale. Polabian society was organised around fortified strongholds (gords) and temple centres such as Rethra and Arkona on the island of Rügen, where the god Svantevit was worshipped. Slavic paganism, with its sacred groves, idols and oracles, persisted in these lands longer than anywhere else in Europe west of Poland.

Wars and Germanisation

From the reign of Charlemagne onward, the Polabian Slavs were in near-constant conflict with Franks, Saxons and Danes. The great Slav uprising of 983 destroyed German bishoprics east of the Elbe and preserved Slavic independence for nearly two centuries. The turning point came with the Wendish Crusade of 1147 and the campaigns of Henry the Lion and Albert the Bear, after which the Polabian lands were conquered, Christianised and progressively Germanised through settlement (the Ostsiedlung).

Legacy

The Polabian language died out in the 18th century, the last speaker dying around 1756 in the Lüchow-Dannenberg region (the "Wendland"), but the related Sorbian languages survive in Lusatia. Polabian heritage endures in hundreds of place names across northern Germany — Berlin, Leipzig, Dresden, Schwerin and Rostock all have Slavic name origins — and in the round village (Rundling) settlements of the Wendland.
