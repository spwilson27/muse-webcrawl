Bloody Sunday (1972)

Bloody Sunday, also called the Bogside massacre, took place on Sunday 30 January 1972 in the Bogside area of Derry, Northern Ireland. Soldiers of the British Army's Parachute Regiment shot 26 unarmed civilians during a protest march organised by the Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association against internment without trial. Fourteen people died, thirteen on the day and one later of his wounds.

The march and the shooting

The march, which had been banned, drew thousands of participants. After clashes at barricades in the Bogside, paratroopers moved in to make arrests and opened fire. Those killed were mostly young men, several of them teenagers; eyewitnesses and journalists maintained that all the victims were unarmed and that some were shot while fleeing or tending the wounded.

Inquiries

The Widgery Tribunal, reporting within weeks, largely exonerated the soldiers and was widely regarded by nationalists as a whitewash. Decades later the Saville Inquiry, established in 1998 and reporting in 2010, concluded that the killings were “unjustified and unjustifiable”: those shot were unarmed, posed no threat, and the soldiers had fired first. Prime Minister David Cameron formally apologised on behalf of the government.

Legacy

Bloody Sunday was a turning point in the Troubles, fuelling recruitment to the Provisional IRA and deepening nationalist alienation from the state. It is commemorated annually in Derry and in the U2 song “Sunday Bloody Sunday”.
