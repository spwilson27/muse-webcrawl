Parmenides

Parmenides of Elea (c. 515 – c. 450 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Elea in Magna Graecia (southern Italy). He is considered the founder of the Eleatic school of philosophy and one of the most influential figures in Western thought: his argument that reality is one, unchanging, and indivisible, and that change and plurality are illusions of the senses, set the agenda for nearly all subsequent Greek philosophy, including the systems of Plato and Aristotle.

The poem

Parmenides' philosophy survives in fragments of a didactic poem in hexameter verse, conventionally called On Nature. In its proem the poet is carried by mares and goddesses to the gates of Night and Day, where a goddess reveals two parts of teaching: the "Way of Truth" and the "Way of Opinion". About 160 lines survive, most of them from the Way of Truth, quoted mainly by later authors.

The Way of Truth

The goddess argues that what-is is, and what-is-not is not and cannot be thought or spoken of. From this Parmenides deduces that being must be ungenerated and imperishable, whole, one, indivisible, homogeneous, and motionless: generation would require being to arise from non-being, and motion would require empty space, which is non-being. Hence the everyday world of change, motion, and diversity is deceptive appearance. The argument is the earliest surviving sustained piece of deductive reasoning in Greek philosophy, and its logic — taken seriously by successors such as Zeno of Elea, whose paradoxes defended it — forced later thinkers either to accept some version of it or to find a way around it.

The Way of Opinion

The second part presents a cosmology — of light and night, the heavens, and human generation — which the goddess calls a deceptive ordering of words, though it may represent the best possible account of appearances. Its purpose remains debated.

Legacy

Plato named one of his most demanding dialogues, the Parmenides, after him and treated him with exceptional reverence. The atomists, Empedocles, and Anaxagoras all shaped their pluralist systems to answer Parmenides' challenge, and his insistence that thought and being are the same has echoed through rationalist philosophy ever since.
