Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants

The Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants is an international environmental treaty, signed in 2001 and entering into force in 2004, that aims to eliminate or restrict the production and use of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) — chemicals that remain intact in the environment for long periods, become widely distributed geographically, and accumulate in the fatty tissue of living organisms.

Key provisions

The Convention initially listed twelve chemicals, the so-called "dirty dozen", including DDT, PCBs, dioxins and furans. Chemicals are listed in annexes: Annex A (elimination), Annex B (restriction — DDT is retained for disease vector control such as malaria), and Annex C (reduction of unintentional production, such as dioxins from burning). A scientific review committee evaluates proposals to add new substances, and the list has grown to more than thirty chemicals, including PFOS and other fluorinated compounds.

Implementation

Parties must develop national implementation plans, eliminate stockpiles and wastes containing POPs, and promote best available techniques to reduce releases. The Global Environment Facility serves as the financial mechanism. More than 180 parties have ratified the treaty; the Basel and Rotterdam conventions form a related cluster of chemicals and waste treaties.
