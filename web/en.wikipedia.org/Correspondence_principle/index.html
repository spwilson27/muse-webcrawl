Correspondence principle
Formulated byNiels Bohr, c. 1920
FieldQuantum mechanics; philosophy of science
StatementQuantum predictions converge to classical ones for large quantum numbers

At large quantum numbers, discrete quantum levels merge into the classical continuum.

The correspondence principle is a principle in quantum mechanics, formulated by Niels Bohr around 1920, stating that the behaviour of systems described by quantum theory must reproduce the predictions of classical physics in the limit of large quantum numbers. It provided a bridge between the old quantum theory and classical mechanics and served as a powerful guide in constructing the new theory.

Bohr's formulation

In Bohr's atomic model, electrons radiate when jumping between stationary states. He observed that for orbits with very large principal quantum number n, the frequency of radiation from a transition between neighbouring levels approaches the classical frequency of the electron's orbital motion, as classical physics (with its continuous radiation) would predict. Bohr elevated this behaviour into a general principle and used it to estimate intensities and selection rules for spectral lines before a full quantum mechanics existed.

Role in modern physics

In modern quantum mechanics the principle is not an independent postulate but a property that the theory satisfies: expectation values of quantum observables follow classical equations of motion for states sharply peaked in position and momentum (Ehrenfest's theorem), and classical behaviour emerges at macroscopic scales. The principle has an analogue in relativity, where relativistic equations reduce to Newtonian ones at velocities far below the speed of light.

Generalised use

Philosophers of science use "correspondence principle" more broadly for the requirement that any new, more general theory should explain why the older theory it supersedes worked as well as it did within its domain — the older theory survives as a limiting case of the new one.
