Cetane number

Overview
MeasuresCombustion quality / ignition delay of diesel fuel
Reference compoundsCetane (n-hexadecane) = 100; heptamethylnonane = 15
Standard methodsASTM D613; derived cetane number ASTM D6890 / D7170
Typical road diesel40–55

Cetane number (or CN) is an indicator of the combustion speed of diesel fuel and the compression needed for ignition. It plays a similar role for diesel as the octane rating does for gasoline. The CN is an important factor in determining the quality of diesel fuel, but not the only one; other measurements of diesel fuel quality include density, lubricity, cold-flow properties, and sulfur content.

Definition

Cetane number is defined by the ignition delay of the fuel relative to reference blends. Pure cetane (n-hexadecane, C16H34), which ignites very readily under compression, is assigned a value of 100, while heptamethylnonane is assigned 15 (replacing the original low reference, alpha-methylnaphthalene, assigned 0). A fuel's cetane number is the percentage by volume of cetane in a reference blend with the same ignition delay, measured traditionally in a standardized Cooperative Fuel Research (CFR) single-cylinder test engine (ASTM D613).

Effects

Higher cetane fuels give shorter ignition delay, easier cold starting, smoother and quieter combustion ("diesel knock" is reduced), and lower emissions of carbon monoxide and unburned hydrocarbons; excessively high cetane offers little benefit. European standards require a minimum cetane number of 51 for road diesel, while U.S. ASTM D975 specifies a minimum of 40. Cetane improver additives such as 2-ethylhexyl nitrate can raise the number.
