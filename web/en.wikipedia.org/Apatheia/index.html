Apatheia

Apatheia is the Stoic state of freedom from destructive passions

Apatheia (Greek: ἀπάθεια, "without passion") is a central concept in Stoic philosophy denoting a state of mind in which one is free from disturbance by the passions (pathē)—irrational emotions such as fear, anger, grief, and excessive desire that arise from false judgments about good and evil. The Stoic sage who achieves apatheia experiences instead the "good feelings" (eupatheiai) such as joy, watchfulness, and well-wishing.

Stoicism

For the Stoics, passions are not raw feelings but mistaken assents of reason: treating indifferent things—wealth, health, reputation—as genuine goods or evils. Through discipline of judgment, a person eliminates these errors and attains equanimity regardless of fortune. Apatheia is thus not apathy in the modern sense of indifference or listlessness, but a stable, rational tranquility that accompanies virtue, and is closely linked to the goal of eudaimonia.

Later usage

The term passed into early Christian ascetic thought, notably through Evagrius Ponticus and the monastic tradition, where apatheia described dispassion as a condition for pure prayer and love. The Cynics and Skeptics used related notions—Pyrrhonists spoke instead of ataraxia, freedom from disturbance, a term also central to Epicureanism.
