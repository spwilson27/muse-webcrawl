Hiranyakashipu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Narasimha slaying Hiranyakashipu, painting

Hiranyakashipu (Sanskrit: हिरण्यकशिपु, "clothed in gold") is an asura king in Hindu mythology, prominent in the Purāṇas, especially the Bhāgavata Purāṇa. He is remembered as the tyrannical father of the saintly Prahlāda and as the enemy slain by Vishnu in his man-lion avatar, Narasimha.

Legend

Hiranyakashipu and his brother Hiranyaksha were, in the Bhāgavata account, incarnations of Vishnu's gatekeepers Jaya and Vijaya, cursed to be born as demons. After Vishnu (as Varaha) killed Hiranyaksha, Hiranyakashipu performed severe penance and obtained from Brahmā a boon that he could be killed neither by man nor beast, neither indoors nor outdoors, neither by day nor by night, neither on earth nor in the sky, and by no weapon.

Emboldened, he conquered the three worlds and forbade the worship of Vishnu, demanding worship of himself. His young son Prahlāda, however, remained devoted to Vishnu and survived every attempt on his life. When Hiranyakashipu mockingly demanded whether Vishnu was in a nearby pillar, Vishnu burst forth from it as Narasimha — half man, half lion — at twilight (neither day nor night), on the threshold (neither indoors nor outdoors), placed the king across his lap (neither earth nor sky), and slew him with his claws (no weapon), thus honouring every term of the boon.

Significance

The story is central to the festival of Holi, whose bonfire (Holikā Dahan) recalls the burning of Hiranyakashipu's sister Holikā, and it remains one of the most popular tales of Vishnu's avatars, dramatised in temples and performance traditions such as Prahlāda Nāṭaka.
