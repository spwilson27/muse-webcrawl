Al-Mutanabbi

Abu al-Tayyib Ahmad ibn al-Husayn al-Mutanabbi (c. 915 – 23 September 965) was an Iraqi poet of the Abbasid era, widely regarded as the greatest poet in the Arabic language. His diwan of some 300 poems, celebrated for their rhetorical power, philosophical aphorisms, and proud self-image, remains central to Arabic literary culture; many of his lines have become proverbs. His pen name al-Mutanabbi means "the would-be prophet," recalling his youthful involvement in a revolt in which he reportedly claimed prophetic status.

Life

Born in Kufa, al-Mutanabbi studied in Damascus and spent time among the Bedouin, perfecting his command of classical Arabic. After imprisonment for his part in a Qarmatian-linked rising, he became a wandering court poet, seeking patrons. His most celebrated years (947–957) were spent at Aleppo in the court of the Hamdanid emir Sayf al-Dawla, whose campaigns against the Byzantines al-Mutanabbi immortalized in panegyrics. Court rivalries forced his departure; he later served the Buyid vizier Ibn al-Amid and Adud al-Dawla in Persia. Returning to Iraq in 965, he was killed by brigands he had previously insulted in verse.

Legacy

Al-Mutanabbi's poetry — including lines such as "The horses, the night, and the desert know me" — shaped Arabic poetics for a millennium and generated a vast commentary tradition. Baghdad's al-Mutanabbi Street, a historic centre of bookselling, is named for him; its recovery after a 2007 car bombing became a symbol of Iraq's cultural resilience.
