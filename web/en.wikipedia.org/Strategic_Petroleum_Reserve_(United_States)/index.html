Strategic Petroleum Reserve (United States)

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve (SPR) is an emergency stockpile of petroleum maintained by the United States Department of Energy (DOE). It is the largest publicly known emergency supply in the world; its underground salt caverns on the Gulf of Mexico coast have an authorised capacity of 714 million barrels (113,500,000 m3). The United States started the petroleum reserve in 1975 to mitigate future supply disruptions as part of the Energy Policy and Conservation Act, following the 1973–74 oil embargo.

The crude oil is stored in huge underground salt caverns at four sites along the Gulf Coast of Louisiana and Texas, chosen for low storage cost and proximity to refineries and pipelines. The SPR can deliver up to about 4.4 million barrels per day into the U.S. market.

History

The reserve was authorised after the 1973 oil crisis exposed U.S. vulnerability to supply shocks. The first oil was delivered in 1977. President George H. W. Bush ordered the first emergency release during the 1991 Gulf War. Subsequent drawdowns followed Hurricane Katrina (2005), the Libyan civil war (2011, as part of an IEA-coordinated release), and a series of congressionally mandated sales in the 2010s. In 2022, following Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the resulting price spike, President Joe Biden ordered the release of 180 million barrels — the largest drawdown in the SPR's history — bringing inventories to their lowest levels since the 1980s.

Facilities

The four storage sites are Bayou Choctaw and West Hackberry in Louisiana and Big Hill and Bryan Mound in Texas. Solution-mined salt caverns store the oil safely and cheaply; each cavern is roughly 2,000 feet below the surface. A fifth site, Richton, Mississippi, was planned and later cancelled.
