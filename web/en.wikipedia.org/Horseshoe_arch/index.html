Horseshoe arch

The horseshoe arch, also called the Moorish arch or keyhole arch, is an arch whose curve extends beyond a semicircle, so that the opening is narrower at its springing line than at its widest point — a shape resembling a horseshoe. It is one of the most recognisable features of Islamic architecture, particularly in the western Islamic world.

History

Horseshoe arches appear in pre-Islamic architecture in Syria and in Visigothic Spain. They were adopted and developed in Umayyad architecture, most famously in the Great Mosque of Córdoba, where rows of double-tiered horseshoe arches support the prayer hall. From Spain and North Africa the form spread through the architecture of al-Andalus and the Maghreb and later into Mudéjar architecture built for Christian patrons in Spain.

Forms

The horseshoe arch may be round, in which the curve is a segment of a circle greater than 180 degrees, or pointed, as in later Moorish and Maghrebi buildings. The alternating red brick and pale stone voussoirs (ablaq) of Córdoba became a signature decorative treatment. The form was revived in the 19th and early 20th centuries in Moorish Revival architecture, including synagogues and theatres in Europe and America.
