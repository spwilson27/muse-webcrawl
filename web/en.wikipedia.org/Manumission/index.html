Manumission

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Roman manumission ceremony]

Manumission, from Latin manumissio ("sending out from the hand"), is the act of a slave owner freeing their slaves. Practices varied greatly across slave-holding societies, ranging from formal legal ceremonies to informal grants, sometimes with conditions attached or reserved rights for the former owner.

Ancient world

In ancient Rome, manumission was common and formalised: a slave freed by vindicta, censu or by will became a freedperson (libertus) and usually a Roman citizen, owing duties of respect and labour (operae) to their patron. Augustus restricted manumission by laws such as the lex Aelia Sentia. In ancient Greece, freed slaves typically remained non-citizen residents (metics).

Subsequent history

In the Islamic world, freeing slaves was encouraged as piety and expiation, and manumission contracts (mukataba) allowed self-purchase. In the Americas, manumission was possible under Spanish and Portuguese law more readily than in British colonies; some slaves purchased their freedom, and some owners freed enslaved children they had fathered. After the American Revolution, northern states' gradual abolition laws and individual manumissions created growing free Black populations, though southern states increasingly restricted the practice in the 19th century.

Relation to abolition

General emancipation—such as the British Slavery Abolition Act 1833 and the U.S. Thirteenth Amendment (1865)—ended slavery wholesale, making individual manumission moot.
