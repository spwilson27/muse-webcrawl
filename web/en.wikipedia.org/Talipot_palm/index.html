Talipot palm

Talipot palm — Corypha umbraculifera, a species of palm native to eastern and southern India and Sri Lanka. One of the largest palms, with fan leaves up to 5 m across; monocarpic, flowering once after 30–80 years and then dying.

The talipot palm (Corypha umbraculifera) is a species of palm native to eastern and southern India (Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu) and Sri Lanka. It is also reportedly naturalized in Cambodia, Myanmar, Thailand and the Andaman Islands. It is a flowering plant with the largest inflorescence in the world. It lives up to 60 to 80 years and can reach heights of 25 metres (82 ft), with a trunk up to 1.3 m in diameter.

Description

The fan-shaped leaves, up to 5 metres (16 ft) in diameter with a petiole up to 4 metres long, are among the largest palmate leaves of any plant. The palm is monocarpic: at maturity it produces a massive terminal inflorescence 6–8 metres tall — the largest of any plant — bearing millions of small cream flowers, then fruits and dies.

Uses

Historically the leaves were used as writing material: the celebrated palm-leaf manuscripts (ola leaves) of South and Southeast Asia were prepared from talipot and related palms. The leaves also serve for thatching and umbrellas; the trunk yields a sago-like starch, and the hard seeds ("Bajurbet nuts") are turned into beads and buttons.

Cultivation

The talipot palm is widely planted as an ornamental in tropical gardens, where a flowering event draws crowds because of its rarity and scale.
