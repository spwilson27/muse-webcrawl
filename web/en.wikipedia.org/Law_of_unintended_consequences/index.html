Law of unintended consequences

The law of unintended consequences (sometimes referred to as the law of unforeseen consequences) is the observation that actions of people — and especially of governments — always have effects that are unanticipated or unintended. The idea dates to Adam Smith and was discussed by Frédéric Bastiat (as that which is seen and that which is not seen) and John Locke, among others. The phrase was popularized in the 20th century by sociologist Robert K. Merton, whose 1936 paper "The Unanticipated Consequences of Purposive Social Action" is the classic treatment.

Merton's analysis

Merton identified several sources of unanticipated consequences: ignorance (incomplete knowledge), error (faulty analysis or habits applied inappropriately), the "imperious immediacy of interest" (short-term concerns overriding long-term effects), basic values that demand action regardless of results, and the self-defeating prophecy, in which a prediction alters behavior so as to falsify itself. He distinguished unintended consequences from related ideas such as the self-fulfilling prophecy.

Types

  • Unexpected benefit — a positive, unforeseen outcome (sometimes called serendipity or a windfall), such as a drug developed for one condition proving useful for another.
  • Unexpected drawback — a negative side effect in addition to the desired effect, such as environmental harm from an otherwise useful technology.
  • Perverse result — an outcome opposite to what was intended, where the intervention worsens the problem it aimed to solve. The related "cobra effect" takes its name from a possibly apocryphal colonial bounty on dead cobras in India that allegedly encouraged cobra breeding.

Examples

Commonly cited examples include Prohibition in the United States, which encouraged organized crime and speakeasies; rent control, argued to reduce housing supply and quality over time; the introduction of invasive species for pest control, such as cane toads in Australia, which became pests themselves; and the "Streisand effect," in which attempts to suppress information draw far more attention to it. In economics, the idea is closely related to the Lucas critique, Goodhart's law, and the cobra effect; in engineering and policy analysis it motivates risk assessment, cost–benefit analysis, and adaptive management.
