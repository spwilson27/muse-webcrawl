Religion

Religion is a range of social-cultural systems, including designated behaviors and practices, morals, beliefs, worldviews, texts, sanctified places, prophecies, ethics, or organizations, that generally relate humanity to supernatural, transcendental, and spiritual elements — although there is no scholarly consensus over what precisely constitutes a religion. Different religions may or may not contain various elements ranging from the divine, sacredness, faith, and a supernatural being or beings.

Many religions have organized behaviors, clergy, a definition of what constitutes adherence or membership, holy places, and scriptures. The practice of a religion may also include rituals, sermons, commemoration or veneration (of deities or saints), sacrifices, festivals, feasts, trance, meditations, prayer, music, art, service to others, ascetic behaviors, and pilgrimages.

Etymology and definitions

The English word religion derives from the Latin religio, whose own origin is disputed: Cicero derived it from relegere ("to go over again"), while Lactantius preferred religare ("to bind"). Scholars have long debated definitions. Substantive definitions focus on belief content (E. B. Tylor's "belief in spiritual beings"), functional definitions on what religion does for society (Émile Durkheim's system uniting adherents into a moral community), and family-resemblance approaches treat religion as a cluster of traits no one of which is essential.

History

Religious practices are evident from the Middle Paleolithic, in burials and cave art. The Neolithic revolution saw the growth of organized cults tied to agriculture. Written religious traditions emerged in the Bronze and Iron Ages: Vedic religion in India, ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian religions, and Israelite religion. The "Axial Age" (roughly 800–200 BCE) produced transformative movements across Eurasia — Buddhism, Jainism, Confucianism, Daoism, Greek philosophy, and Second Temple Judaism. Christianity and later Islam expanded from the Near East to become the world's two largest religions.

Major religious groups

Adherents in 2020 numbered approximately: Christians 2.3 billion, Muslims 1.9 billion, Hindus 1.2 billion, Buddhists around 320–500 million, practitioners of folk religions around 430 million, Jews around 15 million, and others including Sikhs, Jains, Baháʼís, and Shintoists. A growing share of the world's population — over a billion people — is religiously unaffiliated, though many retain some beliefs or practices.

Study of religion

Religious studies approaches religion through history, anthropology, sociology, psychology, and philosophy. Related fields include theology (the study of a religion from within), comparative religion, and the cognitive science of religion, which examines the mental mechanisms underlying religious thought.

Religion and society

Religion interacts with law and politics in varied ways: some states have an established religion, others enforce secularism, and religious freedom is protected as a human right under instruments such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Religion has been cited as a source of social cohesion, charitable provision, and moral codes, and also as a factor in conflicts, though the causes of most wars involving religious groups are mixed.
