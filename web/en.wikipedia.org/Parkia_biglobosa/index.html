Parkia biglobosa

Parkia biglobosa, the African locust bean, is a perennial deciduous tree in the legume family Fabaceae, native to the savannas and parklands of West and Central Africa. It grows 7 to 30 metres tall and is readily recognised by its bright red, ball-shaped flower heads, which hang on long stalks and are pollinated mainly by bats.

The tree is of great economic and cultural importance in West Africa. Its long seed pods contain a sweet, yellow pulp that is eaten or made into a drink, and seeds that are fermented to produce a pungent, protein-rich seasoning known variously as dawadawa, iru, or soumbala — an essential flavouring in regional cooking. The fermentation, traditionally carried out by women, is driven chiefly by Bacillus subtilis and produces one of Africa's oldest condiments.

Uses

Beyond food, nearly every part of the tree is used: bark, roots, and leaves in traditional medicine; husks as a source of potash for soap-making; the wood for tools and fuel; and the deep-rooted tree itself as a pillar of agroforestry parklands, where it improves soil fertility by fixing nitrogen and provides shade for crops beneath it. Because individual trees are valuable, they are deliberately retained when land is cleared for farming, shaping the characteristic parkland landscapes of the Sudanian zone.
