Coffea canephora

Coffea canephora (commonly known as robusta coffee) is a species of coffee plant in the family Rubiaceae. Native to central and western sub-Saharan Africa, it is one of the two main species cultivated for coffee production, alongside Coffea arabica. Robusta accounts for roughly 40% of world coffee production.

Description

The plant is a robust shrub or small tree growing up to 10 metres tall in the wild, though cultivated plants are pruned shorter. It has larger leaves than arabica and produces round cherries that take up to 11 months to mature. The beans are generally smaller and rounder than arabica beans.

Cultivation and use

Robusta is easier to grow than arabica: it tolerates warmer temperatures, lower altitudes, and is more resistant to pests and diseases such as coffee leaf rust. Its beans contain roughly twice the caffeine of arabica and produce a stronger, more bitter flavour with thicker crema, making robusta popular in espresso blends and instant coffee. Vietnam is the world's largest producer.

History

The species was identified in the Lomami River region of the Congo basin in the 19th century and spread through colonial cultivation to Southeast Asia and Brazil, where it is known as conilon.
