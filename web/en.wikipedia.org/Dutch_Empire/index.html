The Dutch Empire comprised the overseas colonies and trading posts established by the Dutch Republic and later the Kingdom of the Netherlands, largely under the chartered Dutch East India Company (VOC) and Dutch West India Company (WIC). Fuelled by the Dutch Golden Age, it made the small republic a leading maritime and commercial power of the seventeenth century.

Rise

The VOC, founded in 1602 and often described as the first multinational corporation, built a network of factories and colonies across Asia: Batavia (Jakarta) as its headquarters from 1619, the Spice Islands (Moluccas), Ceylon, Malacca, Formosa, and the trading post of Dejima in Japan. In the Atlantic world the WIC seized part of Portuguese Brazil (1630–1654), founded New Netherland in North America (including New Amsterdam, later New York), occupied the Cape of Good Hope as a refreshment station (1652) and took Caribbean islands and Guiana plantations worked by enslaved Africans — the Dutch played a major role in the Atlantic slave trade.

Decline

The Anglo-Dutch Wars, the loss of New Netherland to England (1664) and of Brazil to Portugal (1654), and the catastrophic Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–84) shrank Dutch power. The VOC was dissolved in 1799, its possessions passing to the state. In the nineteenth century the empire contracted to the Dutch East Indies (modern Indonesia), Suriname and the Dutch Caribbean; the East Indies were governed intensively, including the Cultivation System in Java.

End of empire

Indonesia proclaimed independence in 1945 and won Dutch recognition in 1949 after war; Dutch New Guinea followed in 1962–63. Suriname became independent in 1975. The remaining Caribbean islands remain within the Kingdom of the Netherlands, and Dutch is still an official language in Suriname and the Caribbean territories. The empire's legacy includes Dutch loanwords and legal traditions abroad, Cape Dutch's descendant Afrikaans, and continuing debate in the Netherlands over slavery and colonial violence.
