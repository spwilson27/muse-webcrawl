Visual perception

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Visual perception is the ability to detect light and interpret it as sight. It is the process by which the visual system acquires and makes sense of information from the environment, allowing organisms to build an internal representation of the surrounding world. In humans, visual perception begins when light enters the eye and is focused onto the retina, where photoreceptor cells convert photons into neural signals.

These signals travel along the optic nerve to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and onward to the visual cortex at the back of the brain, where features such as edges, motion, colour and depth are processed. Perception is not a passive recording: the brain actively constructs a stable scene from ambiguous input, using prior knowledge, context and eye movements.

The visual system

The human visual system consists of the eyes, the optic nerves, the optic chiasm, the optic tracts, the lateral geniculate bodies, the optic radiations and the visual cortex. About thirty percent of the cerebral cortex is involved in visual processing, more than for any other sense. Light reflected from objects is inverted by the lens and projected onto the retina; rods, active in dim light, and cones, responsible for colour vision, transduce the pattern of light into electrical impulses.

Processing streams

A widely accepted model divides cortical visual processing into two streams. The ventral stream, running into the temporal lobe, is concerned with object recognition and form — the "what" pathway. The dorsal stream, running into the parietal lobe, handles spatial awareness and the guidance of actions — the "where" or "how" pathway. Damage to these streams produces characteristic deficits such as visual agnosia or optic ataxia.

Perceptual phenomena

Visual perception exhibits a number of striking properties studied by psychologists since the nineteenth century. Colour constancy and size constancy keep objects looking stable despite changes in illumination and distance. The Gestalt psychologists identified principles of grouping — proximity, similarity, closure and continuity — by which the mind organises sensory elements into wholes. Optical illusions, such as the Müller-Lyer and Ponzo illusions, reveal the assumptions the visual system makes when interpreting depth and size.

Development and disorders

Infants' visual acuity improves rapidly over the first year of life, and critical periods exist during which normal input is required for proper development; deprivation can cause amblyopia. Common disorders of perception include colour blindness, prosopagnosia (face blindness), akinetopsia (motion blindness) and various forms of visual neglect following brain injury.
