Taphonomy

Taphonomy is the study of how organisms decay and become fossilized, and of the processes that affect animal and plant remains from the death of the organism through burial, preservation, and eventual recovery. The term was coined in 1940 by the Russian palaeontologist Ivan Yefremov, from the Greek taphos (burial) and nomos (law).

Processes

Taphonomic processes are commonly divided into two phases. Biostratinomy covers everything between death and burial: scavenging, disarticulation, transport by water or wind, weathering, and breakage. Diagenesis covers the chemical and physical changes after burial, including mineralization (permineralization and replacement), compression, and dissolution.

Significance

Taphonomy matters because the fossil record is a biased sample of past life. Soft-bodied organisms and creatures in environments unfavourable to burial are systematically underrepresented, while durable hard parts in quiet depositional settings are overrepresented. Understanding these biases allows palaeontologists to reconstruct ancient ecosystems more accurately and to recognize exceptional deposits (Lagerstätten) where soft tissues survive.

Taphonomic methods are also applied in archaeology and forensic science: distinguishing cut marks from tooth marks on bone helped establish how early hominins obtained meat, and forensic taphonomy studies how human remains change after death to help reconstruct events at crime scenes.
