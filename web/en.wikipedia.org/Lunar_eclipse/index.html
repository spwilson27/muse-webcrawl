Lunar eclipse

Lunar eclipse
Cause: Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon; the Moon crosses Earth's shadow
Occurs: Only at full moon, near a lunar node
Types: Total, partial, penumbral
Maximum totality: about 1 hour 47 minutes
[Image placeholder: The Moon turning copper-red during a total lunar eclipse]

A lunar eclipse is an astronomical event that occurs when the Moon moves into the Earth's shadow, causing the Moon to be darkened. Such an alignment happens at full moon when the Moon is near one of the nodes of its orbit. Unlike a solar eclipse, which is visible only from a small area, a lunar eclipse can be seen from anywhere on the night side of the Earth and lasts for hours.

Types

In a total lunar eclipse the whole Moon passes through Earth's umbra; sunlight refracted through Earth's atmosphere gives it a coppery red colour — the "Blood Moon". In a partial eclipse only part of the Moon enters the umbra, and in a penumbral eclipse the Moon dims slightly as it passes through the outer shadow.

Frequency

There are at least two and at most five lunar eclipses (of some kind) each year. Eclipses recur in cycles called saros series, repeating after 18 years and 11 days.
