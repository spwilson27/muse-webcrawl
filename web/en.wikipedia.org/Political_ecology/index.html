Political ecology

Political ecology is the study of the relationships between political, economic and social factors with environmental issues and changes. Unlike apolitical ecological perspectives, political ecology politicises environmental problems, arguing that ecological change cannot be understood without consideration of the political and economic structures within which it is embedded.

Origins and scope

The term was coined by Frank Thone in 1935 and was taken up in the 1970s in the work of anthropologists and geographers such as Eric Wolf, who used it in 1972, and later Piers Blaikie, Harold Brookfield, Michael Watts and Richard Peet. The field developed in dialogue with cultural ecology, human geography and development studies, focusing initially on land degradation, resource access and environmental conflict in the developing world.

Approaches

Political ecology typically examines how environmental degradation and conservation are shaped by power relations, markets, state policies and local livelihoods. Characteristic methods include "chains of explanation" linking local land users to regional and global political-economic forces, and concepts such as accumulation by dispossession, environmental justice, and access theory (developed by Jesse Ribot and Nancy Peluso). Later "liberation ecologies" and feminist political ecology broadened the field to gender, race and everyday practices, and to urban and industrial environments in the developed world.

Criticism

Critics, notably Andrew Vayda and Bradley Walters, have argued that some political-ecological studies begin with political conclusions and slight the ecological processes they claim to explain. Others, such as Paul Robbins, present the field as both a critical "hatchet" against apolitical explanations and a "seed" for constructing better environmental policies.
