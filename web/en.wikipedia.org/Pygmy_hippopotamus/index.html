Pygmy hippopotamus

The pygmy hippopotamus (Choeropsis liberiensis, sometimes classified as Hexaprotodon liberiensis) is a small hippopotamid native to the forests and swamps of West Africa, principally Liberia, with small populations in Sierra Leone, Guinea, and Côte d'Ivoire. It is one of only two living hippo species, alongside the much larger common hippopotamus. Unlike its social relative, the pygmy hippo is solitary and nocturnal, spending its days resting in rivers and swamps and emerging at night to browse on forest vegetation.

Description

Adults stand about 75–100 cm (2.5–3.3 ft) at the shoulder and weigh 180–275 kg (400–600 lb)—roughly a tenth the mass of a common hippo. The barrel-shaped body has smooth, dark, hairless skin that secretes a pinkish fluid sometimes called "blood sweat", which acts as a moisturizer and sunscreen. The head is rounder than that of the common hippo, with eyes set more to the sides, an adaptation to life on land rather than in water. Its teeth show two pairs of upper incisors, distinguishing it from the common hippo's three.

Behavior and reproduction

Pygmy hippos are solitary or live in pairs, avoiding each other except to mate. They follow well-worn trails through dense forest to feeding grounds, eating ferns, leaves, grasses, and fallen fruit. After a gestation of about six to seven months, the female gives birth to a single calf, usually in water or on land in seclusion; calves weigh about 4.5–6 kg. Lifespan in captivity can exceed 50 years.

Conservation

The species is listed as Endangered by the IUCN, with an estimated wild population of only 2,000–3,000 mature individuals, threatened by habitat loss to logging, mining, and agriculture, and by hunting for bushmeat. It is a popular zoo animal, and the captive population descends largely from animals collected in Liberia in the early twentieth century. The species gained worldwide fame in 2024 through Moo Deng, a viral pygmy hippo calf born at a Thai zoo.
