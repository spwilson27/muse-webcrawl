Medieval Warm Period

The Medieval Warm Period (MWP), also called the Medieval Climate Optimum or Medieval Climate Anomaly, was a time of warm climate in the North Atlantic region lasting from roughly AD 950 to 1250. It was followed by the cooler Little Ice Age.

Evidence and extent

Evidence for medieval warmth includes Norse colonisation of Greenland, vineyards in England, high treelines in several mountain ranges, and proxy records such as tree rings, ice cores, and lake sediments. Modern reconstructions find that warmth was regionally variable and not synchronous worldwide: different regions peaked at different times, and the period's global average temperature remained below late-20th- and early-21st-century levels. A 2019 analysis concluded the MWP was not a globally coherent warm period, unlike current warming.

Causes

Proposed drivers include relatively high solar activity, fewer major volcanic eruptions, and changes in ocean circulation such as a persistent La Niña-like Pacific state and a warm phase of North Atlantic variability.

Historical effects

In Europe the warm centuries coincided with agricultural expansion, population growth, and cathedral building; elsewhere the same centuries brought severe droughts in the American Southwest associated with the decline of Ancestral Pueblo settlements. The period's end brought the famines and instability of the 14th century as climate cooled.
