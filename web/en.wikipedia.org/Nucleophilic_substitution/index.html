Nucleophilic substitution

Nucleophilic substitution
Reaction typeSubstitution reaction
Main mechanismsSN1, SN2 (aliphatic); SNAr (aromatic)
Key conceptsNucleophile, leaving group, carbocation, Walden inversion

Nucleophilic substitution is a fundamental class of reactions in organic chemistry in which an electron-rich nucleophile attacks an electron-deficient electrophilic atom — typically a carbon bearing a leaving group — and replaces that leaving group. In the general scheme, Nu− + R–LG → R–Nu + LG−, where LG is the leaving group, often a halide.

The SN2 mechanism

In the SN2 (substitution nucleophilic bimolecular) reaction, bond formation and bond breaking occur simultaneously in a single concerted step. The nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group ("backside attack"), passing through a trigonal-bipyramidal transition state and producing complete inversion of configuration at the carbon — the classic Walden inversion. The rate law is second order: rate = k[R–LG][Nu]. SN2 reactions are fastest with methyl and primary substrates and are strongly hindered by steric bulk.

The SN1 mechanism

In the SN1 (substitution nucleophilic unimolecular) reaction, the leaving group departs first, generating a planar carbocation intermediate that the nucleophile can attack from either face, giving a racemic mixture of products at a stereogenic centre. The rate depends only on the concentration of substrate. Tertiary and resonance-stabilised substrates favour SN1, and carbocation rearrangements may occur. The SN1/SN2 framework was developed in the 1930s by Edward Hughes and Christopher Ingold at University College London.

Factors affecting the mechanism

  • Substrate structure: methyl and primary favour SN2; tertiary favours SN1.
  • Nucleophile: strong, small nucleophiles favour SN2; weak nucleophiles (such as the solvent itself, in solvolysis) favour SN1.
  • Leaving group: good leaving groups (I− > Br− > Cl− ≫ F−; weak bases) accelerate both pathways.
  • Solvent: polar protic solvents stabilise ions and favour SN1; polar aprotic solvents (DMSO, acetonitrile, DMF) enhance nucleophilicity and favour SN2.

Related reactions

Aromatic rings undergo nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) through addition–elimination via a Meisenheimer complex when activated by electron-withdrawing groups. Acyl compounds undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution through a tetrahedral intermediate — the basis of ester hydrolysis and amide formation. Competing elimination reactions (E1 and E2) frequently accompany substitution, especially with bulky bases and tertiary substrates.
