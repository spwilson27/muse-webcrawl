Alternative media

Alternative media are media sources and practices that differ from the established or dominant types of media in their content, mode of production, or means of distribution. Alternative media generally aim to give voice to perspectives excluded from mainstream, corporate, or state media — for example those of social movements, minority communities, and dissident political viewpoints — and often experiment with participatory production, collective ownership, and non-commercial funding. The term covers underground and community newspapers, pirate and community radio, public-access television, zines, independent film and video, and, more recently, independent websites, blogs, and social-media outlets.

Characteristics

Scholars such as Chris Atton describe alternative media as defined less by any single format than by their challenge to dominant media relations: de-professionalized production ("ordinary people" as producers), horizontal rather than hierarchical organization, and content oriented toward social change. John Downing's work on "radical media" traces a long history from clandestine Reformation pamphlets and the abolitionist press to samizdat under communist regimes.

Forms and history

  • Print: the radical press of the 19th century, the underground newspapers of the 1960s counterculture (e.g., Los Angeles Free Press, Oz), feminist and ethnic-community presses, and photocopied zines of punk culture.
  • Radio and television: pirate radio (Radio Caroline), U.S. community stations such as Pacifica's KPFA (founded 1949), listener-sponsored and low-power FM stations, and public-access cable channels.
  • Film and video: workers' film leagues, guerrilla television of the 1970s, and independent documentary collectives.
  • Digital: Indymedia, launched for the 1999 Seattle WTO protests with the slogan "Don't hate the media, become the media," pioneered open publishing; later forms include independent news sites, podcasts, and movement livestreams.

Debates

Alternative media have been praised for democratizing communication and criticized for limited reach, precarious funding, and occasional lapses in verification. Since the 2010s the label "alternative" has also been claimed by partisan and conspiracy outlets opposed to legacy media, prompting media scholars to distinguish community-based alternative media from "alt-right" and hyper-partisan media ecosystems that mimic the form while serving different political projects.
