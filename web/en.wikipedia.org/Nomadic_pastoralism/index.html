Nomadic pastoralism

Nomadic pastoralism is a form of pastoralism in which herders move with their livestock from place to place in search of pasture and water, without a permanent fixed settlement. It is an adaptation to environments—arid lands, steppes, tundra, and mountains—where crop agriculture is unreliable.

Characteristics

Nomadic pastoralists depend on domesticated herbivores such as sheep, goats, cattle, camels, yaks, horses, and reindeer for milk, meat, wool, hides, and transport. Movement patterns may be seasonal (transhumance, sometimes treated as a semi-nomadic variant) or follow irregular routes governed by rainfall. Social organization is often kinship-based, and herds function as stored wealth.

Distribution and pressures

Historically prominent nomadic pastoralist societies include the Mongols of the Central Asian steppe, Bedouin of Arabia, Maasai of East Africa, and Sámi reindeer herders of northern Europe. In modern times, nomadic pastoralism has declined under pressure from national borders, land privatization, sedentarization policies, and climate change, though tens of millions of people still practise it.
