Tudor navy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Tudor navy was the naval force of the Kingdom of England under the ruling Tudor dynasty (1485–1603). Although there had been organised English naval forces since the time of Alfred the Great, the Tudor period saw the creation of a permanent standing navy, often called the Navy Royal, administered first by the Council of the Marine (established 1546) and later by the Navy Board.

History

Henry VII began the process of building a permanent royal fleet, constructing new dock facilities at Portsmouth and commissioning large warships such as the Regent and the Sovereign. His son Henry VIII dramatically expanded the fleet, founding dockyards at Woolwich and Deptford, establishing Trinity House in 1514, and overseeing the construction of famous ships including the Mary Rose and the Henri Grâce à Dieu ("Great Harry").

Under Elizabeth I, the navy reached its most celebrated moment with the defeat of the Spanish Armada in 1588. Commanders such as Francis Drake, John Hawkins and Walter Raleigh combined royal service with privateering against Spanish shipping in the Atlantic and the Spanish Main.

Ships and technology

Tudor warships evolved from high-charged carracks, designed for boarding actions, into lower, sleeker race-built galleons better suited to gunnery duels. Bronze muzzle-loading cannon replaced wrought-iron breech-loaders, and ships increasingly fought with broadsides rather than by grappling. The period also saw the development of purpose-built warships rather than converted merchantmen.

Administration

The Council of the Marine, created in 1546, managed the navy's day-to-day affairs: shipbuilding, victualling, repair and finance. Its officers included the Lieutenant of the Admiralty, the Treasurer, the Surveyor, the Comptroller and the Clerk of the Ships — an administrative structure that survived, in evolved form, for centuries.

Legacy

The Tudor navy laid the institutional and technological foundations of the later Royal Navy. Its mixture of state dockyards, professional administration and ocean-going warships made England a durable maritime power.
