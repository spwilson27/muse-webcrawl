Imamate of Futa Jallon

The Imamate of Futa Jallon (also spelled Fouta Djallon or Fuuta Jaloo) was a West African theocratic state based in the Futa Jallon highlands of modern Guinea. It existed from about 1725/1727 until 1896, when it was defeated and absorbed into French West Africa.

History

The state arose from a jihad launched by Fulani (Fulɓe) Muslim clerics against the non-Muslim and nominally Muslim rulers of the region, culminating in victory at the Battle of Talansan around 1727. Karamokho Alfa was proclaimed the first Almamy (imam). He was succeeded by Ibrahim Sori, under whom the state consolidated and expanded. The Imamate was organised as a federation of nine provinces (diwe), centred on Timbo, and was administered according to Islamic law with Quranic schools and mosques across the highlands. Authority alternated, often contentiously, between the two ruling families descended from the founders — the Alfaya and the Soriya factions.

Society and decline

Futa Jallon became a major centre of Islamic scholarship and trade, but its economy also rested substantially on slave labour and slave-raiding against neighbouring peoples. Internal factional rivalry weakened the state in the 19th century; after the death of the last Almamy, Bokar Biro, following defeat by French forces at the Battle of Porédaka in November 1896, the Imamate was dissolved into the French protectorate system.
