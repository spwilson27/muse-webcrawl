Muhi al-Din Muhammad (3 November 1618 – 3 March 1707), commonly known as Aurangzeb and by his regnal title Alamgir ("Conqueror of the World"), was the sixth Mughal emperor, reigning from 1658 until his death in 1707. His reign of 49 years was the longest of any Mughal emperor, and under him the empire reached its greatest territorial extent, ruling over almost the entire Indian subcontinent.

Accession

The third son of Shah Jahan, Aurangzeb proved an able administrator and commander as viceroy of the Deccan. When Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657, Aurangzeb defeated his brothers in a war of succession — notably routing the imperial army under his elder brother Dara Shikoh at Samugarh in 1658 — imprisoned his father in Agra Fort, and crowned himself emperor.

Reign

Austere and devout, Aurangzeb sought to rule according to Islamic law: he commissioned the Fatawa Alamgiri, a great compendium of Hanafi jurisprudence, reimposed the jizya tax on non-Muslims in 1679, and patronised calligraphy, copying the Quran himself. These policies remain controversial among historians, who debate their scope and motives. Militarily, his annexation of Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687) completed the conquest of the Deccan, but the long war against the Marathas under Shivaji and his successors drained the treasury and kept the aged emperor campaigning in the south for the last quarter-century of his reign.

Legacy

Aurangzeb died at Ahmednagar in 1707 and was buried in a simple open grave at Khuldabad, in accordance with his wishes. The empire he left was at its largest but overextended; it fragmented rapidly under his successors. He remains one of the most debated figures of Indian history.
