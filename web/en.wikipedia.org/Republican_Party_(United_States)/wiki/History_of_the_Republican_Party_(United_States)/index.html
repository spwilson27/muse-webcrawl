The Republican Party, also known as the Grand Old Party (GOP), was founded in 1854 by anti-slavery activists opposed to the expansion of slavery into the western territories. Within a decade it had elected Abraham Lincoln, won the Civil War, and become one of the two dominant parties in American history — a position it has held, with shifting coalitions and ideologies, ever since.

Founding and Civil War (1854–1865)

The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, which allowed settlers to decide on slavery, shattered the Whig Party and spurred "anti-Nebraska" meetings, including one at Ripon, Wisconsin, traditionally credited as the party's birthplace. The Republicans united former Whigs, Free Soilers and anti-slavery Democrats against the "Slave Power". After John C. Frémont's losing 1856 campaign, Abraham Lincoln won the presidency in 1860, prompting Southern secession and the Civil War. Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery, and the party led the Union to victory.

Reconstruction and the Gilded Age (1865–1900)

During Reconstruction the party pushed through the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments, protecting citizenship and Black male suffrage, before Northern commitment faded after 1877. In the Gilded Age, Republicans generally favoured protective tariffs, sound money, pensions for veterans and business growth, dominating national politics from their Northern and Midwestern base; presidents included Grant, Hayes, Garfield, Arthur, Benjamin Harrison and McKinley.

Progressive era and New Deal realignment (1900–1945)

Theodore Roosevelt championed progressive reforms and trust-busting; his 1912 "Bull Moose" split handed the White House to Democrat Woodrow Wilson. After a conservative resurgence in the 1920s, the Great Depression and Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal relegated Republicans to minority status, though a conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats constrained liberal legislation from the late 1930s.

Postwar era to the present

Dwight D. Eisenhower's moderate Republicanism won two terms in the 1950s. Barry Goldwater's 1964 campaign reoriented the party toward modern conservatism, and Richard Nixon's "Southern strategy" began the South's long shift to the GOP. Ronald Reagan (1981–1989) consolidated a coalition of economic conservatives, social conservatives and foreign-policy hawks. Later decades brought the 1994 "Republican Revolution" in Congress, the presidencies of George H. W. Bush and George W. Bush, and from 2016 the party's transformation under Donald Trump toward populism, trade scepticism and immigration restriction — illustrating how the GOP's coalition and ideology have repeatedly been remade across its history.
