The Franks (Latin: Franci) were a confederation of Germanic tribes that emerged in the 3rd century AD along the lower and middle Rhine. From their heartland they conquered Roman Gaul and built the Frankish Empire, the dominant power of early medieval western Europe and the ancestor of both France and the Holy Roman Empire. The name of modern France derives from them.

Origins

The Franks first appear in Roman sources around 250 AD as groups raiding across the Rhine frontier. Two main branches are distinguished: the Salian Franks, who settled in the Roman province of Germania Inferior and later Toxandria, and the Ripuarian Franks around Cologne. Under the treaty arrangements of the late Roman Empire, many Franks served as foederati (allied troops) and rose high in Roman service.

The Merovingian kingdom

Clovis I (r. 481–511) unified the Frankish tribes, defeated the last Roman ruler in Gaul, conquered the Visigothic kingdom of Toulouse, and converted to Nicene Christianity — an act that aligned the Franks with the Gallo-Roman population and the Church. His descendants, the Merovingians, ruled a realm repeatedly divided among royal sons.

The Carolingians and empire

In the 7th and 8th centuries power passed to the Carolingian mayors of the palace. Charles Martel halted Muslim expansion at the Battle of Tours (732), and his son Pepin the Short deposed the last Merovingian in 751. Pepin's son Charlemagne (r. 768–814) conquered much of western and central Europe and was crowned Emperor by Pope Leo III in 800. After his death the empire was divided by the Treaty of Verdun (843) into West, Middle, and East Francia — the seeds of France and Germany.

Society and law

Frankish law is recorded in codes such as the Salic law (Lex Salica), with its famous clause later invoked to bar women from inheriting the French crown. Frankish society blended Germanic kinship and war-band traditions with Roman administrative and ecclesiastical structures.
