Vaginal discharge

Normal discharge is produced by the cervix and vaginal walls and varies across the menstrual cycle.

Vaginal discharge is a mixture of liquid, cells and bacteria that lubricate and protect the vagina. It is produced by glands in the cervix and the walls of the vagina, together with sloughed epithelial cells. Discharge is a normal physiological process in women of reproductive age; changes in its amount, colour, consistency or odour may indicate infection or another disorder.

Normal discharge

Healthy discharge is typically clear to milky white, mild-smelling, and varies with the menstrual cycle under the influence of estrogen: it is scant after menstruation, becomes clear and stretchy (like raw egg white) around ovulation to facilitate sperm passage, and thickens again in the luteal phase. Amounts increase in pregnancy and with sexual arousal. Before puberty and after menopause, discharge is normally minimal; new discharge in these groups warrants evaluation.

Abnormal discharge

Abnormal discharge — accompanied by itching, burning, irritation, redness, pain, or a strong or fishy odour — is a common reason for medical consultation. Major causes include:

  • Bacterial vaginosis: thin grey-white discharge with a fishy odour, caused by overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria and loss of lactobacilli; the commonest cause in women of reproductive age.
  • Vulvovaginal candidiasis ("thrush"): thick, white, cottage-cheese-like discharge with intense itching, caused by Candida species, usually C. albicans.
  • Trichomoniasis: frothy yellow-green, malodorous discharge from the sexually transmitted protozoan Trichomonas vaginalis.
  • Cervicitis: mucopurulent discharge from chlamydia or gonorrhoea infection of the cervix.
  • Other causes: retained foreign bodies (e.g., tampons), atrophic vaginitis after menopause, irritant or allergic reactions, and rarely malignancy.

Diagnosis

Assessment includes history (onset, symptoms, sexual history, medications, hygiene practices such as douching, which is discouraged), pelvic examination, measurement of vaginal pH (normally 3.8–4.5), microscopy of a wet preparation, and laboratory tests such as nucleic acid amplification tests for chlamydia, gonorrhoea and trichomonas. The Amsel criteria and Nugent score are used for bacterial vaginosis.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the cause: metronidazole or clindamycin for bacterial vaginosis, azole antifungals for candidiasis, and metronidazole (with partner treatment) for trichomoniasis; sexually transmitted infections are treated with appropriate antibiotics and partner notification. Self-diagnosis is frequently inaccurate, so persistent or recurrent symptoms merit examination.
