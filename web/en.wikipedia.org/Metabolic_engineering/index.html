Metabolic engineering

Metabolic engineering is the practice of optimising genetic and regulatory processes within cells to increase the cell's production of a desired substance. It combines principles from chemical engineering, molecular biology and systems biology, and is a core discipline of industrial biotechnology.

Approach

Metabolic engineers modify an organism's metabolic pathways by overexpressing or deleting genes, introducing heterologous pathways from other organisms, and tuning gene expression. Analysis tools include metabolic flux analysis, genome-scale metabolic models and 13C tracer experiments, which quantify how carbon flows through a network of reactions. Iterative design–build–test–learn cycles refine production strains.

Applications

Classic successes include the production of insulin and other proteins in microbes, the engineering of yeast to produce the antimalarial drug precursor artemisinic acid, and the manufacture of 1,3-propanediol, biofuels, amino acids, vitamins and bioplastics. Engineered strains of Escherichia coli and the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae are the most common chassis organisms.

Relationship to synthetic biology

Metabolic engineering overlaps strongly with synthetic biology, which supplies standardised genetic parts and genome-editing tools such as CRISPR; metabolic engineering focuses on whole-network optimisation for a production phenotype.
