Runabout (car)

A runabout is a small, inexpensive, lightweight car body style popular in the United States from about 1900 to about 1915. Runabouts were characterised by their basic, open design with a single row of seats. Most had no windshield, top, or doors, and many had only a single headlamp. The term is also used in boating for a small powerboat.

Characteristics

The runabout was the cheapest open configuration of the early automobile, essentially a motorised buggy. Typical examples carried two occupants, with the engine under the seat or in front, and some models offered a detachable rear "tonneau" or a small "mother-in-law" seat. Well-known runabouts include the Oldsmobile Curved Dash — the first mass-produced automobile — and the two-seat versions of the early Ford Model T.

Later usage

After the First World War the term faded as closed cars and roadsters took over, but "runabout" has been revived occasionally as a model name or trim description, for example on the Ford Pinto Runabout hatchback of the 1970s and on small three-door hatchbacks marketed in Australia. The name is also applied retrospectively to light cyclecars and voiturettes of the Edwardian era.
