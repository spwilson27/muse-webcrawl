Nullification (United States Constitution)

Nullification, in United States constitutional history, is the legal theory that a US state has the right to nullify, or invalidate, any federal law that the state deems to have been enacted in violation of the United States Constitution. The theory is based on a view that the states, as parties to the constitutional compact that created the federal government, retain ultimate authority to decide the limits of federal power.

Origins

The doctrine is most associated with the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions of 1798, drafted secretly by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison in response to the Alien and Sedition Acts. The resolutions argued that the federal government was the agent of a compact among the states, and that the states could judge when the federal government had exceeded its delegated powers.

The nullification crisis

The doctrine reached its most famous expression in the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33, when South Carolina, led by John C. Calhoun, declared the federal tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null and void within the state. President Andrew Jackson responded with the Force Bill and a proclamation denouncing nullification, and the crisis ended in compromise.

Legal status

Nullification has never been upheld by the federal courts. The Supreme Court rejected related claims in cases such as Ableman v. Booth (1859) and Cooper v. Aaron (1958), and the defeat of the Confederacy in the American Civil War is generally regarded as having settled the question against the theory.
