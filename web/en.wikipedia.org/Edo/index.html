Edo

Edo (Japanese: 江戸, "bay entrance"), renamed Tokyo in 1868, was the seat of power of the Tokugawa shogunate, which ruled Japan from 1603 to 1868. During the Edo period the formerly small castle town grew into the largest city in the world, with a population exceeding one million by the early 18th century.

Edo in the 17th century, from a woodblock print

History

Edo was first fortified by Ōta Dōkan, who built Edo Castle in 1457. Tokugawa Ieyasu made it his headquarters in 1590 and, after becoming shōgun in 1603, the de facto capital of Japan — although the emperor remained in Kyoto. The sankin-kōtai system, requiring daimyō to reside in Edo in alternate years, drove the city's growth and created a vast service economy.

The city

Edo was organised around the castle in a spiral of moats and canals. The samurai estates of the Yamanote high ground contrasted with Shitamachi, the densely packed "low city" of merchants and artisans. Fires were so frequent they were called the "flowers of Edo"; the Great Fire of Meireki in 1657 destroyed much of the city and killed tens of thousands. Despite recurrent disaster, Edo developed sophisticated markets, publishing, kabuki theatre, and the ukiyo-e woodblock art for which it is famous.

End of Edo

After the arrival of Commodore Perry's ships in 1853 and the collapse of the shogunate in the Boshin War, the city was renamed Tokyo ("Eastern Capital") in 1868, when Emperor Meiji moved his residence there. The period name "Edo" survives in modern usage, and present-day Tokyo's districts, canals, and street patterns still trace the shogun's city.
