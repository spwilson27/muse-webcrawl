The Society of the Spectacle (French: La société du spectacle) is a 1967 work of philosophy and Marxist critical theory by Guy Debord, a founding member of the Situationist International. Composed of 221 short theses, it argues that modern capitalist society has replaced authentic lived experience with its representation: "All that once was directly lived has become mere representation."

Argument

For Debord, the spectacle is not a collection of images but "a social relation among people, mediated by images." Under advanced capitalism, commodities and media colonize social life: workers produce a world that confronts them as spectacle, leisure becomes consumption of images, and even dissent is recuperated into marketable form. Building on Marx's theory of commodity fetishism and Lukács's concept of reification, Debord distinguishes the concentrated spectacle of dictatorships from the diffuse spectacle of consumer democracies, predicting their fusion into an "integrated spectacle" — a prophecy he elaborated in his 1988 Comments on the Society of the Spectacle.

Influence

Published months before the events of May 1968 in France, the book (with Raoul Vaneigem's The Revolution of Everyday Life) supplied slogans and ideas to the student and worker revolts; Situationist graffiti drawn from it appeared across Paris. Debord made a film version in 1973. The book has remained a touchstone for critiques of mass media, advertising, celebrity culture, and social media, and its key concepts — spectacle, recuperation, détournement, and the dérive — continue to circulate in art and political theory.
