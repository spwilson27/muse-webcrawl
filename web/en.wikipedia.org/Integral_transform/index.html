Integral transform

In mathematics, an integral transform is a type of transform that maps a function from its original function space into another function space via integration, where some of the properties of the original function might be more easily characterized and manipulated than in the original function space. The transformed function can generally be mapped back to the original function space using the inverse transform.

General form

An integral transform is any transform T of the form (Tf)(u) = ∫ f(t) K(t, u) dt, where the function K of two variables is called the kernel or nucleus of the transform. Different transforms are specified by the choice of kernel and the limits of integration. Some kernels have associated inverse kernels, allowing the original function to be recovered.

Examples

  • Fourier transform — kernel e−2πiθt; converts between time and frequency domains.
  • Laplace transform — kernel e−st; widely used for differential equations and control theory.
  • Mellin transform — kernel ts−1.
  • Hankel transform — kernel involving Bessel functions; used in problems with cylindrical symmetry.
  • Hilbert transform, Radon transform (used in tomography), Abel transform, and the Hartley transform.

Motivation

Integral transforms are useful because they turn operations that are difficult in the original domain into simpler ones: differentiation becomes multiplication under the Laplace and Fourier transforms, and convolution becomes pointwise multiplication. Solving a problem in the transformed domain and then inverting the transform is a standard strategy in mathematical physics and engineering. The discrete analogue of an integral transform is given by infinite series and transforms such as the Z-transform.
