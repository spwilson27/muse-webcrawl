Military Demarcation Line

The Military Demarcation Line (MDL), sometimes referred to as the Armistice Line, is the land border between North Korea and South Korea. It was established by the Korean Armistice Agreement of 27 July 1953, which ended the fighting of the Korean War. The line runs 248 km (154 mi) across the Korean Peninsula, roughly following the 38th parallel but shaped by the positions held by the opposing forces at the ceasefire. A 4 km-wide Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) straddles it, 2 km on each side.

Establishment

The Armistice Agreement fixed the MDL where the front lines stood in July 1953: the North gained territory in the east including the Kaesong area south of the 38th parallel, while the South gained a larger area in the east around the Iron Triangle and the Punchbowl. The line was marked by 1,292 signs along its length. Because the armistice was a military ceasefire rather than a peace treaty, the two Koreas remain technically at war, and the MDL functions as their de facto border.

Features and incidents

The DMZ around the line is one of the most heavily fortified borders in the world, with minefields, anti-tank barriers, and large troop concentrations on both sides, even though the zone itself is largely returned to nature and shelters rare wildlife. At the Joint Security Area at Panmunjom, soldiers of both sides stand metres apart around the blue conference buildings straddling the line. Incidents along the MDL have included infiltration tunnels dug by the North (four discovered between 1974 and 1990), the 1976 axe murder incident, defections such as the 2017 dash across the line at the JSA, and periodic artillery and small-arms exchanges.

Maritime extension

The armistice left the sea boundary unsettled; the UN Command drew the Northern Limit Line in the Yellow Sea, which North Korea does not recognise, leading to naval clashes in 1999, 2002, and 2009 and the shelling of Yeonpyeong Island in 2010.
