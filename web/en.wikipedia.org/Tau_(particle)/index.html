Tau (particle)

The tau (τ), also called the tau lepton, tau particle, or tauon, is an elementary particle similar to the electron, with negative electric charge and spin of 1/2. Together with the electron, the muon, and the three neutrinos, it is a lepton. Like all elementary particles with half-integer spin, the tau has a corresponding antiparticle, the antitau.

The tau is the heaviest lepton. Its mass is 1776.86 MeV/c², about 3,477 times the mass of the electron and 17 times that of the muon. Its lifetime is only 2.9×10⁻¹³ seconds, so it decays before it can be directly observed in most detectors, and is detected through its decay products.

History

The tau was discovered between 1974 and 1977 by a team led by Martin Lewis Perl at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC) and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, using the SPEAR electron-positron storage ring. Perl shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The particle was initially puzzling because it appeared to violate lepton number conservation until it was understood that tau decays produce a tau neutrino, a third neutrino flavor.

Decay

The tau is the only lepton heavy enough to decay into hadrons as well as lighter leptons. About 65 percent of tau decays are hadronic, most often producing pions, while about 17.4 percent decay to an electron and neutrinos and 17.8 percent to a muon and neutrinos.
