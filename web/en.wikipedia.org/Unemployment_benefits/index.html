Unemployment benefits

Unemployment benefits, also called unemployment insurance, unemployment payment, unemployment compensation, or simply unemployment, are payments made by governmental authorities to unemployed individuals. Depending on the country and the status of the person, these amounts may be small, covering only basic needs, or may compensate the lost time proportionally to the previous earned salary.

Unemployment benefits are generally given only to those registering as becoming unemployed through no fault of their own, and often on conditions ensuring that they seek work. In many jurisdictions the compensation is administered as part of a broader social insurance system financed by payroll taxes levied on employers and employees.

History

Trade unions frequently established out-of-work funds in the nineteenth century, and the first modern national unemployment insurance scheme was introduced in the United Kingdom with the National Insurance Act 1911 under Chancellor of the Exchequer David Lloyd George. Other countries followed: Germany introduced a national scheme in 1927, and the United States established unemployment insurance through the Social Security Act of 1935, creating a federal-state system that remains in place.

Eligibility and financing

Typical conditions include a minimum period of insured employment, involuntary job loss, registration with an employment service, and active job search. Benefits usually replace a fraction of prior earnings, often between 40 and 60 percent, for a limited duration; in the United States most states pay up to 26 weeks of regular benefits, with federal extensions during recessions. Systems are usually financed by contributions on wages, sometimes experience-rated so that employers whose workers claim more pay higher rates.

Economic effects

Economists view unemployment insurance as an automatic stabilizer: it sustains consumption during downturns and allows workers time to find jobs matching their skills. Critics argue generous benefits can lengthen job search and raise reservation wages; empirical studies generally find modest disincentive effects that increase in slack labour markets when jobs are scarce.
