Nutrient pollution

Nutrient pollution, a form of water pollution, refers to contamination by excessive inputs of nutrients. It is a primary cause of eutrophication of surface waters (lakes, rivers and coastal waters), in which excess nutrients, usually nitrogen or phosphorus, stimulate algal growth.

Sources of nutrient pollution include surface runoff from farm fields and pastures, discharges from septic tanks and feedlots, and emissions from combustion. Raw sewage is a large contributor and, in the developing world, treated sewage effluent is also a major source.

Causes

The excess nutrients, usually nitrogen and phosphorus, most commonly come from:

  • Agricultural fertilisers applied in excess of crop needs
  • Animal manure from concentrated livestock operations
  • Municipal wastewater and stormwater runoff
  • Atmospheric deposition of nitrogen compounds from fossil-fuel combustion

Effects

Excess nutrients cause dense growth of algae and other aquatic plants. When the algae die and decompose, the process consumes dissolved oxygen, producing hypoxic "dead zones" in which most aquatic life cannot survive. Notable examples include the dead zone in the northern Gulf of Mexico fed by the Mississippi River basin, and recurring hypoxia in the Baltic Sea and Chesapeake Bay. Harmful algal blooms can also produce toxins that sicken people and animals and contaminate drinking water.

Mitigation

Controls include nutrient management on farms, cover crops and buffer strips, wetland restoration, upgraded wastewater treatment with biological nutrient removal, and regulation under frameworks such as the U.S. Clean Water Act and the European Union's Water Framework Directive.
