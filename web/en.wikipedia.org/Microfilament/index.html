Microfilament

Microfilaments, also called actin filaments, are protein filaments in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells that form part of the cytoskeleton. They are primarily composed of polymers of actin, but are heavily modified by and interact with numerous other proteins in the cell. Microfilaments are usually about 7 nm in diameter and made up of two strands of actin polymer wound in a spiral, which is why they are also known as the actin double helix.

Structure

Each microfilament is a helical polymer of globular actin (G-actin) subunits assembled into filamentous actin (F-actin). The filament has polarity: a fast-growing "barbed" (plus) end and a slower-growing "pointed" (minus) end. Assembly is driven by ATP hydrolysis, and at steady state subunits "treadmill" through the filament.

Functions

  • Maintaining cell shape and mechanical resistance to deformation
  • Cell motility, including crawling and amoeboid movement
  • Cytokinesis — the contractile ring that divides a cell in two
  • Muscle contraction, in association with myosin motors
  • Cytoplasmic streaming and intracellular transport
  • Forming microvilli and other surface projections

Regulation

Actin dynamics are controlled by many actin-binding proteins: profilin promotes assembly, cofilin severs and depolymerizes filaments, the Arp2/3 complex nucleates branched networks, and formins build unbranched cables. Toxins such as cytochalasins and phalloidin (from the death cap mushroom) act by capping or stabilizing filaments.
