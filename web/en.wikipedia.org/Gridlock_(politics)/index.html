Gridlock (politics)

Gridlock
FieldPolitical science
CausesDivided government, polarisation, veto points
Related conceptsDeadlock, filibuster, divided government

In politics, gridlock or deadlock is a situation in which there is difficulty passing laws that satisfy the needs of the people. Gridlock often occurs when two legislative houses, or the executive branch and the legislature, are controlled by different political parties, or when institutional rules such as supermajority requirements allow a minority to block action.

Causes

Political scientists attribute gridlock to divided government, ideological polarisation, the proliferation of veto points in systems with separation of powers, and procedural tools such as the United States Senate's filibuster. The term is borrowed from traffic gridlock, in which intersections become so blocked that no vehicle can move.

Debate

Some scholars argue that divided government does not significantly reduce legislative productivity, noting that major legislation has passed under both unified and divided control, while others link rising polarisation since the late 20th century to increased stalemate on budgets, judicial confirmations and debt limits. Critics of the concept note that inaction may reflect genuine public disagreement rather than institutional failure, and that the constitutional system was designed in part to slow lawmaking.

Consequences

Extended gridlock can produce government shutdowns, reliance on executive orders, and declining public trust in institutions. Comparative studies examine how parliamentary systems, with fewer veto points, differ in susceptibility.
