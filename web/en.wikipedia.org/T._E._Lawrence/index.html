T. E. Lawrence

Thomas Edward Lawrence (16 August 1888 – 19 May 1935), known professionally as T. E. Lawrence and widely remembered as Lawrence of Arabia, was a British Army officer, archaeologist, diplomat and writer, renowned for his role during the Arab Revolt (1916–1918) and the Sinai and Palestine campaign of the First World War. His wartime activities and writings made him a widely known figure, an image he ambivalently cultivated and later sought to escape.

Early life

Lawrence was born in Tremadog, Wales, and grew up largely in Oxford, where he read history at Jesus College. Before the war he worked as an archaeologist in the Middle East, principally at the Hittite site of Carchemish in Syria, becoming fluent in Arabic and familiar with the region.

Arab Revolt

Posted to military intelligence in Cairo, Lawrence was sent in 1916 as liaison to the forces of Emir Faisal, son of Sharif Hussein of Mecca. He helped coordinate guerrilla attacks on the Hejaz Railway and Ottoman supply lines, culminating in the capture of Aqaba in 1917 and the advance on Damascus in 1918. His account of the campaigns, Seven Pillars of Wisdom (1926), became a classic of war literature.

Later life and death

After serving as an adviser at the Paris Peace Conference and in the Colonial Office under Winston Churchill, Lawrence enlisted in the Royal Air Force under assumed names (John Hume Ross and T. E. Shaw) to escape his fame. He died in 1935 from injuries sustained in a motorcycle accident near his home at Clouds Hill, Dorset.

Legacy

Lawrence's life inspired the 1962 film Lawrence of Arabia, starring Peter O'Toole, and continues to attract scholarly debate about his writings, his role in wartime diplomacy and the promises made to Arab leaders.
