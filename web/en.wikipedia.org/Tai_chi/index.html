Tai chi

Tai chi (Chinese: 太極; pinyin: tàijí), short for tai chi chuan (太極拳; tàijíquán), is a Chinese martial art practised for defence training, health benefits and meditation. It is an internal martial art (neijia) characterised by slow, flowing movements, deep breathing and mental focus. Tai chi is practised worldwide, often in parks in the early morning, and is especially popular with older adults for its gentle exercise.

History

Tradition credits the legendary Taoist sage Zhang Sanfeng with creating tai chi, but historical evidence points to Chen Wangting, a 17th-century villager of Chenjiagou in Henan, as the founder of what became the Chen style. From Chen style derived the other major family styles: Yang, Wu (Hao), Wu and Sun. Yang Luchan popularised the art in 19th-century Beijing, and his grandson Yang Chengfu standardised the widely practised Yang long form.

Practice

Training centres on solo forms (taolu): choreographed sequences of postures performed slowly and continuously. Two-person exercises such as pushing hands (tui shou) develop sensitivity, balance and the ability to yield to and redirect force. Some styles include weapons forms with sword, sabre, staff and spear. Underlying theory draws on Taoist concepts of yin and yang, the flow of qi, and the maxim that softness overcomes hardness.

Health effects

Research suggests tai chi can improve balance and reduce falls among older people, and it may benefit people with arthritis, Parkinson's disease and chronic pain. In 2020, tai chi was inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Millions practise it in China and abroad, both as a martial art and as a form of moving meditation.
