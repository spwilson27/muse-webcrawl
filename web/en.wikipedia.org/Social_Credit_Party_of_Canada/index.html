The Social Credit Party of Canada (French: Parti Crédit social du Canada) was a Canadian political party founded in 1935 on the social credit economic theories of C. H. Douglas, which held that chronic shortages of purchasing power should be corrected by distributing dividends and controlling credit through the state. Often called the "Socreds", the party drew strength first in Alberta and later in Quebec.

Origins and growth

The movement first swept to power provincially in Alberta in 1935 under William Aberhart. Federally, the party won seats in the House of Commons from 1935 onward and reached a high point in the 1962 election with 30 seats, most of them from Quebec under Réal Caouette.

Split and decline

In 1963 the party split into an English-Canadian wing under Robert Thompson and Caouette's Quebec-based Ralliement des créditistes. The two factions later partially reunited, but the party's support eroded through the 1970s; it won its last seats in 1979 and was deregistered in 1993 after failing to field enough candidates.

Legacy

Provincial Social Credit parties governed Alberta (1935–1971) and British Columbia (for most of 1952–1991) far longer than the federal party was competitive, becoming pragmatic governing parties that largely abandoned Douglas's monetary theories.
