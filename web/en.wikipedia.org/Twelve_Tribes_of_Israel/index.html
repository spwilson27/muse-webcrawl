According to the Hebrew Bible, the Twelve Tribes of Israel, also known as the Twelve Tribes of Jacob, are the descendants of the biblical patriarch Jacob (also known as Israel), his twelve sons, and their wives. The sons were Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Issachar, Zebulun, Joseph, and Benjamin — born to Leah, Rachel, and their handmaids Bilhah and Zilpah. In the biblical account, these tribes formed the people of Israel that emerged from slavery in Egypt, received the covenant at Sinai, and settled the land of Canaan.

The twelve and the land

Because Joseph received a double portion through his sons Ephraim and Manasseh (adopted by Jacob as his own in Genesis 48), and because the tribe of Levi received no territorial allotment — instead holding priestly duties and Levitical cities — the tribal divisions for land (Joshua 13–19) are usually reckoned as twelve: Reuben, Simeon, Judah, Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher, Issachar, Zebulun, Benjamin, Ephraim, and Manasseh (whose territory spanned both banks of the Jordan). The Book of Numbers gives censuses and the marching order of the tribes around the Tabernacle, and Genesis 49 preserves Jacob's blessings, poetic characterizations of each tribe.

History in the biblical narrative

During the period of the Judges the tribes formed a loose confederacy, later united under the monarchy of Saul, David, and Solomon. After Solomon's death the kingdom split: the northern Kingdom of Israel comprised ten tribes, while Judah and Benjamin formed the southern Kingdom of Judah. Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom in 722 BCE and deported much of its population, giving rise to the tradition of the "Ten Lost Tribes," whose fate has generated speculation for two millennia. The tribes of Judah, Benjamin, and Levi largely constitute the ancestors of later Jewry, with "Jew" deriving from Judah.

Modern perspectives

Scholars debate the historicity and development of the twelve-tribe scheme, noting variant lists in the Bible (e.g., Deuteronomy 33, the Song of Deborah in Judges 5, Revelation 7) and treating "twelve" partly as an idealized organizational number. Claims of descent from the lost tribes have been advanced by or on behalf of groups worldwide, including the Beta Israel of Ethiopia, the Bnei Menashe of India, and the Samaritans — the latter preserving a continuous priestly tradition claiming descent from Levi, Ephraim, and Manasseh.
