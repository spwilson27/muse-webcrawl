Oomycetes (pronounced /ˌoʊəˈmaɪsiːts/), commonly known as water moulds, are a group of filamentous, fungus-like eukaryotic microorganisms in the phylum Oomycota. Despite their fungal appearance and absorptive nutrition, molecular studies place them among the stramenopiles, more closely related to brown algae and diatoms than to true fungi. Their cell walls contain cellulose and β-glucans rather than the chitin typical of fungi.

Biology

Oomycetes produce coenocytic (aseptate) hyphae and reproduce asexually by biflagellate zoospores that swim in water — a stramenopile trait. Sexual reproduction involves oogonia and antheridia, giving the group its name ("egg fungi"). Most species are saprotrophs in freshwater and moist soils, but many are parasites of plants, algae, invertebrates, and fish.

Economic importance

Several oomycetes are devastating plant pathogens. Phytophthora infestans causes potato late blight, the disease behind the Great Irish Famine of the 1840s. Other important species include Phytophthora ramorum (sudden oak death), Plasmopara viticola (grape downy mildew), and Pythium species that cause damping-off of seedlings. Saprolegnia species infect fish and their eggs, causing losses in aquaculture.

Classification

Major orders include the Saprolegniales (water moulds proper), Peronosporales (including Phytophthora and the downy mildews), Pythiales, and Albuginales (white rusts). Because they are not true fungi, many fungicides are ineffective against them, and they are managed with dedicated oomycete-targeting compounds such as metalaxyl.
