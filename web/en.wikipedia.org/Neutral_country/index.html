Neutral country

A neutral country is a state that is neutral towards belligerents in a specific war or holds itself as permanently neutral in all future conflicts (including avoiding entering into military alliances). As a type of non-combatant status, nationals of neutral countries enjoy protection under the law of war from belligerent actions to a greater extent than other non-combatants such as enemy civilians and prisoners of war.

Neutrality in international law

The rights and duties of a neutral power are defined in sections 5 and 13 of the Hague Convention of 1907. A permanently neutral state, such as Switzerland, is bound to remain neutral in all future wars; armed neutrality is the posture of states such as Sweden historically, which maintain military forces to deter violation of their neutrality.

Examples

Switzerland is the archetypal permanently neutral country, with neutrality dating from the Congress of Vienna in 1815. Austria declared neutrality in 1955, Ireland has pursued a policy of military neutrality, and Turkmenistan has been recognised by the United Nations as a permanently neutral state. During the Second World War, countries such as Portugal, Spain, Sweden and Switzerland remained formally neutral, though the degree of practical impartiality varied.

Criticism

Critics argue that neutrality may be morally problematic when one belligerent is clearly an aggressor, and modern collective-security arrangements under the United Nations Charter have qualified the classical law of neutrality.
