Polytheism is the belief in and worship of multiple deities, which are usually assembled into a pantheon of gods and goddesses, along with their own religious sects and rituals. Polytheism is a type of theism and contrasts with monotheism, the belief in a single God who is in most cases transcendent. In polytheistic religions, the gods are often distinguished by particular functions and domains — sky, sea, war, love, agriculture, death — and are typically portrayed as complex persons with individual characteristics, desires and flaws.

Historical polytheism

Polytheism characterised the religions of the ancient world: the Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Greek, Roman, Norse, Celtic and Slavic pantheons, among many others. Typically these systems shared myths describing the gods' interactions with humans, cult practices including sacrifice, festivals and oracles, and temples housing cult images. The religious practice of the Greek and Roman worlds was also open to adopting foreign gods, and the interpretation of foreign pantheons through one's own gods (interpretatio) was widespread.

Living traditions

The largest living polytheistic tradition is Hinduism, whose many devas are often understood by adherents as manifestations of a single ultimate reality (Brahman), making its classification debated. Other living examples include Shinto in Japan, traditional Chinese religion, many African and Native American religions, and modern Pagan movements such as Heathenry and Wicca that reconstruct or revive pre-Christian polytheisms.

Concepts

Scholars distinguish "hard" polytheism, which regards the gods as distinct beings, from "soft" polytheism, which sees them as aspects of one reality. Related positions include henotheism (devotion to one god while accepting others), monolatry, and kathenotheism. The term "polytheism" was coined by the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria and later used, often polemically, by Christian writers.
