Glossolalia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Glossolalia, commonly called speaking in tongues, is the religious practice of uttering speech-like sounds that the speaker and often the community understand as a divinely given language. It is a prominent feature of Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, where it is regarded as a gift of the Holy Spirit, echoing the accounts in the Acts of the Apostles of the day of Pentecost and the apostle Paul's discussion of tongues in 1 Corinthians 12–14.

Religious practice

In Pentecostal theology, glossolalia may serve as evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit, as a private prayer language, or, when accompanied by an interpretation, as a message to the congregation. The modern Pentecostal movement traces its origin in part to events at Azusa Street in Los Angeles in 1906. Analogous ecstatic speech has been reported in other religious traditions, including shamanic practice, some forms of Hindu devotion, and ancient Greek religion.

Linguistic and psychological study

Linguists who have analysed recordings of glossolalia find that the utterances lack the structure of natural language: they draw on the sound inventory of the speaker's native language and show no consistent syntax or semantics, leading most researchers to classify them as non-communicative vocalisation rather than unknown real languages. Psychologists and neuroscientists have studied glossolalia in connection with trance, dissociation and altered brain activity during prayer. It is distinguished from xenoglossy, the claimed ability to speak a real language never learned by the speaker.
