Dyskolos

The Bodmer papyrus of the Dyskolos (placeholder image)

Dyskolos (Greek: Δύσκολος, translated as The Grouch, The Misanthrope or The Curmudgeon) is an ancient Greek comedy by Menander, the leading playwright of New Comedy. First produced in 316 BC, it won first prize at the Lenaia festival. It is the only play of Menander to survive complete, recovered from a papyrus codex published in 1958 — the so-called Bodmer papyrus found in Egypt.

Plot

The god Pan, in the prologue, explains that he has made the young Athenian Sostratos fall in love with the daughter of Knemon, a notoriously bad-tempered old farmer who hates mankind and refuses all visitors. Sostratos works in Knemon's fields to win his favour, enduring abuse with the help of his slave and Sostratos's friend Chaireas. When Knemon falls into a well and is rescued by his stepson Gorgias, the near-death experience softens him: he entrusts his property to Gorgias and permits the marriage, and Gorgias in turn marries Sostratos's sister. In the final scene the reluctant old man is teased and dragged into the celebrations.

Significance

The recovery of the Dyskolos transformed knowledge of Menander's art, which had previously been known only through fragments and Roman adaptations by Plautus and Terence. The play displays the stock characters, romantic plot and humane tone characteristic of New Comedy, which strongly influenced later European theatre.
