Jean-Honoré Fragonard

Jean-Honoré Fragonard (5 April 1732 – 22 August 1806) was a French painter and printmaker whose late Rococo manner was distinguished by remarkable fluidity, virtuosity and lightness of touch. His most famous work is The Swing (c. 1767), an emblem of the frivolous elegance of the French ancien régime.

Life and career

Born in Grasse in Provence, Fragonard was apprenticed briefly to Jean-Baptiste-Siméon Chardin and then to François Boucher, whose lush style profoundly influenced him. He won the Prix de Rome in 1752 and studied in Italy, where he made celebrated drawings of gardens and ruins, notably at the Villa d'Este in Tivoli, alongside Hubert Robert.

Works

Fragonard's large history painting Coresus Sacrificing Himself to Save Callirhoe (1765) won him admission as an associate of the Academy, but he soon turned to the private market for erotic and pastoral cabinet pictures. The Swing, commissioned by the Baron de Saint-Julien, depicts a young woman on a swing kicking off her shoe while a hidden lover watches from below. His series The Progress of Love (1771–72), painted for Madame du Barry, was rejected for the pavilion at Louveciennes and is now a centrepiece of the Frick Collection in New York.

Later life

The French Revolution destroyed the aristocratic market on which Fragonard depended and cost him his fortune, which had been placed in assignats. Protected by Jacques-Louis David, he obtained a post as a curator of the new national museum. He died in Paris in 1806, largely forgotten, before a 19th-century revival restored his reputation.
