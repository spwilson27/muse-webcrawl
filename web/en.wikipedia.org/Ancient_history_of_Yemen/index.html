Ancient history of Yemen

The ancient history of Yemen (South Arabia) is especially significant because Yemen's mountainous highlands and coastal plains supported some of the earliest and most prosperous civilizations of the Near East. From around the late 2nd millennium BCE, a series of kingdoms — Saba, Ma'in, Qataban, Hadramawt, and Himyar — grew wealthy controlling the overland incense trade in frankincense and myrrh, and built sophisticated irrigation works, most famously the Marib Dam.

The Sabaean kingdom

The kingdom of Saba (Sheba), with its capital at Marib, was the most powerful of the South Arabian states. Sabaean rulers styled themselves mukarrib (federator) and later malik (king). The Bible's account of the Queen of Sheba visiting Solomon is associated in tradition with Saba. The Great Dam of Marib, progressively enlarged over centuries, irrigated tens of thousands of hectares and underpinned Sabaean prosperity.

Other kingdoms

The Minaean kingdom (Ma'in) was a trading state whose merchants established colonies as far away as Egypt and the Aegean. Qataban, centred on Timna, and Hadramawt, with its capital at Shabwah, were major producers of incense. The kingdom of Awsan briefly flourished in the south. These kingdoms wrote in the Ancient South Arabian script and worshipped a pantheon headed by the moon god Almaqah and the sun goddess Shams.

Himyar and late antiquity

By about 300 CE the Himyarite kingdom had unified South Arabia. Himyar later adopted a monotheistic faith influenced by Judaism; its Jewish king Yusuf As'ar Yath'ar (Dhu Nuwas) persecuted Christians at Najran, provoking intervention by the Christian Ethiopian kingdom of Aksum, which conquered Yemen around 525 CE. Yemen then passed under Persian (Sasanian) control in the 570s, shortly before the coming of Islam in the 7th century, when Yemen became one of the first regions to embrace the new faith.

Legacy

The South Arabian civilization left thousands of inscriptions, monumental temples such as the Awwam temple at Marib, and engineering works that made "Arabia Felix" — "Fortunate Arabia" — proverbial in the Classical world.
