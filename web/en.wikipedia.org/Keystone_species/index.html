A keystone species is a species that has a disproportionately large effect on its natural environment relative to its abundance. The concept was introduced in 1969 by zoologist Robert T. Paine, who likened such a species to the keystone of an arch: remove it, and the structure collapses. Keystone species play a critical role in maintaining the structure of an ecological community and are a focus of conservation biology.

Paine's experiments

On the rocky Pacific coast at Mukkaw Bay, Washington, Paine removed the predatory sea star Pisaster ochraceus from experimental plots. In its absence, mussels (Mytilus californianus) — freed from predation — monopolised the shore, and species diversity fell from fifteen species to eight. With the sea star present, predation kept mussels in check and allowed many other species to coexist.

Classic examples

Sea otters are a textbook example: by preying on sea urchins, they protect kelp forests; where otters were hunted out, urchin "barrens" replaced the kelp. The reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 is widely cited as triggering a trophic cascade affecting elk behaviour, vegetation, beavers and riverbank erosion, though the strength of the effect is debated. Other proposed keystones include prairie dogs, African elephants, beavers (also classic ecosystem engineers), fig trees in tropical forests, and certain hummingbird and bat pollinators.

Criticism and refinement

Ecologists have debated the precision of the concept, noting that "keystone" has been applied loosely to predators, mutualists, engineers and resources. Later definitions emphasise a quantifiable criterion: a species whose community impact is large and disproportionately large compared with its biomass or abundance. Despite definitional debates, the idea remains influential in conservation, where protecting keystone species is seen as an efficient way to preserve whole ecosystems.
