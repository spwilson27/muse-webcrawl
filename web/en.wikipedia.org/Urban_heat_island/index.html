Urban heat island

An urban heat island (UHI) is an urban area that is significantly warmer than its surrounding rural areas because of human activities. The temperature difference is usually larger at night than during the day, and is most apparent in summer and winter. The effect, first systematically described by Luke Howard for London in the early 19th century, arises from the modification of land surfaces: dark, impervious materials such as asphalt and concrete absorb and retain heat, buildings impede ventilation and radiative cooling, vegetation and open water are scarce, and vehicles, industry, and air conditioning discharge waste heat.

Causes

Key contributors include the low albedo and high heat capacity of urban materials, the replacement of evapotranspiring vegetation with paved surfaces, street-canyon geometry that traps radiation, and anthropogenic heat release. Surface temperatures can exceed air temperatures substantially; atmospheric heat islands typically raise night-time air temperatures by 1–3 °C in large cities, and more during heat waves.

Impacts

Heat islands increase energy demand for cooling, elevate emissions and air pollution (including ground-level ozone), impair water quality in warmed runoff, and worsen heat-related illness and mortality, with the burden falling disproportionately on poorer neighbourhoods with less tree cover. They can also alter local weather, enhancing convection and downwind rainfall.

Mitigation

Strategies include increasing tree canopy and parks, green and reflective ("cool") roofs and pavements, preserving ventilation corridors, and reducing waste heat. Many cities have adopted heat action plans and tree-planting targets in response to deadly heat waves.
