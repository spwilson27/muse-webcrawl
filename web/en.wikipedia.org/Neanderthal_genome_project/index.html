The Neanderthal genome project is an effort by a group of scientists, led by Svante Pääbo at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, to sequence the genome of the Neanderthal. Launched in 2006, it produced the first draft sequence of the Neanderthal genome in May 2010.

Methods and findings

Ancient DNA was extracted mainly from bones from Vindija Cave, Croatia, under strict anti-contamination conditions, and read with high-throughput sequencing. Comparing the result with modern human genomes revealed that non-African people alive today carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal-derived ancestry from interbreeding about 50,000–60,000 years ago. The project also helped identify the Denisovans from a Siberian finger bone, and later yielded a high-quality genome from the "Altai Neanderthal".

Significance

The work transformed the study of human origins, showing that extinct archaic humans contributed genes affecting immunity, skin, and metabolism to modern populations. Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discoveries.
