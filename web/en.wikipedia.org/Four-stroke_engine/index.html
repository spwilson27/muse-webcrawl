A four-stroke (also four-cycle) engine is an internal combustion (IC) engine in which the piston completes four separate strokes — intake, compression, power, and exhaust — while turning the crankshaft twice. A stroke is the full travel of the piston along the cylinder in one direction. The four-stroke engine is the most common type used in automobiles and many other applications, in both petrol (spark-ignition) and diesel (compression-ignition) forms.

The four strokes

Intake: the intake valve opens and the piston descends, drawing in the fuel–air charge (or air alone in diesels and direct-injection engines). Compression: both valves close and the piston rises, compressing the charge. Power: near the top of the stroke the charge is ignited — by a spark plug in petrol engines or by compression heat in diesels — and the expanding gases drive the piston down, producing work. Exhaust: the exhaust valve opens and the rising piston pushes out the burned gases.

History and comparison

The four-stroke principle was patented by Alphonse Beau de Rochas in 1862 and first built successfully by Nikolaus Otto in 1876; it is also known as the Otto cycle for spark-ignition engines. Compared with two-stroke engines, four-strokes are generally more fuel-efficient and cleaner-burning, with separate lubrication, but heavier and more complex.
