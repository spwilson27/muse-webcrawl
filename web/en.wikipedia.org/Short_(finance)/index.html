Short (finance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: chart showing profit from a price decline]

In finance, being short in an asset means investing in such a way that the investor will profit if the market value of the asset falls. This is the opposite of a more conventional "long" position, where the investor will profit if the market value of the asset rises.

In short selling, the seller borrows securities (usually from a broker-dealer, using the seller's own holdings or another customer's shares as collateral), sells them on the open market, and later buys them back — "covering" the short — ideally at a lower price, returning them to the lender and pocketing the difference. Short selling requires a margin account, and the seller must pay interest and any dividends issued on the borrowed shares.

Risks

Short positions carry theoretically unlimited risk: while a long investor can lose at most the purchase price, a short seller's loss grows as the price rises. Coordinated buying can trigger a "short squeeze", in which rising prices force shorts to cover at ever-higher prices, as happened with GameStop shares in January 2021. Opponents criticise short selling as destabilising speculation, while defenders argue it improves price discovery and exposes fraud.

Related instruments that create short exposure without borrowing include put options, futures contracts, and inverse exchange-traded funds.
