Phlebotomy

Phlebotomy is the practice of drawing blood from a vein, usually by venipuncture, for laboratory testing, transfusion donation or therapeutic purposes. The word derives from the Greek phleps (vein) and tomia (cutting). A trained practitioner is a phlebotomist.

Modern practice

In contemporary medicine, phlebotomy is among the most common invasive procedures. Blood is typically drawn from the median cubital vein at the elbow into evacuated tubes, with strict protocols for patient identification, site cleaning, order of draw and labelling to prevent the diagnostic errors that faulty samples can cause. Capillary sampling from a fingertip or an infant's heel serves for small-volume tests. Phlebotomists work in hospitals, clinics, blood services and community settings.

History and therapeutic use

Phlebotomy in its historical form — bloodletting — was for two millennia a mainstay of medicine, prescribed under humoral theory for fevers, inflammation and countless other conditions, and practised with lancets, cupping and leeches. It fell from favour in the nineteenth century as physiology advanced and its harms became evident. Therapeutic phlebotomy survives in a narrow set of indications, principally hereditary haemochromatosis and polycythaemia vera, where controlled removal of blood reduces iron overload or red-cell mass.
