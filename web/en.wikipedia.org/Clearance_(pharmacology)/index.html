Clearance (pharmacology)

In pharmacology, clearance is a pharmacokinetic measurement of the volume of plasma from which a substance is completely removed per unit time; the usual units are volume per time, typically millilitres per minute. The quantity reflects the body's efficiency in eliminating a drug and, together with the volume of distribution, determines the drug's half-life and the maintenance dose needed to reach a target steady-state concentration.

Definition

Total body clearance equals the rate of elimination divided by the plasma concentration. It is the sum of clearance by each eliminating organ — chiefly renal clearance by the kidneys and metabolic (hepatic) clearance by the liver, with smaller contributions from the lungs, skin and other routes. For a drug eliminated by first-order kinetics, clearance is constant, and the steady-state concentration during a constant infusion equals the infusion rate divided by clearance.

Renal and hepatic clearance

Renal clearance is determined by glomerular filtration, tubular secretion and tubular reabsorption; it is measured clinically for substances such as creatinine and inulin to estimate glomerular filtration rate. Hepatic clearance depends on liver blood flow, the unbound fraction of drug, and the intrinsic metabolic capacity of hepatocytes. Drugs are classified as high- or low-extraction-ratio compounds depending on how completely the liver removes them in a single pass.

Clinical use

Clearance is used to calculate loading and maintenance doses, to adjust dosing in renal or hepatic impairment, and to predict drug interactions when one agent inhibits or induces the metabolic enzymes that clear another.
