Censorship in Nazi Germany

Censorship in Nazi Germany was extreme and impacted many aspects of life within the Third Reich, from the mass media to the performing arts and literature. During the regime's twelve years in power (1933–1945), the Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda under Joseph Goebbels coordinated the control of news, film, radio, theatre, music and publishing through the Reich Chamber of Culture.

Book burning and literature

On 10 May 1933 students burned some 25,000 "un-German" books at Berlin's Opernplatz and in university towns across Germany. Works by Jewish, pacifist, socialist and modernist authors — including Heinrich Mann, Erich Kästner, Sigmund Freud and Helen Keller — were proscribed. The Reich Chamber of Literature issued lists of harmful and undesirable writings; liberal and Jewish-owned publishing houses were closed or "Aryanised".

Press

The Editors Law (Schriftleitergesetz) of October 1933 made editors personally responsible for content and excluded Jews and political opponents from journalism. The Eher publishing empire absorbed most newspapers; daily press conferences issued directives, and independent papers such as the Frankfurter Zeitung eventually closed (1943).

Film and radio

All film production required approval by the Reich Film Chamber; Jewish artists were purged, and pre-censorship of scripts ensured conformity. Radio was centralised under the Reich Broadcasting Corporation, and cheap "people's receivers" (Volksempfänger) carried regime propaganda; listening to foreign broadcasts became a criminal offence in 1939.

Art and music

Modernist art was condemned as "degenerate" (entartet); the 1937 Degenerate Art exhibition in Munich displayed confiscated works for ridicule, while the House of German Art showed approved art. Jazz and works by Jewish composers were suppressed in favour of ideologically acceptable music.
