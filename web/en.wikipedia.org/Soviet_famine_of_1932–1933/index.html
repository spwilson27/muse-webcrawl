The Soviet famine of 1932–1933 was a man-made famine in the major grain-producing areas of the Soviet Union, including Ukraine, Kazakhstan, the North Caucasus and the Volga regions. It killed several million people — scholarly estimates of total excess deaths generally range between about five and eight million.

Background

The famine followed Joseph Stalin's First Five-Year Plan and the forced collectivisation of agriculture beginning in 1929. Collectivisation destroyed peasant incentives, livestock numbers collapsed, and the liquidation of the "kulaks" removed many experienced farmers. Despite falling harvests, the state imposed high grain procurement quotas, seized food from villages, blacklisted settlements that failed to meet quotas, and blocked starving peasants from leaving affected areas through the internal passport system of 1932.

Regional impact

In Ukraine the famine is known as the Holodomor and is recognised by Ukraine and a number of other countries as a genocide of the Ukrainian people; an estimated 3.3–3.9 million died there. In Kazakhstan, where forced sedentarisation destroyed nomadic pastoralism, roughly 1.5 million people — a very large share of the Kazakh population — perished between 1930 and 1933. Millions more died across Russia's grain regions.

Historiography

Historians debate the relative weight of ideology, nationality policy and economic mismanagement, and whether the Ukrainian famine was intentionally directed, but few dispute that government policies were the principal cause. Western journalists who reported honestly on the famine, such as Gareth Jones and Malcolm Muggeridge, contrasted with the denials of Walter Duranty; archival access after 1991 transformed scholarly understanding.
