Heel spur

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A heel spur, or calcaneal spur, is a bony outgrowth of the calcaneus (heel bone) that develops at the attachment of soft tissue — the plantar fascia on the underside of the heel (plantar spur) or the Achilles tendon at the back of the heel (posterior or dorsal spur). Heel spurs form over months in response to chronic strain and microtearing: the body deposits calcium at the stressed attachment point, gradually producing a hook-shaped protrusion that can extend half an inch or more.

Symptoms and relationship to plantar fasciitis

Heel spurs are frequently discovered incidentally on X-rays taken for other reasons and are often painless. They are strongly associated with plantar fasciitis, inflammation of the band of connective tissue running along the sole of the foot, which causes stabbing pain under the heel that is typically worst with the first steps in the morning. Clinicians generally attribute the pain to the fasciitis rather than to the spur itself, since spurs are also found in a substantial minority of people with no heel pain at all.

Causes and risk factors

Repeated stress on the heel and its attached tissues is the main driver. Risk factors include running and jumping sports, prolonged standing on hard surfaces, obesity, flat feet or unusually high arches, poorly fitting shoes, and tight calf muscles, which increase pull on the Achilles tendon and plantar fascia.

Treatment

Most cases respond to conservative measures: rest and ice, stretching of the calf and plantar fascia, cushioned footwear and heel inserts or orthotics, and anti-inflammatory medication. Corticosteroid injections, night splints, and shock wave therapy are used for persistent symptoms. Surgery to remove the spur or release the plantar fascia is a last resort, reserved for the small minority of patients whose pain fails to improve after many months of conservative care.
