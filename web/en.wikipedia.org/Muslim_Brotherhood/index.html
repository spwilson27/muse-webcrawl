The Muslim Brotherhood (Arabic: الإخوان المسلمون, al-Ikhwān al-Muslimūn) is a transnational Sunni Islamist organization founded in Egypt in 1928 by schoolteacher Hassan al-Banna. It began as a religious and social movement dedicated to Islamic revival and resistance to British colonial influence, and grew into one of the most influential Islamist movements in the world, spawning branches and inspired parties across the Middle East and beyond that combine preaching and social services with political activism.

History in Egypt

The Brotherhood expanded rapidly in the 1930s and 1940s, building schools, clinics, and businesses and developing a paramilitary "Special Apparatus." After a Brotherhood member assassinated Prime Minister Mahmoud El Nokrashy in 1948, the organization was dissolved and al-Banna himself was assassinated in 1949, widely believed to be in retaliation. The Brotherhood initially cooperated with the Free Officers who overthrew the monarchy in 1952, but after an assassination attempt on Gamal Abdel Nasser in 1954 it was crushed; thousands were imprisoned and tortured, among them the ideologue Sayyid Qutb, whose prison writings later inspired more radical movements. Under Anwar Sadat and Hosni Mubarak the group was tolerated as a formally banned but active movement, winning parliamentary seats as independents.

After the Arab Spring

Following Egypt's 2011 revolution, the Brotherhood's Freedom and Justice Party won parliamentary elections and its candidate Mohamed Morsi became president in 2012. Morsi's year in office was polarizing; mass protests in mid-2013 preceded a military coup led by Abdel Fattah el-Sisi. Security forces killed hundreds of Brotherhood supporters dispersing sit-ins in Cairo in August 2013, and the organization was declared a terrorist group, its leaders imprisoned or exiled. Morsi died in custody in 2019.

International presence and views

Brotherhood branches and offshoots include Jordan's Islamic Action Front, Kuwaiti and Bahraini movements, Tunisia's Ennahda (which has distanced itself from the Brotherhood label), and Morocco's PJD; Hamas's founding charter identified it as the Brotherhood's Palestinian wing. Western governments have been divided on the movement, which officially renounced violence in the 1970s but is accused by critics of fostering extremism. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt, and Russia designate it as terrorist; many other states treat its affiliates as legitimate political actors.
