Territorial claims in Antarctica

Seven sovereign states maintain territorial claims in Antarctica: Argentina, Australia, Chile, France, New Zealand, Norway, and the United Kingdom. The claims, mostly wedge-shaped sectors running from stretches of coast to the South Pole, were asserted between 1908 and 1943. They are held in abeyance by the Antarctic Treaty of 1959 (in force 1961), whose Article IV neither recognises nor denies them while prohibiting new claims or enlargement of existing ones. Most countries do not recognise any of the claims; the United States and Russia reserve a basis of claim but have made none.

The claims

The United Kingdom made the first formal claim (Falkland Islands Dependencies, 1908, now British Antarctic Territory). France claims Adélie Land; New Zealand the Ross Dependency; Norway Queen Maud Land and Peter I Island; Australia the Australian Antarctic Territory, the largest claim at some 5.9 million km²; and Argentina and Chile claim sectors that overlap substantially with the British claim and each other. One sector, Marie Byrd Land, remains unclaimed — the largest terra nullius on Earth. Historical curiosities include Nazi Germany's New Swabia (1939–45) and short-lived claims by other states.

Administration and the treaty system

Claimant states administer their sectors through polar institutes, maintain research stations, issue stamps and laws, and enforce domestic legislation, though these acts are generally ignored by non-claimants. The Antarctic Treaty demilitarised the continent and dedicated it to peaceful scientific use; the 1991 Madrid Protocol banned mining and designated Antarctica a natural reserve. Because treaty parties agree to disagree on sovereignty, research stations of many nations operate inside every claimed sector.
