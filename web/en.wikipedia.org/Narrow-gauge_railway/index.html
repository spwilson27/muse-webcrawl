A narrow-gauge railway (or narrow-gauge railroad in the United States) is a railway with a track gauge narrower than standard gauge, 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in). Most narrow-gauge railways use gauges between 600 mm (1 ft 11⅝ in) and 1,067 mm (3 ft 6 in).

Narrow-gauge lines are cheaper to build, especially in mountainous or difficult terrain, because they permit sharper curves, smaller structures, and lighter rails. Against this, they generally have lower capacity, lower speed, and incompatibility with standard-gauge networks, requiring transshipment or bogie exchange at break-of-gauge points.

History and use

The earliest narrow-gauge railways were industrial lines serving mines and quarries; the slate railways of Wales, including the Ffestiniog Railway (1836), pioneered steam haulage on narrow gauge. Colonial administrations adopted 1,067 mm (Cape gauge) widely in Africa, Japan, Australia, and New Zealand, while metre gauge became standard in much of Southeast Asia and parts of South America. In the United States, a narrow-gauge boom in the 1870s–1880s built thousands of miles of 3 ft gauge lines, most later converted or abandoned.

Modern narrow gauge

Today narrow gauge survives on heritage and tourist railways, on intensive suburban networks in Japan, on mining railways, and throughout much of sub-Saharan Africa's rail system. Many mountain railways in Switzerland, India (including the Darjeeling Himalayan Railway), and Austria remain narrow gauge.
