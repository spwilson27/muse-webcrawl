Rural–urban migration

Rural–urban migration is the movement of people from rural areas to urban areas, typically in search of better employment, education, services and living standards. It is the principal driver of urbanisation and has accompanied industrialisation in every region of the world.

Causes

Economists describe the flow in terms of "push" factors in the countryside — land scarcity, agricultural mechanisation, low wages, and limited services — and "pull" factors in cities, including higher wages, more diverse jobs, and better schools, hospitals and infrastructure. The Harris–Todaro model explains migration as a response to expected urban income, even where urban unemployment is high, which helps account for the growth of informal settlements.

Consequences

At destination, migration supplies labour for industry and services but can strain housing, transport and sanitation, contributing to the growth of slums and informal economies. In origin areas it can relieve pressure on land and generate remittances, but may also drain younger and more educated workers, ageing the rural population. Countries such as China, where the hukou household-registration system long restricted permanent settlement, developed large populations of internal migrant workers living in cities without full local entitlements.

Scale

The world's urban population share rose from about 30% in 1950 to over 55% by the early 21st century, a transformation driven largely by rural–urban migration in Asia and Africa, where the fastest flows continue today.
