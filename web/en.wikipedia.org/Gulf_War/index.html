The Gulf War was a conflict between Iraq and a United Nations-authorised coalition of 35 nations led by the United States. It began when Iraq, under Saddam Hussein, invaded and annexed Kuwait on 2 August 1990. After months of sanctions and a military build-up in Saudi Arabia (Operation Desert Shield), the coalition launched an air campaign on 17 January 1991 (Operation Desert Storm), followed by a ground offensive that liberated Kuwait in about 100 hours. Hostilities ended on 28 February 1991.

Background

Iraq accused Kuwait of exceeding OPEC oil quotas and slant-drilling into the Rumaila oil field, disputes rooted partly in debts Iraq had incurred during the Iran–Iraq War. UN Security Council Resolution 660 condemned the invasion, and Resolution 678 authorised the use of force if Iraq did not withdraw by 15 January 1991.

The air and ground campaigns

The air campaign struck Iraqi command centres, air defences and infrastructure over five weeks. The ground war began on 24 February 1991; coalition forces executed a wide left-hook manoeuvre through the desert west of Kuwait, encircling Iraqi forces. Retreating Iraqi troops set fire to hundreds of Kuwaiti oil wells, creating a major environmental disaster.

Aftermath and legacy

Kuwait's sovereignty was restored, but Saddam Hussein remained in power. Sanctions, no-fly zones and weapons inspections continued through the 1990s. The war was notable for extensive live television coverage, the combat debut of precision-guided munitions at scale, and the syndrome later known as Gulf War illness reported by many veterans.
