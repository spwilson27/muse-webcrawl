Gordon growth model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Gordon growth model (GGM), also known as the dividend discount model in its constant-growth form, is a method of valuing a company's stock price. It assumes that a company exists forever and that dividends grow at a constant rate indefinitely, and it solves for the present value of an infinite series of future dividends.

P = D1 / (r − g)

where P is the current stock price, D1 is the expected dividend in the next period, r is the required rate of return (cost of equity), and g is the constant dividend growth rate. The formula derives from the sum of a geometric series and is valid only when r exceeds g.

History

The model is named for Myron J. Gordon of the University of Toronto, who published it in 1956 and 1959, building on earlier work by John Burr Williams in The Theory of Investment Value (1938).

Limitations

The model is highly sensitive to the assumed growth rate and discount rate; small changes produce large valuation swings. It does not apply to companies that pay no dividends, and the assumption of a single perpetual growth rate below the cost of capital is often unrealistic. Multi-stage dividend discount models relax these assumptions.
