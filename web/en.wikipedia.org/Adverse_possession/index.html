Adverse possession

Area of lawProperty law
Colloquial name"Squatter's rights"
Typical periodCommonly 5–30 years depending on jurisdiction (e.g. 10–12 years in England and Wales)
RequirementsActual, open and notorious, exclusive, hostile, continuous possession

Adverse possession, sometimes colloquially described as "squatter's rights", is a legal principle in common law under which a person who does not have legal title to a piece of property — usually land — may acquire legal ownership based on continuous possession or occupation of the land without the permission (licence) of its legal owner, provided the possession lasts for a statutory period and meets certain conditions.

The traditional requirements, often expressed in case law, are that the possession be actual (the claimant uses the land as an owner would), open and notorious (visible, so the owner is on notice), exclusive (not shared with the public or the owner), hostile (without permission and inconsistent with the owner's title), and continuous for the full limitation period. Successive possessors' periods can sometimes be "tacked" together if they are in privity.

Rationale and reform

The doctrine is justified as barring stale claims, encouraging landowners to monitor and use their land, and settling titles in line with long-standing facts on the ground. It derives from statutes of limitations on actions to recover land; its Roman antecedent is usucapio. In England and Wales the Land Registration Act 2002 substantially curtailed the doctrine for registered land: a squatter in possession for ten years may apply to be registered, but the registered owner is notified and can usually defeat the application — a change tested and upheld (with controversy) in the litigation culminating in Pye v Graham.
