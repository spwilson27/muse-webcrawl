Martinique

Martinique (French: Martinique, [maʁtinik]; Martinican Creole: Matinik) is an island in the Lesser Antilles of the West Indies, in the eastern Caribbean Sea, and an overseas department and region of France. It lies between Dominica to the north and Saint Lucia to the south, has an area of about 1,128 km², and a population of roughly 350,000. Its capital and largest city is Fort-de-France.

History

The island was inhabited by Arawaks and later Caribs (Kalinago), who called it Madinina, "island of flowers". Columbus sighted it in 1493 and landed in 1502. French colonisation began in 1635 under Pierre Belain d'Esnambuc; the indigenous population was largely destroyed or displaced, and sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans made the island a rich colony. Britain occupied Martinique several times during the wars of the 18th and early 19th centuries. Slavery was abolished in 1848, championed by Victor Schœlcher. In 1902 the eruption of Mount Pelée destroyed the city of Saint-Pierre, then the island's cultural capital, killing around 30,000 people — one of the deadliest volcanic disasters of the 20th century. Martinique became an overseas department of France in 1946.

Geography and economy

The island is volcanic and mountainous in the north, dominated by Mount Pelée (1,397 m), with gentler hills and white-sand beaches in the south. Mont Pelée and the Pitons du Carbet were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023. The economy rests on tourism, agriculture (bananas, sugarcane and rum — Martinique rum holds an AOC), petroleum refining and public-sector employment supported by metropolitan France. Martinique is part of the European Union as an outermost region and uses the euro. The writer and politician Aimé Césaire, co-founder of the négritude movement, was from Martinique, as was Frantz Fanon.
