Actual malice

Actual malice is a legal term in United States defamation law. Despite its name, it does not mean ill will or spite; it means that a statement was made with knowledge that it was false, or with reckless disregard for whether it was false or not. Public officials and public figures must prove actual malice by clear and convincing evidence in order to recover damages for defamation.

Origin

The standard was established by the Supreme Court in New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964), which held that the First Amendment requires public officials suing over criticism of their official conduct to prove actual malice. The rule was extended to public figures in Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts (1967) and refined for private figures in Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc. (1974), which allowed states to set a lower fault standard — usually negligence — for plaintiffs who are not public figures.

Application

"Reckless disregard" is a subjective standard: the plaintiff must show the defendant in fact entertained serious doubts about the truth of the publication, or deliberately avoided the truth. Failure to investigate, departure from journalistic standards, or mere negligence are not enough by themselves. Because the test examines the defendant's state of mind, defamation cases against the press frequently turn on reporters' notes and editorial discussions.

Debate

The doctrine is credited with giving the press "breathing space" to report on public affairs without fear of ruinous liability for honest error. Critics, including sitting Supreme Court justices who have called for Sullivan to be reconsidered, argue that it licenses carelessness and leaves public figures little recourse against falsehoods in the modern media environment.
