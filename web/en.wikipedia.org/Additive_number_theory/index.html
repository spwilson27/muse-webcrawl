Additive number theory

Additive number theory is the subfield of number theory concerning the subset sum problem: given a set A of integers and an integer n, determine the combinations of elements of A that sum to n, or more broadly the structure of sumsets A + B = {a + b : a ∈ A, b ∈ B}. The field is primarily concerned with direct problems over the integers (classical additive number theory) and with inverse problems describing the structure of sets with small sumsets (now called additive combinatorics in much modern work).

Classical problems

Famous problems include Goldbach's conjecture (every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes), Waring's problem (every natural number is a sum of a bounded number of k-th powers, resolved for suitable bounds by Hilbert and later refined by the Hardy–Littlewood circle method), Lagrange's four-square theorem, and the theory of partitions, initiated by Euler and revolutionised by Hardy and Ramanujan. Schnirelmann density and the concept of an additive basis — a set whose h-fold sumset covers all sufficiently large integers — provide a general framework: Schnirelmann proved every set of positive lower density relative to primes yields a basis, a step toward partial results on Goldbach-type problems (later including Chen's theorem and Vinogradov's three-prime theorem).

Methods

Tools of the field include the Hardy–Littlewood circle method, sieve methods, generating functions, Fourier analysis on groups, and probabilistic methods. Inverse results such as Freiman's theorem — sets with doubling |A+A| ≤ K|A| have structure resembling generalised arithmetic progressions — anchor modern additive combinatorics, with applications to sum-product phenomena (Erdős–Szemerédi) and to theoretical computer science.
