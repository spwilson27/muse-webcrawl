Willamette River

The Willamette River is a major tributary of the Columbia River in northwestern Oregon, United States. Flowing roughly 187 miles (301 km) entirely within the state, it drains the Willamette Valley, the most populous region of Oregon, and joins the Columbia near Portland.

Course

The river's main stem begins near Eugene at the confluence of its Middle Fork and Coast Fork, and flows northward through Albany, Salem, and Oregon City — where it drops over Willamette Falls — before dividing around Sauvie Island at its mouth. Its drainage basin covers about 11,500 square miles and includes tributaries such as the McKenzie, Santiam, Yamhill, Molalla, Tualatin, and Clackamas rivers. The valley's fertile soils were laid down by the Missoula Floods of the last ice age.

History and environment

The river was a central corridor for Kalapuya and other Indigenous peoples, and later for fur traders and the Oregon Trail settlers whose arrival made it the heart of pioneer Oregon. Twentieth-century industrial and municipal pollution severely degraded water quality; cleanup efforts beginning in the 1960s, associated with Governor Tom McCall, restored oxygen levels and salmon habitat, though a stretch near Portland Harbor was designated a federal Superfund site in 2000. The river supports runs of Chinook salmon and steelhead and is heavily used for recreation and irrigation.
