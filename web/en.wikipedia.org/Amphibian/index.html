Amphibian

Amphibians are ectothermic, anamniotic, four-limbed vertebrate animals that constitute the class Amphibia. The name derives from the Greek amphíbios, "double life", reflecting the typical life cycle in which aquatic larvae with gills metamorphose into terrestrial adults with lungs and limbs. Living amphibians comprise three orders: frogs and toads (Anura), salamanders and newts (Urodela), and the limbless caecilians (Gymnophiona) — over 8,000 described species.

Characteristics

Amphibians have glandular, permeable skin through which they can breathe and absorb water, supplementing their lungs. Many secrete toxins for defence. They are ectotherms ("cold-blooded"), so their activity tracks environmental temperature. Most lay jelly-coated eggs in water; frogs undergo metamorphosis from tadpoles, while some species practise direct development or live birth.

Evolution

Amphibians descend from lobe-finned fishes of the Devonian period, about 370 million years ago. Early tetrapods such as Ichthyostega and Acanthostega colonised land during the Carboniferous, when giant temnospondyls dominated wetlands. Modern amphibians (Lissamphibia) probably arose in the Permian or Triassic.

Ecology and conservation

Amphibians inhabit most freshwater and moist terrestrial habitats and serve as both predators of invertebrates and prey for many vertebrates. They are considered sensitive bioindicators, and populations have declined worldwide at alarming rates owing to habitat loss, pollution, climate change and the chytrid fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis and B. salamandrivorans, which have driven numerous species to extinction.
