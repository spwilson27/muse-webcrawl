A cashmere goat is a type of domestic goat bred for its production of cashmere wool, the fine, soft down of the animal's winter undercoat. "Cashmere goat" is not a single breed but a type that includes numerous breeds and landraces developed in the high, cold regions of Central Asia, including the Changthangi (pashmina) goat of Ladakh and Kashmir, the Mongolian cashmere goat, and breeds of China, Iran, Afghanistan, Australia and elsewhere.

Characteristics

Cashmere goats have a two-layered coat: long, coarse guard hairs protect a dense underlayer of extremely fine down that grows in response to shortening days and cold and is shed in spring. Fibre fineness is the key quality criterion — cashmere fibre averages under 19 micrometres in diameter, with the finest pashmina around 12–16 micrometres. A single goat yields only about 100–250 grams of usable down per year, one reason cashmere garments are expensive. Colours range from white (most valued, because it dyes cleanly) through grey and brown to black.

Production

China and Mongolia together supply the great majority of the world's raw cashmere, with smaller quantities from Iran, Afghanistan, India and Central Asian republics. The down is collected either by combing the goats during the spring moult — the traditional method for the finest fibre — or by shearing the whole fleece and later separating down from guard hair mechanically (dehairing).

Environmental concerns

Growing global demand and cheap fast-fashion cashmere have massively increased goat herds on the Mongolian and Inner Mongolian steppe since the 1990s. Goats graze plants down to the roots and their sharp hooves damage fragile soils, and herd expansion is widely cited as a driver of grassland degradation and worsening dust storms in East Asia. These pressures have spurred sustainable-cashmere certification schemes and traceability initiatives among major fashion brands.
