Relaxed stability

A relaxed-stability aircraft is unstable without continuous computer control.

Relaxed stability, also called relaxed static stability (RSS), is an aircraft design approach in which an aeroplane is deliberately made aerodynamically unstable in pitch — its centre of gravity lies at or behind its neutral point, giving it a zero or negative static margin — and is kept controllable by a fly-by-wire flight control system that makes continuous, rapid corrections. Such an aircraft cannot be flown unaided by a human pilot.

Advantages

A conventionally stable aircraft's tailplane must generate downforce to balance it, which the wing must then overcome with extra lift — costing drag and fuel. With relaxed stability the tail can instead produce lift, permitting a smaller wing and tail, lower trim drag, better fuel efficiency and greater manoeuvrability, since control surfaces no longer fight the aircraft's inherent stability. Fighters benefit from faster pitch response and higher turn rates.

History

The Wright brothers' Flyer was in fact unstable in pitch, but deliberate relaxed stability had to await digital flight controls. The General Dynamics F-16 Fighting Falcon (first flown in 1974) was the first production aircraft designed with relaxed static stability and fly-by-wire, using quadruplex redundancy for safety. The concept is now standard in fighters (F/A-18, Eurofighter Typhoon, Rafale, Gripen, F-35) and appears in milder form in airliners such as the Airbus A320 family, which have reduced — though still positive — stability augmented by computer control. The experimental Grumman X-29 demonstrated extreme instability with forward-swept wings.

Risks

Because the aircraft depends utterly on its control system, redundancy and failure handling are critical; loss of all flight-control computing means loss of the aircraft.
