Algebraic number

DefinitionA (real or complex) root of a non-zero polynomial with integer (equivalently rational) coefficients
Notationℚ̄ (algebraic closure of ℚ)
Field propertiesForms a countable field; algebraically closed
ComplementTranscendental numbers (e.g. π, e)

An algebraic number is a number that is a root of a non-zero polynomial equation with integer coefficients. Every rational number p/q is algebraic (a root of qx − p = 0), as are all square and higher roots, the imaginary unit i (a root of x² + 1 = 0), and the golden ratio (a root of x² − x − 1 = 0). Numbers that are not algebraic, such as π and e, are called transcendental.

Properties

The algebraic numbers form a field: sums, differences, products and quotients of algebraic numbers are algebraic, and every root of a polynomial whose coefficients are themselves algebraic is algebraic. The degree of an algebraic number is the degree of its minimal polynomial (the lowest-degree monic polynomial with rational coefficients having it as a root). Although the algebraic numbers are dense in the real line, they are countable, so almost all real numbers — in the sense of measure and cardinality — are transcendental.

History

The theory grew out of nineteenth-century work on solvability and constructibility: Liouville first constructed explicit transcendental numbers in 1844; Hermite proved e transcendental (1873) and Lindemann proved π transcendental (1882), settling the ancient problem of squaring the circle. Algebraic number theory — the study of algebraic number fields and their rings of integers — became a central branch of mathematics through the work of Dedekind, Kronecker and others.
