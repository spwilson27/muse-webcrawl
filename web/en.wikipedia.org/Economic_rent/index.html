Economic rent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Economic rent is the payment to a factor of production above its opportunity cost.

In economics, economic rent is any payment to the owner or factor of production in excess of the costs needed to bring that factor into production. In classical economics, economic rent was understood as income from land and other natural resources in fixed supply; David Ricardo developed the theory of differential rent, in which rent arises from differences in the fertility or location of land.

Definition

The modern definition generalises the concept: economic rent is the surplus earned by any factor — land, labour, capital or a unique talent — above the minimum amount needed to keep it in its current use, its transfer earnings or opportunity cost. A superstar athlete or musician, for example, earns substantial economic rent because their supply is essentially fixed and their next-best employment would pay far less.

Rent-seeking

Rent-seeking describes efforts to capture economic rent through political or institutional means — lobbying for tariffs, licences or monopolies — rather than by creating new wealth. Economists since Gordon Tullock and Anne Krueger have argued that rent-seeking wastes resources and distorts economies.

Policy implications

Because taxing pure economic rent does not change the supply of the factor, economists since Henry George have proposed land value taxation as an efficient revenue source. Quasi-rent, a related concept introduced by Alfred Marshall, is the short-run surplus earned by capital equipment whose supply is temporarily fixed.
