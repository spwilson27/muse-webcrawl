Agency shop

Agency shop agreements require non-union employees to pay a fee to the union.

An agency shop is a form of union security agreement in which the employer may hire both union and non-union workers, and where employees are not required to join the union, but all employees in the bargaining unit must pay a fee — often called an agency fee or fair share fee — to the union to cover the costs of collective bargaining, contract administration and grievance handling. The rationale is to prevent "free riding" by workers who benefit from the union's contract without contributing to its costs.

Comparison with other arrangements

The agency shop sits between the closed shop, in which only union members may be hired (illegal in the United States under the Taft–Hartley Act of 1947), and the open shop, in which workers are not required to join or financially support the union. A union shop requires employees to join the union after being hired, while an agency shop requires only payment of a fee.

Legal status

In the United States, agency shops are permitted in the private sector under the National Labor Relations Act, except in states with right-to-work laws, which prohibit requiring fees as a condition of employment. Non-members may object to paying for union activities unrelated to collective bargaining, a right established in cases such as Communications Workers v. Beck (1988), and fees are then reduced to the "chargeable" share.

In the public sector, the U.S. Supreme Court upheld agency fees in Abood v. Detroit Board of Education (1977), but overturned that precedent in Janus v. AFSCME (2018), holding that requiring public employees to pay agency fees violates the First Amendment. Agency fee arrangements remain common in Canada, where the Rand formula, established in 1946, requires dues contributions from all employees in a bargaining unit whether or not they join the union.
