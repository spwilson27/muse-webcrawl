Evangelicalism

Evangelicalism is a worldwide, interdenominational movement within Protestant Christianity that emphasizes the authority of the Bible, the necessity of being "born again" through personal conversion, and active evangelism — sharing the Christian gospel. The word derives from the Greek euangelion, "good news". There are an estimated 600 million evangelicals worldwide, with especially large populations in the United States, Brazil, Nigeria, and South Korea.

Characteristics

Historian David Bebbington identified four hallmarks of evangelical religion, the "Bebbington quadrilateral": conversionism (the belief that lives must be transformed by the new birth), activism (energetic evangelism and social action), biblicism (a high regard for the Bible), and crucicentrism (a stress on Christ's atoning death on the cross).

Origins and history

Evangelicalism emerged in the 1730s from Pietism, Puritanism, and the Moravian movement, flowering in the transatlantic revivals of the First Great Awakening associated with John Wesley, George Whitefield, and Jonathan Edwards. In the 19th century evangelicals drove missionary expansion and social reform campaigns including abolitionism. After the fundamentalist–modernist controversies of the early 20th century, "neo-evangelicalism" — promoted by Billy Graham, the National Association of Evangelicals (founded 1942), and Christianity Today — sought a more engaged stance. Pentecostal and charismatic forms have driven much of the movement's explosive growth in the Global South since the late 20th century.

Diversity and influence

Evangelicalism spans Baptists, Methodists, Pentecostals, nondenominational churches, and evangelical wings of historic denominations. In the United States, white evangelical voters form a politically influential constituency. Globally, evangelical missions, media, and megachurches exert wide cultural influence.
