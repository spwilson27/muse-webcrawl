Sallust

Gaius Sallustius Crispus (86 – c. 35 BC), usually Anglicised as Sallust, was a Roman historian and politician. He is the earliest known Latin-language historian with surviving works, and his monographs on the Catilinarian conspiracy and the Jugurthine War remain important sources for the late Roman Republic.

Born86 BC, Amiternum, Italy
Diedc. 35 BC, Rome
OccupationHistorian, politician
Notable worksBellum Catilinae; Bellum Jugurthinum; Historiae

Life

Sallust was born at Amiternum in the Sabine country. He pursued a political career in Rome, serving as quaestor and as tribune of the plebs in 52 BC, when he opposed the faction of Milo. He was expelled from the Senate in 50 BC on grounds of immorality, though political motives likely played a part. A supporter of Julius Caesar, he was restored to office and governed the province of Africa Nova after Caesar's victory in the civil war, returning to Rome wealthy. After Caesar's assassination he retired from politics and devoted himself to writing history.

Works

The Bellum Catilinae recounts the conspiracy of Catiline in 63 BC, framing it as a symptom of moral decay following the destruction of Carthage. The Bellum Jugurthinum describes Rome's war against Jugurtha, king of Numidia (112–105 BC), and attacks the corruption of the Roman nobility. His later Historiae, covering the years 78–67 BC, survives only in fragments, including speeches and letters. Sallust's compressed, archaizing style, influenced by Thucydides and Cato the Elder, was much admired and imitated by later historians such as Tacitus.

Legacy

Sallust's moralizing interpretation of Rome's decline profoundly shaped later Roman historiography. The magnificent gardens he laid out on the Quirinal Hill with his African wealth, the Horti Sallustiani, later became an imperial property.
