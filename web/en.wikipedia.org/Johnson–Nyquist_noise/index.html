Johnson–Nyquist noise

Thermal noise sets a fundamental floor on electronic measurements.

Johnson–Nyquist noise (also called thermal noise, Johnson noise, or Nyquist noise) is the electronic noise generated by the thermal agitation of charge carriers — usually electrons — inside an electrical conductor at equilibrium, which happens regardless of any applied voltage. Thermal noise is present in all electrical circuits and is a fundamental limit on the sensitivity of electronic measurements.

History

The phenomenon was first measured by John B. Johnson at Bell Labs in 1926 and published in 1928; his colleague Harry Nyquist provided the theoretical explanation the same year, relating the noise power to temperature and Boltzmann's constant through what is now regarded as a consequence of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

Characteristics

The mean-square open-circuit noise voltage across a resistance R at absolute temperature T over bandwidth Δf is given by V² = 4kBTRΔf, where kB is Boltzmann's constant. The noise is approximately white — its power spectral density is nearly constant over a wide range of frequencies — and Gaussian in amplitude distribution. The available noise power is P = kBTΔf, independent of the resistance value. At room temperature, the noise power density is about −174 dBm/Hz, a reference figure widely used in radio engineering.

Applications and consequences

Johnson noise limits the performance of amplifiers, radio receivers and precision instruments; engineers minimise it by cooling input stages, narrowing bandwidth and choosing appropriate resistances. Conversely, noise thermometry uses the effect as a primary method of measuring temperature, and thermal noise is exploited as an entropy source in some random number generators.
