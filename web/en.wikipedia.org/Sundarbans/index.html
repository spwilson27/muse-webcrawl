Sundarbans

The Sundarbans is a vast mangrove forest region in the delta formed by the confluence of the Ganges, Brahmaputra and Meghna rivers in the Bay of Bengal. Spanning parts of West Bengal in India and the Khulna Division of Bangladesh, it is the largest mangrove forest in the world, covering roughly 10,000 square kilometres of land and water, and is famed for its population of Bengal tigers.

Geography

The Sundarbans consists of a network of tidal waterways, mudflats and small islands of salt-tolerant mangrove forest. About 60 percent lies in Bangladesh and 40 percent in India. The name may derive from the sundari tree (Heritiera fomes), abundant in the region, or from Bengali words meaning "beautiful forest".

Ecology

The forest supports a rich fauna, including the largest remaining population of Bengal tigers adapted to a semi-aquatic life, spotted deer, wild boar, fishing cats, saltwater crocodiles, river dolphins and hundreds of bird species. Mangroves protect the densely populated hinterland from cyclones and tidal surges, a role demonstrated during major storms in the Bay of Bengal.

Human settlement

Millions of people live around the forest's margins, depending on fishing, honey collection, crab catching and agriculture. Human–tiger conflict is a persistent danger for those who enter the forest. Settlements are threatened by erosion, salinisation and sea-level rise.

Conservation

The Indian Sundarbans National Park and Bangladeshi reserved forest are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, and the region is a designated Ramsar wetland. Conservation challenges include poaching, freshwater diversion upstream, cyclones and the effects of climate change.
