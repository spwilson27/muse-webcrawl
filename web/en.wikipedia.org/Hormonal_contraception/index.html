Hormonal contraception

Hormonal contraceptives come in many delivery forms, including pills, patches, rings, injections, implants, and hormonal IUDs.

Hormonal contraception refers to birth control methods that act on the endocrine system. Almost all methods are composed of steroid hormones, either progestogens alone or progestogens combined with estrogens. The first hormonal method, the combined oral contraceptive pill, was introduced in 1960 and is used by more than 100 million women worldwide.

Mechanism of action

Hormonal contraceptives prevent pregnancy primarily by suppressing ovulation: the exogenous hormones inhibit the release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone and the pituitary gonadotropins LH and FSH, so no mature egg is released. They also thicken cervical mucus, impeding sperm passage, and thin the endometrial lining.

Methods

  • Combined methods contain estrogen and a progestin: the combined oral contraceptive pill, the contraceptive patch, and the vaginal ring.
  • Progestogen-only methods include the progestogen-only pill, the contraceptive implant, depot medroxyprogesterone acetate injections, and the levonorgestrel intrauterine system.
  • Emergency contraception uses higher doses of hormones after unprotected intercourse.

Effectiveness

With perfect use, most hormonal methods are over 99 percent effective. Long-acting reversible methods such as the hormonal IUD and the implant are the most effective because they do not depend on daily user action.

Health effects

Combined hormonal contraceptives slightly increase the risk of venous thromboembolism but reduce the risks of ovarian and endometrial cancers and can treat acne, heavy menstrual bleeding, and endometriosis pain. They do not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
