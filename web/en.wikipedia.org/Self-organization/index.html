Self-organization

Self-organization is a process in which some form of overall order emerges from the local interactions between parts of an initially disordered system, without control by any external agent. The process may be spontaneous when sufficient energy is available. Self-organization occurs throughout nature and technology: in the patterns formed by chemical reactions, the flocking of birds, the synchronisation of fireflies, the formation of crystals and convection cells, the behaviour of ant colonies, and the dynamics of markets and neural networks.

History of the concept

Precursors appear in Descartes and Kant, and the idea took scientific shape in cybernetics: W. Ross Ashby introduced "self-organizing systems" in 1947, and Heinz von Foerster developed the principle of "order from noise." Ilya Prigogine's work on dissipative structures showed how systems held far from thermodynamic equilibrium can spontaneously generate order while exporting entropy, work recognised by the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Hermann Haken's synergetics and later complexity science generalised these ideas.

Principles

Self-organizing systems characteristically involve many components interacting by simple local rules, positive and negative feedback, and sensitivity to fluctuations that nucleate new patterns. No component directs the whole; the global pattern is an emergent property. Well-studied models include Turing's reaction–diffusion systems, which generate spots and stripes, cellular automata such as Conway's Game of Life, and flocking models in which three local rules — separation, alignment and cohesion — reproduce the behaviour of bird flocks.

Applications

Engineers exploit self-organization in swarm robotics, self-assembling materials and nanotechnology, adaptive communication networks and traffic systems. In biology it frames explanations of morphogenesis, ecosystems and the origin of life; in social science, of spontaneous order in economies and institutions, an idea associated with Friedrich Hayek and earlier with Adam Smith's "invisible hand."
