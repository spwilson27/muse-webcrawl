A putto (Italian: [ˈputto]; plural putti) is a figure in a work of art depicted as a chubby male child, usually nude and sometimes bearing wings. Originally limited to profane passions in symbolism, the putto came to represent the sacred cherub in Baroque art, and in the present day is more often simply called a cherub in popular usage.

History

Putti derive from the classical erotes — attendants of Eros and Venus — and from the genii of Roman art, appearing on sarcophagi, lamps and decorative reliefs. After largely disappearing in the Middle Ages, they were revived in early Renaissance Italy, notably in the sculpture of Donatello, and became ubiquitous in Renaissance, Mannerist, Baroque and Rococo painting, sculpture and architecture, where they crowd ceilings, fountains, tombs and altarpieces.

Distinctions

Art historians distinguish the putto from the cherub proper (a member of the second order of angels) and from Cupid, who is a specific deity identified by bow and arrows; putti are decorative, anonymous spirit-children who may carry garlands, musical instruments, or the attributes of gods and allegorical figures. In secular contexts they are sometimes called amorini or spiritelli.
