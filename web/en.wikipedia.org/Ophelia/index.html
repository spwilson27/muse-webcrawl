Ophelia is a character in William Shakespeare's tragedy Hamlet (c. 1600). A young noblewoman of Denmark, she is the daughter of Polonius, sister of Laertes, and the beloved of Prince Hamlet. Her descent into madness and death by drowning have made her one of the most enduring and frequently depicted figures in Western art and literature.

Role in the play

Ophelia is obedient to her father, who — together with Laertes — warns her against Hamlet's courtship and orders her to return his letters and tokens. Hamlet's behaviour toward her becomes erratic and cruel, most famously in the "get thee to a nunnery" scene. After Hamlet kills Polonius, Ophelia loses her reason; in Act 4 she appears mad, singing bawdy songs and distributing symbolic flowers. Gertrude later reports that she fell from a willow branch into a brook and drowned, "incapable of her own distress".

Interpretation

Critics have long debated whether Ophelia's madness is theatrical performance, genuine breakdown, or both, and whether her death was suicide — a question debated within the play itself at her burial, where the gravediggers argue the point and the priest grudgingly permits her Christian burial. Feminist readings have emphasised how completely Ophelia is defined and controlled by the men around her.

In art and culture

John Everett Millais's painting Ophelia (1851–52), for which Elizabeth Siddal modelled in a bathtub, is among the most famous works of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. Ophelia has inspired poems, songs, and countless stage performances, and the figure of the drowned, flower-strewn maiden remains a powerful cultural archetype.
