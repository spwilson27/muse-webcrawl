Marginalism

Marginalism
Key figures: William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, Léon Walras
Key concepts: marginal utility, marginal cost, diminishing returns
Era: Marginal Revolution, c. 1870s

Marginalism is a method of economic analysis that centers on the change in an outcome resulting from a unit change in an input or decision—analysis "at the margin." It holds that economic choices are made by comparing the additional (marginal) benefits and costs of a small change, rather than totals or averages. Marginalism resolved the classical paradox of value by showing that prices are determined not by total utility but by the marginal utility of the last unit consumed.


Diminishing marginal utility

The Marginal Revolution

Around 1870, three works appeared independently: William Stanley Jevons's Theory of Political Economy (1871) in England, Carl Menger's Principles of Economics (1871) in Austria, and Léon Walras's Elements of Pure Economics (1874) in Switzerland. Their shared innovation—locating value in marginal utility—displaced the classical labor theory of value and founded what became neoclassical economics. Precursors included Johann Heinrich von Thünen, Augustin Cournot, Jules Dupuit, and Hermann Heinrich Gossen, whose "laws" of pleasure maximization anticipated the marginal calculus.

Core concepts

Marginal utility is the additional satisfaction from consuming one more unit of a good; the law of diminishing marginal utility holds that this increment falls as consumption rises, explaining why water, though essential, is cheap while diamonds are dear. Marginal cost and marginal revenue govern the profit-maximizing output of a firm: produce until marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Marginal productivity theory extends the logic to wages and interest, treating factor prices as the value of the marginal product of labor and capital. Consumers in equilibrium allocate spending so that marginal utility per dollar is equal across goods.

Influence and criticism

Marginalist reasoning underlies modern microeconomics: supply and demand curves, elasticity, consumer surplus, and cost-benefit analysis. Critics—from institutionalists to behavioral economists—have questioned its assumptions of rational calculation, divisible goods, and known preferences, noting that people often reason in averages or categories rather than margins. Nonetheless, "thinking at the margin" remains a defining habit of the discipline.
