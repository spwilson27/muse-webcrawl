Commodus (31 August 161 – 31 December 192 AD) was Roman emperor from 177 to 192, ruling jointly with his father Marcus Aurelius until the elder emperor's death in 180. The last ruler of the Nerva–Antonine dynasty, he broke the succession of adoptive "good emperors" by being the first since Domitian to succeed his own father. Ancient sources portray his reign as a descent into megalomania and neglect, ending with his assassination and the Year of the Five Emperors.

Early life and accession

Born at Lanuvium, Commodus was made Caesar in 166 and Augustus and co-emperor in 177. He accompanied Marcus Aurelius on the Danube frontier and, on his father's death at Vindobona in March 180, quickly concluded the Marcomannic Wars and returned to Rome, abandoning plans to annex new provinces.

Reign

Commodus delegated administration to favourites — the chamberlain Saoterus, the praetorian prefect Perennis, and later Cleander — whose corruption and fall fuelled instability. A conspiracy led by his sister Lucilla in 182 failed and led to purges of the Senate. Increasingly identifying himself with Hercules, Commodus renamed Rome and its institutions after himself, appeared in the arena as a gladiator (a scandalous act for an emperor), and demanded divine honours. Senatorial historians such as Cassius Dio, a contemporary, depict a reign of terror and caprice, though modern historians note some continuity in provincial administration.

Assassination and legacy

On 31 December 192 Commodus was strangled in his bath by the wrestler Narcissus, in a plot involving his mistress Marcia and the praetorian prefect Laetus. The Senate declared him a public enemy and attempted to erase his memory, though Septimius Severus later had him deified. He was succeeded by Pertinax, whose murder months later opened the civil wars of 193. Commodus remains popularly known through his portrayal as the villain of the film Gladiator (2000).
