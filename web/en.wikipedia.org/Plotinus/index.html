Plotinus (Greek: Πλωτῖνος; c. AD 204/5 – 270) was a Hellenistic philosopher born and raised in Egypt, generally considered the founder of Neoplatonism. He is one of the most influential figures in late antiquity, and his metaphysical writings, edited by his student Porphyry as the Enneads, profoundly shaped later pagan, Christian, Jewish, and Islamic thought.

Life

According to Porphyry's biography, Plotinus refused to speak of his birthplace or parentage and seemed "ashamed of being in a body." He studied philosophy for eleven years in Alexandria under Ammonius Saccas, joined the emperor Gordian III's Persian expedition hoping to learn Eastern philosophy, and after Gordian's assassination settled in Rome in 244, where his lectures drew leading senators and the patronage of the emperor Gallienus. He began writing at about fifty; his last years were spent in Campania, where he died in 270.

Philosophy

Plotinus held that all reality derives by necessary emanation from a single, ineffable first principle, the One (or the Good), which is beyond being and description. From the One proceeds Intellect (Nous), the realm of the Platonic Forms; from Intellect proceeds Soul, which animates and orders the sensible cosmos; matter marks the lowest limit of emanation, associated with privation and evil. The human soul, descended into the body, can ascend through philosophy, virtue, and contemplation to mystical union with the One—an experience Plotinus reportedly attained four times in Porphyry's presence.

The Enneads

Plotinus's treatises were arranged by Porphyry into six sets of nine (hence Enneads, from the Greek for "nines"), grouped thematically from ethics to the One. Written in dense Greek, they were translated in part into Arabic (as the so-called Theology of Aristotle) and into Latin by Marsilio Ficino in 1492, making Plotinus central to Renaissance humanism.

Influence

Through Porphyry, Iamblichus, Proclus, and pseudo-Dionysius, Plotinian ideas entered Christian theology (notably Augustine), medieval Islamic philosophy (al-Farabi, Avicenna), and Jewish thought; they also influenced Romanticism and modern Idealism. Arthur Schopenhauer and Henri Bergson acknowledged his significance, and he is often described as one of the greatest of ancient metaphysicians.
