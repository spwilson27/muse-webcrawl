Ket language

The Ket language is spoken along the middle Yenisei in Krasnoyarsk Krai, Siberia.

Ket
Native to: Russia (Siberia)
Ethnicity: Ket people
Native speakers: a few hundred at most, declining
Family: Yeniseian; the only surviving member
Status: severely endangered

Ket is a Siberian language, the sole surviving language of the Yeniseian language family, formerly spoken along the Yenisei River and its tributaries. Its relatives — Yugh, Kott, Assan, Arin and Pumpokol — all became extinct in the 18th and 19th centuries, leaving Ket an isolate of great interest to linguists.

Features

Ket is famous for its extraordinarily complex verb morphology: the verb is built from a template of as many as eight slots, incorporating markers for subject, object, tense, mood and various derivational elements in a pattern that has been described as one of the most intricate in the world. Ket also has a simple tone system — unusual in Siberia — and grammatical gender classes that divide nouns into masculine, feminine and inanimate categories, with agreement patterns that cut across the semantic categories in surprising ways.

The Dene–Yeniseian hypothesis

In 2008 the linguist Edward Vajda presented morphological evidence linking Yeniseian with the Na-Dene languages of North America (such as Navajo and Tlingit), which share complex prefixing verb templates. The proposed Dene–Yeniseian family, if correct, would be the first demonstrated genealogical link between Old World and New World language families, and it has received a cautiously favourable reception from many specialists.

Endangerment

The Ket people number around a thousand; the great majority now speak Russian, and fluent Ket speakers — mostly older — number only in the low hundreds or fewer. Soviet-era boarding schools and collectivisation disrupted transmission to children, although recent decades have seen documentation work, school primers and community revitalisation efforts.
