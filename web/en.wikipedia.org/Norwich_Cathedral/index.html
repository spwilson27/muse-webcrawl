Norwich Cathedral, formally the Cathedral Church of the Holy and Undivided Trinity, is a Church of England cathedral in Norwich, Norfolk, dedicated to the Holy Trinity. It is one of the finest and most complete Norman cathedrals in England, and the centre of the Diocese of Norwich.

History

The cathedral was begun in 1096 by Herbert de Losinga, the first Bishop of Norwich, who moved the see from Thetford. The building, constructed of flint and mortar faced with Caen stone, was completed by about 1145. The church forms the centre of a large enclosed precinct, the Cathedral Close, entered through the Erpingham and Ethelbert gates.

Architecture

The cathedral retains more of its original Norman fabric than any other English cathedral: the great round-arched nave of fourteen bays, the transepts and the apsidal east end all survive, as does the Norman crossing tower, crowned by a 15th-century spire that at 96 metres is the second tallest in England after Salisbury. After a fire caused by riots in 1272 and later lightning damage, the chancel clerestory was rebuilt and magnificent stone vaults — the only two-storey vault of its kind — were added, decorated with over a thousand carved and painted roof bosses depicting Bible stories. The cathedral has the second largest monastic cloisters in England.

Notable features

The Close contains the tomb of Nurse Edith Cavell, executed in Brussels in 1915, just outside the cathedral. The cathedral library and the Hostry visitor centre stand within the precinct, and a pair of peregrine falcons nests on the spire, watched by a popular viewing platform each spring.
