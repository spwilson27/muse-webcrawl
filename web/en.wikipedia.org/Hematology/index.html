Hematology

Hematology (also spelled haematology) is the branch of medicine concerned with the study of blood, blood-forming organs and blood diseases. It encompasses the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of disorders affecting red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, haemoglobin, blood proteins, bone marrow, blood vessels and the mechanisms of coagulation. Physicians specialising in the field are called hematologists.

Scope

Hematology covers both benign (non-cancerous) conditions and blood cancers. Benign disorders include the anaemias, such as iron-deficiency anaemia and the inherited haemoglobin disorders sickle cell disease and thalassaemia; bleeding disorders such as haemophilia and von Willebrand disease; and clotting disorders such as deep vein thrombosis. Malignant diseases include the leukaemias, lymphomas, multiple myeloma and the myeloproliferative neoplasms. In many countries hematology is closely linked with oncology in the combined specialty of hematology-oncology.

Diagnosis

The cornerstone laboratory test is the complete blood count (full blood count), which measures haemoglobin, cell numbers and indices, supplemented by microscopic examination of a stained blood film (peripheral smear). Other core techniques include bone marrow aspiration and biopsy, flow cytometry, cytogenetic and molecular genetic testing, coagulation studies such as the prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time, and haemoglobin electrophoresis. Modern molecular diagnostics can detect specific mutations, such as the BCR-ABL fusion in chronic myeloid leukaemia, that guide targeted therapy.

Treatment

Treatments range from iron and vitamin supplementation and blood transfusion, through anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs, to chemotherapy, targeted agents such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, immunotherapy including CAR T-cell therapy, and haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (bone marrow transplant). Hematologists also supervise transfusion medicine and blood banking services in many hospitals.

History

The field developed from the microscopic study of blood in the 19th century, the classification of the leukaemias and anaemias, and discoveries such as the ABO blood groups by Karl Landsteiner in 1901, which made transfusion safe. The 20th century brought chemotherapy for leukaemia, the first successful bone marrow transplants by E. Donnall Thomas, and the molecular revolution that turned chronic myeloid leukaemia into a chronically manageable disease with imatinib.
