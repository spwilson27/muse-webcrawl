Eternal return

Eternal return (also eternal recurrence) is a concept that the universe and all existence and energy have been recurring, and will continue to recur, in a self-similar form an infinite number of times across infinite time or space. The concept appears in ancient Egyptian and Indian religions, in Pythagoreanism and Stoicism, and most famously in the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche.

Antiquity

Stoic philosophers held that the cosmos periodically dissolves in fire (ekpyrosis) and is reborn identically. Similar cyclical cosmologies appear in Hindu concepts of kalpas and in Buddhist time cycles. The ouroboros, a serpent eating its own tail, symbolised such cyclicality in the ancient world.

Nietzsche

Nietzsche presented eternal recurrence in The Gay Science (1882) as a thought experiment: if a demon told you that you must live your life over and over exactly as before, would you despair or affirm it? In Thus Spoke Zarathustra it became linked to amor fati, the love of one's fate, and to the ideal of affirming life without appeal to an afterlife. Whether Nietzsche also believed it as a literal cosmology drawn from contemporary physics remains debated.

Modern interpretations

Mathematically, Poincaré's recurrence theorem shows that certain dynamical systems return arbitrarily close to their initial state. Writers such as Jorge Luis Borges and Milan Kundera explored the idea literarily; Kundera contrasted eternal return with the lightness of a life lived once in The Unbearable Lightness of Being.

Criticism

Critics note that thermodynamic entropy and the expansion of the universe pose problems for exact physical recurrence. See also Friedrich Nietzsche, Amor fati and Stoicism.
