Wycliffe's Bible

Wycliffe's Bible (also Wycliffite Bible) is the name given to a group of Bible translations into Middle English made under the direction of John Wycliffe and his followers, the Lollards, in the late 14th century. They were the first complete translations of the Bible into English, rendered from the Latin Vulgate.

Versions

Two main versions survive. The Earlier Version (c. 1382) is a fairly literal translation of the Vulgate, sometimes awkward in its adherence to Latin word order. The Later Version (c. 1395), associated with John Purvey, is more idiomatic and is accompanied by a General Prologue explaining the translators' aims and methods. Some 250 manuscripts survive, making it the most common Middle English text.

Suppression

The translation was controversial because it placed Scripture in the hands of lay readers outside clerical control. Archbishop Thomas Arundel's Constitutions of 1409 forbade unauthorised translation and ownership of Wycliffite Bibles, driving the work underground, although copies continued to circulate and were read into the 16th century.

Legacy

Wycliffe's Bible anticipated the printed English Bibles of the Reformation, from William Tyndale's New Testament onward, though Tyndale worked from Greek and Hebrew texts rather than the Vulgate.
