Guthrum (Old Norse: Guðormr; died c. 890), baptised with the name Æthelstan, was a Danish Viking leader who led the Great Heathen Army in its later campaigns against the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, fought Alfred the Great of Wessex, and after defeat became the first Danish king of East Anglia and a recognised ruler of the Danelaw.

Career

Guthrum appears in the sources as one of the leading kings of the Danish army in England during the 870s. In 875 he led part of the army to Cambridge; in 876 he seized Wareham in Wessex and then Exeter, before withdrawing. His masterstroke came in January 878, when a midwinter surprise attack on Alfred's royal estate at Chippenham drove Alfred into hiding in the Somerset marshes at Athelney and put Guthrum in control of nearly all Wessex.

Defeat at Edington and baptism

In May 878 Alfred rallied his forces and decisively defeated Guthrum at the Battle of Edington in Wiltshire, then besieged the Danes at Chippenham until they surrendered. Under the resulting peace, Guthrum accepted baptism at Aller near Athelney, with Alfred as his godfather, took the Christian name Æthelstan, and withdrew from Wessex. The parties subsequently agreed the Treaty of Alfred and Guthrum, which fixed the boundary between Alfred's realm and Guthrum's territory roughly along Watling Street and the Thames–Lea line, and set out rules for trade and compensation between the two peoples.

King of East Anglia

From about 880 Guthrum settled in East Anglia and ruled as a Christian king: he issued coinage in his baptismal name Æthelstan, modelled on Alfred's types, suggesting a deliberate adoption of Anglo-Saxon kingship. He died around 890 and, according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, was buried at Hadleigh in Suffolk. After his death, his kingdom came under pressure from Alfred's son Edward the Elder, and East Anglia was eventually incorporated into the English kingdom in the 910s.

Legacy

Guthrum is one of the most prominent Viking antagonists of Alfred in popular culture, appearing in Bernard Cornwell's The Last Kingdom novels and the television adaptation, as well as in other historical fiction about the Viking Age in England. Historians credit his conversion and treaty with stabilising the frontier between English and Danish England for a generation.
