The Bible is a collection of religious texts, or scriptures, that are held to be sacred in Christianity, and partly in Judaism and other religions. It is an anthology compiled over many centuries by many authors, originally in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Koine Greek.

Structure

The Christian Bible comprises the Old Testament, largely corresponding to the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) — the Torah (Pentateuch), the Prophets, and the Writings — and the New Testament, containing the four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, the epistles attributed to Paul and other apostles, and the Book of Revelation. Canons differ between traditions: Roman Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include deuterocanonical books absent from most Protestant Bibles, which typically contain 66 books; Catholic Bibles contain 73.

History

The biblical texts were composed, edited, and transmitted over roughly a thousand years. The oldest surviving major manuscripts include the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint (Greek translation), and the Masoretic Text. The invention of printing made the Bible among the first mass-printed books; the Gutenberg Bible appeared around 1455.

Translation and influence

The Bible is the best-selling book of all time and has been translated into hundreds of languages in full, and thousands in part. Translations such as the Latin Vulgate and the English King James Version (1611) have profoundly shaped religion, literature, law, and language across the world. The Bible is also a primary historical and literary source studied by scholars of antiquity.

Interpretation

Approaches to the Bible range from literal readings and doctrines of biblical inerrancy to historical-critical scholarship examining its sources, genres, and contexts. Its narratives — creation, the Exodus, the life of Jesus — remain central to the faith and culture of billions of people.
