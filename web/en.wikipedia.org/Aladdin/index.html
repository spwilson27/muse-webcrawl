Aladdin

Aladdin (Arabic: علاء الدين, ʿAlāʾ al-Dīn) is a Middle Eastern folktale, famous as one of the stories of One Thousand and One Nights (the Arabian Nights). It tells of a poor young man who discovers a magic lamp containing a powerful jinn (genie) who grants his wishes.

The tale

In the story, Aladdin is a lazy, impoverished youth in a Chinese city (in most tellings; the tale mixes Chinese and Middle Eastern settings) who is recruited by a sorcerer from the Maghreb posing as his uncle. The sorcerer sends Aladdin into a booby-trapped cave to retrieve an oil lamp. Trapped in the cave, Aladdin discovers that the lamp, when rubbed, summons a mighty jinn, and that a ring he was lent summons a lesser jinn. With the lamp's power he becomes wealthy, marries the Sultan's daughter, and builds a magnificent palace. The sorcerer steals the lamp by trickery, but Aladdin recovers it and defeats the sorcerer and his brother.

Origins

No Arabic manuscript of the tale predates its inclusion in Antoine Galland's French translation of the Nights (1704–1717). Galland heard the story from the Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab in Paris in 1709, and Aladdin is one of the "orphan tales" added to the collection through that channel. Despite this, the story has become one of the best-known of all the Nights tales in world culture.

Adaptations

The tale has been adapted countless times, including in pantomime, opera, and film. The most famous modern version is Disney's animated film Aladdin (1992), followed by a live-action remake in 2019 and a Broadway musical. Earlier adaptations include the 1924 film The Thief of Bagdad.
