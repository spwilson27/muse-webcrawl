Automatic writing, or psychography, is a claimed supernatural or subconscious ability that allows a person to produce written words without consciously writing. It is claimed to arise from a spiritual, supernatural, or subconscious source, with the writer acting essentially as an amanuensis. Practitioners engage the ability during a variety of mental states — ranging from a semi-conscious state of reverie, to self-induced or externally induced hypnosis or trance states, to outright spirit possession.

History

Automatic writing became prominent in the Spiritualist movement of the later 19th century, when mediums produced long texts attributed to the spirits of the dead; in Brazil, the medium Chico Xavier became famous for psychographed books attributed to deceased authors. The phenomenon was also investigated by early psychologists and psychiatrists, including Pierre Janet, who interpreted it as evidence of dissociation and the subconscious.

Surrealism

In the 20th century the technique was taken up for purely artistic purposes. André Breton and the Surrealists adopted "automatism" as a creative method, seeking to bypass rational control and tap the unconscious; Breton and Philippe Soupault's Les Champs magnétiques (1920) was an early product of the method, and poetic works such as Robert Desnos's writings extended it. Mainstream science regards automatic writing as an instance of the ideomotor effect — unconscious muscular movement without any supernatural agency.
