Estoppel

Field: Common law, equity

Principal types: Promissory, proprietary, by deed, by representation, issue estoppel

Estoppel is a judicial device in common law legal systems whereby a court may prevent or "estop" a person from making assertions or from going back on their word. It arises where one party has made a representation or assumption on which another has relied to their detriment, so that it would be unconscionable to allow the first party to depart from it.

Types

Promissory estoppel prevents a party from reneging on a promise that another has reasonably relied upon, even without consideration, and is central to cases such as Central London Property Trust v High Trees House (1947). Proprietary estoppel protects expectations concerning rights in land. Other forms include estoppel by deed, estoppel by representation, equitable estoppel, and issue estoppel (and the related doctrines of res judicata), which bar relitigation of matters already decided.

Elements

Although formulations vary by jurisdiction and type, estoppel typically requires a clear representation or promise, reliance by the party asserting the estoppel, and detriment or unconscionability if the representation is not honored. In many systems estoppel operates as a shield rather than a sword — a defence, not itself a cause of action — though proprietary estoppel in English law can ground a claim.

Related doctrines

Estoppel is related to waiver, laches, and the doctrine against approbating and reprobating, and forms of it appear in public and international law, where states may be bound by their prior representations.
