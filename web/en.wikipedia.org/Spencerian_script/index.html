Spencerian script is a form of cursive handwriting developed by Platt Rogers Spencer and widely taught in the United States from about 1850 to 1925. Characterised by graceful oval forms, smooth loops and shaded downstrokes made by varying pressure on a flexible pen, it was the standard of American business and personal correspondence in the later 19th century — the era sometimes called the "Golden Age of Ornamental Penmanship".

Development and spread

Spencer, an Ohio educator and penmanship teacher, published his system in copybooks from 1848 (notably with Victor M. Rice's Spencerian Key to Practical Penmanship, 1866, issued after his death by his sons). Spencerian schools trained commercial penmen, and the style's flowing capitals became the model for business logos: the Coca-Cola script (1886) and the Ford Motor Company logo both descend from it.

Characteristics and decline

The script is written at a slant of about 52 degrees with whole-arm ("muscular") movement rather than finger motion, producing uniform, rhythmic letterforms. Its ornamental demands made it slow to learn; from the 1890s the simpler Palmer Method — a plainer, faster business hand — displaced it in schools, and typewriters displaced both. Spencerian survives as an art form among calligraphers and in the master-penman tradition kept alive by organisations such as IAMPETH.
