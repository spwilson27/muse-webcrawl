The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act (PPACA), commonly called the Affordable Care Act (ACA) or "Obamacare", is a landmark United States federal statute enacted by the 111th Congress and signed into law by President Barack Obama on March 23, 2010. It represents the most significant overhaul of the U.S. healthcare system since Medicare and Medicaid in 1965.

Provisions

The ACA expanded health insurance coverage through several mechanisms: it required most Americans to carry health insurance (the individual mandate, whose penalty was reduced to zero in 2019), expanded Medicaid eligibility to adults with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level (made optional for states by a 2012 Supreme Court ruling), and created health insurance marketplaces (exchanges) offering premium tax credits and subsidies.

Insurance reforms prohibited denial of coverage or higher premiums based on pre-existing conditions, allowed young adults to remain on a parent's plan until age 26, eliminated annual and lifetime coverage limits, and required plans to cover a set of essential health benefits.

Effects and legal challenges

The uninsured rate in the United States fell substantially after implementation, reaching historic lows, with tens of millions gaining coverage through Medicaid expansion and the marketplaces. The law survived major Supreme Court challenges in National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius (2012), King v. Burwell (2015), and California v. Texas (2021), and repeated repeal attempts in Congress failed.

Criticism

Critics have cited rising premiums for some plans, insurer exits from some markets, the mandate's intrusion on individual choice, and the law's cost. Supporters point to coverage gains, consumer protections, and slower growth in healthcare spending than projected.
