Prenatal testing

Prenatal testing (also prenatal diagnosis) is the testing of a foetus before birth to detect chromosomal abnormalities, genetic disorders and structural defects, as well as aspects of maternal health. It is usually offered as part of routine prenatal care.

Screening tests

Screening tests estimate risk rather than giving a definitive diagnosis. First-trimester combined screening measures nuchal translucency by ultrasound together with blood markers (PAPP-A and free β-hCG) to estimate the risk of Down syndrome (trisomy 21), trisomy 18 and trisomy 13. Second-trimester serum screening (the "quad screen") adds other markers. Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), analysing cell-free foetal DNA in maternal blood from about 10 weeks, has high accuracy for the common trisomies but remains a screening test requiring confirmation. Ultrasound scans, including the mid-pregnancy anomaly scan, detect many structural abnormalities and monitor growth.

Diagnostic tests

Diagnostic procedures obtain foetal cells for karyotyping, chromosomal microarray or DNA testing: chorionic villus sampling (CVS), performed at 10–13 weeks, and amniocentesis, usually from 15 weeks. Both carry a small risk of miscarriage, now generally estimated at well under 1 per cent in experienced hands. Cordocentesis (foetal blood sampling) is used rarely. Preimplantation genetic testing screens embryos created by IVF before transfer.

Purposes and issues

Results allow parents and clinicians to plan delivery, prepare for a child with special needs, arrange in-utero treatment where available, or decide about continuing the pregnancy. Prenatal testing raises ethical debates about disability, selective abortion, routinisation of screening and informed consent, which guidelines address through non-directive genetic counselling.
