Oresteia

The Oresteia (Ancient Greek: Ὀρέστεια) is a trilogy of Greek tragedies written by Aeschylus in the 5th century BC, concerning the murder of Agamemnon by Clytemnestra, the murder of Clytemnestra by Orestes, the trial of Orestes, the end of the curse on the House of Atreus, and the pacification of the Erinyes (Furies). The trilogy — consisting of Agamemnon, The Libation Bearers, and The Eumenides — is the only surviving example of an ancient Greek theatre trilogy. The Oresteia was originally performed at the Dionysia in 458 BC, where it won first prize.

Agamemnon

The first play recounts the homecoming of Agamemnon, king of Argos, from the Trojan War. His wife Clytemnestra, nursing grievances over the sacrifice of their daughter Iphigenia and having taken Aegisthus as a lover, murders Agamemnon in his bath and kills the captive Trojan prophetess Cassandra as well.

The Libation Bearers

In the second play, Orestes returns from exile, reunites with his sister Electra at their father's tomb, and, under Apollo's command, avenges Agamemnon by killing Clytemnestra and Aegisthus. He is immediately pursued by the Erinyes, visible only to him.

The Eumenides

The final play moves to Delphi and then Athens, where Orestes is tried by a jury of citizens in the first court of homicide — the mythic founding of the Areopagus court. With Athena presiding and casting the tie-breaking vote, Orestes is acquitted; Athena persuades the Erinyes to accept a role as benevolent guardians of Athens, transforming them into the Eumenides ("Kindly Ones"). The trilogy thus traces a movement from blood vengeance to civic justice.

Influence

The Oresteia has been adapted in Eugene O'Neill's Mourning Becomes Electra, operas, and countless modern productions, and remains a cornerstone of Western drama and of theories of tragedy from Hegel to modern political readings of justice and reconciliation.
