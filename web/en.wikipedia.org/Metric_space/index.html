Metric space

In mathematics, a metric space is a set together with a notion of distance between its elements, usually called a metric. The metric generalises the familiar ideas of Euclidean distance. Metric spaces were introduced by Maurice Fréchet in his 1906 work on functional analysis.

Definition

A metric space is an ordered pair (M, d) where M is a set and d is a function d : M × M → ℝ satisfying, for all x, y, z in M:

  • Non-negativity and identity: d(x, y) ≥ 0, and d(x, y) = 0 if and only if x = y.
  • Symmetry: d(x, y) = d(y, x).
  • Triangle inequality: d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z).

Examples

The real numbers with d(x, y) = |x − y| form a metric space, as does Euclidean space ℝⁿ with the usual distance. Other examples include the taxicab (Manhattan) metric, the discrete metric, and spaces of functions equipped with the supremum metric. Every metric space is a topological space, with open balls forming a basis for the topology.

Key concepts

Metric spaces support notions of convergence of sequences, continuity, completeness (every Cauchy sequence converges), and compactness. Complete metric spaces in which contractions have unique fixed points are the subject of the Banach fixed-point theorem.
