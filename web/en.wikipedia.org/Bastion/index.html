TypeFortification element

SystemBastion fort / star fort (trace italienne)
PartsFaces, flanks, gorge, salient angle

A bastion is a structure projecting outward from the main enclosure of a fortification, situated at the corners of the fortress so that defenders on it can fire along the walls and into the surrounding ground in several directions. Bastions are the defining feature of the bastion fort, or star fort, developed in Renaissance Italy in response to gunpowder artillery.

Design

A classical bastion is a flat-topped, earth-filled masonry work, typically arrowhead-shaped in plan, with two faces meeting at a salient angle pointing toward the enemy and two shorter flanks connecting it to the curtain wall. Guns on the flanks sweep the ditch in front of the adjacent curtain and the face of the neighboring bastion, eliminating the dead ground that plagued medieval round towers.

History

The bastion emerged in Italy in the late 15th and early 16th centuries, with designs by architects such as Francesco di Giorgio Martini and the Sangallo family, and spread across Europe and its colonies as the trace italienne. Engineers such as Vauban in 17th-century France brought bastioned fortification to its height. The system became obsolete in the 19th century as rifled artillery and explosive shells overwhelmed masonry defenses.
