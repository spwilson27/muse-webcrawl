Underwriting

An underwriter evaluates risk before a financial contract is issued.

Underwriting is the process by which an individual or institution takes on financial risk for a fee. The term derives from the practice, originating at Lloyd's of London, in which risk-takers (called underwriters) literally wrote their names under the total amount of risk they were willing to accept for a specified premium.

Underwriting is central to the insurance industry, to investment banking in the issuance of securities, and to lending decisions in banking and real estate.

Insurance underwriting

Insurance underwriters evaluate the risk and exposures of potential clients and decide how much coverage a client should receive, how much they should pay for it, and whether to accept the risk at all. Underwriting involves measuring risk exposure and determining the premium that needs to be charged to insure that risk. Common inputs include the applicant's age, health, occupation, claims history, and, for property insurance, the construction and location of the insured asset.

Securities underwriting

In securities underwriting, an investment bank agrees to purchase securities from an issuer and resell them to the public, as in an initial public offering (IPO). Under a firm commitment, the underwriter buys the entire issue and bears the risk of unsold shares; under best efforts, the underwriter sells what it can without guaranteeing the full amount. The underwriting spread is the difference between what the underwriter pays the issuer and the price at which the securities are offered to the public.

Loan underwriting

Lenders underwrite mortgages and other loans by assessing a borrower's creditworthiness, typically through the "three Cs": credit history, capacity to repay, and collateral. Automated underwriting systems are widely used for standard consumer credit products.
