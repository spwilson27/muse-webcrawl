Imru' al-Qays

Imru' al-Qays ibn Hujr al-Kindi (Arabic: امرؤ القيس ابن حجر الكندي) was an Arab poet of the 6th century CE, generally regarded as the greatest of the pre-Islamic Arabian poets and one of the most famous poets in Arabic literature. He is traditionally considered the author of one of the Mu'allaqat, the celebrated "Seven Odes" (or "Hanging Poems"), and is credited with inventing the qasida form's conventions.

Life

According to tradition, Imru' al-Qays was the youngest son of Hujr, the last king of the central Arabian tribe of Kinda. Banished by his father for his dissolute lifestyle and composition of amatory verse, he wandered Arabia — earning the epithet "the wandering king" (al-malik al-dillil). When his father was assassinated by the Banu Asad, Imru' al-Qays swore vengeance and sought support from various tribes and, eventually, from the Byzantine emperor Justinian I in Constantinople.

Legend holds that he died around 544 CE near Ancyra (modern Ankara) in Anatolia, poisoned — according to one famous account — by a poisoned robe sent by the emperor, after a rumored liaison with a Byzantine princess.

Poetry

His Mu'allaqa opens with the famous halt at the abandoned campsite ("Halt, both of you, and let us weep for the memory of a beloved and a dwelling place"), establishing the nasib (elegiac prelude) that structured the classical qasida for centuries. His verse is celebrated for its vivid descriptions of desert landscapes, storms, horses, and hunting, and for the frankness of its erotic passages. A divan of poems attributed to him survives, though its authenticity is debated by scholars.

Legacy

Imru' al-Qays remains a towering figure in Arabic literary culture; his name is proverbial, and his tomb, identified near Ankara in later tradition, became a site of literary pilgrimage.
