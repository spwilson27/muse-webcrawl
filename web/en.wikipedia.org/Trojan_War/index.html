The Trojan War was, in Greek mythology, a legendary conflict between the Achaeans (Greeks) and the city of Troy, fought after Paris of Troy abducted Helen, wife of Menelaus of Sparta. It is one of the most important events in Greek mythology and is narrated in many works of Greek literature, most famously Homer's Iliad and Odyssey.

Legend

According to the myth, the war began after the Judgement of Paris: the Trojan prince Paris awarded the Apple of Discord to Aphrodite, who promised him the most beautiful woman in the world, Helen of Sparta. Paris carried Helen off to Troy, and Menelaus's brother Agamemnon led a great expedition of Achaean kings and heroes to recover her. The siege of Troy lasted ten years. The Iliad describes a few weeks in the final year of the war, focusing on the wrath of Achilles after Agamemnon seized his war prize Briseis, the death of Patroclus, and Achilles' killing of the Trojan prince Hector.

The Trojan Horse and the fall of Troy

After a decade of inconclusive fighting, the Greeks resorted to a stratagem devised by Odysseus: they built a huge wooden horse, hid a picked force of men inside it, and pretended to sail away. The Trojans dragged the horse into the city as a trophy; at night the Greeks emerged and opened the gates for the returning army. Troy was sacked and burned, King Priam was killed, and the surviving Trojan women were enslaved. The hero Aeneas escaped, in later tradition going on to found the line that led to Rome.

The returns

The Greek victors suffered disastrous homecomings (nostoi): Agamemnon was murdered by his wife Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus; Odysseus wandered ten further years before reaching Ithaca, as told in the Odyssey.

Historicity

The ancient Greeks believed the Trojan War was a real historical event of the 13th or 12th century BC. Modern scholars debate how much historical truth underlies the epics. Excavations by Heinrich Schliemann and later archaeologists at Hisarlik in northwestern Turkey revealed a wealthy Bronze Age city repeatedly destroyed, and Hittite records mention a land called Wilusa — plausibly (W)ilios, Troy — and conflicts involving Ahhiyawa, plausibly the Achaeans. A war or series of wars over control of the straits near Troy in the Late Bronze Age remains a plausible kernel for the legend.

In literature and art

Beyond Homer, the war was treated in the lost poems of the Epic Cycle, in Athenian tragedy (Aeschylus's Oresteia, Euripides' Trojan Women), and in Virgil's Aeneid. It has remained a fertile subject for art, opera, and film into the modern era.
