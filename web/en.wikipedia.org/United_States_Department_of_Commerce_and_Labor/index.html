The United States Department of Commerce and Labor was a short-lived Cabinet department of the United States federal government, which controlled the foreign and domestic commerce and labour affairs of the United States from 1903 to 1913. It was created by an act signed by President Theodore Roosevelt on February 14, 1903, and its first secretary was George B. Cortelyou.

History

The department consolidated a collection of previously scattered bureaus, including the Bureau of Labor (transferred from the Department of the Interior), the Lighthouse Board and lighthouse establishment, the Bureau of Statistics, the Coast and Geodetic Survey, the Bureau of Immigration, the Bureau of Corporations, and the newly authorised Bureau of the Census. Its Bureau of Corporations was given powers of investigation over interstate corporations that anticipated the Federal Trade Commission, and the department played a role in Roosevelt-era antitrust enforcement.

Division

Business and labour interests within the single department frequently conflicted, and labour organisations had long sought a department of their own headed by a secretary from their ranks. On March 4, 1913 — President William Howard Taft's last full day in office — legislation took effect dividing the department into the United States Department of Commerce and the United States Department of Labor. William C. Redfield became the first Secretary of Commerce and William B. Wilson the first Secretary of Labor.
