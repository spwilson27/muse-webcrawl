Naqshbandi

The Naqshbandi (Persian: نقشبندی), also spelled Naqshbandiyya, is a major Sunni Sufi order (tariqa) of Islam. It takes its name from Baha al-Din Naqshband Bukhari (1318–1389) of Bukhara in present-day Uzbekistan, though its chain of transmission (silsila) traces back through earlier Khwajagan masters to Abu Bakr, the first caliph — unusual among Sufi orders, most of which trace through Ali.

Practice

The order is distinguished by its emphasis on strict adherence to the sharia and the sunna, sober spirituality, and — in most branches — silent dhikr (remembrance of God) rather than vocal chanting. Its principles include the eleven Naqshbandi principles, such as awareness in breathing, watching one's step, and "solitude in the crowd" — outward engagement in society with inward attention to God. The order spread widely through Central and South Asia, Turkey, the Caucasus and the Balkans, and later worldwide.

Notable branches and figures

Important figures include Ahmad Sirhindi, the "Mujaddid Alf-i Thani" of the Mujaddidi branch in Mughal India; the Khalidi branch of Mawlana Khalid al-Baghdadi in the Ottoman world; and in the modern West the Naqshbandi-Haqqani order associated with Shaykh Nazim al-Haqqani of Cyprus.
