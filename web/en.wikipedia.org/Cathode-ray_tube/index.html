Cathode-ray tube

TypeVacuum tube display device
Invented1897 (Karl Ferdinand Braun)
Working principleThermionic emission; electron beam excites phosphor screen
Former usesTelevision sets, computer monitors, oscilloscopes, radar

A cathode-ray tube (CRT) is a vacuum tube containing one or more electron guns, which emit electron beams that are manipulated to display images on a phosphorescent screen. The images may represent electrical waveforms (in an oscilloscope), radar targets, or pictures (in television sets and computer monitors). The CRT was the dominant display technology of the twentieth century until displaced by flat-panel LCD, plasma, and LED displays in the 2000s.

Operation

Inside the sealed, evacuated glass envelope, a heated cathode emits electrons that are accelerated by a high voltage (often 15–30 kV in a colour television) toward the screen. Deflection coils or electrostatic plates steer the beam, scanning it across a phosphor coating in a raster pattern. In colour CRTs, three electron guns correspond to red, green, and blue phosphors, and a shadow mask or aperture grille ensures each beam strikes only its intended colour.

History

Cathode rays were studied in the nineteenth century by Plücker, Hittorf, Crookes, and others; J. J. Thomson's 1897 experiments showed they were streams of particles — electrons. Karl Ferdinand Braun built the first CRT oscilloscope the same year. Vladimir Zworykin's kinescope and Philo Farnsworth's image dissector made electronic television practical in the 1930s. CRTs later served as computer memory (the Williams tube) and as the standard computer display for decades.

Decline and disposal

CRT glass contains several kilograms of lead and the tubes are classified as hazardous waste in many jurisdictions; recycling programmes handle their disposal. Enthusiasts retain CRTs for retro gaming because of their low input latency and native rendering of low-resolution video.
