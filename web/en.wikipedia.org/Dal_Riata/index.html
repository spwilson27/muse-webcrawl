Dál Riata

Dál Riata or Dál Riada (also Dalriada) was a Gaelic kingdom that encompassed the western seaboard of Scotland and north-eastern Ireland, on each side of the North Channel. At its height in the late 6th and early 7th centuries, it covered what is now Argyll in Scotland and part of County Antrim in Northern Ireland.

History

Later tradition held that the kingdom was founded by Irish settlers under Fergus Mór in the late 5th century, bringing Gaelic language and culture from Ireland into western Scotland; the depth and timing of this migration are debated by historians, since the sea had long linked the two coasts. The kingdom's royal centre was the hillfort of Dunadd in the Kilmartin Glen. Its society was organized into kindreds (cenéla), including the Cenél nGabráin, Cenél Loairn, and Cenél nÓengusa.

The monastery of Iona, founded by St Columba in 563, made Dál Riata a centre of Gaelic Christianity, and Irish annals record its kings and sea-battles, such as the Battle of Mag Rath (637), after which the Irish portion declined, and the defeat of Domnall Brecc at Strathcarron (642). Viking raids severely weakened the kingdom in the 9th century, and its kings — Kenneth MacAlpin being traditionally counted among them — became associated with the union of the Scots and Picts that produced the Kingdom of Alba.

Legacy

Dál Riata gave its name, through "Scoti" (the Latin name for its Gaelic people), to Scotland itself. The Stone of Destiny tradition and the Senchus fer n-Alban ("History of the men of Scotland") preserve its genealogies and military obligations.
