Académie Française

Founded: 1635
Founder: Cardinal Richelieu
Seats: 40 ("les immortels")
Seat: Institut de France, Paris

The Académie Française (French Academy) is France's principal authority on matters pertaining to the French language. It was founded in 1635 by Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister to King Louis XIII, and is the pre-eminent French learned body on the language.

The Academy consists of 40 members, known informally as les immortels ("the immortals"), who are elected for life by the existing members. New members are received in a formal ceremony wearing the famous green embroidered uniform, the habit vert, complete with ceremonial sword.

Role

The Academy's official charge is to work "with all possible care and diligence" to give definite rules to the French language and to maintain its purity. Its most visible work is the Dictionnaire de l'Académie française, the official dictionary of French, first published in 1694 and periodically revised; work on the ninth edition proceeded for much of the twentieth century and beyond. The Academy also rules on proposed terminology, particularly official replacements for foreign loanwords in government and technical usage.

History

The Academy grew out of informal meetings of writers at the home of Valentin Conrart in the early 1630s. Richelieu, seeing a means of bringing literary life under royal patronage, offered the group official status, and letters patent were issued in 1635. The body was suppressed during the French Revolution, restored under Napoleon as part of the Institut de France, and has met ever since under the dome of the Institut on the Quai de Conti in Paris.

Notable members have included Racine, Voltaire, Victor Hugo, Louis Pasteur, and Marguerite Yourcenar, the first woman elected, in 1980.
