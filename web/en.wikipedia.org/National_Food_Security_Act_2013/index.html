National Food Security Act 2013

The National Food Security Act 2013 (NFSA 2013) is an Indian Act of Parliament which aims to provide subsidized food grains to approximately two-thirds of the country's 1.4 billion people. It was signed into law on 12 September 2013, retroactive to 5 July 2013. The Act converted into legal entitlements existing food security programmes of the Government of India, including the Midday Meal Scheme, the Integrated Child Development Services scheme, and the Public Distribution System (PDS). It is one of the largest food security programmes in the world.

Overview

The Act provides coverage for up to 75 percent of the rural population and up to 50 percent of the urban population, who receive 5 kilograms of food grains per person per month at subsidized prices: rice at ₹3 per kg, wheat at ₹2 per kg, and coarse grains at ₹1 per kg. Pregnant women, lactating mothers, and certain categories of children are eligible for daily free meals. The Act also provides for a maternity benefit of ₹6,000 to pregnant and lactating mothers, and, in case of non-supply of entitled food grains or meals, a food security allowance from the concerned state government.

Eligible households comprise "priority households" and the poorest households covered by the Antyodaya Anna Yojana, which receive 35 kg per family per month. Identification of eligible households is left to state governments, guided by criteria set at the state level.

Background

The Act originated in a promise in the Congress party's 2009 election manifesto and was shaped partly by the "right to food" campaign and Supreme Court litigation that had, since 2001, converted several nutrition schemes into legal entitlements through interim orders. A National Advisory Council draft of 2010 proposed near-universal coverage; the bill introduced in Parliament in December 2011 was scaled back for fiscal reasons. It was first promulgated as an ordinance in July 2013, then passed by both houses of Parliament in August and September 2013 amid debate over cost, targeting, and effects on agricultural markets.

Implementation

Implementation proceeded state by state; by 2016 the Act was being implemented in all states and union territories. Reforms accompanying implementation included digitization of ration cards, Aadhaar-based authentication at fair price shops, and the "One Nation One Ration Card" initiative, which allows migrant workers to draw rations anywhere in India. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the government announced additional free grain allocations (under the Pradhan Mantri Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana) over and above NFSA entitlements; from January 2023 the government made NFSA food grains free of charge, subsuming the pandemic-era scheme.

Cost and criticism

The food subsidy required to implement the Act was estimated at around ₹1.25 trillion (about US$20 billion) annually at enactment, borne mainly by the central government through the Food Corporation of India, which procures grain at minimum support prices and distributes it to states. Critics have questioned the fiscal burden, leakages and corruption in the PDS (which studies showed declining substantially in the 2000s and 2010s), storage losses, and the distortion of cropping patterns toward rice and wheat. Supporters respond that the Act gives legal rights to food for some 800 million people, improves nutrition security, and that state-level reforms demonstrated the PDS could function effectively.
