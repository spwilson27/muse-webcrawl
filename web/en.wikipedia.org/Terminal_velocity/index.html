Terminal velocity

Terminal velocity is the maximum speed attainable by an object as it falls through a fluid (air or liquid). It occurs when the sum of the drag force and buoyancy equals the downward force of gravity, so the net force on the object is zero and it no longer accelerates. For a typical skydiver in a spread-eagle position, terminal velocity is about 53 m/s (190 km/h or 120 mph); in a head-down dive it can exceed 80 m/s (290 km/h).

Physics

For objects at moderate Reynolds numbers the drag force is approximately proportional to the square of the speed: Fd = ½ ρ v² Cd A, where ρ is the fluid density, Cd the drag coefficient, and A the reference (usually cross-sectional) area. Terminal velocity therefore follows as vt = √(2mg / ρCdA): it increases with mass and gravity and decreases with drag area and fluid density. Small, light objects such as dust grains, mist droplets, and insects reach very low terminal velocities, which is why small animals can survive falls that would kill a human. For very small particles in viscous flow, Stokes' law gives drag proportional to velocity instead.

Approach to terminal velocity

An object dropped from rest asymptotically approaches terminal velocity; the speed follows a hyperbolic-tangent profile in the quadratic-drag regime. A skydiver typically takes about 12 seconds and roughly 450 m (1,500 ft) of fall to reach 99% of terminal velocity. Opening a parachute greatly increases drag area, lowering terminal velocity to a few metres per second and permitting a survivable landing.

Examples

Raindrops fall at roughly 6–9 m/s depending on size; hailstones, being denser, can reach 30 m/s or more. A baseball dropped from a height reaches about 33 m/s (120 km/h). The concept also applies in reverse to objects rising through fluids, such as bubbles and sedimenting particles, and in fluidised beds and settling tanks in engineering.
