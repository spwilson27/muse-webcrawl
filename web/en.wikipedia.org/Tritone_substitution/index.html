Tritone substitution

A dominant seventh chord can be replaced by the dominant seventh chord a tritone away, which shares the same guide tones.

The tritone substitution is a chord substitution common in jazz and other styles of harmony, in which a dominant seventh chord is replaced by another dominant seventh chord whose root is a tritone away. The substitution works because the two chords share the same "guide tones" — the third and seventh — with the third of one chord functioning as the seventh of the other and vice versa.

How it works

For example, in the key of C major, the dominant chord G7 (G–B–D–F) may be replaced by D♭7 (D♭–F–A♭–C♭). The third and seventh of G7 are B and F; in D♭7, the seventh is C♭ (enharmonically B) and the third is F. Because these two tones — which define the dominant function and pull toward resolution — are preserved, the substitute chord can resolve to the tonic (C major or C minor) much as the original did. The bass line, however, moves by semitone (D♭ down to C) instead of by perfect fifth, giving a smooth chromatic approach to the tonic.

Use in jazz

The tritone substitution is a hallmark of bebop and later jazz harmony. It is frequently applied to ii–V–I progressions, turning, for instance, Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 into Dm7–D♭7–Cmaj7. It also underlies the "tritone sub" of secondary dominants and is related to the German augmented sixth chord of classical harmony, which resolves in a comparable way. Pianists and guitarists use tritone substitutions to create chromatic bass lines, to reharmonize standards, and to add harmonic tension.
