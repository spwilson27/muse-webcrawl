Designated survivor

A designated survivor (or designated successor) is a member of the United States Cabinet who is kept at a physically distant, secure and undisclosed location when the president, vice president and other officials in the line of succession gather at a single place, such as during State of the Union addresses or presidential inaugurations. The practice, dating to the Cold War era of nuclear fears, ensures continuity of government: if a catastrophic event killed the assembled leadership, the designated survivor would become acting president under the Presidential Succession Act of 1947.

Practice

The designated survivor is typically a Cabinet secretary chosen by the White House chief of staff, eligible to serve as president (a natural-born citizen over 35), and receives a full security detail, briefing materials and access to emergency communications for the duration of the event. Members of Congress of both parties also remain away from such gatherings as designated successors for the legislative branch. Identities are usually announced shortly before the event; past State of the Union designated survivors have included secretaries of agriculture and energy as well as, in 2025, the secretary of veterans affairs.

History

The precise origin of the practice is unclear, but it is generally dated to the 1950s or 1960s amid planning for a Soviet nuclear strike on Washington. It became publicly known in the 1980s. The procedure forms part of the broader continuity-of-government planning maintained by the executive branch.

In popular culture

The concept entered mainstream fiction through the television series Designated Survivor (2016–2019), starring Kiefer Sutherland as a housing secretary who becomes president after an attack on the Capitol, and in novels such as Tom Clancy's Debt of Honor. Similar arrangements exist in some other countries; several U.S. states also name designated successors for gubernatorial events.
