Saccharomyces cerevisiae

Saccharomyces cerevisiae is a species of yeast (single-celled fungal microorganisms). It is the microorganism behind the most common type of fermentation: it has been instrumental in winemaking, baking and brewing since ancient times. It is believed to have been originally isolated from the skin of grapes.

S. cerevisiae cells are round to ovoid, 5–10 μm in diameter. It reproduces by budding. Its name derives from the Greek-Latin for "sugar fungus" and "beer".

In biology

S. cerevisiae is one of the most intensively studied eukaryotic model organisms in molecular and cell biology, much like Escherichia coli as the model bacterium. Its genome was the first eukaryotic genome to be completely sequenced, published in 1996, comprising about 12 million base pairs and roughly 6,000 genes on 16 chromosomes. Studies of its cell cycle, mating types and metabolism have informed fundamental biology, and much of the knowledge of the human genome's housekeeping genes derives from yeast orthologs.

Uses

In brewing and winemaking, S. cerevisiae converts sugars to ethanol and carbon dioxide. In baking, the carbon dioxide leavens dough. Strains are sold as baker's yeast and brewer's yeast, and the species is also used in biofuel production and as a probiotic relative (S. boulardii).
