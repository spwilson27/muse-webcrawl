The masque was a form of festive courtly entertainment that flourished in early modern Europe, though it was developed most fully in England. A masque involved music, dancing, singing and acting within an elaborate stage design, in which the architectural framing and costumes were created by an architect, to present a deferential allegory flattering to the patron. Professional actors and musicians were hired for the speaking and singing parts of the masques, but the "masquers" — who danced and performed without speaking — were courtiers and sometimes members of the royal family themselves.

The Stuart masque

The form reached its height at the court of James I and Charles I in the collaborations of poet Ben Jonson and designer Inigo Jones, whose productions at Whitehall featured pioneering movable scenery and perspective staging. Jonson introduced the antimasque, a disorderly comic or grotesque interlude that preceded and contrasted with the harmony of the main masque. Queen Anne of Denmark and later Queen Henrietta Maria performed in masques themselves, a striking break with older convention.

Decline and legacy

The outbreak of the English Civil War in 1642 ended the court masque tradition. Elements of the form passed into the Restoration semi-opera of Purcell's era and into masque-like works such as Milton's Comus (1634), written for a noble household. The masque's spectacle, machinery, and fusion of the arts anticipated later developments in opera and ballet.
