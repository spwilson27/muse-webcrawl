Facial nerve

Facial nerve (CN VII)
Innervates: Muscles of facial expression, stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior digastric
Also: Taste (anterior two-thirds of tongue), lacrimal and salivary glands

The facial nerve, also known as the seventh cranial nerve or CN VII, is a mixed cranial nerve that emerges from the pons of the brainstem and controls the muscles of facial expression. It also carries taste sensations from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, supplies parasympathetic fibres to the lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands, and innervates the stapedius muscle of the middle ear.

Course

The nerve exits the skull through the internal acoustic meatus alongside the vestibulocochlear nerve, travels through the facial canal in the temporal bone — giving off the greater petrosal nerve, the nerve to stapedius and the chorda tympani — and emerges at the stylomastoid foramen. It then passes through the parotid gland, where it divides into five terminal branches, remembered as the temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular and cervical branches ("To Zanzibar By Motor Car").

Clinical significance

The most common disorder of the facial nerve is Bell's palsy, an idiopathic unilateral paralysis of the muscles of facial expression involving the whole face, including the forehead; strokes affecting the face, by contrast, typically spare the forehead because of bilateral cortical input to the upper face. Other causes of facial weakness include Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster of the geniculate ganglion), Lyme disease, tumours of the parotid gland, and trauma. Damage to the chorda tympani can disturb taste and salivation, and loss of the stapedius reflex causes hyperacusis.
