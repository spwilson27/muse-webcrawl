Pierre Curie

Pierre Curie (15 May 1859 – 19 April 1906) was a French physicist, a pioneer in crystallography, magnetism, piezoelectricity, and radioactivity. In 1903 he shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with his wife, Marie Curie, and Henri Becquerel for their work on radioactivity.

Early work

With his brother Jacques, Pierre Curie discovered piezoelectricity in 1880 — the generation of an electric charge by crystals under mechanical stress — and invented instruments including the Curie electrometer. His doctoral research on magnetism produced Curie's law and the concept of the Curie temperature, above which ferromagnetic materials lose their permanent magnetism.

Radioactivity

Joining Marie Curie's investigations of the mysterious "uranium rays" discovered by Becquerel, the Curies isolated two new elements, polonium and radium, in 1898, processing tonnes of pitchblende by hand. Pierre studied the physical properties of the radiation, including its heating effect and its deflection in magnetic fields. His health, already damaged by radiation exposure, was cut short when he was struck by a horse-drawn cart while crossing the Rue Dauphine in Paris in 1906. The unit of radioactivity, the curie, was named in honour of Pierre and Marie Curie.
