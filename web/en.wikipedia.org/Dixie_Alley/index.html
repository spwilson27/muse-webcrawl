Dixie Alley

Dixie Alley is a nickname for an area of the United States, in the Deep South and Tennessee Valley, that experiences a disproportionately high frequency of strong tornadoes, rivalling the better-known Tornado Alley of the Great Plains. The term was popularised by meteorologist and storm researcher Allen Pearson, former director of the National Severe Storms Forecast Center, in reference to tornadoes in the region from the lower Mississippi Valley across Alabama, Mississippi, Tennessee and neighbouring states.

Geography and climatology

Dixie Alley roughly encompasses parts of Arkansas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Tennessee, Georgia, Kentucky, Missouri, Florida and the Carolinas. Several factors make the region vulnerable: proximity to the Gulf of Mexico supplies warm, moist air; the terrain of hills and dense forests makes tornadoes harder to see and to track; and the tornado season extends into autumn and winter, with many storms occurring at night. Storms in Dixie Alley also tend to move faster and to be rain-wrapped, obscuring the tornado itself. Research has suggested that tornado activity in the United States has been shifting eastward from the Plains toward the Southeast in recent decades.

Risk factors

Population vulnerability in Dixie Alley is comparatively high. The region has a high proportion of mobile and manufactured homes, which offer little protection in strong tornadoes; a higher population density than the classic Tornado Alley; and many nocturnal tornadoes, when people are asleep and less likely to receive warnings. As a result, states such as Alabama and Mississippi frequently record among the highest tornado fatality rates in the country.

Notable outbreaks

The region has been struck by some of the deadliest tornado events in American history, including the 1974 Super Outbreak, which devastated northern Alabama, and the Super Outbreak of 27 April 2011, the largest tornado outbreak on record, in which violent long-track tornadoes struck Alabama, Mississippi and Tennessee, killing more than 300 people across the Southeast. Earlier disasters include the Great Natchez Tornado of 1840 and the Tupelo tornado of 1936, among the deadliest single tornadoes in US history.
