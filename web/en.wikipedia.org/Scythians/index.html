Scythians

The Scythians were an ancient Eastern Iranian equestrian nomadic people who migrated from Central Asia to the Pontic Steppe in modern Ukraine and southern Russia between the 9th and 4th centuries BC. Masters of mounted warfare, they dominated the steppe north of the Black Sea, traded and fought with the Greek colonies on its shores, and left behind rich burials filled with gold artwork in a distinctive animal style.

History

Greek historian Herodotus gives the fullest ancient account of the Scythians, describing their customs, burial rites and successful resistance to the Persian king Darius I, whose invasion of Scythia around 513 BC failed as the nomads retreated across the steppe, burning supplies. In the 4th century BC King Ateas briefly united the Scythian tribes before being defeated and killed by Philip II of Macedon in 339 BC. Thereafter Scythian power waned; they were displaced by the related Sarmatians and a reduced Scythian kingdom survived in Crimea into the Roman period.

Culture

Scythian society was led by kings and a warrior aristocracy buried in large mounds (kurgans) with sacrificed horses, servants and grave goods of gold, much of it made by Greek craftsmen for the Scythian market. They were famous as horse archers using the composite bow and the gorytos (combined bow-case and quiver), wore trousers and pointed caps, and tattooed their bodies, as shown by the preserved skin of the Pazyryk burials in the Altai.

Legacy

The name "Scythian" was applied loosely by classical and Byzantine writers to many steppe peoples. Scythian goldwork, exhibited in museums in Kyiv and St Petersburg, is among the most celebrated art of the ancient nomadic world.
