Galliformes

The Galliformes are an order of heavy-bodied, ground-feeding birds that includes the turkeys, chickens, quail, grouse, pheasants, peafowl, guineafowl, and chachalacas. The order contains about 290 species in five families: Phasianidae (pheasants, chickens, partridges), Numididae (guineafowl), Odontophoridae (New World quail), Cracidae (chachalacas, guans, and curassows), and Megapodiidae (megapodes).

Characteristics

Galliforms are adapted for a terrestrial life: they have strong legs and feet for scratching the ground in search of seeds, leaves, and insects, short rounded wings suited to brief bursts of flight, and stout bills. Many species are strongly sexually dimorphic, with males bearing ornate plumage, wattles, and spurs. Their chicks are precocial, able to feed themselves soon after hatching.

Relationship with humans

The order is of great economic importance. The domestic chicken, descended mainly from the red junglefowl, is the world's most numerous domestic bird, and the turkey, guinea fowl, and Japanese quail are also widely kept. Many species — grouse, pheasants, partridges, and wild turkeys — are hunted as game birds. Because they are ground-dwelling and conspicuous, several island and forest species, such as many cracids and megapodes, are threatened by habitat loss and hunting.
