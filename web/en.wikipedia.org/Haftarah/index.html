Haftarah

The haftarah (Hebrew: הפטרה, "parting" or "conclusion"; plural haftarot) is a series of selections from the books of Nevi'im (the Prophets) of the Hebrew Bible that is publicly read in synagogue as part of Jewish religious practice. The haftarah reading follows the Torah reading on each Sabbath and on Jewish festivals and fast days.

Practice

The person called to the final Torah reading (the maftir) traditionally chants the haftarah, which is thematically linked to the Torah portion or to the season — for example, the seven haftarot of consolation from Isaiah read after Tisha B'Av, and the "Four Special Sabbaths" before Passover. Haftarot are chanted to a distinctive cantillation melody. In most communities the text is read from a printed book rather than a scroll, though some congregations use a parchment haftarah scroll. Bar and bat mitzvah celebrants commonly chant the haftarah of their week.

Origins

The origins of the custom are uncertain. One traditional explanation holds that when the Seleucid king Antiochus IV prohibited Torah reading in the 2nd century BCE, Jews substituted passages from the Prophets resembling the forbidden portions. Another theory connects the practice to debates with sects, such as the Samaritans and later the Karaites, that rejected the Prophets, prompting an affirming public reading. The Mishnah already attests the practice by the early centuries CE.

Variations

Selections differ between Ashkenazi, Sephardi, Yemenite and other rites, and some communities (such as Rome and Aleppo) preserve distinctive local haftarot. Reform congregations sometimes choose alternative passages.
