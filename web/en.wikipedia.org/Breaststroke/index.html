Breaststroke

Breaststroke is a swimming style in which the swimmer is on their chest and the torso does not rotate. It is the most popular recreational style due to the swimmer's head being out of the water a portion of the time, and it can be swum comfortably at slow speeds. In most swimming classes, beginners learn either the breaststroke or the front crawl first. In competitive swimming, however, breaststroke is regarded as one of the most difficult strokes, requiring comparable endurance and leg strength to other strokes.

The breaststroke is swum with the body on the breast, the arms moving simultaneously in a half-circular motion in front of the body, and the legs performing a whip kick (often called a "frog kick"). Breathing is coordinated with the arm cycle, with the head lifting out of the water once per stroke. It is the slowest of the four competitive strokes, because the recovery phases of both the arms and the legs take place in the water and create substantial drag.

History

Breaststroke is the oldest known swimming stroke, depicted in Egyptian cave drawings dating back thousands of years. It was the dominant competitive stroke in the nineteenth century: the first swimming races in England in the 1830s were swum breaststroke, and Captain Matthew Webb used the stroke for his pioneering swim of the English Channel in 1875. The stroke's rules were refined over the twentieth century, notably in separating breaststroke from the butterfly stroke, which originated as a variant with an over-water arm recovery in the 1930s.

Technique

The stroke cycle consists of an outsweep and insweep of the hands, a breath taken as the hands sweep inward, and a leg kick timed to propel the body during the streamlined glide that follows. Modern elite technique emphasises a narrow, fast kick and a wave-like body undulation. Under the rules of World Aquatics (formerly FINA), all movements of the arms and legs must be simultaneous and in the same horizontal plane, and the head must break the surface of the water once per complete stroke cycle, except at the start and turns where one underwater pullout is permitted.

Competitions

Breaststroke events are contested over 50, 100, and 200 metres at the Olympic Games and World Championships, and the stroke forms one leg of the individual medley and medley relay. Notable breaststrokers include Adam Peaty of Great Britain, dominant in the 100 m event through the late 2010s, and Kosuke Kitajima of Japan, a four-time Olympic gold medallist.
