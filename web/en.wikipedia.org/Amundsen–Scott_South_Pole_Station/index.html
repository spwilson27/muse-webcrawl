Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station

The Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station is a United States scientific research station at the geographic South Pole, the southernmost place on Earth. Situated on the high plateau of Antarctica at an elevation of about 2,835 metres (9,300 feet) above sea level, it is administered by the United States Antarctic Program and operated year-round.

History

The station was established in 1956–57 for the International Geophysical Year and was named after Roald Amundsen and Robert F. Scott, who reached the Pole in 1911 and 1912 respectively. Because the ice sheet moves about ten metres per year, the station's structures are periodically replaced. A geodesic dome served as the station's centre from 1975 until 2010; the current elevated station, designed so that wind scours snow away beneath it rather than burying it, was dedicated in 2008.

Research

Research at the Pole includes astronomy and astrophysics — notably the IceCube Neutrino Observatory, buried in the ice, and the South Pole Telescope — as well as glaciology, geophysics, meteorology, and atmospheric science. The exceptionally cold, dry, stable air makes the Pole one of the best observing sites on Earth for certain wavelengths.

Life at the station

During the winter the station is isolated for about eight months, with a small crew (roughly 50 people, the "winterovers") enduring temperatures below −60 °C and complete polar night. In summer the population rises to around 150 as researchers arrive by ski-equipped LC-130 Hercules aircraft.
