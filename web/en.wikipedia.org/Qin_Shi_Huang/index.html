Qin Shi Huang

Qin Shi Huang (Chinese: 秦始皇; pinyin: Qín Shǐhuáng; c. 259 – 12 July 210 BC) was the founder of the Qin dynasty and the first emperor of China. Rather than maintain the title of "king" (wang) borne by the previous Shang and Zhou rulers, he created the new title of "emperor" (huangdi) for himself, which would become the standard title for successive imperial rulers of China for the next two millennia.

Early life and the conquest of the states

Born Ying Zheng (or Zhao Zheng) in Handan, capital of the state of Zhao, he was the son of King Zhuangxiang of Qin, then a hostage prince, and a concubine named Zhao Ji. The wealthy merchant Lü Buwei aided him as a prince, eventually succeeding as king of Qin after Zhuangxiang's death in 247 BC, when Zheng was only 13.

By 221 BC, Qin had conquered all the other warring states — Han (230 BC), Zhao (228 BC), Wei (225 BC), Chu (223 BC), Yan (222 BC) and Qi (221 BC) — unifying China for the first time. Zheng then declared himself the First Emperor, a title intended to indicate that his heirs would be the Second Emperor, Third Emperor, and so on in perpetuity.

Reign

Working with his chancellor Li Si, Qin Shi Huang enacted major economic and political reforms aimed at standardising the diverse practices of earlier Chinese states. He is traditionally said to have banned and burned many books and executed scholars, although recent historians have questioned these claims. His public works projects included the unification of diverse state walls into a single Great Wall of China and a massive new national road system, as well as the city-sized mausoleum guarded by the life-sized Terracotta Army.

His policies included the standardisation of the Chinese script, currency (the round coin with a square hole, ban liang), weights and measures, and the axle widths of carts. He established a centralised administration divided into commanderies and counties, abolishing the feudal system of the Zhou dynasty.

Death and legacy

Qin Shi Huang died in 210 BC during his fifth tour of eastern China, reportedly from ingesting mercury pills prepared by his court alchemists and astrologers in his quest for an elixir of life — ironically, the mercury most probably hastened his death. His death was kept secret by Li Si and the eunuch Zhao Gao, who manipulated the succession in favour of the younger son Huhai (Qin Er Shi) rather than the rightful heir Fusu.

His dynasty, intended to last for generations, collapsed only three years after his death amid widespread peasant revolts. Despite its brevity, the Qin dynasty left a profound legacy: the unified, centralised imperial system he created endured in China for over two thousand years.
