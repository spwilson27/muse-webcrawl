Acupuncture

Acupuncture is a form of alternative medicine and a component of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) in which thin needles are inserted into the body at specific points. According to traditional theory, these acupoints lie on meridians through which qi, or vital energy, flows, and stimulating them is held to correct imbalances in that flow. Acupuncture is most often used in attempts to relieve pain, although it is also applied to a wide range of other conditions.

History

Acupuncture's origins are debated; practices resembling it are described in Chinese texts from around the second century BCE, including the Huangdi Neijing. The practice spread throughout East Asia and, much later, to Europe and the Americas. It gained wide attention in the United States in the 1970s, following reports accompanying President Nixon's 1972 visit to China.

Scientific evaluation

Scientific investigation has not found evidence for the existence of qi, meridians or acupoints as physical entities. Clinical trials vary in quality, and systematic reviews generally conclude that acupuncture may produce small benefits for some chronic pain conditions compared with no treatment, while differences from sham acupuncture are small and of questionable clinical significance. Acupuncture is generally safe when performed by trained practitioners using sterile single-use needles; rare serious adverse events such as pneumothorax have been reported. Because its traditional theoretical basis lacks scientific support, acupuncture is often classified as pseudoscience.

Practice and regulation

Variants include electroacupuncture, in which a small electric current is passed between needle pairs, auricular (ear) acupuncture, and Japanese and Korean styles that differ in point location and needling depth. Regulation varies widely by country: in many jurisdictions acupuncturists must be licensed, and physicians may practise medical acupuncture after additional training.
