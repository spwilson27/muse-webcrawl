Monmouth Rebellion

The Monmouth Rebellion, also known as the Pitchfork Rebellion, the Revolt of the West, or the West Country rebellion, was an attempt in 1685 to depose James II of England. James had succeeded his brother Charles II on 6 February 1685. James Scott, 1st Duke of Monmouth — Charles II's eldest illegitimate son, a Protestant — claimed the throne on the false grounds that his parents had been married, and that the Catholic James was an illegitimate usurper.

Course of the rebellion

After a period of exile in the Dutch Republic and plotting with English exiles, Monmouth landed at Lyme Regis in Dorset on 11 June 1685 with just 82 companions and supplies for a small army. He raised several thousand recruits in the West Country — Somerset, Dorset and Devon — drawn largely from Protestant artisans, clothworkers and farm labourers. He was proclaimed king at Taunton, but failed to capture Bristol or Bath, and his inexperienced, poorly armed force was pursued by the royal army under the Earl of Feversham and John Churchill, the future Duke of Marlborough. On the night of 5–6 July 1685 Monmouth attempted a surprise attack on the royal camp on the Sedgemoor marshes near Bridgwater. The Battle of Sedgemoor — often called the last pitched battle fought on English soil — ended in a crushing defeat for the rebels.

Aftermath

Monmouth was captured hiding in a ditch near Ringwood, allegedly with peas in his pocket, and was beheaded on Tower Hill on 15 July 1685; the executioner needed several blows of the axe. The suppression that followed, the "Bloody Assizes" presided over by Judge Jeffreys, tried some 1,400 prisoners: roughly 300 were executed and over 800 transported to the West Indies. The brutal reprisals deepened opposition to James II and contributed to his overthrow in the Glorious Revolution three years later.
