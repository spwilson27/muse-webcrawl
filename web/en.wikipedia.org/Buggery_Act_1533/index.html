Buggery Act 1533

The Buggery Act 1533, formally An Acte for the punishment of the vice of Buggerie (25 Hen. 8 c. 6), was an Act of the Parliament of England that made sodomy a capital offence under secular law for the first time in English history. It remained in force, with interruptions, until 1861.

Provisions

The Act declared "the detestable and abominable Vice of Buggery committed with mankind or beast" to be a felony punishable by death, with no benefit of clergy and forfeiture of goods and lands. Previously such offences had been handled by ecclesiastical courts. Passed during the Reformation Parliament of Henry VIII — whose chief minister Thomas Cromwell promoted the transfer of moral offences to royal courts — it was renewed several times and made permanent under Elizabeth I in 1563.

Enforcement

Prosecutions were sporadic under the Tudors but increased in later centuries. Executions continued into the 19th century; James Pratt and John Smith, hanged in 1835, were the last men executed for sodomy in England. The Offences Against the Person Act 1861 replaced the death penalty with imprisonment, and in England and Wales consensual adult homosexual acts were decriminalised by the Sexual Offences Act 1967.

Legacy

Exported through colonisation, the language of the Buggery Act shaped anti-sodomy statutes throughout the British Empire, many of which persisted long after independence in parts of Africa, the Caribbean and Asia. Historians of sexuality regard the Act as a foundational text of the modern legal regulation of homosexuality.
