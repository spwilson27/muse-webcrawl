Void (philosophy)

The void is the philosophical concept of nothingness manifested as empty space. It has been a central topic in metaphysics from ancient atomism to contemporary discussions of existence, emptiness and the vacuum.

Ancient philosophy

The Greek atomists Leucippus and Democritus held that reality consists of atoms moving in the void (κενόν, kenon) — an empty receptacle that makes motion and plurality possible. Parmenides and his followers denied that the void could exist, arguing that "what is not" cannot be; Plato's Timaeus instead posited a receptacle (chora) in which forms are instantiated. Aristotle rejected a separate void, maintaining that nature abhors a vacuum and that place is always the boundary of a containing body.

Eastern thought

In Buddhist philosophy, śūnyatā (emptiness) describes the absence of inherent, independent existence in all phenomena — a doctrine developed by Nāgārjuna in the Madhyamaka school. In the Tao Te Ching, the usefulness of things such as vessels and rooms is attributed to their emptiness.

Modern philosophy and physics

Seventeenth-century natural philosophy revived the void through the experiments of Torricelli and Pascal on the barometric vacuum, which showed that a space devoid of air could be produced. In modern physics, the vacuum is not truly empty but seethes with quantum fluctuations, complicating the older metaphysical notion of absolute nothingness. Existentialist writers, notably Sartre, used nothingness (le néant) to describe the gap between consciousness and the world that makes freedom possible.
