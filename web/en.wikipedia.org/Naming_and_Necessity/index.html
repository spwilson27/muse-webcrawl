Naming and Necessity

Naming and Necessity is a 1972 book by the American philosopher Saul Kripke, based on three lectures delivered at Princeton University in 1970. It is regarded as one of the most important works of twentieth-century analytic philosophy, reshaping debates in philosophy of language and metaphysics about reference, necessity and a posteriori truths.

Main arguments

Kripke argues against descriptivist theories of names associated with Frege and Russell, according to which a proper name is synonymous with a cluster of descriptions. Instead, he proposes the causal-historical picture: a name is fixed by an initial "baptism" and passed along a causal chain of speakers. Proper names are rigid designators — they refer to the same object in every possible world in which that object exists.

From this Kripke draws modal conclusions: some truths are necessary a posteriori, knowable only empirically yet true in all possible worlds — for example, that Hesperus is Phosphorus, and that water is H₂O. Conversely, some truths are contingent a priori, such as the length of the standard metre fixed by definition. The book also argues for a form of mind–body dualism problem by contending that pain cannot be identical to a brain state in the way that heat is identical to molecular motion.

Influence

The lectures helped revive metaphysics within analytic philosophy and underpin later essentialist and direct-reference theories, as well as work on natural kinds by Hilary Putnam and others.
