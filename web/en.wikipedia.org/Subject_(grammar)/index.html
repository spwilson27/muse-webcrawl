Subject (grammar)

Subject (grammar)
FieldGrammar, syntax, linguistics
DefinitionThe noun phrase about which the clause predicates something
Typical caseNominative (in nominative–accusative languages)

The subject of a sentence (or clause) is the person, place, thing, or idea that is doing or being something — more precisely, the noun phrase about which the rest of the clause, the predicate, makes a statement. In "The cat sleeps," the cat is the subject. Traditional grammar divides the simple sentence into subject and predicate, a division going back to Aristotle.

Identifying subjects

Subjects are identified by several tests. In English the subject typically precedes the verb in declaratives, controls verb agreement ("she runs," not "she run"), appears in the nominative case for pronouns (I, he, she, we, they), and is what moves in questions ("Is the cat sleeping?") and what is echoed in tag questions ("The cat is sleeping, isn't it?").

Types of subject

Subjects may be simple nouns, pronouns, gerund phrases ("Swimming is fun"), infinitive phrases, or whole clauses ("What he said surprised everyone"). Some sentences have a dummy or expletive subject with no semantic content, as in "It is raining" or "There is a problem." Imperatives usually have an implied subject "you."

Cross-linguistic variation

Languages differ in how subjects behave. In ergative–absolutive languages the grouping of arguments differs from the nominative–accusative pattern familiar from European languages, and linguists debate whether "subject" is a universal category or a language-specific bundle of properties — typically including the agent role, topic status, case marking, and agreement control. In pro-drop languages such as Spanish or Japanese, subjects are frequently omitted when recoverable from context or verb inflection.
