Repression (psychology)

In Freud's model, the ego represses unacceptable impulses of the id.

In psychoanalytic theory, repression is a defence mechanism by which the ego unconsciously excludes distressing or unacceptable thoughts, memories, and impulses from conscious awareness, consigning them to the unconscious mind, where they continue to influence behaviour indirectly. Sigmund Freud regarded repression as the cornerstone of psychoanalysis: the mechanism by which the mind manages the conflict between instinctual drives and the demands of civilisation and conscience.

Freudian theory

Freud distinguished primal repression, which fixes the earliest instinctual representatives outside consciousness, from repression proper, in which material that has reached awareness is pushed back down. Repressed material, he held, returns in disguised forms — dreams, slips of the tongue, and neurotic symptoms — and psychoanalytic treatment aimed to undo repression by making the unconscious conscious. Later theorists, including Anna Freud, systematised repression among the ego's defences, and the concept shaped literary criticism and everyday language far beyond the clinic.

Evidence and criticism

Experimental psychology has provided only limited support for repression as classically conceived. Critics note the difficulty of distinguishing repression from ordinary forgetting, suppression, and non-disclosure, and the construct's role in the recovered memory controversy damaged its scientific standing. Some researchers have nonetheless proposed related phenomena, such as motivated forgetting and retrieval inhibition, studied in think/no-think paradigms, as partial modern analogues.
