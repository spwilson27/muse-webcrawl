Proto-Uralic language

The Proto-Uralic homeland is generally placed in the forest zone west of the Urals.

Proto-Uralic is the unattested reconstructed ancestor of the Uralic languages, which include Finnish, Estonian, Hungarian, the Sami languages and the Samoyedic languages of Siberia. It is reconstructed by the comparative method from correspondences among its descendants, since no written records of the language exist.

Scholars generally date Proto-Uralic to roughly 4,000–6,000 years ago, though estimates vary widely. Its homeland (Urheimat) is usually located in the forest zone of the northern Eurasian taiga, somewhere between the Volga region and western Siberia, often near the Ural Mountains — the range from which the family takes its name.

Reconstruction

Reconstructed Proto-Uralic had a modest consonant inventory and a vowel system with front/back harmony in at least some reconstructions. Typical features inherited by the daughters include agglutinative morphology, a rich case system for nouns, negative verbs (a special auxiliary used to negate clauses, preserved in Finnish), and the absence of grammatical gender. Core vocabulary that can be reconstructed includes words for body parts, kinship, hunting and fishing implements, and natural features of the northern forest, such as names for the spruce and the Siberian pine.

The deepest split in the family is traditionally between Samoyedic and the Finno-Ugric languages, though some recent scholarship questions whether "Finno-Ugric" forms a valid node, proposing instead a rake-like series of early branches.

Contacts

Loanword layers show that early Uralic speakers were in contact with Indo-European-speaking peoples: early borrowings are associated with Proto-Indo-Iranian and later with Baltic, Germanic and Slavic languages, providing evidence for dating and locating the proto-language's dispersal.
