Parkerizing

Parkerizing is a method of protecting a steel surface from corrosion and increasing its resistance to wear through the application of a chemical phosphate conversion coating. The process, also known generically as phosphating or phosphate coating, involves immersing the steel part in a heated solution of phosphoric acid and metal salts, which reacts with the surface to form a layer of insoluble crystalline phosphates. The name derives from the Parker Rust-Proof Company of Detroit, which commercialised and licensed the process in the early twentieth century.

Parkerized components, showing the characteristic matte grey finish.

Process

Parts are first cleaned, degreased, and often grit-blasted, then immersed in a bath of heated phosphoric-acid solution containing zinc or manganese salts. As the acid attacks the steel, hydrogen is evolved and the local pH at the surface rises, causing zinc or manganese iron phosphates to precipitate as a tightly adherent crystalline coating. The resulting finish is matte grey to black and slightly porous, so it is normally sealed with oil, lacquer, or paint, which the porous crystals hold well; much of the corrosion protection comes from this retained oil film.

History and use

Phosphate rustproofing was developed in Britain in the early 1900s and refined in the United States by the Parker family, whose patents and licensing agreements spread the process through the automotive and arms industries. During the Second World War it became the standard finish on American military firearms such as the M1 Garand, replacing bluing on many weapons because it was cheaper, faster, and less reflective. Zinc phosphating remains an important pretreatment under paint for car bodies and appliances, while manganese phosphating is used on gears, bearings, and other parts where wear resistance and oil retention matter.
