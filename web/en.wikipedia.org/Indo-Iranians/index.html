Indo-Iranians

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Indo-Iranians are the peoples who speak Indo-Iranian languages, a major branch of the Indo-European language family. In historical linguistics and archaeology the term also denotes the prehistoric speakers of Proto-Indo-Iranian, who are generally associated with the Sintashta and Andronovo cultures of the Eurasian steppe in the late third and second millennia BCE.

Branches

Indo-Iranian divides into three groups: Indo-Aryan languages (including Sanskrit, Hindi-Urdu, Bengali, Punjabi, and Romani), Iranian languages (including Persian, Pashto, Kurdish, and Ossetian), and the small Nuristani group of the Hindu Kush. Together they are today spoken by well over a billion people across South and Western Asia.

Origins and expansion

The Sintashta culture (c. 2100–1800 BCE) of the southern Urals is noted for early chariot burials and fortified settlements; its successor, the Andronovo horizon, spread across the steppe to Central Asia. Groups moving south interacted with the Bactria–Margiana Archaeological Complex and entered the Iranian plateau and the Indian subcontinent, where Indo-Aryan speakers composed the Rigveda (c. 1500–1200 BCE). Related names and gods appear in fourteenth-century BCE Mitanni treaties in Syria.

Culture and religion

Early Indo-Iranian society was pastoral, organised in clans, with cattle wealth, horse sacrifice, and a pantheon reflected in both the Vedas and the Avesta — including figures such as Mitra/Mithra and the ritual drink soma/haoma. Their religious traditions diverged into Vedic religion (ancestor of Hinduism) and Zoroastrianism.
