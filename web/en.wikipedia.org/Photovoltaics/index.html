Photovoltaics

Photovoltaics (PV) is the conversion of light into electricity using semiconducting materials that exhibit the photovoltaic effect, a phenomenon studied in physics, photochemistry, and electrochemistry. The photovoltaic effect is commercially used for electricity generation in solar panels and as photosensors.

A photovoltaic system employs solar modules, each comprising a number of solar cells, which generate electrical power. PV installations may be ground-mounted, rooftop-mounted, wall-mounted, or floating. The mount may be fixed or use a solar tracker to follow the sun across the sky.

History

The photovoltaic effect was first observed by Alexandre-Edmond Becquerel in 1839. The first practical silicon solar cell was developed at Bell Laboratories in 1954. Solar power was long confined to space applications and niche uses, but steep cost declines since around 2010 have made PV one of the cheapest sources of electricity in much of the world.

Technology

Most commercial solar cells are made of crystalline silicon, either monocrystalline or polycrystalline. Thin-film technologies include cadmium telluride, CIGS, and amorphous silicon, while perovskite solar cells are an emerging technology with rapidly improving efficiencies. Commercial module efficiencies typically range from about 15% to over 23%. Photovoltaic systems require inverters to convert direct current to alternating current for grid use.

Deployment

Cumulative global installed PV capacity passed one terawatt in 2022 and continues to grow rapidly, led by China, the United States, India, and Europe. Solar power is now a major contributor to electricity generation worldwide and a central pillar of strategies to decarbonize energy systems.
