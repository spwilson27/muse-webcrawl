Late blight

Causal agent: Phytophthora infestans (oomycete)
Hosts: Potato, tomato, other Solanaceae
Historical impact: Principal cause of the Great Irish Famine (1845–1852)

Late blight is a devastating disease of potato and tomato caused by the water mould (oomycete) Phytophthora infestans. It is notorious as the organism responsible for the European potato failures of the 1840s, most catastrophically the Great Famine in Ireland, and it remains the most destructive potato disease worldwide, costing billions of dollars annually.

Symptoms and life cycle

Infection begins with water-soaked lesions on leaves that rapidly turn dark brown or black, often ringed by a pale halo; in humid conditions a white, downy mildew grows on the undersides where sporangia form. Sporangia spread by wind and rain splash; in cool, wet weather they can release motile zoospores that infect new tissue, driving explosive epidemics that blacken whole fields within days. Tubers develop a reddish-brown, granular rot that often gives way to secondary bacterial soft rot. The pathogen overwinters in infected tubers, volunteer plants, and cull piles, and — since the global spread of the A2 mating type in the late 20th century — can also form long-lived sexual oospores in soil.

Management

Control relies on resistant varieties, destruction of inoculum sources, forecasting systems that trigger fungicide applications during blight-favourable weather, and protectant and systemic fungicides — though the pathogen readily evolves resistance, as with metalaxyl. Researchers draw resistance genes from wild potato relatives such as Solanum demissum and S. bulbocastanum; the genome of P. infestans was sequenced in 2009.
