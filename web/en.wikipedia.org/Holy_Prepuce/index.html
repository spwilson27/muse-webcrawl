Holy Prepuce

The Holy Prepuce, or Holy Foreskin (Latin sanctum praeputium), is one of several relics attributed to Jesus, consisting of the foreskin removed during the circumcision of Jesus. At various points in history, a number of churches in Europe have claimed to possess the Holy Prepuce, sometimes at the same time. Various miraculous powers have been ascribed to it.

History

According to Jewish custom, Jesus was circumcised eight days after his birth. Leo Allatius, a 17th-century Vatican librarian, wrote a treatise arguing that the foreskin ascended to heaven with Jesus and became the rings of Saturn. Claims to possess the relic were numerous in the Middle Ages: the Abbey of Charroux claimed Charlemagne received it from an angel; other claimants included the Lateran in Rome, the abbey of Coulombs, Antwerp, and the church of Calcata in Italy.

The abbey church of Charroux claimed the Holy Foreskin was presented to the monks by Charlemagne; it was lost during the Wars of Religion and reportedly rediscovered in a wall in 1856. In Calcata, the relic was carried in procession on the Feast of the Circumcision (1 January) until 1983, when the reliquary was stolen.

Veneration and criticism

The Holy Prepuce was associated with fertility miracles, and pregnant women or women in labour made pilgrimages to churches possessing it. During the Reformation and the Enlightenment the relic was an object of ridicule; Voltaire treated it ironically in his Treatise on Tolerance. In 1900 the Catholic Church resolved that anyone writing or speaking publicly of the Holy Prepuce would be excommunicated, and the Second Vatican Council later removed the Feast of the Circumcision from the liturgical calendar, renaming it the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God.

In theology

The circumcision of Jesus is the subject of the doctrinal question of how, if Christ's flesh ascended to heaven whole, the foreskin could remain on earth. The question was discussed by medieval theologians; the apocryphal Arabic Infancy Gospel relates that the foreskin was preserved in an alabaster box of spikenard by an old Hebrew woman, identified in some traditions as Mary Magdalene.
