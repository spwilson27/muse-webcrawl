Deuterocanonical books

The deuterocanonical books (from the Greek words deuteros, "second", and kanōn, "rule" or "measure") are books and passages considered by the Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Oriental Orthodox Churches, and the Church of the East to be canonical books of the Old Testament, although generally held to be secondary in authority to the protocanonical books.

They are included in the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible, and generally date from the period between approximately 300 BC and 100 BC. Most Protestant traditions do not regard them as canonical scripture, and refer to them as the Apocrypha.

Books included

The deuterocanonical books typically include Tobit, Judith, the additions to Esther, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch (including the Letter of Jeremiah), the additions to Daniel (the Prayer of Azariah and Song of the Three Holy Children, Susanna, and Bel and the Dragon), 1 Maccabees, and 2 Maccabees.

History

The term "deuterocanonical" was coined by Sixtus of Siena in 1566 to distinguish these books, whose canonicity was affirmed later, from the protocanonical books received earlier. The Council of Trent (1546) formally affirmed their canonical status for the Catholic Church. Some of these texts were written in Hebrew or Aramaic, though several survive only in Greek.
