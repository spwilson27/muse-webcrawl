The Gospel of John (Ancient Greek: Εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Ἰωάννην, romanized: Euaggélion katà Iōánnēn) is the fourth of the four canonical gospels in the New Testament. It contains a highly schematic account of the ministry of Jesus, opening with an elaborate theological prologue—"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God"—followed by a "Book of Signs" (chapters 1–12), the extended Farewell Discourse, and an account of Jesus's arrest, crucifixion and resurrection, ending with the words "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God".

John differs sharply from the three Synoptic Gospels: it contains no parables, no exorcisms and no account of the Last Supper as an institution of the Eucharist (substituting the washing of the disciples' feet), and it presents long discourses on theological themes and a ministry spanning several Passovers rather than one. Its Christology is notably "high", presenting Jesus as the pre-existent Logos (Word) made flesh, and it records seven "I am" sayings and seven signs, including the wedding at Cana, the raising of Lazarus and Jesus walking on water.

Authorship and dating

Tradition attributes the gospel to John the Apostle, though the text itself is anonymous and its final chapter refers to the "beloved disciple" as its witness and apparent source. Most scholars hold that it was written in stages by an author or community (the "Johannine community") and reached its final form around 90–100 AD, possibly at Ephesus. The oldest surviving fragment of any New Testament book, the Rylands Papyrus P52, is a portion of John dated to the first half of the 2nd century.
