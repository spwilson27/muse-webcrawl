Surgical staple

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Surgical staples are specialised staples used in surgery in place of sutures to close skin wounds or to connect and remove parts of internal tissues such as the bowel and lungs. The use of staples over sutures reduces local inflammatory reaction and the amount of time required to close most wounds. Modern staples for use on the skin are placed on top of the skin and are typically removed with a specialised staple remover; staples for internal use are delivered by disposable applicators (staplers) that fire rows of staples and often incorporate a knife to cut between them.

History

The technique was pioneered by Hungarian surgeon Hümér Hültl in 1908, whose prototype stapler weighed 3.6 kg (8 lb) and took two hours to assemble and load. The design was refined in the Soviet Union in the 1950s, leading to the first commercially produced reusable stapling devices for gastrointestinal and thoracic surgery, and in the United States by Mark Ravitch and others, leading to lightweight disposable staplers that became widespread from the 1970s.

Internal staples

Internal staples are usually made of titanium or stainless steel (and occasionally absorbable polymers) and are fired in patterns: linear staplers place straight double or triple staggered rows; linear cutters staple and divide tissue simultaneously; circular staplers create end-to-end anastomoses of hollow organs such as the colon; and skin-type staplers are also adapted to laparoscopic use. Staple size is matched to tissue thickness via colour-coded cartridges. Internal staples remain in the body permanently (unless later removed surgically) and, being titanium, are generally compatible with magnetic resonance imaging, though they produce some imaging artifact.

Skin staples

Skin staples offer fast closure of long incisions and lacerations, particularly on the scalp and trunk, with wound-edge eversion and cosmetic results comparable to sutures. They must be removed, usually after 7–14 days depending on location, using a remover that bends the staple's centre downward and lifts its ends out of the skin. They are not recommended over the face or hands where sutures give finer control, and are contraindicated where they would interfere with imaging or where patients require MRI within the early healing period in the case of some non-titanium products.

Advantages and risks

Advantages include speed of closure, reduced operating time, and in bowel surgery, reliable uniform anastomoses. Risks include staple-line bleeding and leakage, misfiring or malformed staples, infection, and, for skin staples, poorer results if left in too long (cross-hatch scarring) — risks balanced against those of hand-sewn closures in comparative studies.
