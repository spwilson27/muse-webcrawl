The Harvard College Observatory, founded in 1839.

The Harvard College Observatory (HCO) is an institution managing a complex of buildings and multiple instruments used for astronomical research by the Harvard University Department of Astronomy. Located in Cambridge, Massachusetts, United States, it was founded in 1839 and is among the oldest astronomical observatories in the United States.

History

Harvard's first telescope, the 15-inch "Great Refractor", was installed in 1847 and was for two decades the largest telescope in the United States; with it, William Cranch Bond and John Adams Whipple produced pioneering daguerreotypes of the Moon and of the star Vega, among the earliest astronomical photographs. Under director Edward Charles Pickering (1877–1919), the observatory became a world center for stellar photometry and spectroscopy.

The Harvard Computers

Pickering employed a large staff of women — the so-called "Harvard Computers" — to classify stars on photographic plates. Their achievements include Williamina Fleming's system of stellar classification, Antonia Maury's spectral catalogue, Annie Jump Cannon's simplification of the classification scheme into the O–B–A–F–G–K–M sequence still used today, and Henrietta Swan Leavitt's discovery of the period–luminosity relation for Cepheid variables, a key rung of the cosmic distance ladder. The observatory's collection of roughly half a million astronomical photographic plates remains a unique archive of the sky over more than a century.

Later history

Directors included Harlow Shapley (1921–1952), who debated the scale of the universe with Heber Curtis and established the observatory's graduate program, and Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin, who became the first woman to head a Harvard department. In 1973 the observatory's research functions were combined with those of the Smithsonian Astrophysical Observatory to form the Harvard–Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics (CfA). The observatory's historic site on Garden Street now houses CfA offices, a library, and the plate stacks.
