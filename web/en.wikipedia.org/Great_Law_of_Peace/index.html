The Great Law of Peace (Mohawk: Kaianere'kó:wa) is the oral constitution of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), the league of initially five (later six) Indigenous nations in what is now New York State and surrounding regions. According to tradition, it was delivered by the prophet known as the Great Peacemaker (Deganawida), assisted by the leaders Hiawatha and Jigonhsasee, who persuaded the warring Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca nations to end their cycle of bloodshed and unite under a common council and law.

Tradition of the founding

In the traditional narrative, the Peacemaker crossed Lake Ontario in a stone canoe, won over the nations one by one, and finally overcame the feared Onondaga sorcerer-chief Tadodaho, whose hair of snakes was combed out by Hiawatha. The weapons of war were buried beneath a great white pine, the Tree of Peace, at Onondaga, and an eagle was set atop it to watch for approaching danger. The date of the founding is debated; some Haudenosaunee traditions and researchers associate it with a solar eclipse in 1142, while other scholars argue for dates in the 15th or 16th centuries.

Contents and structure

The Great Law, transmitted orally and encoded in wampum belts, comprises over a hundred articles (commonly enumerated as 117). It establishes the Grand Council of fifty hoya:ne (chiefs or sachems), whose titles are inherited within clans and whose holders are nominated — and can be removed — by clan mothers. It prescribes procedures for deliberation between the elder brothers (Mohawk, Seneca, Onondaga) and younger brothers (Oneida, Cayuga, later Tuscarora), condolence ceremonies for mourning and installing chiefs, laws on adoption, emigration, and the rights of foreign nations to join the league. Decision-making is by consensus, and the Onondaga serve as the council's firekeepers and keepers of the wampum records.

Influence debate

Some scholars and Haudenosaunee advocates argue that the Great Law influenced the development of American federal democracy, pointing to Benjamin Franklin's familiarity with the Confederacy and the Albany Plan of Union; in 1988 the United States Senate passed a resolution acknowledging the Iroquois Confederacy's contribution to the Constitution. Other historians regard the direct influence as limited and have criticised the thesis as overstated, while accepting that the Confederacy impressed colonial observers as a working model of federal union.

Transmission

The law was preserved by official wampum keepers and recited in full at condolence councils, a recitation that can take days. Written English versions became widely available in the 19th and 20th centuries through the work of ethnologists and Haudenosaunee authorities such as Arthur C. Parker, who published The Constitution of the Five Nations in 1916.
