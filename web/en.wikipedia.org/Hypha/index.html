Hypha

A hypha (plural hyphae, from Greek ὑφή, "web") is a long, branching, filamentous structure of a fungus, oomycete, or actinobacterium. In most fungi, hyphae are the main mode of vegetative growth, and are collectively called a mycelium. A hypha consists of one or more cells surrounded by a tubular cell wall composed largely of chitin and glucans.

Structure and growth

Hyphae grow at their tips, driven by internal pressure and the delivery of vesicles to an organising structure called the Spitzenkörper. Many hyphae are divided by cross-walls called septa, each septum having a pore that allows cytoplasm and organelles to pass between cells; some groups, such as the Mucoromycota, have largely coenocytic hyphae without septa. Nuclei can migrate along hyphae, and genetically distinct hyphae can fuse (anastomosis) to form heterokaryotic mycelia.

Functions and modifications

Beyond absorbing nutrients through their large surface area, hyphae can be modified for special tasks: haustoria penetrate host cells in parasitic fungi; appressoria generate enormous pressure to breach plant cuticles; mycelial cords and rhizomorphs transport water and nutrients; and hyphae aggregate into the tissues of mushrooms and other fruiting bodies. In lichens and mycorrhizas, hyphae form the interface between fungus and partner organism.
