The Enchiridion (Greek: Ἐγχειρίδιον, "handbook" or "manual", literally "that which is held in the hand") is a short manual of Stoic ethical advice compiled by Arrian, a pupil of the Greek philosopher Epictetus, in the early second century CE. Epictetus, a former slave who taught in Nicopolis, wrote nothing himself; Arrian also preserved his teaching at length in the Discourses, from which the Enchiridion's 53 brief chapters are drawn.

Content

The handbook opens with Stoicism's foundational distinction: "Some things are up to us and some things are not up to us." Things up to us are our judgements, impulses, desires, and aversions — in short, our own mental activity; things not up to us include the body, property, reputation, and external events. The recurring prescription is to desire only what depends on oneself, to treat externals as indifferent, and to meet events as they come without demanding that the world conform to one's wishes. Later chapters give practical maxims: do not grieve as others grieve, remember the fragility of what you love (Epictetus advises saying of a cup that it is a cup, and of a child that he is mortal), act the part assigned to you in the drama of life well, and attend to the impressions that strike the mind before assenting to them.

Influence

The Enchiridion was one of the most widely read handbooks of practical philosophy in late antiquity, the Byzantine world, and early modern Europe. Christian monks adapted it in paraphrases attributed to figures such as Nilus of Ancyra, and it was a favourite of thinkers as different as the Emperor Marcus Aurelius (who echoes it in his Meditations), Blaise Pascal, and Frederick the Great. In modern times it has been championed by Albert Ellis as an ancestor of cognitive behavioural therapy, and by writers on resilience and military leadership; James Stockdale credited it with sustaining him as a prisoner of war in Vietnam. Elizabeth Carter's 1758 English translation remained standard for two centuries.
