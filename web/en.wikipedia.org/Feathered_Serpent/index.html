Feathered Serpent

The Feathered Serpent is a prominent supernatural entity or deity found in the mythologies and iconography of many pre-Columbian cultures of Mesoamerica. It is best known from its central Mexican names, Quetzalcóatl in Nahuatl and Kukulkan in Yucatec Maya, and appears in the art and religion of cultures including the Olmec, Teotihuacan, Toltec, Aztec and Maya.

RegionMesoamerica
NamesQuetzalcóatl, Kukulkan, Gukumatz, Q'uq'umatz
AssociationsWind, Venus, learning, priesthood

Origins

The earliest known representations of a feathered serpent date to the Olmec culture (around 900 BCE), including a carving at La Venta. At Teotihuacan, the Temple of the Feathered Serpent (the third-largest pyramid at the site) bears monumental serpent heads, dated to around the third century CE.

Significance

The deity combined the attributes of the quetzal bird and the serpent, joining sky and earth. Quetzalcóatl was associated with the wind, the planet Venus, dawn, learning and books, and was said in some traditions to have created humanity from the bones of the dead. The feathered serpent remained a recurring symbol in Mesoamerican religion for well over a thousand years.
