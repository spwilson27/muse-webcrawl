Retroactive legislation

Retroactive legislation (or retrospective legislation) is a law that extends its application to events that occurred, or transactions that were completed, before the law was enacted. Retroactivity is generally disfavoured in legal systems because it can upset settled expectations and the rule of law.

Criminal law

Applying criminal law retroactively is widely prohibited. The United States Constitution bans ex post facto laws; the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 7) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights likewise forbid punishment for acts that were not criminal when committed, or heavier penalties than those then applicable — with a notable exception for acts criminal under general principles of international law (as applied at Nuremberg).

Civil and tax law

In civil matters, courts often apply a presumption against retroactivity, but legislatures may enact retrospective laws where constitutional rules allow, for instance to close tax loopholes, validate defective acts, or change procedural rules. Retroactive tax legislation remains controversial and is limited in some jurisdictions.
