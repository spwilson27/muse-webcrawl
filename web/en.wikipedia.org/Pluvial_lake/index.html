Pluvial lake

Wave-cut shorelines of pluvial Lake Bonneville in Utah

A pluvial lake is a body of water that accumulated in a basin because of increased rainfall and reduced evaporation during a wetter climatic period — typically a glacial epoch — and that has since shrunk or vanished entirely as the climate became more arid. The term derives from the Latin pluvia, "rain". Pluvial lakes are most conspicuous in present-day deserts, where their abandoned shorelines, beach ridges and lake-bed sediments (playas) record past climates.

Formation

During the Pleistocene glaciations, storm tracks were displaced toward the equator and cooler temperatures reduced evaporation, so closed basins in regions such as the Great Basin of North America filled with deep freshwater lakes despite receiving only modestly more rain. When the ice sheets retreated, evaporation outpaced inflow and the lakes desiccated, leaving salt flats and small saline remnants such as Utah's Great Salt Lake — the shrunken residue of Lake Bonneville — and California's Salton Sea basin, once filled by Lake Cahuilla.

Notable examples

Lake Bonneville covered some 51,000 km² of Utah, Nevada and Idaho and reached depths of over 300 m; about 18,000 years ago it catastrophically drained through Red Rock Pass in the Bonneville Flood, one of the largest known freshwater floods. Lake Lahontan filled much of northwestern Nevada, leaving Pyramid Lake and Walker Lake as remnants and distinctive tufa formations. In the Mojave Desert, a chain of lakes along the Mojave River included Lake Manix and Lake Mojave, while Lake Searles left rich borax and trona deposits. In Africa, Lake Chad expanded to form "Mega-Chad", and paleolake Makgadikgadi filled the Okavango region of Botswana. Pluvial lakes also filled basins in Central Asia and the Australian interior, where Lake Eyre periodically refills today.

Scientific significance

Because their high stands can be dated — by radiocarbon dating of shells and tufa, and by shoreline geomorphology — pluvial lakes are key archives of Quaternary climate change. They demonstrate that desert hydrology is highly sensitive to climate shifts, a finding with implications for water resources under modern climate change. Shorelines of Bonneville and Lahontan were foundational evidence in G. K. Gilbert's classic 19th-century studies of lake history and isostatic rebound: the Earth's crust, depressed by the weight of the water, has measurably rebounded since the lakes dried.
