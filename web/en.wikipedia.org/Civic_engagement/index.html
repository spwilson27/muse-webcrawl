Civic engagement

A public meeting is one common form of civic engagement.

Civic engagement is the participation of individuals and groups in the life of their communities in order to improve conditions for others or to help shape the community's future. It encompasses political activities such as voting, campaigning and contacting officials, as well as non-political activities such as volunteering, community service, membership in civic associations, charitable giving and neighbourhood problem-solving.

Forms

Scholars commonly distinguish several forms of civic engagement: civic activity (volunteering, associational membership, community work), electoral activity (voting, campaigning, donating to candidates), political voice (contacting officials, protesting, boycotting, signing petitions) and attentive engagement (following public affairs and discussing politics). Digital media have added online forms such as e-petitions, crowdfunding for civic causes and social-media activism.

Social capital and decline debates

Civic engagement is closely linked to the concept of social capital — the networks, norms and trust that facilitate collective action. Robert Putnam's book Bowling Alone (2000) argued that participation in civic associations in the United States had declined markedly since the 1960s, weakening democracy and community life. Later research has debated the extent of the decline, pointing to new forms of engagement among younger generations and online, and to differences across countries.

Determinants

Research consistently finds that civic engagement increases with education, income, age (up to later life), residential stability and membership in religious and community organisations, and that parents' participation predicts that of their children. Civic education programmes and service-learning in schools are widely promoted as ways of building habits of participation. See also civil society and community organizing.
