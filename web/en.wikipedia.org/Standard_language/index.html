Standard language

Regional varieties and a standardized written norm.

A standard language (also standard variety, standard dialect or standardized language) is a variety of a language that has undergone substantial codification of its grammar, pronunciation and vocabulary, and is widely used in education, government, law and the media. Standardization typically involves the selection of one dialect as the basis, its codification in dictionaries and grammars, elaboration of its functions, and its acceptance by the community.

Development

Sociolinguist Einar Haugen described standardization in four stages: selection, codification, elaboration of function, and acceptance. Standard languages often emerge around a political or cultural capital—Standard English around London, Standard French around Paris. Institutions such as the Académie française (founded 1635) were created to regulate the standard.

Characteristics

A standard language is usually taught in schools, used in official documents and supported by reference works. It coexists with non-standard dialects, and speakers may shift between them according to context, a situation related to diglossia. Pluricentric languages such as English, German and Spanish have multiple national standards.

Criticism

Linguists note that the standard is not inherently superior to other dialects; its prestige is social. Language standardization has been criticized for marginalizing minority varieties, while its defenders emphasize the value of a shared medium for administration, literature and science.
