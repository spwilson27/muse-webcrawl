Cultural capital

Cultural capital is a sociological concept developed by French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu (with Jean-Claude Passeron) in the 1970s. It denotes the non-financial social assets — knowledge, skills, education, tastes, and cultural goods — that a person can mobilize to gain advantages in society, alongside economic capital and social capital. Bourdieu argued that cultural capital helps explain how class inequality is reproduced, especially through education, even where schooling is formally meritocratic.

Three forms

Bourdieu distinguished three states of cultural capital. The embodied state consists of long-lasting dispositions of mind and body: accent, manners, knowledge, and cultivated tastes acquired mainly in childhood through family socialization. The objectified state comprises cultural goods such as books, artworks, and instruments, which require embodied capital to be fully appreciated. The institutionalized state consists of formally recognized credentials, such as academic degrees, which certify cultural competence and can be converted into economic capital in the labor market.

Education and reproduction

In Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture (1970), Bourdieu and Passeron argued that schools reward the cultural capital of dominant classes — linguistic styles, familiarity with high culture, ease with abstraction — while presenting that reward as individual talent. Children of privileged families therefore convert inherited cultural capital into scholastic success, legitimating social hierarchy.

Criticism and development

Critics have charged the concept with vagueness and with underestimating agency and resistance; others have extended it to new domains, including "emerging" cultural capital of popular and digital culture, and to immigrant communities whose cultural resources schools may fail to recognize. The concept remains central to the sociology of education and culture.
