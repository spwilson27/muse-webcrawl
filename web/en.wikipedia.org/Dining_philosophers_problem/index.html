Dining philosophers problem

The dining philosophers problem is an example problem often used in concurrent algorithm design to illustrate synchronisation issues and techniques for resolving them. It was originally formulated in 1965 by Edsger Dijkstra as a student exam exercise, framed in terms of computers competing for tape drive peripherals, and was soon recast in its dining-philosophers form by Tony Hoare.

Problem statement

Five philosophers sit around a circular table, alternating between thinking and eating. Between each pair of adjacent plates is a single fork (or chopstick in some variants); a philosopher needs both neighbouring forks to eat. The challenge is to design a protocol by which each philosopher can eat without the system reaching deadlock — every philosopher holding one fork and waiting forever for the other — and without starvation, where some philosopher never gets to eat.

Solutions

Classic solutions include imposing a resource hierarchy (picking up forks in a fixed global order, breaking circular waiting), introducing a waiter (arbitrator) who grants permission to eat, limiting the number of philosophers seated at once with a semaphore, and Dijkstra's semaphore-based algorithms with careful atomic pickup of both forks. Chandy and Misra proposed a fully distributed solution in 1984 based on a dynamic precedence graph, guaranteeing fairness.

Significance

The problem remains a standard teaching example for deadlock conditions — mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no pre-emption and circular wait — and for the distinction between deadlock freedom, liveness and fairness in concurrent systems.
