District of Columbia v. Heller

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

District of Columbia v. Heller, 554 U.S. 570 (2008), is a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court ruling that the Second Amendment to the U.S. Constitution protects an individual's right to keep and bear arms for traditionally lawful purposes such as self-defense in the home, and that the District of Columbia's handgun ban and requirement that lawfully owned rifles and shotguns be kept "unloaded and disassembled or bound by a trigger lock" violated that right.

Background

The Firearms Control Regulations Act of 1975, enacted by the District of Columbia, prohibited residents from possessing handguns except those registered before 1978, and required permitted firearms kept in the home to be unloaded and disassembled or locked. Dick Anthony Heller, a D.C. special police officer who carried a handgun on duty, applied for a registration certificate for a handgun he wished to keep at home and was refused. He sued, arguing the law violated his Second Amendment rights.

Opinion of the Court

Writing for the majority, Justice Antonin Scalia held that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to possess a firearm unconnected with service in a militia, and to use that arm for traditionally lawful purposes. The Court reasoned from the text and history of the Amendment that its prefatory militia clause announces a purpose but does not limit the operative clause. The Court nevertheless stated that the right is not unlimited and that longstanding prohibitions — such as those on carrying firearms in sensitive places, on possession by felons and the mentally ill, and on dangerous and unusual weapons — remain presumptively permissible.

Dissents

Justice John Paul Stevens, in dissent, argued that the Amendment protects a right connected to militia service and that the majority's interpretation was a dramatic departure from precedent. Justice Stephen Breyer argued that even under an individual-rights reading, the District's law was a reasonable regulation.

Subsequent developments

Two years later, in McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010), the Court held that the Second Amendment right recognized in Heller is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. Heller remains the foundational modern precedent on the Second Amendment and has shaped extensive later litigation, including New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen (2022).
