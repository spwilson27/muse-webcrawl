Riemannian geometry

Riemannian geometry is the branch of differential geometry that studies Riemannian manifolds: smooth manifolds equipped with a Riemannian metric, an inner product on the tangent space at each point that varies smoothly from point to point. It generalises the differential geometry of curves and surfaces in Euclidean space to arbitrary dimensions and curvatures.

History

The subject originates in Bernhard Riemann's 1854 habilitation lecture "On the hypotheses which lie at the foundations of geometry", which introduced manifolds, metrics and the curvature tensor, freeing geometry from the assumption of flat Euclidean space. The work was developed by Christoffel, Ricci, Levi-Civita and others into the tensor calculus later used by Einstein in general relativity.

Basic concepts

A Riemannian metric g allows measurement of lengths of curves, angles between vectors, and volumes. From the metric one obtains the Levi-Civita connection, geodesics (locally length-minimising curves), parallel transport, and the Riemann curvature tensor together with its contractions, the Ricci and scalar curvatures. The distance function induced by the metric makes a complete Riemannian manifold a metric space, as stated in the Hopf–Rinow theorem.

Main themes

Classical theorems include Gauss's Theorema Egregium, the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, and comparison theorems relating curvature bounds to topology. Modern Riemannian geometry includes the study of manifolds with Ricci curvature bounds, Cheeger–Gromov compactness, and geometric flows such as the Ricci flow used by Grigori Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture.
