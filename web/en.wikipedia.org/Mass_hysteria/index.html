Mass hysteria

Collective spread of symptoms or fears without an organic cause

Mass hysteria — in modern medical terminology, mass psychogenic illness or mass sociogenic illness — is the rapid spread of illness signs and symptoms, beliefs or behaviours through a group, without a corresponding physical or environmental cause. Affected people may experience dizziness, fainting, nausea, headaches or twitching, genuine in themselves but transmitted by social and psychological mechanisms such as anxiety, expectation and observation of others.

Historical examples

Famous episodes include the dancing manias of late-medieval Europe, in which groups danced uncontrollably for days; the Salem witch trials of 1692–93, partly interpreted through the fits of the accusers; the Tanganyika laughter epidemic of 1962, which spread through schools and villages; and recurrent outbreaks in schools in which students fall ill after a perceived smell or contamination that investigators cannot detect. The 2020s saw discussion of social-media-associated functional tic-like behaviours as a possible new vector.

Explanation

Researchers distinguish conversion-type outbreaks (involuntary motor symptoms, often after prolonged stress) from anxiety-type outbreaks (rapid, benign symptoms triggered by a perceived threat). Outbreaks spread along social networks and line of sight, are more often reported among adolescents and in close-knit institutions, and typically resolve once the group disperses and reassurance is given. The term "hysteria" is now avoided by clinicians because of its pejorative history.
