Jackson's Valley Campaign, also known as the Shenandoah Valley Campaign of 1862, was Confederate Major General Thomas J. "Stonewall" Jackson's spring campaign through the Shenandoah Valley in Virginia during the American Civil War.

Course of the campaign

With fewer than 17,000 men, Jackson marched his "foot cavalry" some 646 miles in 48 days and won five major battles — Kernstown (a tactical defeat that still alarmed Washington), McDowell, Front Royal, First Winchester and Port Republic — against combined Union forces of about 52,000 under Banks, Frémont and Shields.

Strategic effect

Fearing an attack on Washington, President Lincoln withheld McDowell's corps from reinforcing McClellan's Peninsula Campaign against Richmond, blunting the Union offensive at a critical moment. Jackson then slipped away to join Robert E. Lee for the Seven Days Battles.

Legacy

The campaign is studied at military academies worldwide as a masterclass in economy of force, deception, mobility and audacity, and it cemented Jackson's reputation as one of the most gifted tactical commanders of the war.
