Amaravati Stupa

The Amarāvati Stupa, also known as the Amaravati Mahachaitya, is a ruined Buddhist stupa at Amaravati in Andhra Pradesh, India, probably built in phases between the third century BCE and about 250 CE under the Satavahanas and their Ikshvaku successors. It was one of the largest stupas in ancient India, about 50 metres in diameter and 27 metres high.

Amaravati Stupa
Location: Amaravati, Andhra Pradesh, India
Built: c. 3rd c. BCE – c. 250 CE
Style: Amaravati school of sculpture

Art

The stupa was faced with pale limestone ("Palnad marble") slabs carved in the celebrated Amaravati style — sinuous, crowded narrative reliefs depicting Jataka tales and events of the Buddha's life, with the Buddha represented both symbolically (footprints, throne, wheel) and anthropomorphically. Railings and gateways surrounded the paved circumambulatory path.

Decline and excavation

The site declined after the 14th century and was rediscovered by Colin Mackenzie in 1797; many sculptures were removed in the 19th century. The "Elliot Marbles" are now among the highlights of the British Museum, with further major holdings in the Government Museum, Chennai, and a site museum at Amaravati. Only the stupa mound and scattered remains survive in situ.
