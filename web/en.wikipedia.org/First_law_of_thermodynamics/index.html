First law of thermodynamics

The first law of thermodynamics is a formulation of the law of conservation of energy in the context of thermodynamic processes. It states that the increase in the internal energy of a thermodynamic system is equal to the amount of energy added to the system in the form of heat, minus the amount of energy the system expends in the form of work done on its surroundings. In equation form, using the physics sign convention in which work is done by the system:

ΔU = Q − W

where ΔU is the change in internal energy, Q is the heat added to the system, and W is the work done by the system. (In chemistry and much of engineering, the opposite sign convention for work is used, giving ΔU = Q + W with W the work done on the system.) Because internal energy is a state function, its change depends only on the initial and final states, whereas heat and work are path-dependent transfer quantities.

History

The law emerged in the middle of the nineteenth century from the work of several scientists. In the 1840s James Prescott Joule's experiments on the mechanical equivalent of heat showed that a definite amount of mechanical work always produces the same amount of heat. Julius Robert von Mayer had published an early statement of energy conservation in 1842, and Hermann von Helmholtz gave a general formulation in 1847. Rudolf Clausius is generally credited with the first clear statement of the first law in 1850, in his paper On the Motive Power of Heat, and by the 1850s and 1860s the law was also articulated by William Rankine, William Thomson (Lord Kelvin), and others. The law rules out a perpetual motion machine of the first kind: a machine that produces work without any energy input.

Formulations

For a closed system undergoing a cycle, the net heat transferred equals the net work done, because the system returns to its initial state and ΔU = 0. For open systems (control volumes), the first law is written as a rate balance accounting for enthalpy carried by mass flows, shaft work, and heat transfer; the steady-flow energy equation used throughout engineering is of this kind. In differential form the law is written dU = δQ − δW, where the inexact differentials δQ and δW emphasise that heat and work are not state functions.

Applications

The first law underlies the analysis of heat engines, refrigerators, compressors, turbines, and chemical reactors. Combined with the second law of thermodynamics, which limits the fraction of heat that can be converted into work, it provides the framework within which all energy conversion devices are designed and assessed.
