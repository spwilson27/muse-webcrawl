Katakana (片仮名, "fragmentary kana") is one of the three scripts used in the Japanese writing system, alongside hiragana and kanji. It is a syllabary of 46 basic characters, each representing a mora (roughly, a syllable), characterised by angular, straight strokes. Katakana characters were derived in the early Heian period by Buddhist monks who simplified fragments of kanji used phonetically (man'yōgana) when annotating Chinese texts.

Usage

Modern Japanese uses katakana chiefly for transcription of foreign loanwords (gairaigo) and foreign personal and place names; for onomatopoeia; for emphasis, functioning somewhat like italics; and in scientific and technical contexts, such as species names and company terminology. Before 1945 it was also the standard script for telegrams and many official documents. Extended combinations of katakana with small vowels represent sounds found in foreign words but not native Japanese, such as ファ (fa) and ティ (ti).

Relation to hiragana

Katakana and hiragana encode exactly the same set of sounds; they differ in form and function. Where hiragana is cursive and rounded, katakana is sharp and minimal — for example, か (hiragana ka) versus カ (katakana ka). A half-width form of katakana is used in some computing and signage contexts.
