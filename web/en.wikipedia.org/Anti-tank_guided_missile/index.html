Anti-tank guided missile

A Javelin anti-tank guided missile being fired

An anti-tank guided missile (ATGM), also called an anti-tank guided weapon (ATGW), is a guided missile primarily designed to hit and destroy heavily armoured military vehicles, especially tanks. ATGMs range from shoulder-launched infantry weapons to large tripod-, vehicle- and helicopter-mounted systems, and use shaped-charge (HEAT) warheads, often in tandem configuration to defeat explosive reactive armour.

Generations

  • First generation (1950s–60s): manual command to line of sight (MCLOS) — the operator steers the missile with a joystick, e.g. the Soviet 9M14 Malyutka and the French SS.10.
  • Second generation (late 1960s–80s): semi-automatic command to line of sight (SACLOS) — the operator keeps the sight on the target while the system steers the missile, guided by wire, radio or laser, e.g. the American BGM-71 TOW and the Franco-German MILAN.
  • Third generation (1990s–): "fire-and-forget" missiles with imaging infrared or radar seekers that lock on before launch and can attack the thin top armour of tanks, e.g. the American FGM-148 Javelin and the Israeli Spike.

Employment

ATGMs proved decisive in the 1973 Yom Kippur War, where Egyptian infantry with Malyutka missiles inflicted heavy losses on Israeli armour, and have remained central to infantry anti-tank defence, ambush tactics and the anti-armour armament of attack helicopters such as the AGM-114 Hellfire-armed Apache. Countermeasures include explosive reactive armour, active protection systems that shoot down incoming missiles, smoke and multispectral obscurants, and cage ("cope cage") armour fitted against top-attack weapons.
