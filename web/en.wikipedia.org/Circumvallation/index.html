Circumvallation

Circumvallation is a military tactic in siege warfare: a line of fortifications, built by a besieging army around a fortified place, facing inward so as to prevent the besieged from escaping or receiving help by sorties, and to protect the besiegers from attacks by the besieged. A second, outward-facing line, protecting the besiegers against a relieving army, is called a contravallation; using both together is known as double circumvallation.

History

The technique was used in antiquity, famously by Alexander the Great at Tyre and by Scipio Aemilianus at Numantia. Its most celebrated example is Julius Caesar's siege of Alesia in 52 BC, where his army built roughly 16 km of inward-facing circumvallation and 21 km of outward-facing contravallation, with ditches, ramparts, towers and traps, and then withstood simultaneous attacks from the besieged Gauls and a relief army. Circumvallation remained standard in siegecraft through the gunpowder era — for example the Spanish lines around Breda in 1624–25 and the Union lines at Petersburg in 1864–65.
