Lunar mare

Lunar maria (singular: mare)
Composition: Basaltic lava plains
Age: Mostly 3.0–3.8 billion years old
Coverage: About 31% of the near side; only ~2% of the far side
Largest: Oceanus Procellarum

The lunar maria (Latin for "seas"; singular mare) are large, dark, basaltic plains on Earth's Moon, formed by ancient asteroid impacts that triggered volcanic eruptions flooding the basins with lava. Early telescopic observers mistook them for actual seas, and the misleading name survives.

Formation

Between about 3.8 and 3.0 billion years ago, enormous impacts excavated basins in the young Moon's crust. Later, basaltic magma from the interior flooded these low-lying basins and solidified into smooth plains darker than the surrounding ancient highlands (terrae). Because the near-side crust is thinner, maria cover nearly a third of the hemisphere facing Earth but very little of the far side.

Principal maria

The largest feature is Oceanus Procellarum ("Ocean of Storms"). Others visible to the naked eye include Mare Tranquillitatis (site of the Apollo 11 landing in 1969), Mare Imbrium, Mare Serenitatis, Mare Nubium and Mare Crisium.
