Nucleosynthesis is the process that creates new atomic nuclei from pre-existing nucleons (protons and neutrons) and nuclei. It explains the origin and cosmic abundances of the chemical elements: primordial nucleosynthesis in the early universe produced hydrogen, helium, and traces of lithium, while later generations of stars and supernovae forged nearly everything else.

Big Bang nucleosynthesis

In the first few minutes after the Big Bang, the universe was hot and dense enough for nuclear reactions, which fixed the primordial abundances — about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium-4 by mass, with small amounts of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7. Because stable nuclei do not exist at masses 5 and 8, synthesis stalled, leaving heavier elements to be made in stars.

Stellar and explosive nucleosynthesis

Stars fuse hydrogen to helium, then (if massive enough) helium to carbon and oxygen via the triple-alpha process, and onward through carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon burning up to iron — the most tightly bound nucleus, beyond which fusion absorbs rather than releases energy. The landmark 1957 B²FH paper (Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler, and Hoyle) systematised these processes. Elements heavier than iron are built mainly by neutron capture: the slow s-process in red giant stars, and the rapid r-process in supernovae and neutron star mergers, confirmed by the kilonova observed with gravitational-wave event GW170817. Cosmic-ray spallation accounts for much of the lithium, beryllium, and boron.
