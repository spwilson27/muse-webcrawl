Richter magnitude scale


A seismogram; Richter magnitude is computed from the maximum amplitude

The Richter magnitude scale — properly the local magnitude scale, symbol ML — is a logarithmic scale for measuring the size of earthquakes, developed by Charles F. Richter of the California Institute of Technology in 1935, in collaboration with Beno Gutenberg. It assigns a single number to quantify the energy released at an earthquake's source, based on the maximum amplitude recorded by a seismograph, corrected for the distance to the epicentre.

Definition

Richter defined the local magnitude of an earthquake as the base-10 logarithm of the maximum seismic wave amplitude (in micrometres) recorded on a standard Wood–Anderson torsion seismometer located 100 kilometres from the epicentre. The scale is logarithmic: each whole-number increase corresponds to a tenfold increase in measured amplitude and roughly 31.6 times more energy released.

Use and limitations

The scale was designed for shallow, local earthquakes in Southern California and becomes unreliable for very large or distant events, because the amplitudes it measures saturate: magnitudes above about 7 cannot be distinguished properly. Seismologists therefore now prefer the moment magnitude scale (Mw), based on the seismic moment, which remains accurate for the largest earthquakes. Nevertheless, "Richter scale" remains the term most often used in news reporting for earthquake magnitude generally. Earthquakes below magnitude 2.0 are micro-earthquakes, generally not felt; damage usually begins around magnitude 4–5, and great earthquakes exceed 8.0 — the largest recorded, the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile, measured about 9.5 on the moment magnitude scale.
