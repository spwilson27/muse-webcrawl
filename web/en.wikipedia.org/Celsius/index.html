Celsius

The degree Celsius is a unit of temperature on the Celsius scale (originally known as the centigrade scale), one of two temperature scales used in the International System of Units (SI), the other being the Kelvin scale. The degree Celsius (symbol: °C) is defined relative to the Kelvin: a temperature difference of 1 °C equals 1 K, and a temperature in degrees Celsius is the temperature in kelvins minus 273.15.

History

The scale is named after the Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius (1701–1744), who proposed a similar scale in 1742 — in his original version, 0° marked the boiling point of water and 100° the freezing point; the scale was inverted to its modern orientation shortly after his death, an adjustment associated with Carl Linnaeus and Daniel Ekström. The name "Celsius" replaced "centigrade" internationally in 1948 to honour Celsius and to avoid confusion with the centesimal angular unit.

Definition and use

Since 1954 the degree was defined via absolute zero and the triple point of water (0.01 °C); since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, the kelvin is fixed by the Boltzmann constant, and the Celsius scale inherits that definition. The Celsius scale is used almost worldwide for everyday temperatures such as weather and cooking, while the United States retains Fahrenheit for non-scientific use. Conversion: °F = (9/5) × °C + 32.

Common temperatures

Water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure; average human body temperature is about 37 °C; comfortable room temperature is around 20–22 °C.
