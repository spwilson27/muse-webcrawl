The Great Mosque of Mecca, known in Arabic as Masjid al-Haram ("the Sacred Mosque"), is the largest mosque in the world and surrounds Islam's holiest site, the Kaaba, in the city of Mecca, Saudi Arabia. Muslims face the Kaaba in prayer (the qibla) and circumambulate it during the Hajj pilgrimage and the Umrah. The mosque is the destination of the Hajj, one of the five pillars of Islam, which draws millions of pilgrims annually.

Sacred features

Within and around the central courtyard (the mataf) lie several venerated sites: the Kaaba itself, draped in the black kiswah; the Black Stone (al-Ḥajar al-Aswad) set in its eastern corner; the Maqam Ibrahim, bearing the footprints of Abraham; the Zamzam Well; and the hills of Safa and Marwah, now enclosed in the mosque's sa'i gallery, between which pilgrims walk in memory of Hagar's search for water. Islamic tradition attributes the first raising of the Kaaba's foundations to Abraham and Ishmael.

History and expansions

The sanctuary predates Islam; the Prophet Muhammad cleansed the Kaaba of idols in 630. Successive caliphs — Umar, Uthman, the Umayyads and Abbasids — enlarged the mosque, and the Ottomans under Selim II and Murad III rebuilt it in the 16th century with the arcades that long defined its form. The Saudi era brought colossal expansions: under King Saud and King Fahd (1980s–90s) the mosque gained vast prayer halls, escalators and air conditioning, and under King Abdullah a further expansion added the northern courtyards and the Mecca Royal Clock Tower complex nearby, raising capacity to around two and a half million worshippers.

Incidents

The mosque was seized in November–December 1979 by armed millenarian insurgents led by Juhayman al-Otaybi, in a two-week siege ended by Saudi forces with heavy loss of life. Deadly crowd disasters have repeatedly struck the Hajj, most catastrophically the 1990 tunnel stampede (1,426 deaths) and the 2015 Mina stampede; in September 2015 a construction crane collapsed onto the mosque, killing more than a hundred people.
