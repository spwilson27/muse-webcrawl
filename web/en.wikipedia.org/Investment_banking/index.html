Investment banking

Core activitiesUnderwriting, M&A advice, capital raising, market making
Major firmsGoldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup
Key regulationGlass–Steagall Act (1933, repealed 1999), Dodd–Frank Act (2010)

Investment banking is an advisory-based division of banking that helps corporations, governments, and institutions raise capital and provides advice on mergers and acquisitions (M&A) and other complex financial transactions. Unlike commercial banks, investment banks do not primarily take deposits or make conventional loans.

Activities

Core activities include underwriting new issues of shares and bonds, in which the bank helps design the security, prices it, and distributes it to investors; advising buyers and sellers in mergers, takeovers, and restructurings; and arranging financing such as leveraged buyouts. Many firms also run trading, research, and asset-management businesses. The largest "bulge bracket" banks handle the biggest transactions worldwide, while boutique firms specialise in advisory work.

History

Investment banking grew out of merchant banks and the great underwriting houses of the 19th and early 20th centuries. In the United States, the Glass–Steagall Act of 1933 separated commercial from investment banking after the crash of 1929; that separation was repealed in 1999. The 2008 financial crisis transformed the industry: Lehman Brothers collapsed, Bear Stearns and Merrill Lynch were absorbed, and Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley became bank holding companies subject to fuller regulation.

Conflicts and criticism

Because a bank may advise a client while also trading or underwriting, investment banking is perennially subject to conflicts of interest, addressed through information barriers ("Chinese walls"), disclosure rules, and securities regulation.
