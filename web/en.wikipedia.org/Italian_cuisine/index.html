Italian cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Italian cuisine is a Mediterranean cuisine consisting of the ingredients, recipes, and cooking techniques developed in Italy since Roman times and later spread around the world, together with its gastronomic culture. Significant changes occurred with the colonisation of the Americas and the introduction of new ingredients such as potatoes, tomatoes, bell peppers, and maize, which are now central to the cuisine but were not introduced in quantity until the 18th century.

Characteristics

Italian cooking is strongly regional — the country's unification came only in 1861, and each region (and often each town) preserves distinct dishes, ingredients, and traditions. Common threads include an emphasis on few, high-quality, seasonal ingredients; the use of olive oil, wheat (bread and pasta), cheese, wine, and coffee; and a meal structure of antipasto (starter), primo (pasta, rice, or soup), secondo (meat or fish) with contorno (side dish), and dolce (dessert).

Key foods

Pasta exists in hundreds of shapes, dried (pasta secca) and fresh (pasta fresca), from spaghetti and penne to filled forms such as ravioli and tortellini; classic sauces include ragù alla bolognese, carbonara, amatriciana, arrabbiata, and Ligurian pesto. Pizza, originating in Naples — where the margherita was famously created in 1889 — is protected by EU Traditional Speciality status for the Neapolitan style. Risotto dominates in the north, as does polenta. Cheeses include Parmigiano-Reggiano, mozzarella di bufala, pecorino, and gorgonzola; cured meats include prosciutto di Parma and salame. Desserts include tiramisu, panna cotta, cannoli, and gelato. Italy is the world's largest wine producer, with DOCG wines such as Barolo, Chianti Classico, and Prosecco.

History

Roman cuisine, recorded in Apicius's De re coquinaria, laid early foundations; Arab rule in Sicily introduced citrus, rice, and sugar techniques. Renaissance courts, notably in Florence, refined banqueting — Catherine de' Medici's marriage to Henri II in 1533 is legendarily (if dubiously) credited with carrying Italian techniques to France. Pellegrino Artusi's Science in the Kitchen and the Art of Eating Well (1891) codified a national cuisine, and 20th-century emigration spread Italian-American and other diaspora variants worldwide. The Mediterranean diet, with Italian traditions at its core, was inscribed by UNESCO in 2010 on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
