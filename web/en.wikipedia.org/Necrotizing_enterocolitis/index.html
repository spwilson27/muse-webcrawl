Necrotizing enterocolitis

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC)
Specialty: Neonatology, pediatric gastroenterology
Affects: Primarily premature infants
Complications: Bowel perforation, sepsis, short bowel syndrome

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a devastating disease that affects mostly the intestines of premature infants. The word necrotizing refers to death of tissue, entero to intestine, and colitis to inflammation. NEC typically occurs in the first weeks of life in infants born preterm, especially those of very low birth weight, after feeding has begun; the intestinal wall becomes inflamed and begins to die, which may lead to perforation, peritonitis, and sepsis. It is the most common and most serious gastrointestinal emergency among premature infants.

Signs and diagnosis

Signs include feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, bloody stools, bile-stained vomiting, temperature instability, apnea, and lethargy. Abdominal X-ray may show pneumatosis intestinalis — gas within the bowel wall — dilated loops, or free air indicating perforation. Disease severity is classified with the Bell staging criteria.

Causes and prevention

The exact cause is not fully understood; it involves an immature gut, abnormal bacterial colonization, impaired intestinal blood flow, and an exaggerated inflammatory response. Risk factors include prematurity (the dominant factor), formula feeding, and rapid advancement of feeds. Mother's own milk and donor human milk reduce risk compared with formula, and standardized feeding protocols and probiotics have been studied as preventive measures, with probiotics remaining debated.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment includes stopping feeds, nasogastric decompression, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and supportive intensive care. Roughly a fifth to a third of cases require surgery — resection of dead bowel, sometimes with an ostomy — which may result in short bowel syndrome. Mortality is substantial, around 20–30% overall and higher in the smallest infants and those needing surgery, and survivors face increased risk of neurodevelopmental impairment.
