Misuse of statistics

Misuse of statistics occurs when a statistical argument asserts a falsehood. In many cases the misuse is accidental; in others it is deliberate, intended to persuade an audience of a conclusion the data do not support. Misused statistics can be found in advertising, politics, journalism, and even scientific literature.

"There are three kinds of lies: lies, damned lies, and statistics." — popularised by Mark Twain, attributing it to Benjamin Disraeli

Common forms

Faulty sampling and polling

A sample that is not representative of the population invalidates generalisations drawn from it. Self-selected polls, loaded questions, and small sample sizes are frequent sources of error. The famous 1936 Literary Digest poll, which predicted the wrong winner of the U.S. presidential election despite millions of responses, illustrates how a biased sampling frame can overwhelm a large sample.

Correlation and causation

A recurring error is treating correlation as proof of causation. Two variables may move together because one causes the other, because both are driven by a third factor, or by coincidence. Simpson's paradox shows that a trend visible in combined data can reverse when the data are split into subgroups.

Misleading presentation

Truncated axes, cherry-picked time ranges, and selective reporting of results exaggerate or conceal effects. In research, p-hacking — testing many hypotheses and reporting only the statistically significant ones — inflates false positives, while publication bias skews the visible literature toward positive results.

Base-rate neglect

Ignoring the base rate of a condition leads to dramatic overestimation of the significance of a positive test result, a problem in medical screening and forensic evidence alike.

Countermeasures

Critical readers are advised to ask who funded a study, how the sample was drawn, whether effect sizes as well as statistical significance are reported, and whether the findings have been replicated. Darrell Huff's 1954 book How to Lie with Statistics remains a classic popular treatment of the subject.
