GW170817 was a gravitational wave signal observed by the LIGO and Virgo detectors on 17 August 2017, produced by the inspiral and merger of two neutron stars in the galaxy NGC 4993. It was the first gravitational wave event observed to have an electromagnetic counterpart, and the first unambiguous detection of a binary neutron star merger. The event inaugurated the era of multi-messenger astronomy with gravitational waves.

Detection

The signal lasted roughly 100 seconds in the detectors' sensitive band, sweeping upward in frequency as the two neutron stars — each with a mass between about 1.1 and 1.6 solar masses, and a combined mass of about 2.74 solar masses — spiraled together. A single-detector trigger at Hanford and the initially puzzling quiet in Livingston (caused by a loud instrumental glitch overlapping the signal) delayed the alert slightly, but Virgo data helped localize the source to a sky region of about 28 square degrees.

Electromagnetic counterparts

Just 1.7 seconds after the merger, NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope detected a short gamma-ray burst, GRB 170817A, confirming the long-suspected link between neutron star mergers and short GRBs. About 11 hours later, the Swope Telescope in Chile found a new optical transient, AT 2017gfo, in NGC 4993 — a kilonova powered by the radioactive decay of heavy r-process elements synthesized in the ejected neutron-rich matter. The transient evolved rapidly from blue to red, as predicted for ejecta containing lanthanides. More than 70 observatories followed the event across the spectrum; a relativistic jet was later inferred from radio and X-ray brightening months afterward.

Scientific significance

GW170817 confirmed that neutron star mergers are a major site of r-process nucleosynthesis, producing gold, platinum and other heavy elements. The near-simultaneous arrival of gravitational and gamma waves constrained the difference between the speed of gravity and the speed of light to within about one part in 1015, ruling out many modified-gravity theories. The event also provided an independent "standard siren" measurement of the Hubble constant, and constraints on the neutron star equation of state through tidal deformability.
