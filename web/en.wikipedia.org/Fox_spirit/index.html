Fox spirit

The nine-tailed fox, a powerful fox spirit in Chinese, Japanese and Korean folklore.

Fox spirits are mythical fox entities originating in Chinese mythology that have spread throughout East Asia. In Chinese tradition they are called huli jing (狐狸精); in Japan, magical foxes are associated with the deity Inari and termed kitsune; in Korea the fox spirit is the kumiho (gumiho). Fox spirits are believed to gain power and additional tails as they age — a fox that lives a thousand years may become a nine-tailed fox — and to possess abilities such as shapeshifting, most famously transformation into a beautiful woman.

Chinese tradition

Foxes appear in some of the earliest Chinese texts. In early lore the nine-tailed fox could be an auspicious omen, but over time fox spirits came to be portrayed ambivalently: as seductresses who drain the life force of men, as tricksters, and occasionally as benevolent helpers or lovers. The infamous Daji, consort of King Zhou of Shang, was said in later legend to have been possessed by a nine-tailed fox. Fox tales flourished in the Ming and Qing dynasties, notably in Pu Songling's Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio (Liaozhai Zhiyi), where fox spirits are often sympathetic characters.

Japan and Korea

In Japan, kitsune are messengers of the kami Inari, guardian deities of rice and prosperity; helpful foxes are zenko while mischievous or malevolent wild foxes are yako. Kitsune are credited with intelligence, long life, magical powers and a love of fried tofu (abura-age, hence the dish inarizushi and kitsune udon). The Korean kumiho is usually more sinister: it transforms into a beautiful woman to seduce men and eat their hearts or livers, though modern Korean popular culture has softened the figure into romantic heroines.

In modern culture

Fox spirits remain popular in anime, manga, video games, television dramas and fantasy fiction throughout East Asia and beyond, from the Pokémon Ninetales to Korean dramas such as My Girlfriend Is a Gumiho.
