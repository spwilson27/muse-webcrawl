Viol

The viol (also called the viola da gamba) is a family of bowed, fretted, and stringed instruments that were especially popular in the Renaissance and Baroque periods. Viols typically have six strings (some seven), gut frets tied around the neck, sloping shoulders, and a flat back, and are played held vertically on or between the legs (Italian: da gamba, "of the leg"), with an underhand bow grip — distinguishing them from the violin family.

History

The viol developed in late 15th-century Spain and Italy, influenced by the vihuela and Moorish bowed instruments, and spread across Europe. In England, consorts of viols became central to chamber music under the Tudors and Stuarts, with composers such as William Byrd, John Dowland, and William Lawes writing for them. In France the bass viol flourished as a solo instrument through Marin Marais and Antoine Forqueray, and J. S. Bach wrote three sonatas for viola da gamba and harpsichord. The family declined in the later 18th century as the louder violin family dominated, before the early music revival of the 20th century restored its repertoire and playing technique.

Construction and playing

Viols are built in sizes forming a consort — treble, tenor, and bass most commonly — tuned in fourths with a major third in the middle (for example, D–G–C–E–A–D for the bass). The frets aid intonation in ensemble playing and produce the viol's characteristic clear, soft, resonant tone, suited to polyphonic music. The largest member, the violone, sometimes served as a bass instrument.

Repertoire

Notable repertoire includes English consort music and "In nomine" settings, French suites for solo bass viol, the works of Marin Marais (including the Pièces de viole), and modern compositions written for revived instruments. Saint-Colombe, teacher of Marais, is credited with adding the seventh string to the French bass viol.
