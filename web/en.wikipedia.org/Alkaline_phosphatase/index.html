Alkaline phosphatase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is a family of enzymes that catalyse the hydrolysis of phosphate groups from a wide variety of molecules, including nucleotides, proteins and alkaloids, working most effectively in an alkaline environment. It is found throughout the body — especially in the liver, bile ducts, bone, intestine, kidney and placenta — and in organisms across all kingdoms of life.

Function

Alkaline phosphatases remove phosphate groups (dephosphorylation), the counterpart of the phosphorylation carried out by kinases. In bone, the tissue-nonspecific isoenzyme produced by osteoblasts is involved in mineralisation; in the intestine, ALP helps regulate fat absorption and detoxifies bacterial lipopolysaccharide; in the liver it is associated with the bile duct membranes. Humans have four main isozymes: intestinal, placental, germ-cell and tissue-nonspecific (liver/bone/kidney) ALP.

Clinical significance

Measuring blood ALP is a standard part of liver function tests. Elevated levels occur in cholestasis and bile duct obstruction, in hepatitis, and in bone conditions with increased osteoblast activity such as Paget's disease, healing fractures, rickets and bone metastases; levels are also normally higher in growing children and during pregnancy. Abnormally low levels are seen in the inherited disorder hypophosphatasia, caused by mutations in the ALPL gene. In the laboratory, bacterial and calf intestinal alkaline phosphatase are everyday molecular-biology reagents, used to remove phosphate groups from DNA ends and as reporter enzymes in assays such as ELISA.
