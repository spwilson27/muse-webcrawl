Cnidaria is a phylum of aquatic animals that includes jellyfish, corals, sea anemones, and hydroids. Cnidarians are distinguished by specialized stinging cells called cnidocytes, which they use to capture prey and defend themselves. They are among the most ancient of animal groups, with a fossil record extending back more than 500 million years.

Body plan

Cnidarians have a simple, radially symmetric body built from two layers of cells surrounding a gelatinous layer, the mesoglea. Their single body cavity, the gastrovascular cavity, serves for digestion and circulation and has only one opening, which functions as both mouth and anus. Most species occur in one of two body forms: the sessile polyp, like a sea anemone, and the free-swimming medusa, like a jellyfish; many species alternate between the two during their life cycle. Rather than a brain, they possess a loose nerve net.

Classes

The major classes are the Anthozoa (corals and sea anemones, which exist only as polyps), the Scyphozoa (true jellyfish), the Cubozoa (box jellyfish, some with potent venom), and the Hydrozoa (a diverse group including the Portuguese man o' war and the freshwater hydra). Reef-building corals, which harbor symbiotic algae, create some of the most productive ecosystems on Earth.

Ecology

Cnidarians are predators of small animals and are in turn prey for turtles, fish, and other marine life. Stony corals' calcium carbonate skeletons build coral reefs, structures of global ecological significance now threatened by ocean warming and acidification.
