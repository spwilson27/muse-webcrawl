Astronomy in the medieval Islamic world

Islamic astronomy

Period: c. 8th–16th centuries

Key figures: al-Battani, Ibn al-Haytham, al-Biruni, Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, Ibn al-Shatir, Ulugh Beg

Institutions: Observatories at Baghdad, Maragheh, Samarkand

Astronomy in the medieval Islamic world was developed between the eighth and sixteenth centuries by scholars writing mainly in Arabic (and later Persian) across a region stretching from Iberia to Central Asia. Building on Greek, Persian, and Indian sources — above all Ptolemy's Almagest, translated in Abbasid Baghdad — these astronomers made extensive observations, built large observatories, and developed new mathematical models of planetary motion.

Motivations and institutions

Religious needs drove much of the work: determining prayer times, the direction of the qibla toward Mecca, and the lunar calendar. Court patronage supported observatories at Baghdad, Damascus, Maragheh (founded 1259 under Nasir al-Din al-Tusi), and Samarkand, where Ulugh Beg's observatory produced a star catalogue of exceptional accuracy in the 15th century.

Achievements

Islamic astronomers refined the measurement of the precession of the equinoxes, the obliquity of the ecliptic, and planetary parameters. Al-Battani improved on Ptolemy's values and his tables were used in Europe for centuries. Critics of Ptolemy, including Ibn al-Haytham, objected to the physical incoherence of the equant; al-Tusi's "Tusi couple" and the models of Ibn al-Shatir eliminated the equant using compounded circular motions — devices that reappear in the work of Copernicus. Instruments such as the astrolabe were perfected, and many star names in modern use (Betelgeuse, Rigel, Aldebaran) are of Arabic origin.

Transmission

From the 12th century, Latin translations made in Spain and Sicily carried Islamic astronomical tables and treatises into Europe, where they shaped university astronomy until the Scientific Revolution.
