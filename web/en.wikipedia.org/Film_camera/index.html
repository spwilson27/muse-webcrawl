Movie camera

A movie camera (also known as a film camera and cine-camera) is a type of photographic camera that rapidly takes a sequence of photographs on an image sensor or on a film stock. In contrast to a still camera, which captures a single snapshot at a time, the movie camera takes a series of images; each image is a frame of the film.

Mechanism

Film cameras use an intermittent mechanism to advance the film, holding each frame stationary behind the lens while the shutter opens, then rapidly moving the film to the next frame. Most professional cameras shoot at 24 frames per second, the sound-film standard.

History

Early devices such as Edison's Kinetograph and the Lumière Cinématographe in the 1890s established the basic design. Manufacturers such as Arriflex, Panavision, and Mitchell dominated professional production through the twentieth century, before digital cinema cameras displaced film cameras in most production during the 2010s.
