Friedrich Ludwig Gottlob Frege ( /ˈfreɪɡə/ ; German: [ˈɡɔtloːp ˈfreːɡə] ; 8 November 1848 – 26 July 1925) was a German philosopher, logician, and mathematician. He was a mathematics professor at the University of Jena, and is understood by many to be the father of analytic philosophy, concentrating on the philosophy of language, logic, and mathematics. Though he was largely ignored during his lifetime, Giuseppe Peano (1858–1932), Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), and, to some extent, Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) introduced his work to later generations of philosophers. Frege is widely considered to be the greatest logician since Aristotle, and one of the most profound philosophers of mathematics ever.

Life

Frege was born in Wismar, then in the Grand Duchy of Mecklenburg-Schwerin. He studied at the University of Jena and then Göttingen, where he took his doctorate in 1873. Returning to Jena, he habilitated in 1874 and taught there as Privatdozent and later professor until his retirement in 1918, leading a quiet, studious life. His work received little recognition in Germany; he died in Bad Kleinen in 1925.

Work

Begriffsschrift

His first work, the Begriffsschrift ("Concept-Writing", 1879), introduced a formal system of quantificational logic — variables, quantifiers, and function–argument analysis — that revolutionised logic and made possible modern predicate calculus. It also contained the first axiomatisation of propositional logic and a definition of the ancestral of a relation, used to define the sequence of natural numbers.

Logicism and the Grundlagen

In The Foundations of Arithmetic (1884), Frege argued that arithmetic is reducible to logic (logicism): he defined number in terms of extensions of concepts, stating what is now called Hume's Principle — the number of Fs equals the number of Gs if and only if the Fs and Gs are in one-to-one correspondence. The technical development in his Grundgesetze der Arithmetik (vol. I 1893, vol. II 1903) rested on Basic Law V, linking concepts to their extensions. As the second volume was in press, Bertrand Russell wrote to Frege in 1902 pointing out that Basic Law V yields a contradiction (Russell's paradox). Frege acknowledged the blow in an appendix, writing that hardly anything more unfortunate could befall a scientific writer; the paradox destroyed the system's consistency, though later work (especially "neo-logicism") has revived much of the programme using Hume's Principle alone.

Philosophy of language

Frege's essays "On Sense and Reference" (1892), "Function and Concept", and "Concept and Object" founded the modern philosophy of language. He distinguished the reference (Bedeutung) of an expression — the object it picks out — from its sense (Sinn), the mode of presentation of that object, explaining how identity statements like "the morning star is the evening star" can be informative. Other enduring doctrines include the context principle ("never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation, but only in the context of a proposition"), compositionality, and the sharp concept/object distinction.

Legacy

Through Russell, Wittgenstein (who visited him and was directed by Frege to study with Russell), Carnap (who attended his lectures), and later Dummett's scholarship, Frege became a central figure of analytic philosophy. His logic underlies modern mathematical logic and, through it, computer science; his philosophy of language remains a touchstone for theories of meaning.
