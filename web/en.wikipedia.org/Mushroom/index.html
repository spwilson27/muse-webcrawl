Mushroom

A mushroom is the fleshy, spore-bearing fruiting body of a fungus, typically produced above ground, on soil, or on its food source. The standard mushroom has a stem (stipe), a cap (pileus), and gills (lamellae) on the underside of the cap, from which spores are released. In a broader sense, "mushroom" is applied to many kinds of fleshy fungal fruiting bodies. The main body of the fungus — the mycelium — lives hidden in soil or wood, and the mushroom is only its reproductive structure.

Amanita muscaria, the fly agaric, is among the most recognisable mushrooms — and is toxic.

Biology

Mushrooms belong mainly to the division Basidiomycota, class Agaricomycetes. A mushroom begins when fungal hyphae of compatible mating types fuse; the fruiting body grows rapidly by cell inflation with water, sometimes overnight, and releases millions or billions of spores that are dispersed by wind. Many mushrooms form mycorrhizal partnerships with tree roots, exchanging soil nutrients for sugars; others are saprotrophic decomposers of dead wood and litter, and a few are parasites.

Edible and poisonous species

Humans have gathered wild mushrooms since prehistory. Commercially cultivated species include the white button mushroom (Agaricus bisporus, also sold as cremini and portobello), shiitake, oyster mushrooms, and enoki. Prized wild edibles include chanterelles, porcini (boletes), morels, and truffles. However, many species are poisonous: the death cap (Amanita phalloides) causes most fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide, destroying the liver with amatoxins, and several toxic species closely resemble edible ones, making expert identification essential. Some mushrooms, such as Psilocybe species, contain the psychedelic compound psilocybin.

Cultivation and uses

Mushrooms are grown commercially on composted straw, manure, logs, or sawdust under controlled humidity. They are valued as food for their savoury umami flavour and B-vitamin content, and some are used in traditional medicine. Fungi are also cultivated for enzymes, antibiotics such as penicillin, and the immunosuppressant ciclosporin. More recently, mycelium has been developed into packaging materials, leather substitutes, and meat alternatives.

In culture

Mushrooms figure in folklore worldwide — fairy rings were attributed to dancing elves, and the red-and-white fly agaric is a fixture of fairy-tale illustration, Christmas decoration in parts of Europe, and the Super Mario video game series. The largest known organism on Earth is a honey fungus (Armillaria ostoyae) in Oregon whose mycelium spans several square kilometres.
