Grammatical number

In linguistics, grammatical number is a grammatical category of nouns, pronouns, adjectives, and verb agreement that expresses count distinctions — typically how many referents a word denotes. The most common distinction is between singular (one) and plural (more than one), but many languages distinguish additional values such as the dual (exactly two), trial (exactly three), and paucal (a few).

Number systems

English distinguishes only singular and plural, marking plural nouns (usually with -s), plural demonstratives, and limited verb agreement. Slovene and Sorbian preserve a full dual, so that "two hands" takes distinct dual forms on nouns, adjectives, and verbs. Classical Arabic has singular, dual, and plural. Sanskrit and Ancient Greek had a dual alongside singular and plural. Some languages of New Guinea and Australia have trial and paucal numbers; the paucal (a small number, roughly "a few") also appears in languages such as Bayso and some Slavic constructions.

Plural formation

Plurals are formed by suffixation (English cat/cats), vowel change or ablaut (foot/feet, German Umlaut plurals), reduplication (Indonesian orang-orang, "people"), or suppletion (person/people). Some nouns are plural-only (pluralia tantum: "scissors", "trousers"), while others are uncountable mass nouns ("information", "furniture") that resist pluralisation in English.

Agreement and pronouns

Number interacts with person in pronouns and verb agreement: English distinguishes I/we and he/they, and present-tense verbs mark third-person singular. Many languages also make an inclusive/exclusive distinction in "we" — whether the hearer is included — built on top of the number system.
