Portuguese Inquisition

The Portuguese Inquisition (Portuguese: Inquisição Portuguesa), formally the Holy Office of the Inquisition in Portugal, was established in 1536 at the request of King John III and remained active until 1821. Modelled on the Spanish Inquisition but answerable to the Portuguese crown, it had tribunals in Lisbon, Évora, and Coimbra, and later in Goa in Portuguese India (the Goa Inquisition, 1560–1812).

History

Following the forced conversion of Portugal's Jews in 1497, the "New Christians" (cristãos-novos) were suspected of secretly practising Judaism. Authorised by papal bull in 1536 — with the first auto-da-fé held in Lisbon in 1540 — the Inquisition prosecuted suspected heresy for nearly three centuries. Over its existence it held tens of thousands of trials and executed roughly 1,200–1,800 people, while many more suffered imprisonment, penances, and confiscation of property. Its jurisdiction extended across the Portuguese empire, including Brazil (visited by inquisitorial deputies) and Goa, where it also suppressed Hindu practices.

Decline and abolition

In the 18th century, the Marquis of Pombal curbed the Inquisition's power, abolished the legal distinction between Old and New Christians (1773), and subordinated the tribunal to the crown. The Cortes abolished the Inquisition in 1821 during the Liberal Revolution.
