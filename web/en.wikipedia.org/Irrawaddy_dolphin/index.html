The Irrawaddy dolphin (Orcaella brevirostris) is a euryhaline species of oceanic dolphin found in scattered subpopulations near sea coasts and in estuaries and rivers in parts of the Bay of Bengal and Southeast Asia. It closely resembles the beluga whale in appearance, with a rounded head and no prominent beak, though it is more closely related to the killer whale.

Description

Adults measure about 2–2.75 metres in length and weigh 90–200 kilograms. The body is bluish-grey to dark slate-grey, paler underneath. Unlike most dolphins, the Irrawaddy's U-shaped blowhole is placed on the left of the midline and opens towards the front. The dorsal fin is small and triangular, set about two-thirds of the way along the back. The flippers are long and broad.

Distribution and habitat

The species occurs in coastal waters from the Bay of Bengal to New Guinea and the Philippines, and enters several large river systems: the Ayeyarwady (Irrawaddy) in Myanmar, the Mahakam in Indonesia, the Mekong in Cambodia, Laos and Vietnam, and the Ganges–Brahmaputra system. Freshwater subpopulations are small and isolated; the Mekong subpopulation numbered fewer than 100 individuals in recent surveys.

Behaviour

Irrawaddy dolphins are shy and slow-moving, travelling in groups of 2–10. They are known for a cooperative relationship with traditional cast-net fishers in Myanmar and India, herding fish towards nets in exchange for a share of the catch. They communicate with clicks, creaks and buzzes, and are thought to use echolocation to navigate turbid waters.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the species as Endangered; several freshwater subpopulations are Critically Endangered. Principal threats include drowning in gillnets, habitat degradation, dam construction, electrofishing and pollution. The species is protected under CITES Appendix I.
