Gosplan


The former Gosplan building on Okhotny Ryad, Moscow.

Gosplan (Russian: Госплан, from Государственный плановый комитет, "State Planning Committee") was the agency responsible for central economic planning in the Soviet Union. Established in February 1921 on Lenin's initiative, it developed from an advisory body into the command centre that drafted the famous Five-Year Plans and allocated resources across the entire Soviet economy.

Function

Gosplan worked by "material balances": for each of thousands of products, planners tallied projected sources (production, imports, stocks) against uses (inputs to other industries, consumption, exports) and issued output targets and supply allocations to ministries and enterprises. Under Stalin the committee, through the First Five-Year Plan (1928–32), drove crash industrialization and collectivization-era investment; its scope eventually encompassed prices, wages, investment and regional development.

Criticism and reform

Economists criticized the system for chronic shortages, hoarding, poor quality, distorted information flowing upward from enterprises, and an inability to coordinate an economy of millions of products by administrative command. Successive Soviet leaders attempted reforms — notably Kosygin's in 1965 and Gorbachev's perestroika — but the planning apparatus remained largely intact until the dissolution of the USSR in 1991, when Gosplan's functions were absorbed into post-Soviet government ministries.
