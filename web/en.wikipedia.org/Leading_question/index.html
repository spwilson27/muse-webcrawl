Leading question

A leading question is a question which suggests a particular answer and contains information that the examiner is looking to have confirmed. In common law systems that rely on testimony by witnesses, leading questions are generally not permitted during direct examination, but they are allowed in cross-examination.


A witness being examined in court

The classic example of a leading question is asking a witness "Were you at the store on that evening?" instead of "Where were you on that evening?" The former suggests the desired answer, while the latter is open-ended.

Use in trials

In the United States, leading questions are typically not permitted during the direct examination of a witness, but are permitted on cross-examination. Under the Federal Rules of Evidence, a court may allow leading questions on direct examination when a party calls a hostile witness, an adverse party, or a witness identified with an adverse party.

Other contexts

Outside the courtroom, leading questions are common in political debates, opinion polling, and sales, where the phrasing of a question can strongly influence the response. Survey researchers caution that leading questions introduce bias into polling results, since respondents may simply confirm the answer embedded in the question rather than expressing their true view.
