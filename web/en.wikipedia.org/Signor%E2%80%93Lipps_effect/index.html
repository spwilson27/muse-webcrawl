Signor–Lipps effect

The Signor–Lipps effect is a paleontological principle proposed by Philip W. Signor and Jere H. Lipps in 1982, stating that because the fossil record is incomplete, the last known fossil of a taxon will almost never coincide with its true time of extinction, and the first known fossil will postdate its true origin. An abrupt extinction can therefore appear gradual in the fossil record.

Explanation

Fossilisation is a rare event, so the probability of sampling an organism exactly at the boundary of its existence is tiny. Species approaching a mass extinction disappear from the record one by one before the actual event, producing an apparently stepped or gradual pattern. The effect is a central caution in interpreting extinction events such as the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction.

Implications

Statistical confidence intervals can be placed around observed fossil ranges to estimate true origination and extinction times. The effect also runs in reverse for first appearances, and it underlies debates about "dead clades walking" and apparently sudden radiations.
