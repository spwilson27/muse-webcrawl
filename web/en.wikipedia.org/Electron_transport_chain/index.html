Electron transport chain

An electron transport chain (ETC) is a series of protein complexes and small organic molecules embedded in a membrane that transfer electrons from electron donors to electron acceptors via redox reactions, coupling this exergonic flow to the transport of protons (H+) across the membrane. The resulting electrochemical proton gradient drives the synthesis of ATP by ATP synthase — the process of oxidative phosphorylation proposed by Peter Mitchell's chemiosmotic theory.

The mitochondrial chain

In eukaryotes the chain sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electrons enter from NADH at Complex I (NADH dehydrogenase) and from succinate at Complex II (succinate dehydrogenase, part of the citric acid cycle). The lipid-soluble carrier ubiquinone (coenzyme Q) ferries electrons to Complex III (cytochrome bc₁ complex), and cytochrome c carries them to Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase), which reduces oxygen to water. Complexes I, III and IV pump protons into the intermembrane space; ATP synthase (Complex V) uses the returning proton flow to make ATP from ADP and phosphate.

Other chains

Similar chains operate in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts during photosynthesis, where water is the electron donor and NADPH the product, and in bacteria, which may use a wide variety of donors and acceptors (such as nitrate, sulfate or ferric iron) in anaerobic respiration. Inhibitors such as rotenone, antimycin A, cyanide and carbon monoxide block specific complexes and are potent poisons precisely because they halt ATP production.
