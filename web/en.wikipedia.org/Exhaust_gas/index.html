Exhaust gas

SourcesInternal combustion engines, furnaces, power stations
Main componentsNitrogen, water vapour, carbon dioxide
PollutantsCO, NOx, particulates, unburned hydrocarbons, SO₂
ControlsCatalytic converter, particulate filter, exhaust gas recirculation

Exhaust gas or flue gas is emitted as a result of the combustion of fuels such as natural gas, gasoline (petrol), diesel fuel, fuel oil, biodiesel blends, or coal. According to the type of engine, it is discharged into the atmosphere through an exhaust pipe, flue gas stack, or propelling nozzle. The bulk of exhaust gas is innocuous nitrogen, water vapour, and carbon dioxide, but a small fraction consists of harmful substances that are regulated as air pollutants.

Composition

From a petrol engine, exhaust typically contains nitrogen (about 71%), carbon dioxide (about 14%), water vapour, and oxygen, together with pollutants: carbon monoxide (CO) from incomplete combustion, nitrogen oxides (NOx) formed at high temperatures, unburned hydrocarbons, and fine particulate matter. Diesel exhaust contains more particulates (soot) and NOx but less CO. Sulphur dioxide arises from sulphur in fuels, especially coal and heavy oil, and contributes to acid rain. Carbon dioxide is not toxic at these concentrations but is the principal greenhouse gas from transport and energy use.

Health and environmental effects

Carbon monoxide impairs oxygen transport in blood; nitrogen oxides and hydrocarbons react in sunlight to form ground-level ozone and photochemical smog; and fine particulates penetrate deep into the lungs, with well-established links to cardiovascular and respiratory disease. Diesel exhaust is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1).

Emission control

Controls have tightened steadily since the 1970s: three-way catalytic converters oxidise CO and hydrocarbons and reduce NOx; exhaust gas recirculation lowers combustion temperatures and NOx; diesel particulate filters trap soot and selective catalytic reduction injects urea to convert NOx to nitrogen and water. Periodic emissions testing, on-board diagnostics, and standards such as Euro 6 and U.S. EPA rules govern type approval and in-use compliance — systems whose real-world performance became a major public issue after the 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal.
