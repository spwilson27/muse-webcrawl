Everyman (play)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Everyman, also known in full as The Summoning of Everyman (or The Somonyng of Everyman), is a late 15th-century English morality play. Like John Bunyan's later The Pilgrim's Progress, it is an allegory of the Christian life and of the soul's journey toward death and judgment. It is generally considered the finest example of the morality play genre.

Plot

God, offended by mankind's absorption in worldly goods, sends Death to summon Everyman to a reckoning. Terrified, Everyman begs for company on his journey and successively asks Fellowship, Kindred, and Goods (his wealth), all of whom refuse — Goods even confessing that he has damaged Everyman's soul. Only Good Deeds, weakened by Everyman's neglect, agrees to accompany him, and she is strengthened after Everyman confesses and does penance, assisted by Knowledge. Strength, Discretion, Beauty, and the Five Wits join the journey but abandon Everyman at the grave's edge; only Good Deeds descends with him into the grave, and his soul is received by an angel into heaven.

Sources and text

The play, printed by Richard Pynson in the early 16th century, is believed by most scholars to be a translation or adaptation of the Dutch play Elckerlijc (c. 1495), attributed to Peter van Diest, though the direction of the borrowing has been debated. Its 921 lines are written in irregular rhyming verse.

Legacy

After centuries of neglect, Everyman was revived by the Elizabethan Stage Society in 1901 and became a staple of amateur, university, and festival performance. Hugo von Hofmannsthal's German adaptation Jedermann (1911) is performed annually at the Salzburg Festival. The play's central device — the personified abstractions that accompany and desert a universal protagonist — shaped later allegorical drama across Europe.
