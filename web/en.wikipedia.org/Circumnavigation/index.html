Circumnavigation

Circumnavigation is the complete navigation around an entire island, continent, or (more usually) astronomical body (e.g. a planet or moon). This article focuses on the circumnavigation of Earth. To circumnavigate Earth by sea, a vessel must cross every meridian of longitude and pass through two antipodal points; analogous criteria apply to voyages by air and land.

First circumnavigation

The first recorded circumnavigation of Earth was the Magellan–Elcano expedition, which sailed from Spain in 1519 under Ferdinand Magellan and returned in 1522 under Juan Sebastián Elcano after Magellan's death in the Philippines. Of the five ships and roughly 270 men that set out, one ship, the Victoria, returned with 18 survivors. The voyage proved practically the spherical shape of Earth, established the vastness of the Pacific Ocean, and opened the spice-route westward from Europe.

Later voyages

Francis Drake's expedition of 1577–1580 was the second circumnavigation and the first completed by a commander who survived the whole voyage. Subsequent milestones include the first circumnavigation by a woman (Jeanne Baret, in disguise, 1766–1769), the first solo circumnavigation by Joshua Slocum (1895–1898), the first flight around the world (by U.S. Army Air Service aircraft in 1924), the first nonstop aerial circumnavigation (1949, with refuelling), and solo nonstop voyages by balloon and aircraft in the later twentieth century.

Modern records

Today, circumnavigations are undertaken in racing yachts — events such as the Vendée Globe record solo nonstop passages of under 75 days — and by cyclists, runners, and aviators contesting speed records governed by bodies such as Guinness World Records and the World Sailing Speed Record Council, which impose rules on route, distance (at least the length of the Tropic of Cancer, about 36,788 km, for sailing), and assistance.
