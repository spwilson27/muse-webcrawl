Composite order

The Composite order is a mixed order of classical architecture, combining the volutes of the Ionic order capital with the acanthus leaves of the Corinthian order. In many versions the Composite capital also carries echinus molding ornamented with egg-and-dart. The Composite order is one of the five classical orders recognised by Renaissance architectural theorists, alongside the Tuscan, Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian.

History

Although the combination of Ionic volutes with Corinthian foliage appeared in Roman architecture as early as the Arch of Titus (c. 82 AD), the Composite was not treated as a distinct order until the Renaissance. Sebastiano Serlio and other sixteenth-century writers on architecture codified it as the most refined and ornate of the orders, typically reserving it for the highest storey of a building or for especially important commissions.

Characteristics

The defining feature is the capital: large Ionic volutes, often angled diagonally, spring from a bell of Corinthian acanthus leaves, usually arranged in two rows. The shaft is typically fluted, and the entablature may carry rich ornament. Because it combines elements of two orders, the Composite is sometimes described as an augmented Corinthian rather than an entirely independent order.

Use

The Composite order was widely used in Roman triumphal arches and later in Renaissance, Baroque, and Neoclassical buildings. Notable examples include the Arch of Titus and the Arch of Septimius Severus in Rome, and numerous churches and palaces across Europe.
