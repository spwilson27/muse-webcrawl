Active radar homing

Active radar homing (ARH) is a missile guidance method in which the missile itself carries a radar transceiver. Unlike semi-active radar homing, where the missile tracks radar energy transmitted by the launching platform and reflected from the target, an active-radar missile illuminates its own target and therefore gives the launching aircraft much greater freedom to manoeuvre or leave after firing — the classic "fire-and-forget" capability.

Operation

Most ARH missiles do not use their own radar for the entire flight, because the small seeker has limited range. Instead, the missile flies most of its course using inertial guidance with mid-course updates from the launch aircraft via a datalink, and activates its own seeker only in the terminal phase when the target is within acquisition range. The launcher may therefore turn away or engage other targets once the missile goes "active".

Advantages and disadvantages

Active homing allows simultaneous engagement of multiple targets and frees the launch platform, improving survivability. Its disadvantages are greater cost, weight and complexity, and the fact that the missile's radar emissions can warn the target. Because an active seeker's radar is small, its detection range is shorter than that of the launch aircraft's radar, so targets must be found and tracked by other means during mid-course flight.

Examples

Well-known active radar homing missiles include the American AIM-120 AMRAAM and AIM-54 Phoenix air-to-air missiles, the French MICA, the anti-ship AGM-84 Harpoon and Exocet, and many modern surface-to-air and anti-ship weapons such as the Aster and SM-6.
