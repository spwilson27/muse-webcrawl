Sewer gas

A water-filled trap blocks sewer gas from entering a building.

Sewer gas is the mixture of gases produced by the decomposition of organic matter in sewage systems. Its composition varies but typically includes hydrogen sulfide, methane, ammonia, carbon dioxide, nitrogen and water vapour, along with trace volatile compounds. The characteristic "rotten egg" odour comes chiefly from hydrogen sulfide.

Hazards

Hydrogen sulfide is toxic: at moderate concentrations it irritates the eyes and respiratory system, and at high concentrations it can rapidly cause unconsciousness and death; it also deadens the sense of smell, removing the warning odour. Methane and hydrogen sulfide are flammable and can produce explosions in confined spaces, which is why sewer work is treated as a hazardous confined-space operation. Long-term exposure at low levels is associated with headaches, fatigue and irritation, and sewer gas leaking into buildings through dry or faulty traps is a common source of odour complaints.

Prevention

Plumbing systems exclude sewer gas through water traps (such as the U-bend) under fixtures and through vent stacks that carry gases safely to the roof. Gas accumulates indoors when traps dry out in unused drains, when vents are blocked, or when seals fail; running water in seldom-used fixtures and maintaining trap primers are standard remedies. Sewer utilities also ventilate and monitor manholes and control hydrogen sulfide to limit corrosion of concrete pipes, since sulfur-oxidising bacteria convert the gas to sulfuric acid on pipe walls.
