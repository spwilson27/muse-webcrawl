Aztecs

RegionsValley of Mexico and central Mexico
LanguagesNahuatl
Periodc. 1300 – 1521 (and continuing as Nahua peoples)

The Aztecs were a Mesoamerican civilization that flourished in central Mexico in the post-classical period from 1300 to 1521. The Aztec people included different ethnic groups of central Mexico, particularly those groups who spoke the Nahuatl language and who dominated large parts of Mesoamerica from the 14th to the 16th centuries. Their capital and largest city, Tenochtitlan, was built on an island in Lake Texcoco and grew into one of the largest cities in the world at the time.

Terminology and identity

In the Nahuatl language, "Aztec" means "people from Aztlan", a mythical place of origin. The term is normally used for the Mexica people of Tenochtitlan and their allies; modern scholars also apply it to the wider Nahua culture. The Aztec Empire was a confederation of three city-states — Tenochtitlan, Texcoco and Tlacopan — known as the Triple Alliance, formed in 1428.

Society and culture

Aztec society was highly stratified, with an emperor (tlatoani), nobility, priests, warriors, merchants (pochteca), artisans and commoners, as well as slaves. Agriculture was based on chinampa raised-bed farming in the lakes of the Valley of Mexico. The Aztecs built monumental pyramids and temples, developed an accurate calendar system, and practised a complex religion involving many deities and ritual human sacrifice; see also Aztec religion. Education was unusually universal, with schools for both noble and commoner children.

Conquest

In 1519 the Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico; allied with Tlaxcala and other peoples subject to Aztec rule, he conquered Tenochtitlan in 1521 after a siege, aided by a devastating smallpox epidemic. The territory became the core of the colony of New Spain. Nahua people and the Nahuatl language survive to the present day, and many Nahuatl words — chocolate, tomato, avocado, coyote — have entered world languages.
