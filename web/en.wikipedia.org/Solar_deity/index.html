Solar deity

A solar deity is a god or goddess representing the Sun, or an aspect of it, usually in terms of its perceived power and strength. Solar deities and Sun worship are found throughout most of recorded history in religions and mythologies around the world. The Sun's daily cycle of rising and setting made it a natural symbol of life, death and rebirth, order, and kingship.

Ancient Egypt

In ancient Egyptian religion the Sun was central. Ra (or Re) was the principal solar god, who sailed across the sky in his solar barque and through the underworld at night. The pharaoh Akhenaten's religious revolution elevated the Aten, the visible disk of the Sun, to sole god — an early experiment in monotheism. Other solar figures include Khepri, the scarab god of the morning sun, and Atum of the evening.

Mesopotamia and the Near East

The Sumerian sun god Utu, later Shamash in Akkadian, was associated with justice and law because the Sun sees everything. Shamash appears on the stele of the Code of Hammurabi handing the laws to the king.

Greece and Rome

In Greek religion Helios personified the Sun and drove his chariot across the sky; Apollo later absorbed many solar functions. In Rome, Sol Invictus ("the Unconquered Sun") became prominent in the late Empire, and his festival on 25 December influenced later traditions. The emperor Aurelian built a great temple to Sol in Rome in the 3rd century CE.

Other traditions

In Hinduism, Surya is the chief solar deity and one of the five deities of the Smarta tradition, honored in the Gayatri mantra and the Surya Namaskar sequence. In Japan, Amaterasu, the sun goddess, is the chief kami of Shinto and mythical ancestor of the imperial line. In Aztec religion, the sun god Huitzilopochtli and the sun Tonatiuh required nourishment through sacrifice; the Inca worshipped Inti, whose temple in Cusco was the empire's holiest shrine. Norse mythology personified the Sun as the goddess Sól, chased by the wolf Sköll.

Solar symbolism

Solar deities are frequently associated with sight, truth, healing, and royal authority — pharaohs and emperors styled themselves children of the Sun. The solar journey through the night sky or underworld often symbolizes death and resurrection. Eclipses were widely interpreted as attacks on the sun god by monsters or demons, as in the Egyptian serpent Apophis or the Chinese heavenly dog.
