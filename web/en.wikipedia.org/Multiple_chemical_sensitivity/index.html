Multiple chemical sensitivity

Other namesIdiopathic environmental intolerance
SpecialtyImmunology, neurology, psychiatry (disputed)
SymptomsHeadaches, fatigue, nausea, dizziness, difficulty concentrating, attributed to chemical exposures

Multiple chemical sensitivity (MCS), also known as idiopathic environmental intolerance (IEI), is a disputed chronic condition in which people report symptoms they attribute to low-level exposure to commonly tolerated chemicals and environmental agents, such as fragrances, solvents, cleaning products, vehicle exhaust, and electromagnetic fields. Because no consistent physiological mechanism or diagnostic biomarker has been established, MCS is not recognized as a distinct medical diagnosis by most major medical organizations, though the suffering reported by affected people is widely acknowledged.

People with MCS commonly report reactions to fragrances, cleaning products, and other everyday chemicals.

Signs and symptoms

Reported symptoms are broad and non-specific: headaches, fatigue, cognitive difficulties ("brain fog"), nausea, dizziness, respiratory irritation, and musculoskeletal pain. Sufferers often describe the condition as beginning with a single high-dose chemical exposure — for example a pesticide application or solvent spill — after which sensitivity is said to generalize to many unrelated substances at levels that do not affect most people.

Proposed mechanisms

Hypotheses have included immune system dysfunction, neurogenic inflammation, limbic kindling and neural sensitization, oxidative stress, and genetic differences in detoxification enzymes. Double-blind provocation studies, in which patients are exposed to chemicals or placebos without knowing which, have generally not shown that people with MCS can reliably distinguish real exposures from sham ones, leading many researchers to favour psychological and psychosomatic explanations, including conditioned responses and the nocebo effect.

Diagnosis and treatment

No validated diagnostic test exists. Diagnosis rests on patient history using criteria proposed in the clinical ecology literature. There is no proven pharmacological treatment; management typically centres on avoiding reported triggers, which for some people leads to significant social isolation and disability, and on treating comorbid conditions such as anxiety and depression.

Society and recognition

Some jurisdictions recognize MCS for accommodation purposes under disability law, and fragrance-free policies have been adopted by some hospitals, schools, and workplaces. Medical bodies including the World Health Organization prefer the term "idiopathic environmental intolerance," emphasizing that the condition is real to those who experience it while its cause remains undetermined.
