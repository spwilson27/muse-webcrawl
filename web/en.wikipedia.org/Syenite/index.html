Syenite

Syenite is a coarse-grained intrusive igneous rock with a composition similar to granite but deficient in quartz, which generally makes up less than 5 percent of the rock. It consists essentially of alkali feldspar (typically orthoclase or microcline) with subordinate plagioclase, hornblende, biotite, and pyroxene. Syenite is the plutonic equivalent of the volcanic rock trachyte.

Name and history

The rock takes its name from Syene (modern Aswan) in Egypt, where the ancient Egyptians quarried a hornblende-bearing granite-like stone — actually a granodiorite by modern classification — used for obelisks and the pavement of temples. The term was fixed in its modern sense by nineteenth-century petrologists for feldspar-rich rocks lacking significant quartz.

Composition and varieties

In the QAPF classification, syenites plot in field 7, dominated by alkali feldspar. Varieties high in feldspathoid minerals such as nepheline are called nepheline syenites (or foyaite), unusual alkaline rocks that host rare minerals and are mined for ceramic-grade feldspar and nepheline; notable localities include the Kola Peninsula in Russia, the Ilímaussaq complex in Greenland, and Bancroft, Ontario. Larvikite, a decorative iridescent syenite from Norway, is widely used as a facing stone on buildings.

Occurrence and uses

Syenites form relatively small intrusions, ring complexes, and parts of layered complexes, often associated with continental rifting and alkaline magmatism. Polished syenite is used as a dimension and decorative stone, and crushed syenite serves as aggregate.
