Nobel Peace Prize

The Nobel Peace Prize is one of the five Nobel Prizes established by the will of Swedish industrialist Alfred Nobel in 1895. It is awarded annually by the Norwegian Nobel Committee in Oslo "to the person who shall have done the most or the best work for fraternity between nations, for the abolition or reduction of standing armies and for the holding and promotion of peace congresses". Unlike the other Nobel Prizes, it is presented in Norway rather than Sweden, on 10 December, the anniversary of Nobel's death.

Selection

The five-member Norwegian Nobel Committee is appointed by the Norwegian parliament (Storting). Nominations may be made by national legislators, members of international courts, university rectors, previous laureates and others; nominations are kept secret for 50 years. The prize may be shared by up to three recipients, may be awarded to organizations, and may be withheld in any year.

Laureates and controversies

Laureates include the International Committee of the Red Cross (three times), Martin Luther King Jr. (1964), Mother Teresa (1979), Lech Wałęsa (1983), Desmond Tutu (1984), the Dalai Lama (1989), Nelson Mandela and F. W. de Klerk (1993), and — for the Oslo Accords — Yasser Arafat, Shimon Peres and Yitzhak Rabin (1994). Some awards have proven controversial, including those to Henry Kissinger (1973), Barack Obama (2009) and the European Union (2012). Mahatma Gandhi, famously, was never awarded the prize.
