Oslo Accords

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Oslo Accords are a pair of agreements between Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO): the Oslo I Accord, formally the Declaration of Principles on Interim Self-Government Arrangements, signed in Washington, D.C. in 1993; and the Oslo II Accord, formally the Interim Agreement on the West Bank and the Gaza Strip, signed in Taba, Egypt, in 1995. They marked the first direct, face-to-face agreement between Israel and the PLO and provided a framework intended to resolve the Israeli–Palestinian conflict.

Background and terms

The accords grew out of secret talks hosted by Norway after the Madrid Conference of 1991 and the First Intifada. Under them, Israel recognised the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people, and the PLO recognised Israel's right to exist and renounced terrorism. A Palestinian Authority was created to exercise limited self-government in parts of the West Bank and Gaza Strip during a five-year interim period, after which final-status negotiations were to settle the core issues: borders, Jerusalem, refugees, settlements and security.

Division of the West Bank

Oslo II divided the West Bank into Areas A (full Palestinian civil and security control), B (Palestinian civil control, joint security) and C (full Israeli control) — arrangements that, though designed as temporary, largely persist decades later. Rabin, Peres and Arafat shared the 1994 Nobel Peace Prize.

Collapse and legacy

Rabin was assassinated by a Jewish extremist in 1995; the failure of the 2000 Camp David Summit and the outbreak of the Second Intifada derailed the process. The accords are widely debated: supporters credit them with mutual recognition and Palestinian self-government institutions, while critics argue they entrenched occupation and fragmented Palestinian territory. Their interim structures still underpin the governance of the West Bank and Gaza.
