The United Nations (UN) is an intergovernmental organization founded in 1945, after the Second World War, to maintain international peace and security, develop friendly relations among nations, and promote human rights and international cooperation. It is the world's largest international organization, with 193 member states and two observer states, the Holy See and Palestine.

Structure

The UN Charter established six principal organs. The General Assembly is the plenary deliberative body in which all members are represented; the Security Council, with five veto-holding permanent members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States) and ten elected members, bears primary responsibility for peace and security and can authorize sanctions and peacekeeping missions. The Secretariat, headed by the Secretary-General, administers the organization. The International Court of Justice in The Hague is the principal judicial organ. A large system of specialized agencies — including WHO, UNESCO, UNICEF and UNHCR — carries out economic, social and humanitarian work.

History and role

The UN succeeded the League of Nations and was shaped by wartime conferences at Dumbarton Oaks, Yalta and San Francisco. It adopted the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948, oversaw decolonization, and has deployed peacekeepers to conflicts from Korea to Cyprus — the latter still active. In the Middle East it has mediated armistices, created the first peacekeeping force (UNEF, 1956), mandated UNRWA for Palestinian refugees, and set the diplomatic framework (Resolutions 242 and 338) for Arab–Israeli peace efforts.
