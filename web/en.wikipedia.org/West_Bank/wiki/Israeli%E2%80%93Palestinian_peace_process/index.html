Israeli–Palestinian peace process

The Israeli–Palestinian peace process refers to the intermittent negotiations and agreements since the early 1990s aimed at resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict, principally through a two-state solution: an independent State of Palestine alongside the State of Israel.

Madrid and Oslo

The 1991 Madrid Conference opened the first public bilateral talks. Secret negotiations in Norway produced the Oslo Accords: the 1993 Declaration of Principles, in which Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization recognized each other, and the 1995 Interim Agreement (Oslo II), which created the Palestinian Authority and divided the West Bank into Areas A, B and C. Final-status issues — borders, Jerusalem, refugees, settlements and security — were deferred.

Later negotiations

The 2000 Camp David Summit between Ehud Barak and Yasser Arafat ended without agreement and was followed by the Second Intifada; the Taba talks of 2001 narrowed some gaps but were not concluded. The Quartet's 2003 Road Map, the 2007 Annapolis Conference and US Secretary of State John Kerry's 2013–2014 initiative all failed to produce a final agreement. Negotiations have been effectively stalled since 2014.

Obstacles and alternatives

Key obstacles include continued Israeli settlement building, Palestinian political division between Fatah and Hamas, violence on both sides, and disagreement over Jerusalem and refugees. Alongside the two-state framework, proposals debated include a one-state solution, confederation models, and the 2020 US plan ("Peace to Prosperity"), which Palestinians rejected.
