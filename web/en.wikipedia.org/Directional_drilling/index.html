Directional drilling

Directional drilling (or slant drilling) is the practice of drilling non-vertical bores. It can be broken down into three main groups: oilfield directional drilling, horizontal directional drilling (HDD, used for installing utilities), and directional boring. Directional wells are drilled to reach targets that are not directly beneath the drilling rig, to follow the geometry of a reservoir, or to intersect a formation over a long horizontal section.

Oilfield directional drilling

Historically, ostensibly vertical wells often deviated unintentionally, and early directional techniques — whipstocks and single-shot survey instruments — were developed in the 1920s and 1930s, famously to drill relief wells and, controversially, to slant-drill into neighbouring leases (as under the East Texas field). Modern directional drilling relies on mud motors and rotary steerable systems to deflect the bit, and on measurement while drilling (MWD) tools that transmit inclination and azimuth measurements to the surface in real time, together with logging while drilling (LWD) formation data. Directional drillers plot the well path using the minimum curvature method and aim to build angle from the kick-off point, hold a tangent section, and in horizontal wells land the well in the target zone and drill laterally for thousands of metres.

Reasons for directional wells

  • Reaching reservoirs beneath cities, lakes or environmentally sensitive areas where a surface location is impractical.
  • Drilling many wells from a single offshore platform or pad.
  • Horizontal sections through thin reservoirs to maximise contact and production — the technique, combined with hydraulic fracturing, that enabled the shale oil and gas boom.
  • Relief wells to intercept a blowing-out well.
  • Sidetracking around stuck equipment or to a new target.
  • Crossing a fault or salt dome at a favourable angle, or following a dipping formation.

Extended reach drilling pushes horizontal departures beyond 10 km from the rig; famous examples include wells at Sakhalin, Russia, and the Wytch Farm field in England, where reservoirs under Poole Bay were reached from onshore.

Horizontal directional drilling (utilities)

HDD for utilities drills a shallow-arced pilot hole along a designed path, then reams and pulls back pipelines, cables or ducts. It allows crossings under rivers, roads and railways without open trenching, and is widely used for fibre-optic and power installations.
