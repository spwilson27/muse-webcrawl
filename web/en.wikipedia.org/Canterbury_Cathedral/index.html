Canterbury Cathedral, formally the Cathedral and Metropolitical Church of Christ, Canterbury, is the cathedral of the Archbishop of Canterbury, the leader of the Church of England and the symbolic leader of the worldwide Anglican Communion. Located in Canterbury, Kent, it is one of the oldest and most famous Christian structures in England. It forms part of a World Heritage Site, along with the nearby St Augustine's Abbey and St Martin's Church.

Founded in 597 by Augustine of Canterbury, sent by Pope Gregory the Great, the cathedral was completely rebuilt in stone after a fire in 1067 under Archbishop Lanfranc, and again transformed after the catastrophic fire of 1174, when the choir was rebuilt by the French mason William of Sens in what is among the earliest examples of Early English Gothic architecture.

Thomas Becket and pilgrimage

On 29 December 1170 four knights of King Henry II murdered Archbishop Thomas Becket in the north-west transept of the cathedral, after Henry's exasperated outburst against the troublesome priest. Becket was canonised within three years, and his shrine in the Trinity Chapel became one of the great pilgrimage destinations of medieval Europe, immortalised in Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales. The shrine was destroyed on the orders of Henry VIII in 1538 during the English Reformation. A modern memorial and a perpetually lit candle now mark the site of the murder and the lost shrine.

Architecture

The cathedral displays an unusually complete stylistic sequence: the Romanesque crypt, the largest of its kind in England, survives from Lanfranc's and Anselm's church; the choir of 1174–1184 introduced pointed arches, rib vaults and flying buttresses to England; the nave was rebuilt in Perpendicular Gothic in the late fourteenth century, and the great central "Bell Harry" tower was completed around 1498. The cathedral's stained glass, much of it from the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, is among the finest series of medieval glass in Britain, including the celebrated Bible windows of the Corona Chapel and Trinity Chapel.

The cathedral today

Canterbury remains a working cathedral with daily services, a renowned choir, and the enthronement of each new Archbishop of Canterbury on the ancient Chair of St Augustine. It receives around a million visitors a year and was the scene of the funeral of Prince Albert Victor and numerous royal occasions; in 1982 Pope John Paul II prayed at the site of Becket's martyrdom with Archbishop Robert Runcie, the first visit of a pope to the cathedral since the Reformation.
