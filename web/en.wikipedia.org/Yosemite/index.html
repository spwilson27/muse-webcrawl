Yosemite most often refers to Yosemite National Park, a United States national park in California's Sierra Nevada, and to Yosemite Valley, the glacial valley at its heart, famed for granite cliffs such as El Capitan and Half Dome, and for some of the tallest waterfalls in North America, including Yosemite Falls.

The name

The word Yosemite derives from the Miwok name for the Ahwahneechee people of the valley, often translated as "killers" — a name given by neighbouring tribes. The valley's Indigenous inhabitants called it Ahwahnee. Non-native Americans first entered the valley in 1851 with the Mariposa Battalion during the Mariposa War, which displaced the Ahwahneechee.

Protection

Alarmed by commercial exploitation, conservationists led by Galen Clark and Senator John Conness persuaded President Abraham Lincoln to sign the Yosemite Grant of 1864, ceding the valley and the Mariposa Grove of giant sequoias to California for public use — the first time land was set aside by the US federal government for preservation, and a precedent for the national park idea. Naturalist John Muir's advocacy led to the creation of Yosemite National Park in 1890; the valley and grove were returned to federal control in 1906. The park covers about 3,000 km², is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and receives around four million visitors a year.

Features

Beyond the valley, the park encompasses high Sierra country along the Tuolumne and Merced rivers, groves of giant sequoias, and the Hetch Hetchy valley, whose damming in 1913 — over Muir's fierce opposition — remains a landmark controversy in conservation history. Big-wall climbing on El Capitan, including Alex Honnold's 2017 free solo ascent, has made Yosemite central to modern rock climbing.
