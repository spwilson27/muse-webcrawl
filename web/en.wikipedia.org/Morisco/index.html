Morisco

[Image: Sixteenth-century depiction of Moriscos in Spain]

The Moriscos (Spanish: moriscos, "Moorish ones") were the Muslims of Spain who converted to Christianity after the fall of the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada in 1492, and their descendants. Following decrees issued between 1502 (in Castile) and the 1520s (in the Crown of Aragon), Islam was prohibited throughout the Spanish kingdoms, and the remaining Muslim population was compelled to convert, leave, or profess Christianity. In total, Moriscos numbered perhaps 300,000 - 350,000 by the early seventeenth century, concentrated in Valencia, Aragon, Granada, Murcia and other regions.

Persecution and revolt

Although legally Christian, many Moriscos continued to practise Islamic customs in private, writing Spanish in Arabic script (aljamiado literature) and maintaining distinct dress, bathing customs and foodways. They were subject to surveillance by the Spanish Inquisition and to repeated cultural restrictions, including a 1567 prohibition by Philip II on Arabic language and Moorish dress in Granada. The ensuing Rebellion of the Alpujarras (1568-1571), led by Abén Humeya, was crushed and followed by the deportation of the Granadan Morisco population across Castile.

Expulsion of 1609-1614

Fearing collaboration with Ottoman and Barbary enemies and doubting the sincerity of their conversions, Philip III decreed the expulsion of the Moriscos beginning in 1609. Over about five years, roughly 300,000 Moriscos were transported, mainly to North Africa; the Kingdom of Valencia alone lost around a third of its population, with serious consequences for agriculture and the economy. Many expelled families were integrated into communities in Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia, while some returned clandestinely to Spain.
