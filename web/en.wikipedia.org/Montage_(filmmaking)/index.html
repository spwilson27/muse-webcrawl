Montage (filmmaking)

Montage (from French monter, "to assemble") is a film editing technique in which a series of short shots is assembled into a sequence to condense space, time and information, or to create meaning through the juxtaposition of images. The term carries two historically distinct senses: in European and Soviet usage it refers broadly to editing itself as the essence of cinema; in Hollywood usage it denotes a specific device — the "montage sequence" — that compresses a process, such as an athlete's training or a newspaper's rise, into a brief, often music-driven passage.

Soviet montage theory

In the 1920s Soviet filmmakers developed montage into a theory of film meaning. Lev Kuleshov's experiments suggested that viewers derive more meaning from the interaction of two shots than from either in isolation — the Kuleshov effect. Sergei Eisenstein radicalised this into a dialectical method: meaning arises from the collision of shots, not their linkage. In films such as Strike (1925), Battleship Potemkin (1925) and October (1928) Eisenstein classified montage types — metric, rhythmic, tonal, overtonal and intellectual — the last juxtaposing images to produce abstract concepts, as when Kerensky is intercut with a mechanical peacock. Vsevolod Pudovkin, by contrast, treated montage as constructive editing that links shots into a narrative chain, and Dziga Vertov pursued a documentary variant in Man with a Movie Camera (1929).

Hollywood montage sequences

In American studio cinema, especially the 1930s and 1940s, montage sequences — often made by specialists such as Slavko Vorkapich — condensed time and information using dissolves, superimpositions, spinning newspaper headlines, calendar pages and maps with moving lines. The device survived in the sports and training montage popularised by films like Rocky (1976), and in the musical montage passages of contemporary trailers and dramas.

Legacy

Montage theory founded much of film studies' account of editing, influencing everything from the French New Wave to music video. The Hollywood sense has meanwhile passed into everyday language, where any rapid compilation set to music — from wedding videos to sports highlights — is called a montage.
