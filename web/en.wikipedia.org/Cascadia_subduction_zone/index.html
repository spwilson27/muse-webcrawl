The Cascadia subduction zone is a 1,000 km (620 mi) long convergent plate boundary, from northern California to Vancouver Island in Canada, that separates the Juan de Fuca and North America plates. The oceanic Juan de Fuca Plate is subducting beneath the continent at a rate of about 30–40 mm per year, fuelling the volcanoes of the Cascade Range and storing strain for rare, giant megathrust earthquakes.

The 1700 earthquake

The last full rupture occurred on 26 January 1700, with an estimated magnitude between 8.7 and 9.2. The event is known from geological evidence — subsided coastal forests and tsunami deposits — from Indigenous oral traditions, and from Japanese records of an "orphan tsunami" that arrived without a locally felt earthquake. Turbidite studies indicate about 19–20 full or partial ruptures over the last 10,000 years, with an average interval of roughly 500 years for the largest events.

Volcanism

Subduction generates the Cascade Volcanic Arc, including Mount St. Helens, Mount Rainier, Mount Hood, and Mount Shasta. The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens was the most destructive volcanic event in United States history.

Risk and preparedness

Scientists estimate a significant probability (often cited as roughly 37% for a magnitude 8+ event in southern Cascadia within 50 years) of another megathrust earthquake, which would cause strong shaking in cities such as Seattle, Portland, and Vancouver and generate a tsunami reaching the coast within 15–30 minutes. The hazard has driven updated building codes, tsunami evacuation planning, and public attention in the Pacific Northwest.
