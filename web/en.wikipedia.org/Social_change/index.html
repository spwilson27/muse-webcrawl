Social change

Social change is the alteration of the social order of a society, including changes in nature, social institutions, social behaviours, and social relations. It may also refer to the notion of social progress — the idea that societies and human cultures are, or ought to be, evolving towards improvement. Social change is studied in sociology, economics, and history, and ranges from small shifts in a community to transformations of entire civilisations.

Collective action is one driver of social change

Theories

Classical sociologists offered competing accounts of change. Auguste Comte saw societies progressing through stages of knowledge; Karl Marx attributed change to conflicts between classes over the means of production; Max Weber emphasised ideas, religion, and rationalisation, notably in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism; and Émile Durkheim described the transition from mechanical to organic solidarity as populations grow denser. Later theories include modernization theory, world-systems analysis, and models of cyclical rise and decline.

Sources of change

Major drivers of social change include technological innovation (such as the printing press, steam power, and the internet), demographic shifts, environmental pressures, cultural diffusion and conflict, economic development, and organised collective action through social movements — including the labour, civil rights, feminist, and environmental movements. Change may be gradual or revolutionary, intended or unintended; sociologists such as William F. Ogburn noted "cultural lag", in which material culture changes faster than the norms that regulate it.
