The Federalist Papers

The Federalist Papers is a collection of 85 articles and essays written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay under the collective pseudonym "Publius" to promote the ratification of the Constitution of the United States. The collection was commonly known as The Federalist until the name The Federalist Papers emerged in the twentieth century.

Background and publication

After the Constitutional Convention of 1787 sent the proposed Constitution to the states, opponents ("Anti-Federalists") attacked it in the press. Hamilton organised a defence in New York newspapers, recruiting Madison and Jay. The essays appeared between October 1787 and August 1788, first in New York papers and then syndicated widely; the first 36 were collected in book form in March 1788, and the remainder followed. Because New York's ratifying convention was expected to be difficult, the essays were aimed first at its delegates, but they became a general exposition of the Constitution's principles.

Notable essays

Federalist No. 10, by Madison, argues that an extended republic best controls the mischief of faction. No. 39 defines the Constitution as partly federal, partly national. No. 51 explains the system of checks and balances ("Ambition must be made to counteract ambition"). No. 70 defends an energetic single executive, and No. 78, by Hamilton, sets out the case for judicial review and life-tenured judges.

Influence

The papers are treated by courts and scholars as a leading primary-source commentary on the meaning of the Constitution; the U.S. Supreme Court has cited them in hundreds of decisions. Authorship of twelve essays was long disputed between Hamilton and Madison, but twentieth-century stylometric analysis attributed most of them to Madison.
