Demand

In economics, demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at each possible price during a given period of time. Demand is not simply desire: it is desire backed by purchasing power. The relationship between price and quantity demanded, other things equal (ceteris paribus), is summarised by the demand curve.

Law of demand

The law of demand states that, other things equal, the quantity demanded of a good falls as its price rises, and rises as its price falls. The standard explanations are the substitution effect (consumers switch toward relatively cheaper goods) and the income effect (a price fall increases real income). Rare exceptions are hypothesised for Giffen goods and Veblen goods, where higher prices may stimulate demand for status or staple goods under particular conditions.

Determinants of demand

Besides the good's own price, demand depends on consumers' incomes, the prices of related goods (substitutes and complements), tastes and preferences, expectations about future prices and income, and the number of buyers. A change in price causes a movement along the demand curve; a change in any of these other determinants shifts the whole curve — to the right for an increase in demand, to the left for a decrease.

Elasticity

The price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to price: the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is called elastic when the absolute value exceeds one and inelastic when below one. Elasticity governs how total revenue responds to price changes and how tax burdens are shared between buyers and sellers.

Demand and supply

Together with supply, demand determines market equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Alfred Marshall's famous analogy likened the two to the blades of a scissors — asking which one determines price is as meaningless as asking which blade cuts the paper. Aggregated over the whole economy, aggregate demand (consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports) is the central variable of Keynesian macroeconomics.
