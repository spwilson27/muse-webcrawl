The Great Red Spot is a persistent high-pressure region in the atmosphere of Jupiter, producing an anticyclonic storm that is the largest in the Solar System. It is located 22 degrees south of Jupiter's equator and has been observed since at least 1831, and possibly as early as 1664. The storm rotates counterclockwise with a period of about six days, and its winds peak at around 425 km/h (265 mph). Once large enough to swallow two or three Earths, the Spot has been shrinking for more than a century; in recent measurements it is about 16,350 km (10,160 mi) wide — still wider than Earth, whose diameter is 12,742 km.

Observation history

A large spot on Jupiter was described by Robert Hooke in 1664 and observed repeatedly by Giovanni Domenico Cassini between 1665 and 1713 as the "Permanent Spot". Whether Cassini's spot is the same feature as the modern Great Red Spot is uncertain, because no spot was recorded between 1713 and 1830; the modern Spot has been continuously monitored since 1831 and was drawn prominently by Heinrich Schwabe. Spacecraft observations began with Pioneer 10 (1973) and the Voyager flybys (1979), followed by Galileo, Cassini's Jupiter flyby, New Horizons, the Hubble Space Telescope's annual OPAL imaging, and the Juno orbiter, whose close passes have revealed the storm's depth — its roots extend perhaps 300–500 km below the cloud tops.

Structure and colour

The Great Red Spot is an anticyclone — a high-pressure system — confined between two jet streams flowing in opposite directions. Its distinctive red-orange colour is thought to arise from compounds produced when gases from Jupiter's interior, such as ammonium hydrosulfide, are broken apart by ultraviolet sunlight at the cloud tops, though the exact chromophore remains debated. The spot's colour varies over time, sometimes fading to a pale orange or nearly white and being defined mainly by the "Red Spot Hollow" in the surrounding cloud band.

Shrinkage

Long-term observations show the Spot's east–west extent has shrunk from about 40,000 km in the late 19th century to roughly half that by the 21st, while its shape has become more circular. It has also been observed to "flake" off red material in encounters with smaller vortices. Whether the storm will eventually dissipate entirely, and over what timescale, is unknown.
