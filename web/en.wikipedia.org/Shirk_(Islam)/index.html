Shirk (Arabic: شِرْك, shirk) in Islam is the sin of idolatry or polytheism — the deification or worship of anyone or anything besides God (Allah), or the belief that God has partners, equals, or offspring. It is the exact opposite of tawhid, the doctrine of God's absolute oneness affirmed in the Shahada. The Quran calls shirk an enormity: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive association with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills" (Quran 4:48), and Luqman's counsel to his son calls shirk "a tremendous injustice" (31:13).

Categories

Islamic theologians commonly classify shirk in several ways. Major shirk (shirk al-akbar) is overt idolatry: directing worship — prayer, sacrifice, vows, ultimate reliance — to other than God, whether idols, saints, graves, or created beings, and is held to expel a person from Islam. Minor shirk (shirk al-asghar) covers ostentation in worship (riyāʾ, performing devotion to be seen by others) and certain oaths and superstitious practices; it is a grave sin but does not itself constitute unbelief. Some classifications add "hidden shirk," subtle reliance on created means as if independent of God, and distinguish shirk in lordship (rubūbiyya), in worship (ulūhiyya), and in God's names and attributes (al-asmāʾ wa-ṣ-ṣifāt).

Significance and debate

Avoidance of shirk structures much of Islamic practice and aesthetics, from the rejection of images in mosques to strict doctrines of divine transcendence. The charge of shirk has also been a potent weapon in intra-Muslim controversy: reform movements from the medieval theologian Ibn Taymiyya to the 18th-century Wahhabi movement condemned widespread customs — such as seeking intercession at saints' tombs — as shirk, while defenders regard many of those practices as legitimate veneration. The pre-Islamic Arabs who opposed Muhammad are called mushrikūn in the Quran.
