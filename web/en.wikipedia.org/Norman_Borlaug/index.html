Norman Ernest Borlaug (25 March 1914 – 12 September 2009) was an American agronomist who led initiatives worldwide that contributed to extensive increases in agricultural production, termed the Green Revolution. He is often credited with saving over a billion people from starvation, and was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 for his work in feeding a hungry world.

Work in Mexico

Trained as a plant pathologist at the University of Minnesota, Borlaug joined the Rockefeller Foundation's cooperative programme in Mexico in 1944. There he bred semi-dwarf, disease-resistant wheat varieties — notably by introducing dwarfing genes from the Japanese Norin 10 wheat and using shuttle breeding between two Mexican growing seasons to speed selection and confer broad adaptation. By 1956 Mexico had become self-sufficient in wheat, and the varieties were exported widely.

The Green Revolution

In the 1960s Borlaug's high-yielding wheats were adopted in India and Pakistan amid famine fears, where national wheat output roughly doubled within a few years; similar gains followed with semi-dwarf rice elsewhere in Asia. He helped found the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT) in Mexico and trained thousands of young scientists. Later he co-founded the Sasakawa Africa Association to bring the approach to sub-Saharan Africa, and established the World Food Prize (1986).

Views and criticism

Borlaug argued that high-yield farming spared wilderness by producing more food on less land ("land sparing"), and he vigorously defended plant biotechnology. Critics of the Green Revolution point to its dependence on fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation, and to social consequences of input-intensive farming; Borlaug countered that critics underestimated the hunger the alternative would have brought. He remains one of only a handful of people to receive the Nobel Peace Prize, the Presidential Medal of Freedom and the Congressional Gold Medal.
