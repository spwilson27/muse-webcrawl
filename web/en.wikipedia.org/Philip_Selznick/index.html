Philip Selznick (January 8, 1919 – June 12, 2010) was an American sociologist and legal scholar, a professor at the University of California, Berkeley. A foundational figure in organizational theory, he is best known for his concepts of co-optation and institutional leadership, and for his contributions to the sociology of law.

Career and work

Selznick studied at City College of New York and earned his doctorate at Columbia University, where he was influenced by Robert K. Merton. His first book, TVA and the Grass Roots (1949), a study of the Tennessee Valley Authority, showed how an organization's formal commitments could be diverted through co-optation of local interests — a classic statement of the institutional school of organization theory. In Leadership in Administration (1957) he distinguished mere management from institutional leadership, the work of defining an organization's mission and embodying its values.

Selznick later turned to legal sociology, co-founding the Center for the Study of Law and Society at Berkeley. With Philippe Nonet he wrote Law and Society in Transition: Toward Responsive Law (1978), which proposed "responsive law" as a stage beyond repressive and autonomous law. His late work, The Moral Commonwealth (1992), synthesized his thinking on community, morality, and institutions.

Legacy

Selznick's ideas about how organizations become "institutions" infused with value beyond their technical tasks profoundly influenced organizational sociology, management studies, and legal theory.
