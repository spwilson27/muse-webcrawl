Battle of Quebec (1690)

Quebec, successfully defended by Frontenac against the New England expedition of October 1690.

Date16–24 October 1690
LocationQuebec City, New France
ResultFrench victory
CommandersCount Frontenac vs. Sir William Phips

The Battle of Quebec was fought in October 1690 between the forces of New France and the English colony of Massachusetts Bay during King William's War, the North American theatre of the Nine Years' War. A New England expedition under Sir William Phips attempted to capture Quebec City and was repulsed by the Governor General of New France, the Count de Frontenac.

Background

French and Native raids on the New England frontier, including the destruction of Schenectady and Salmon Falls, prompted Massachusetts to retaliate on a grand scale in 1690. Phips first captured Port Royal in Acadia, then sailed up the St. Lawrence with a fleet of 34 ships and some 2,300 militia to take Quebec itself. Delays meant the expedition arrived dangerously late in the season.

The battle

Phips anchored before Quebec on 16 October and sent an envoy demanding surrender within the hour. Frontenac, who had rushed back from Montreal and concentrated some 2,700 defenders, returned the famous answer that he would reply "by the mouths of my cannon and muskets". Phips landed troops at Beauport under John Walley, but they were harassed by Canadian militia and Native skirmishers and never broke through; meanwhile his ships bombarded the town for days, wasting ammunition while suffering damage from the shore batteries. Smallpox, dwindling supplies and approaching winter forced Phips to withdraw on 24 October.

Aftermath

The expedition lost several hundred men, mostly to disease, and some ships on the stormy return voyage. Quebec's successful defence became a founding triumph of French Canada — Frontenac's defiance remains celebrated — while the failure left Massachusetts saddled with a debt that drove it to issue the first paper money in colonial America to pay its soldiers.
