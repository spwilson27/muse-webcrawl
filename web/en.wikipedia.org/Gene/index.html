Gene

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology, a gene is a sequence of nucleotides in DNA (or, in some viruses, RNA) that encodes the synthesis of a functional product, either a protein or a functional RNA molecule. Genes are the basic units of heredity: they are passed from parents to offspring and carry the information that determines many of an organism's traits.

Structure

A typical eukaryotic gene consists of a promoter region that controls when the gene is transcribed, coding sequences (exons) that specify the product, intervening non-coding sequences (introns) that are spliced out of the RNA transcript, and terminator signals. Protein-coding genes are transcribed into messenger RNA, which is then translated into a chain of amino acids that folds into a protein. Other genes encode ribosomal RNA, transfer RNA, or regulatory RNAs that are never translated.

Expression and regulation

Gene expression is tightly regulated by transcription factors, enhancers and silencers, and by epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modification, allowing cells with identical genomes to differentiate into muscle, nerve, blood and other cell types. Changes in the gene sequence — mutations and alleles — generate genetic variation, the raw material of evolution by natural selection.

Genomes

The human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, only a small fraction of its three billion base pairs; much of the remainder consists of regulatory elements, introns, repetitive sequences and genes for non-coding RNAs. The word "gene" was coined in 1909 by the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen, building on Gregor Mendel's earlier concept of inherited factors.
