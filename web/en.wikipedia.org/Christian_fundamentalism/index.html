Christian fundamentalism

Christian fundamentalism, also known broadly as fundamental Christianity or fundamentalism, is a religious movement emphasizing biblical literalism. It arose in the late 19th and early 20th centuries among British and American Protestants as a reaction to theological modernism and liberal theology, seeking to uphold what its adherents defined as the fundamental doctrines of the Christian faith.

The Fundamentals (1910–1915), the series of essays that gave fundamentalism its name

Origins

The movement's defining statement was a five-point summary adopted by the Presbyterian Church's General Assembly in 1910: the inerrancy of Scripture, the virgin birth of Christ, substitutionary atonement, the bodily resurrection of Christ, and the authenticity of miracles (with the literal Second Coming often added). Between 1910 and 1915, ninety essays by conservative scholars were published as The Fundamentals: A Testimony to the Truth, financed by the oil magnates Lyman and Milton Stewart; those who affirmed them began to be called "fundamentalists," a term coined in 1920 by the Baptist editor Curtis Lee Laws.

Development

Fundamentalists initially sought to reform mainline denominations but, after public setbacks such as the 1925 Scopes "Monkey Trial" over the teaching of evolution in Tennessee, many withdrew into separate churches, schools, colleges, and mission agencies, building a parallel subculture. After World War II, a more outward-looking "neo-evangelicalism" emerged from within the movement under figures such as Billy Graham, while self-identified fundamentalists — Baptists, dispensationalists, and Pentecostals among them — maintained stricter separatism. From the late 1970s, politically mobilized fundamentalists and evangelicals, organized in efforts such as the Moral Majority, became a significant force in American politics.

Beliefs and characteristics

Defining emphases commonly include the infallibility or inerrancy of the Bible in all matters, literal interpretation where the text is taken to intend it, the virgin birth, substitutionary atonement, bodily resurrection, and the imminent premillennial return of Christ. Sociologically, fundamentalism is often associated with doctrinal militancy and separation from perceived apostasy, though usage of the term varies widely and some scholars extend it by analogy to movements in other religions.
