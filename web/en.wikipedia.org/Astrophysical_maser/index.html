Astrophysical maser

An astrophysical maser is a naturally occurring source of stimulated spectral line emission, typically in the microwave portion of the electromagnetic spectrum. This emission may arise in molecular clouds, comets, planetary atmospheres, stellar atmospheres, or the conditions created in a galaxy's active nucleus. As with a laboratory maser, the emission comes from a medium with a population inversion; the radiation is amplified along long path lengths through the medium.

Discovery

In 1965, radio astronomers detected unexpectedly intense emission from hydroxyl (OH) molecules in interstellar clouds — initially dubbed "mysterium" — which was soon explained as maser action. Water vapour masers at 22 GHz were found in 1969, followed by methanol and silicon monoxide masers.

Maser species

Common maser transitions include OH (at 1612, 1665, 1667 and 1720 MHz), water (22 GHz), methanol (6.7 and 12.2 GHz, exclusive tracers of high-mass star formation), silicon monoxide (in the atmospheres of evolved stars) and ammonia. Pumping may be collisional, radiative, or a combination of both.

Astronomical uses

Because maser spots are compact and extremely bright, very-long-baseline interferometry can measure their positions and motions with microarcsecond precision. Maser observations yield the proper motions and parallaxes of star-forming regions, the structure of circumstellar envelopes, and the rotation curves of accretion discs around supermassive black holes.

Megamasers

In external galaxies, "megamasers" are millions of times more luminous than typical Galactic masers. The water megamaser in NGC 4258 provided a precise geometric distance to that galaxy and strong evidence for a central supermassive black hole; such systems now contribute to measuring the Hubble constant.
