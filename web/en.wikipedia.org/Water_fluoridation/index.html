Water fluoridation

Water fluoridation is the controlled adjustment of fluoride to a public water supply purely for the purpose of reducing tooth decay. Fluoridated water maintains fluoride levels known to prevent cavities; it can occur naturally or be adjusted by adding fluoride. It became an official policy of the US Public Health Service in 1951, and the CDC has named it one of the ten great public health achievements of the twentieth century.

History

The practice traces to early twentieth-century observations by dentist Frederick McKay of “Colorado brown stain” — mottled enamel in areas whose water naturally contained fluoride — coupled with lower rates of decay. Research led by H. Trendley Dean established that moderate fluoride reduced cavities. Grand Rapids, Michigan, became the first city in the world to fluoridate its water in 1945, and comparisons with control cities showed large reductions in children’s cavities. The US recommendation was set at 0.7 milligrams per litre in 2015.

Evidence and use

Reviews by health authorities in the US, UK, Australia and elsewhere conclude that fluoridation at recommended levels reduces tooth decay in children and adults, though effect sizes in recent studies are smaller than in earlier ones because of the widespread use of fluoride toothpaste. About 25 countries practise fluoridation to some degree; in the United States roughly three-quarters of people on public water systems receive fluoridated water. Many countries instead fluoridate salt or milk, or rely on topical fluoride.

Controversy

Fluoridation has been controversial since its introduction, with opposition citing ethical objections to mass medication, concerns about fluorosis, and disputed claims about broader health effects. Scientific reviews generally find no convincing evidence of harm at recommended concentrations apart from an increased rate of mild dental fluorosis, but periodic reassessments — including studies of possible neurodevelopmental effects at higher exposures — continue to fuel public debate, legal challenges and the discontinuation of fluoridation by some communities.
