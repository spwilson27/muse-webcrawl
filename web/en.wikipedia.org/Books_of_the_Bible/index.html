The Bible as a collection of books written over many centuries

The books of the Bible are the writings that different religious traditions regard as sacred scripture. Christianity divides them into the Old Testament, largely corresponding to the Hebrew Bible, and the New Testament, which concerns the life of Jesus and the early church. The exact list of books — the biblical canon — differs between denominations.

The Old Testament

The Protestant Old Testament contains 39 books. It begins with the Pentateuch (Torah): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. Historical books follow — Joshua, Judges, Ruth, Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, and Esther — then the poetic and wisdom books (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs), and the prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Daniel, and the twelve Minor Prophets). The Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox canons include additional deuterocanonical books such as Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees.

The New Testament

The New Testament contains 27 books accepted by virtually all Christian traditions: the four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John), the Acts of the Apostles, thirteen letters attributed to Paul, the Epistle to the Hebrews, seven general epistles (James, Peter, John, Jude), and the Book of Revelation.

Development of the canon

The Hebrew Bible's canon took shape over centuries; its final section, the Writings, was settled by around the 1st–2nd century AD. The Christian Old Testament inherited the wider Greek Septuagint tradition, which included the deuterocanonical books. The New Testament canon developed over the first four centuries: Athanasius's Easter letter of 367 AD gives the first list matching the present 27 books, and the synods of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) ratified it in the West. The Ethiopian Orthodox canon is the broadest, with 81 books including the Book of Enoch.
