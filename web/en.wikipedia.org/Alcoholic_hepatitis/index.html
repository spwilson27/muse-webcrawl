Alcoholic hepatitis is an acute inflammation of the liver caused by prolonged, heavy consumption of alcohol. It occurs on the spectrum of alcohol-related liver disease, between fatty liver (steatosis) and cirrhosis, and typically presents after years of drinking, sometimes precipitated by a recent binge or intercurrent illness.

Signs and symptoms

Patients commonly present with the rapid onset of jaundice, fever, loss of appetite, right-upper-quadrant abdominal pain, muscle wasting and an enlarged, tender liver. In severe disease there may be ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, kidney failure and susceptibility to infection. Blood tests typically show a raised bilirubin and a characteristic pattern of transaminases, with AST exceeding ALT, usually by more than 2:1.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis is largely clinical, based on the history of heavy alcohol use, jaundice and laboratory findings; liver biopsy, when performed, shows steatosis, ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes, neutrophil infiltration, Mallory–Denk bodies and pericellular fibrosis. Severity is graded with scores such as the Maddrey discriminant function and the MELD score, which guide treatment decisions.

Treatment

Complete abstinence from alcohol is the single most important measure and the strongest predictor of survival. Supportive care includes nutritional support, treatment of infection and management of complications. In severe alcoholic hepatitis (Maddrey score ≥ 32) without contraindications, corticosteroids such as prednisolone may improve short-term survival in patients who respond (assessed by the Lille score). Selected patients who do not respond may be considered for early liver transplantation. Pentoxifylline has been used but evidence of benefit is weak.

Prognosis

Mild cases may resolve fully with abstinence. Severe alcoholic hepatitis carries a high short-term mortality — roughly 20–50% within six months — from liver failure, infection and gastrointestinal bleeding, and survivors remain at high risk of progression to cirrhosis.
