Continuity editing

Continuity editing keeps screen direction and positions consistent across cuts.

Continuity editing is the process, in film and video production, of combining shots into a sequence so that the action appears continuous and spatially coherent, despite being filmed at different times and camera positions. It is the dominant editing system of mainstream narrative cinema, sometimes called the "invisible style" because the cuts are designed not to call attention to themselves.

Techniques

Core conventions include the 180-degree rule (keeping the camera on one side of an axis of action so screen direction stays consistent), match on action (cutting mid-movement), shot/reverse shot for dialogue, eyeline matches, and the establishment and maintenance of consistent props, costumes, lighting and screen positions. A continuity supervisor (script supervisor) documents these details during shooting.

History

The system was consolidated in Hollywood between roughly 1909 and the 1920s, in the work of directors such as D. W. Griffith, and refined in the studio era. Alternatives and challenges to continuity editing include Soviet montage theory, jump cuts in the French New Wave, and the deliberate discontinuities of modernist and some contemporary cinema.
