Total internal reflection

Total internal reflection (TIR) is the phenomenon in which waves arriving at the interface between two media are completely reflected back into the first medium instead of being refracted into the second. It occurs when a wave travels from a medium of higher refractive index to one of lower refractive index at an angle of incidence greater than a threshold called the critical angle.

Physics

According to Snell's law, light passing from a denser to a rarer medium bends away from the normal. As the angle of incidence increases, the refracted ray approaches 90°. Beyond the critical angle — given by θc = arcsin(n₂/n₁) — refraction becomes impossible and all the light is reflected internally. An evanescent wave still extends a short distance into the second medium, a fact exploited in sensors and frustrated-TIR devices. For light in glass (n ≈ 1.5) facing air, the critical angle is about 42°; for water it is about 49°.

Applications

Total internal reflection is the operating principle of optical fibres, which carry telecommunications signals as light pulses trapped within a glass core, and of the brilliant cut of diamonds, whose high refractive index traps light to maximise sparkle. It is also used in binocular prisms, fingerprint readers, rain sensors and the reflective faces seen by divers looking up at the underside of a water surface.
