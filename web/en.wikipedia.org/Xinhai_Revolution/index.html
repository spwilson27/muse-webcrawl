The Xinhai Revolution (Chinese: 辛亥革命) was a revolution in China that overthrew the Qing dynasty, the country's last imperial dynasty, and established the Republic of China. Beginning with the Wuchang Uprising on 10 October 1911 and ending with the abdication of the child emperor Puyi on 12 February 1912, it ended more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China. The revolution is named after Xinhai, the designation of the year 1911 in the Chinese sexagenary cycle. Both the Republic of China (Taiwan) and the People's Republic of China regard themselves as heirs of the revolution, and its outbreak, 10 October — the "Double Tenth" — is the national day of the Republic of China.

Background

The Qing dynasty had been weakened by the Taiping and other rebellions, defeat in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), the failure of the Hundred Days' Reform of 1898 and the catastrophe of the Boxer Rebellion (1900). Revolutionary organisations, notably Sun Yat-sen's Revive China Society and later the Tongmenghui (United League), founded in Tokyo in 1905, propagated Sun's Three Principles of the People — nationalism, democracy and people's livelihood — and launched a series of unsuccessful uprisings. Anger at the court's plan to nationalise provincial railways, which provoked the Railway Protection Movement in Sichuan in the summer of 1911, drew Qing troops westward and left the garrison at Wuchang understrength.

Wuchang Uprising

On 9 October 1911 a bomb accidentally exploded at the revolutionaries' Hankou headquarters, exposing the plot. Facing arrest, soldiers of the New Army in Wuchang mutinied the following night, seized the arsenal and the governor-general's residence, and installed a military government under Li Yuanhong. Province after province declared independence from the Qing in the following weeks.

Founding of the Republic

Sun Yat-sen, returning from exile in the United States and Europe, was elected provisional president by delegates at Nanjing and inaugurated on 1 January 1912, proclaiming the Republic of China. Meanwhile the Qing court recalled Yuan Shikai, commander of the powerful Beiyang Army, who negotiated from strength between court and republic. Under an agreement brokered by Yuan, the Xuantong Emperor abdicated on 12 February 1912 under an edict issued by Empress Dowager Longyu, with Articles of Favourable Treatment allowing him to retain his title and residence in the Forbidden City. Sun resigned, and in March 1912 Yuan Shikai became provisional president in Beijing.

Aftermath

The revolution's political settlement soon unravelled: the assassination of parliamentary leader Song Jiaoren in 1913, Yuan's dissolution of parliament and his attempt to found his own dynasty in 1915–16, followed by his death, plunged China into the Warlord Era. Nonetheless the Xinhai Revolution permanently ended the imperial system and introduced republican ideals, the Gregorian calendar's official use in China, and new social norms such as the abolition of foot-binding and the queue hairstyle.
