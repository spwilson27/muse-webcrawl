The Santa Fe Trail was a 19th-century transportation and trade route through the south-western United States connecting Missouri with Santa Fe, New Mexico. Opened in 1821, shortly after Mexico won independence from Spain and lifted restrictions on foreign trade, it became a vital commercial highway between the United States and northern Mexico, and later an invasion route during the Mexican–American War.

Route

The trail began at Franklin, Missouri, and later at Independence and Westport (now Kansas City). Wagons crossed the plains of Kansas, following the Arkansas River. Beyond the Great Bend of the Arkansas, travellers chose between the shorter but waterless Cimarron Cutoff across the Cimarron Desert and the longer Mountain Branch through present-day Colorado, which passed Bent's Fort. The branches rejoined in north-eastern New Mexico before climbing to Santa Fe. The journey typically took eight to ten weeks.

Trade and history

William Becknell's 1821 expedition is credited with opening the trail. American manufactured goods — textiles, tools, hardware — were exchanged in Santa Fe for silver, furs and mules, and the trade grew steadily for six decades, with traders such as Josiah Gregg (author of Commerce of the Prairies, 1844) documenting its operations. In 1846 General Stephen W. Kearny's Army of the West marched down the trail to occupy New Mexico. The trail also carried mail, emigrants and military supplies, with forts such as Fort Larned and Fort Union guarding it.

Decline and legacy

When the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railway reached Santa Fe in 1880, the wagon trade ended almost at once. Ruts carved by thousands of wagons remain visible at many sites. Congress designated the route the Santa Fe National Historic Trail in 1987, administered by the National Park Service across five states.
