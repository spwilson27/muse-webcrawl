Catecholamine

ClassMonoamine neurotransmitters / hormones
MembersDopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine
PrecursorTyrosine
Named forCatechol (1,2-dihydroxybenzene) core

A catecholamine is a monoamine neurotransmitter, an organic compound that has a catechol (benzene with two hydroxyl side groups next to each other) and a side-chain amine. Included among catecholamines are epinephrine (adrenaline), norepinephrine (noradrenaline), and dopamine.

Catecholamines are derived from the amino acid tyrosine, which is derived from dietary sources as well as synthesis from phenylalanine. They are water-soluble and are 50% bound to plasma proteins in the blood.


The three principal catecholamines

Synthesis and release

Catecholamines are produced mainly by the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla and by postganglionic fibres of the sympathetic nervous system, as well as in the brain. Tyrosine is converted to L-DOPA by tyrosine hydroxylase (the rate-limiting step), then to dopamine, norepinephrine, and finally epinephrine. Release into the blood is triggered by physical or emotional stress, producing the "fight or flight" response: increased heart rate, blood pressure, and blood glucose.

Degradation

Catecholamines have a half-life of a few minutes in circulation. They are broken down by the enzymes monoamine oxidase (MAO) and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT), ultimately forming metabolites such as vanillylmandelic acid, which are excreted in urine.

Clinical significance

Excess catecholamine secretion occurs in pheochromocytoma, a tumour of the adrenal medulla. Drugs that alter catecholamine synthesis, release, or reuptake — including many antidepressants, stimulants, and antihypertensives — are central to psychiatry, cardiology, and anaesthesia.
