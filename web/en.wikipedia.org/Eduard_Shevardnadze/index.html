Eduard Shevardnadze

Eduard Amvrosiyevich Shevardnadze (25 January 1928 – 7 July 2014) was a Soviet and Georgian politician and diplomat who served as the Minister of Foreign Affairs of the Soviet Union from 1985 to 1991 and as the head of state, then President, of Georgia from 1992 to 2003. As Mikhail Gorbachev's foreign minister he was one of the architects of perestroika-era diplomacy that helped end the Cold War.

Early career in Georgia

Shevardnadze rose through the Communist Party in the Georgian SSR, serving as minister of internal affairs of Georgia, where he gained a reputation fighting corruption, and from 1972 as First Secretary of the Georgian Communist Party, leading anti-corruption and modest economic-reform campaigns.

Soviet foreign minister

In 1985 Gorbachev appointed him foreign minister, replacing Andrei Gromyko. Shevardnadze helped negotiate the INF Treaty (1987), the withdrawal of Soviet troops from Afghanistan, the reunification of Germany within NATO, and the arms-control and diplomatic settlements that closed the Cold War, working closely with U.S. Secretary of State James Baker. He resigned in December 1990, warning publicly of a coming dictatorship — months before the August 1991 coup attempt against Gorbachev — and briefly returned as foreign minister of the USSR in late 1991 before its dissolution.

Leader of Georgia

Shevardnadze returned to Georgia in 1992 after the overthrow of President Zviad Gamsakhurdia, becoming chairman of the State Council and then, in 1995, President. He ended the civil war but lost effective control of Abkhazia and South Ossetia, survived assassination attempts in 1995 and 1998, and oversaw market reforms and Georgia's westward orientation, including the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline project. His second term was marred by corruption and economic stagnation, and he resigned on 23 November 2003 during the Rose Revolution, after mass protests against a disputed parliamentary election.

Death and legacy

Shevardnadze died in Tbilisi on 7 July 2014. He is remembered internationally as a key figure in ending the Cold War and in Georgia as a leader who restored a measure of stability in the 1990s but failed to build accountable institutions.
