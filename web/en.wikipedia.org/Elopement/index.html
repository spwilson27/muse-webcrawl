Elopement

Elopement is a marriage conducted in a sudden and secretive fashion, usually involving a hurried flight away from home together with one's beloved, without parental consent or public ceremony. In modern usage it also describes small, intimate weddings planned in advance but held without a large guest list.

History

Elopement became notorious in eighteenth-century Britain after the Marriage Act 1753 tightened the rules for marrying in England and Wales. Couples fled across the border to Scotland, where the law remained permissive; the border village of Gretna Green, and its blacksmith's anvil weddings, became famous for marrying runaway couples. In the United States, eloping couples traditionally travelled to jurisdictions with short waiting periods, such as Las Vegas, Nevada.

Motivations

Historically, elopement circumvented parental opposition, differences of class, religion or caste, dowry disputes, or legal obstacles. In some societies, elopement by consenting couples is distinguished in law from abduction, though the two have sometimes been conflated, with serious consequences for women in communities where family honour is policed.

Modern practice

Contemporary elopements are often a deliberate lifestyle choice: couples cite the cost of large weddings, stress, or a preference for privacy and travel. A commercial "elopement" industry offers photography and small-ceremony packages in scenic locations.
