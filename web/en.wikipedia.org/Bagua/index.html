Bagua

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The bagua (Chinese: 八卦; pinyin: bāguà; lit. "eight trigrams") is a fundamental philosophical concept in ancient China. It is a set of eight symbols, each consisting of three lines, each line either broken (yin) or unbroken (yang), representing the fundamental principles of reality and used in Taoist cosmology, the I Ching (Yijing), feng shui, and Chinese martial arts.

The eight trigrams

The eight trigrams are: ☰ Qián (Heaven), ☱ Duì (Lake), ☲ Lí (Fire), ☳ Zhèn (Thunder), ☴ Xùn (Wind), ☵ Kǎn (Water), ☶ Gèn (Mountain), and ☷ Kūn (Earth). Each trigram is associated with a family role, a natural phenomenon, a direction, and other correspondences. Combining two trigrams produces the 64 hexagrams of the I Ching, each with its own name and divinatory text.

Arrangements

Two classic arrangements of the trigrams exist. The Earlier Heaven (Fuxi) arrangement places opposite trigrams in balanced pairs around the circle and is associated with the ideal cosmic order; the Later Heaven (King Wen) arrangement reflects the dynamic cycle of the seasons and is the arrangement most used in feng shui, where a bagua map is overlaid on a building plan to analyse the flow of qi. Bagua mirrors — octagonal mirrors bearing the trigrams — are hung above doors to deflect harmful influences. The concept also gives its name to the internal martial art baguazhang, whose movements trace the circle of the trigrams.
