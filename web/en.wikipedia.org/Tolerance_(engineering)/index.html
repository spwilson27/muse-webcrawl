Tolerance (engineering)

Engineering tolerance specifies the permissible variation in a dimension or measured value.

Engineering tolerance is the permissible limit or limits of variation in:

  1. a physical dimension;
  2. a measured value or physical property of a material, manufactured object, system, or service;
  3. other measured values (such as temperature, humidity, etc.); or
  4. in engineering and safety, a physical distance or space (tolerance), as in a truck, train or boat under a bridge.

Dimensions, properties, or conditions may in some cases have an effect on the performance of the device in a favourable or an adverse manner. A salient point is that a tolerance is not the same as an allowance, which is a planned deviation between corresponding parts.

Specifying tolerances

Tolerances are typically shown on engineering drawings as a nominal dimension plus or minus an allowed deviation (for example 25.00 ± 0.05 mm), as explicit upper and lower limits, or through standardised fit designations such as the ISO system of limits and fits, where letters denote the tolerance position and numbers the grade (e.g. H7/g6 for a shaft in a hole). Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T) extends the idea to form, orientation, location and run-out of features.

Why tolerances matter

No manufacturing process produces parts of exactly the intended size, so tolerances define how much variation is acceptable while preserving function, interchangeability and assembly. Tighter tolerances generally raise production cost sharply, so engineers balance performance requirements against manufacturability. When several toleranced parts are assembled, their individual variations accumulate—a "tolerance stack-up"—which must be analysed to ensure the assembly still works.
