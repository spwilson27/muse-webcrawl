Extra-parliamentary opposition

An extra-parliamentary opposition is political opposition that organises outside the elected legislature rather than through parties represented within it. The term is particularly associated with the Außerparlamentarische Opposition (APO) of West Germany in the late 1960s, but it is used generally for movements that pursue change through protest, strikes, civil disobedience and alternative institutions rather than parliamentary politics.

The West German APO

The APO emerged after 1966, when the Social Democratic Party entered a grand coalition with the Christian Democrats, leaving only the small Free Democrats as parliamentary opposition — a situation many students and intellectuals saw as a failure of democracy. The Socialist German Student Union (SDS) became the movement's core, campaigning against the Vietnam War, the proposed Emergency Acts (Notstandsgesetze), and authoritarian structures in universities. Its leading figures included Rudi Dutschke. The shooting of the student Benno Ohnesorg by a police officer during a demonstration in June 1967, and the attempted assassination of Dutschke in 1968, radicalised the movement.

Legacy

The APO fragmented by 1969, but its veterans undertook the "long march through the institutions", entering teaching, media and politics, and the movement helped lay the groundwork for the Green Party founded in 1980. A radical fringe turned to armed struggle in the Red Army Faction, while the mainstream current reshaped West German political culture toward greater openness.

General use

More broadly, historians use the term for opposition in systems where legislatures are weak or unrepresentative — from 19th-century movements such as Chartism in Britain to dissident networks under authoritarian regimes.
