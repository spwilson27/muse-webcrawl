Plant pathology studies the organisms and conditions that cause disease in plants.

Plant pathology (also phytopathology) is the scientific study of diseases in plants caused by pathogens and environmental conditions. It covers the identification of causal agents, disease cycles, plant–pathogen interactions, epidemiology, and methods of managing plant disease — a field of major economic importance, since pathogens destroy an estimated 10–16 percent of global crop production each year.

Causal agents

Most plant diseases are caused by fungi, which produce blights, rusts, smuts, mildews and rots; famous examples include Phytophthora infestans (an oomycete) that caused the Irish potato famine, and wheat stem rust Puccinia graminis. Bacteria cause wilts, spots and galls — crown gall results from Agrobacterium tumefaciens. Viruses and viroids, spread by insect vectors or grafting, cause mosaics and stunting. Parasitic nematodes, phytoplasmas and even parasitic plants such as dodder and mistletoe are also studied, as are abiotic disorders from nutrient deficiency, drought, salinity or pollution.

The disease triangle

Disease develops only when three factors coincide: a susceptible host, a virulent pathogen, and a favourable environment (such as leaf wetness and warm temperatures). This "disease triangle" frames management strategies — resistant varieties, pathogen exclusion and sanitation, and environmental modification. Koch's postulates, adapted for plant pathogens, remain the classic standard for proving causation.

Management

Control follows integrated pest management: quarantine and certification of clean seed, crop rotation, resistant cultivars bred or engineered with resistance genes, biological control organisms, and chemical fungicides and bactericides used judiciously to limit resistance. Modern plant pathology increasingly uses genomics and remote sensing to forecast epidemics and breed durable resistance.

History

Anton de Bary, working in the 1860s, demonstrated that fungi cause plant diseases and founded the modern discipline; Norman Borlaug's rust-resistant wheats later showed its power to avert famine.
