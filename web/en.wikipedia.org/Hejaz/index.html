Hejaz

The Hejaz (Arabic: الْحِجَاز, romanized: al-&Hdot;ijāz, lit. 'the barrier') is a region that includes the majority of the west coast of Saudi Arabia, covering the cities of Mecca, Medina, Jeddah, Tabuk, Yanbu, Taif and Baljurashi. It is thus known as the "Western Province" of Saudi Arabia. It is separated from the rest of the country by the Hejaz Mountains, running parallel to the Red Sea coast.

History

The Hejaz is the birthplace of Islam: Mecca is the birthplace of the Prophet Muhammad and the site of the Kaaba, and Medina is where he established the first Muslim community and where he is buried. The region was ruled successively by the Rashidun, Umayyad, Abbasid, Fatimid, Mamluk, and Ottoman powers, usually through the Sharifs of Mecca.

During the First World War, Sharif Hussein bin Ali launched the Arab Revolt (1916) against the Ottomans, assisted by T. E. Lawrence ("Lawrence of Arabia"). The Hashemite Kingdom of Hejaz was proclaimed, but in 1924–1925 Ibn Saud of Nejd conquered Mecca and Medina, uniting the Hejaz with his other territories to form the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932.

Geography and significance

The region consists of a narrow coastal plain (the Tihamah), the rugged Hejaz mountain range, and plateaus to the east. Jeddah, its largest port, is the traditional gateway for pilgrims travelling to Mecca for the Hajj and Umrah. The Hejaz Railway, built by the Ottomans between Damascus and Medina (1900–1908), was famously attacked during the Arab Revolt.
