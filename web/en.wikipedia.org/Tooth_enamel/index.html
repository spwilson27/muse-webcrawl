Tooth enamel is one of the four major tissues that make up the tooth in humans and many other animals, including some species of fish. It makes up the normally visible part of the tooth, covering the crown that is exposed above the gum. Enamel is the hardest substance in the human body and contains the highest percentage of minerals (96%), with water and organic material composing the rest. The primary mineral is hydroxyapatite, a crystalline calcium phosphate.

Structure

Enamel is organised into microscopic rods or prisms, each about 4–8 micrometres in diameter, packed tightly together and extending from the dentine–enamel junction to the tooth surface. The arrangement of the prisms — including their decussation in inner enamel, which produces Hunter–Schreger bands — gives enamel great hardness while limiting brittleness. Unlike dentine and bone, enamel contains no living cells after formation and cannot regenerate.

Development

Enamel is formed by cells called ameloblasts during the process of amelogenesis, before the tooth erupts into the mouth. The ameloblasts lay down enamel matrix proteins — chiefly amelogenin, enamelin and ameloblastin — which are then largely removed as the mineral crystallites grow during maturation. Once formation is complete, the ameloblast layer is lost, so the mature tissue is acellular.

Damage and repair

Because it has no living cells, enamel cannot repair itself once destroyed; damage from tooth decay, erosion by dietary and gastric acids, abrasion and attrition is permanent, though early demineralisation can be reversed by remineralisation from saliva, a process promoted by fluoride. Fluoride incorporated into the mineral forms fluorapatite, which is more acid-resistant. Loss of enamel exposes the underlying dentine and may cause sensitivity, discoloration and eventual pulp involvement. Developmental defects, including enamel hypoplasia and the hereditary condition amelogenesis imperfecta, result from disturbances in enamel formation.
