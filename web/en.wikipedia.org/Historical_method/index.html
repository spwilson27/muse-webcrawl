The historical method is the body of techniques by which historians collect, evaluate and interpret evidence about the past in order to construct reliable accounts. Because the past cannot be observed directly or re-run as an experiment, history depends on traces — documents, artefacts, inscriptions, oral traditions — whose credibility must be tested before they are used.

Source criticism

Critics distinguish external criticism, which asks whether a source is authentic (who produced it, when, is the material and handwriting right, could it be forged?), from internal criticism, which asks whether its content is credible: Was the author in a position to know? Biased? Writing for what audience and purpose? The Lorenzo Valla exposure of the Donation of Constantine (1440) is a famous early triumph of the method.

Corroboration and synthesis

Independent sources that agree strengthen a claim; where they conflict, historians weigh proximity, competence and motive. Evidence is then synthesised into narrative and argument, with attention to chronology, causation and context. Auxiliary disciplines — palaeography, diplomatics, numismatics, archaeology, linguistics and now genetics and climate science — supply specialised tests.

Professionalisation

The method was codified in the 19th century by Leopold von Ranke's seminar practice ("wie es eigentlich gewesen" — as it actually was) and manuals such as Langlois and Seignobos's Introduction to the Study of History (1898). Later schools — Marxist, Annales, feminist, postcolonial and postmodern historians — have contested what counts as evidence and whose past gets written, while retaining criticism of sources as common ground.
