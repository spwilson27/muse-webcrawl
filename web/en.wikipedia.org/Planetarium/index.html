Planetarium

A planetarium theatre with a hemispherical projection dome

A planetarium is a theatre built primarily for presenting educational and entertaining shows about astronomy and the night sky, or for training in celestial navigation. A dominant feature of most planetariums is the large dome-shaped projection screen onto which scenes of stars, planets, and other celestial objects can be made to appear and move realistically to simulate the complex motions of the heavens. The celestial scenes can be created using a wide variety of technologies, from precision opto-mechanical star projectors to full-dome digital video systems.

History

The concept descends from orreries — mechanical models of the Solar System — and from devices such as the Gottorf globe of the seventeenth century, inside which viewers could sit among painted stars. The modern planetarium dates from the 1920s, when the Zeiss company, working with Walther Bauersfeld, developed an optical star projector; the first modern planetarium opened at the Deutsches Museum in Munich in 1923 (with public demonstrations beginning in 1923–25). The first planetarium in the United States was the Adler Planetarium in Chicago, opened in 1930.

Modern planetariums

Today there are thousands of planetariums worldwide, many attached to science museums and universities. Digital ("fulldome") systems can display astronomical data from catalogues and simulations, allowing presenters to fly audiences through the Solar System and beyond, and many venues also host films, laser shows and lectures. Portable inflatable planetariums bring the experience to schools. Notable examples include the Hayden Planetarium in New York, the Griffith Observatory planetarium in Los Angeles, and large domes in Beijing, Nagoya and Moscow.
