Gender biases in medical diagnosis

Gender biases in medical diagnosis are systematic differences in how the symptoms of men and women are interpreted, investigated and treated, which can lead to misdiagnosis, delayed diagnosis or unequal care. Research in medical sociology and health services has documented such biases across many conditions, including heart disease, pain, autoimmune disorders and mental health.

Documented patterns

Cardiovascular disease. Women's heart attacks have historically been more likely to be missed, because the "typical" symptom picture was derived mainly from male patients; women's symptoms were labelled "atypical", contributing to delays in treatment and worse outcomes.

Pain. Studies and reviews report that women's pain is more likely to be attributed to psychological causes, and that women wait longer for analgesia in emergency departments; the phrase "Yentl syndrome", coined by cardiologist Bernadine Healy in 1991, describes the expectation that women must present like men to receive equal treatment.

Diagnostic delay. Conditions that predominantly affect women, such as endometriosis, commonly involve delays of several years before diagnosis, while men face under-recognition of conditions stereotyped as female, such as osteoporosis, eating disorders and depression.

Causes and responses

Proposed causes include the historical exclusion of women from clinical trials, male-normative medical training materials, time pressure in consultations, and clinicians' implicit stereotypes. Responses include sex- and gender-sensitive medicine, requirements to include women in research cohorts, reporting results disaggregated by sex, and bias-awareness training.
