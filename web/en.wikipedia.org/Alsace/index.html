Alsace

Alsace (French: Alsace; German: Elsass; Alsatian: 's Elsàss) is a cultural and historical region in northeastern France, on the west bank of the upper Rhine between the Vosges mountains and the river, bordering Germany and Switzerland. Since 2021 it has been administered as the European Collectivity of Alsace, within the Grand Est region, comprising the former departments of Bas-Rhin and Haut-Rhin. Its largest city and traditional capital is Strasbourg, seat of the European Parliament; other major towns include Colmar and Mulhouse. Alsace is famous for its white wines, half-timbered villages, and a bilingual heritage shaped by centuries of alternation between French and German rule.

History

Settled by Alemannic tribes in late antiquity, Alsace formed part of the Frankish realm, the Holy Roman Empire, and the Habsburg sphere before France annexed most of it in the 17th century (Treaty of Westphalia, 1648; Strasbourg was seized in 1681). Alsatian towns such as those of the Décapole enjoyed imperial immediacy and prospered from Rhine trade and wine.

After the Franco-Prussian War, Germany annexed Alsace (with northern Lorraine) as the imperial territory of Alsace-Lorraine in 1871. France recovered it after the First World War; Germany annexed it again de facto in 1940, conscripting some 130,000 Alsatians into the Wehrmacht (the Malgré-nous), and it returned to France in 1945. This history left Alsace with a distinctive local legal regime (droit local) preserved from German-era law — including the Napoleonic Concordat still in force for churches and distinct public holidays.

Culture and economy

Alsatian identity is reflected in its dialect, cuisine (tarte flambée/flammekueche, choucroute, baeckeoffe), and architecture. The region's economy combines industry (Mulhouse's textile and automotive heritage), agriculture, tourism along the Alsace wine route, and European institutions in Strasbourg. Since 2016 Alsace has been part of the merged Grand Est region, a change many Alsatians resisted; in 2021 the two departments merged into a single European Collectivity of Alsace with some cross-border competencies.
