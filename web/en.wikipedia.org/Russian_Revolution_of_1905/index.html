The Russian Revolution of 1905, also known as the First Russian Revolution, was a wave of mass political and social unrest that spread through vast areas of the Russian Empire. It included worker strikes, peasant unrest, and military mutinies, and it forced Tsar Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, which promised civil liberties and the creation of an elected legislative assembly, the State Duma.

Background

At the turn of the twentieth century the Russian Empire combined rapid industrialisation with an autocratic political system. Workers laboured long hours for low wages, peasants remained burdened by redemption payments and land hunger, and national minorities chafed under Russification. Russia's humiliating defeats in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905) further undermined the prestige of the tsarist regime.

Bloody Sunday

On 22 January 1905 (9 January Old Style), a peaceful procession of workers led by the priest Georgy Gapon marched to the Winter Palace in Saint Petersburg to present a petition to the Tsar. Troops opened fire on the demonstrators, killing and wounding hundreds. "Bloody Sunday" shattered popular faith in the Tsar and ignited strikes and uprisings across the empire.

Course of the revolution

Strikes spread through industry and the railways; in June 1905 the crew of the battleship Potemkin mutinied at Odessa. Soviets (councils) of workers' deputies appeared, most famously in Saint Petersburg, where Leon Trotsky became a leading figure. A general strike in October 1905 paralysed the country and compelled Nicholas II to issue the October Manifesto, promising freedom of speech, assembly and association, and a Duma without whose approval no law would take effect.

An armed uprising in Moscow in December 1905 was crushed by troops. Over the following two years the government of Pyotr Stolypin combined repression with agrarian reform, and the revolutionary movement subsided. The Fundamental Laws of 1906 reasserted the Tsar's autocratic powers, and electoral changes in June 1907 sharply reduced representation of workers and peasants in the Duma.

Significance

Although the revolution failed to overthrow the monarchy, it created Russia's first parliament, legalised trade unions and political parties, and served — in Lenin's phrase — as a "dress rehearsal" for the revolutions of 1917.
