Hilary Putnam

Hilary Whitehall Putnam (31 July 1926 – 13 March 2016) was an American philosopher, mathematician, and computer scientist, and a major figure in analytic philosophy in the second half of the twentieth century. He made significant contributions to philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, philosophy of mathematics, and philosophy of science. Outside philosophy, Putnam contributed to mathematics and computer science as well, proving (with Martin Davis) results on the decidability of Diophantine equations that fed into the solution of Hilbert's tenth problem.

Philosophy of mind

In the 1960s Putnam argued for multiple realizability: the same mental state can be realized by different physical states (in humans, animals, or machines), which told against identifying mental states with particular brain states. He proposed machine-state functionalism, comparing minds to Turing machines — then, in later work, became one of functionalism's sharpest critics.

Philosophy of language: Twin Earth

Putnam's 1975 paper "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" introduced the Twin Earth thought experiment: imagine a planet where the liquid called "water" is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ. Since speakers there are internally identical to Earth speakers yet refer to something different, Putnam concluded that "meanings just ain't in the head" — reference is fixed partly by the environment and by a social division of linguistic labor. This doctrine, semantic externalism, transformed philosophy of language.

Realism and its revisions

Putnam successively defended scientific realism, then "internal realism" (in Reason, Truth, and History, 1981), which introduced the famous "brain in a vat" argument against radical skepticism, and later a pragmatic, "second naïveté" realism influenced by William James and Wittgenstein. He was known for candidly revising his own positions. With Martha Nussbaum he also wrote on ethics and literature, and in his later years on Jewish philosophy and religion.

Mathematics and computer science

The Davis–Putnam algorithm (1960) improved automated theorem proving; its refinement, DPLL, underlies modern SAT solvers. Putnam's work with Julia Robinson and Martin Davis on exponential Diophantine equations (1961) was a key step toward Yuri Matiyasevich's 1970 completion of the negative solution to Hilbert's tenth problem.
