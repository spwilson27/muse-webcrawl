Claude Steele

Claude Mason Steele (born January 1, 1946) is an American social psychologist and the Lucie Stern Professor in the Social Sciences at Stanford University. He is best known for his work on stereotype threat and its application to minority student academic performance, and for his concept of "wise interventions" and self-affirmation theory.

Early life and education

Steele was born in Chicago, Illinois. He grew up during the civil rights movement, an experience he has credited with shaping his interest in how identity interacts with social situations. He earned his bachelor's degree in psychology from Hiram College in 1967, and his PhD in social psychology from Ohio State University in 1971, studying under Thomas Ostrom.

Career

Steele taught at the University of Utah, the University of Washington, and the University of Michigan, where he was a professor from 1987 to 1997. He joined Stanford in 1997, later serving as dean of the Stanford Graduate School of Education (2011–2014). He has also served as provost of Columbia University and executive vice chancellor and provost of the University of California, Berkeley.

Research

In a landmark 1995 paper with Joshua Aronson, Steele introduced stereotype threat: the risk of confirming a negative stereotype about one's group, which can depress performance in domains such as academics and athletics. His 2010 book Whistling Vivaldi: How Stereotypes Affect Us and What We Can Do summarised decades of research on the phenomenon and on interventions, including self-affirmation exercises, that reduce its effects. His earlier work studied alcohol's role in disinhibition and self-attention.

Honours

Steele is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. His twin brother is the writer and linguist Shelby Steele.
