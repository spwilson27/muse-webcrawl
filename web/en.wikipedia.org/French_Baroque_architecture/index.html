French Baroque architecture

French Baroque architecture, often called French classicism, was the architectural style that developed in France during the reigns of Louis XIII (1610–1643), Louis XIV (1643–1715), and Louis XV (1715–1774). It adapted the Italian Baroque to French taste, combining grandeur and symmetry with a classical restraint that avoided the dramatic exuberance of Roman buildings. The style expressed the power and glory of the absolute monarchy and culminated in the Palace of Versailles.

Development

Under Louis XIII and his minister Cardinal Richelieu, architects such as Jacques Lemercier began applying classical orders and regular planning to palaces and churches. The reign of Louis XIV brought the style to maturity: Louis Le Vau, Charles Le Brun, and Jules Hardouin-Mansart created the enlarged Versailles (from 1661), the Louvre Colonnade by Claude Perrault (1667–1670), and the Dôme des Invalides in Paris (1706). François Mansart gave his name to the mansard roof; Salomon de Brosse built the Luxembourg Palace for Marie de' Medici. Gardens by André Le Nôtre, with their geometric parterres and vast axial vistas, were integral to the architectural conception.

Characteristics

French Baroque buildings feature symmetrical façades organized by giant classical orders, rusticated ground floors, mansard roofs, domes, and carefully graded sequences of courtyards. Interiors, notably the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, used painting, sculpture, and gilding to celebrate the monarch. In the 18th century the style softened into the lighter Rococo for interiors, while public architecture under Louis XV (such as Ange-Jacques Gabriel's Place de la Concorde and Petit Trianon) moved toward neoclassicism.

Influence

Versailles became the model for palaces across Europe, from Schönbrunn in Vienna to Peterhof in Russia, and French classical planning influenced city design for centuries.
