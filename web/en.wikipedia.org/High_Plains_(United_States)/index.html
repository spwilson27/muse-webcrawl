High Plains

The High Plains are a subregion of the Great Plains of North America, mainly in the Western United States but also partly in the Midwest. The region is characterized by high elevation — rising from about 2,000 feet (600 m) in the east to more than 5,000 feet (1,500 m) near the Rocky Mountains — and a semi-arid climate with shortgrass prairie. It stretches from the Mexican border in western Texas north through New Mexico, Oklahoma, Kansas, Colorado, Nebraska, Wyoming, and into South Dakota and Montana.

Geography and geology

The High Plains are a high, relatively flat plateau surface formed by sediments eroded from the Rocky Mountains and spread eastward, capped in many areas by the Ogallala Formation. The surface is cut by the Canadian, Cimarron, Arkansas, and Platte river valleys. In Nebraska the Sandhills — the largest sand dune formation in the Western Hemisphere, stabilized by grass — lie at the region's northeastern margin, while the Llano Estacado forms a strikingly level tableland in western Texas and eastern New Mexico.

Water: the Ogallala Aquifer

Beneath much of the region lies the Ogallala Aquifer, one of the world's largest, which since the mid-twentieth century has powered extensive center-pivot irrigation of corn, wheat, cotton, and sorghum. Pumping greatly exceeds recharge in the southern plains, and water levels have fallen by more than 100 feet (30 m) in parts of Texas, Kansas, and Oklahoma, prompting conservation programs and shifts to dryland farming and less water-intensive crops.

History

The plains were home to nomadic Plains Indian peoples following the bison, including the Comanche, Cheyenne, Arapaho, and Kiowa. Nineteenth-century settlement brought cattle ranching on the open range, then homesteading; overplowing of marginal land contributed to the Dust Bowl of the 1930s, centered on the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles. Oil and natural gas production, wind farms, and cattle feedlots are major modern industries. Population density remains among the lowest in the United States, and many counties have declined in population for decades.
