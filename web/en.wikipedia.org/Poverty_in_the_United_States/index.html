Poverty in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Visible homelessness is one face of poverty in the United States.

Poverty in the United States is measured primarily by the official poverty measure of the US Census Bureau, based on income thresholds originating in the early 1960s. In recent years the official poverty rate has hovered around 11–12% of the population — roughly 37–38 million people — while the Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM), which counts tax credits and in-kind benefits as resources and subtracts necessary expenses such as medical and work costs, gave a rate of about 12.4% in 2022. The United States has markedly higher poverty than most other wealthy democracies, especially child poverty.

Threshold (2023, family of four): about $30,000

Measured by: US Census Bureau (official and supplemental measures)

Measurement

The official thresholds were developed in 1963–64 by Mollie Orshansky of the Social Security Administration, who took the cost of the Agriculture Department's economy food plan and multiplied it by three, reflecting the share of food in household budgets at the time. Thresholds are updated only for inflation and vary by family size and composition, but not by region. Critics note the formula ignores modern costs such as housing, childcare, and health care. The SPM, published since 2011, was designed to address many of these shortcomings and showed, for example, that pandemic-era relief measures — stimulus payments and the expanded Child Tax Credit — cut measured child poverty dramatically in 2021, to a record low of 5.2%, before it more than doubled in 2022 when those measures expired.

Distribution and programmes

Poverty rates are highest among children, single-parent households, and Black, Hispanic, and Native American populations, and vary considerably by state, with the highest rates concentrated in the South and in parts of Appalachia and the Southwest. Major federal anti-poverty programmes include the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP, "food stamps"), the Earned Income Tax Credit, Medicaid, Supplemental Security Income, housing assistance, and Temporary Assistance for Needy Families. The "War on Poverty" declared by President Lyndon B. Johnson in 1964 launched many of the foundational programmes, and the official poverty rate fell from about 19% in 1964 to a low near 11% in the early 1970s, fluctuating with the business cycle since.
