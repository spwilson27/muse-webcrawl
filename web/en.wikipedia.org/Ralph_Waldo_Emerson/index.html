Ralph Waldo Emerson (May 25, 1803 – April 27, 1882) was an American essayist, lecturer, philosopher, abolitionist, and poet who led the transcendentalist movement of the mid-19th century. He was seen as a champion of individualism and a prescient critic of the countervailing pressures of society, and his ideology was disseminated through dozens of published essays and more than 1,500 public lectures across the United States.

Life

Emerson was born in Boston, Massachusetts, the son of a Unitarian minister. He graduated from Harvard College and briefly served as a Unitarian minister himself, resigning in 1832 over doctrinal differences. A journey to Europe in 1832–33, where he met Thomas Carlyle, John Stuart Mill and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, shaped his thought.

Works

His anonymously published pamphlet Nature (1836) laid out the foundations of transcendentalism, arguing for a direct, intuitive relationship between the soul and nature. His Phi Beta Kappa address "The American Scholar" (1837) was called "America's intellectual Declaration of Independence" by Oliver Wendell Holmes. The essays "Self-Reliance," "The Over-Soul," "Circles" and "Experience," collected in Essays: First Series (1841) and Second Series (1844), remain central texts of American literature. "Self-Reliance" contains his famous injunction: "Trust thyself" and the maxim that "a foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds."

Views and influence

Emerson was a mentor and friend of Henry David Thoreau and a central figure in the intellectual life of Concord, Massachusetts. From the 1850s he spoke increasingly against slavery. His philosophy influenced American literature, pragmatic philosophy, and writers from Walt Whitman to Friedrich Nietzsche.
