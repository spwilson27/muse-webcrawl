The Peace of Lodi was a peace agreement signed on 9 April 1454 at Lodi in Lombardy between the Duchy of Milan, the Republic of Venice and the Republic of Florence, ending the Wars in Lombardy that had occupied the Italian states for a generation. It is celebrated as one of the earliest examples of a deliberate balance of power system in European diplomacy.

Background

Decades of war between Milan and Venice, with Florence and Naples variously involved, had exhausted the belligerents. After the death of the last Visconti duke of Milan in 1447 and the seizure of power by the condottiere Francesco Sforza in 1450, Florence under Cosimo de' Medici allied with Milan, shifting the balance and pushing Venice toward peace.

Terms and the Italic League

The treaty fixed the boundary between Milan and Venice roughly along the Adda River: Milan recovered most of its territory, while Venice retained gains in the east including Brescia, Bergamo and Crema. Within a year the settlement was extended into the Italic League (1455), a mutual-defence pact joining Milan, Venice, Florence, the Papal States and the Kingdom of Naples — the five great powers of the peninsula — guaranteeing the territorial status quo for 25 years.

Legacy

The system established at Lodi gave Italy some four decades of relative peace, during which the Italian Renaissance flourished. Renaissance diplomacy — resident ambassadors, alliances calibrated to equilibrium — developed in this environment. The balance collapsed after the death of Lorenzo de' Medici (1492) and the French invasion of 1494, which opened the Italian Wars.
