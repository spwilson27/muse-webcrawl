Boycott

A boycott is an act of nonviolent, voluntary abstention from a product, person, organisation or country as an expression of protest, usually for moral, social, political or environmental reasons. The word entered the English language during the Irish "Land War" of 1880, named after Captain Charles Boycott, an English land agent who was ostracised by his local community in County Mayo.

History

In 1880, following guidance from Charles Stewart Parnell, tenants and labourers refused to work for or trade with Captain Boycott, who had refused to lower rents; the tactic forced him to import labourers under military protection at great expense and made his name a universal byword. Earlier analogues include non-importation agreements in colonial America and abolitionist boycotts of slave-produced goods.

Notable boycotts

Major boycotts include the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955–56, a cornerstone of the U.S. civil rights movement; the international boycott and divestment campaign against apartheid South Africa; consumer boycotts over labour practices, animal testing and environmental damage; and national boycotts of sporting events such as the 1980 and 1984 Olympic Games. A secondary boycott targets third parties that deal with the primary target.

Legality and effectiveness

Boycotts are generally protected as free expression in the United States, though certain organised secondary boycotts by labour unions are restricted, and some jurisdictions have enacted anti-boycott laws aimed at particular movements. Their effectiveness depends on participation, publicity and the target's economic exposure.
