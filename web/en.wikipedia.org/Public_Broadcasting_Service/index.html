The Public Broadcasting Service (PBS) is an American non-profit public broadcaster and television programme distributor. Founded in 1969 as the successor to National Educational Television, it is a membership organisation of more than 330 public television stations across the United States, which collectively own the network. PBS distributes programming produced by member stations and independent producers but, unlike commercial networks, does not itself produce most of its schedule or own its stations.

Programming

PBS is known for educational children's programmes such as Sesame Street (from 1969), Mister Rogers' Neighborhood, and Reading Rainbow; science series including Nova and Nature; the documentary strands Frontline, American Experience, and POV; Ken Burns's landmark documentary series such as The Civil War (1990); the flagship PBS News Hour; and drama anthologies such as Masterpiece (formerly Masterpiece Theatre), which introduced many British serials — including Downton Abbey — to American audiences.

Funding and structure

PBS and its stations are funded by a mixture of viewer contributions ("viewers like you"), corporate and foundation underwriting, station dues, and federal support channelled through the Corporation for Public Broadcasting (CPB), created by the Public Broadcasting Act of 1967. Because stations are locally owned — by universities, state authorities, school boards, and community non-profits — schedules vary, and stations choose which PBS programmes to carry. The decentralised model, and periodic political debates over federal funding for public media, have been recurring themes in the network's history.
