Glyptics


A carved gemstone (cameo).

Glyptics, or glyptic art, is the art of carving or engraving small designs on gemstones, hardstones, glass, ivory or similar materials. The word comes from the Greek glyphein, "to carve".

Forms

The two main techniques are intaglio, in which the design is cut into the surface — used for seals, since the incised design produces a raised impression in wax or clay — and cameo, in which the design is carved in relief, often exploiting layered stones such as sardonyx so that the figure appears in a different colour from the background.

History

Gem carving is among the oldest decorative arts, practised in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Minoan Crete and Mycenaean Greece, where seal stones served administrative and magical purposes. Greek and Roman engravers brought the art to a high point; famous Roman works include the Gemma Augustea and the great cameo portraits of emperors. The art revived in the Renaissance, when ancient gems were eagerly collected and copied, and again in the Neoclassical period, when craftsmen such as the Pichler family produced intaglios for aristocratic collectors.

Materials and techniques

Carvers traditionally used quartz-family stones — carnelian, agate, amethyst, jasper, onyx — as well as garnet, emerald and sapphire, working with bow-driven drills, abrasive powders and wheels. Hardstone carving on a larger scale, such as the works produced by the Fabergé workshops, is a related craft.
