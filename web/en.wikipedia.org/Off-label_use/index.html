Off-label use

Off-label use is the use of pharmaceutical drugs for an unapproved indication or in an unapproved age group, dosage, or route of administration. Both prescription drugs and over-the-counter drugs (OTCs) can be used in off-label ways, although studies suggest that most off-label use lacks strong scientific support.

Legality and regulation

In the United States, physicians may legally prescribe approved drugs for any indication they judge medically appropriate, a practice protected as the practice of medicine; the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulates manufacturers, not prescribers. Pharmaceutical companies, however, are generally prohibited from marketing drugs for off-label uses, and violations have led to major settlements, including cases involving gabapentin (Neurontin) and olanzapine (Zyprexa).

Prevalence and examples

Off-label prescribing is common, particularly in paediatrics, oncology, psychiatry, and rare diseases where approved options are few. Well-known examples include aspirin used for cardiovascular prevention before formal approval in that role, minoxidil (originally an antihypertensive) for hair loss, and numerous chemotherapy regimens used across cancer types. Estimates have suggested that a substantial share — in some studies around a fifth — of prescriptions in outpatient practice are off-label.

Ethics and evidence

Supporters argue off-label use enables innovation and treatment where trials lag behind practice, especially for rare conditions. Critics note the lack of rigorous efficacy and safety evidence, higher rates of adverse events in unsupported uses, and the financial incentives created by promotion. Insurers may decline to reimburse off-label prescriptions that lack compendia support.
