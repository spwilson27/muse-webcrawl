In theoretical physics, especially quantum field theory, regularization is a method of modifying divergent mathematical expressions — typically integrals that evaluate to infinity — so that they become temporarily finite and manipulable. The infinities are parameterised by one or more regulator parameters, and the physical result is recovered by a subsequent procedure called renormalization, in which the dependence on the regulator is absorbed into a finite number of physical parameters.

Background

When computing scattering amplitudes in quantum field theory using Feynman diagrams, diagrams containing closed loops lead to integrals over all possible momenta of virtual particles. Because the momentum can be arbitrarily large (the ultraviolet regime), many of these integrals diverge. Regularization introduces a systematic way of suppressing the high-momentum contributions so that the integrals yield finite, though regulator-dependent, expressions.

Methods

Cutoff regularization

The simplest approach imposes a maximum momentum Λ (an ultraviolet cutoff) beyond which contributions are discarded. It is intuitive but can break symmetries such as gauge invariance and Lorentz invariance.

Dimensional regularization

Developed by Gerard 't Hooft and Martinus Veltman in 1972, dimensional regularization analytically continues spacetime to d = 4 − ε dimensions, where loop integrals converge. Divergences appear as poles in ε, which are then subtracted in renormalization. Because it preserves gauge invariance, it became the standard tool for calculations in quantum chromodynamics and the electroweak theory.

Pauli–Villars regularization

This method adds fictitious heavy "regulator" particles with wrong-sign statistics whose contributions cancel the divergences; the regulator masses are sent to infinity at the end of the calculation.

Lattice regularization

In lattice gauge theory, spacetime is replaced by a discrete grid; the lattice spacing acts as a natural ultraviolet cutoff, allowing non-perturbative numerical computation.

Role in renormalization

Regularization by itself does not remove infinities; it makes them explicit and controllable. Renormalization then redefines the bare mass, charge and field normalisation appearing in the Lagrangian so that observable predictions are finite. The way physical quantities change with the energy scale is described by the renormalization group.
