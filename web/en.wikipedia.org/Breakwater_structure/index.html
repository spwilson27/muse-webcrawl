Breakwater (structure)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A rubble-mound breakwater

A breakwater is a coastal structure built in the sea to protect a harbour, anchorage or shoreline from the effects of waves and weather. Breakwaters intercept incoming wave energy, dissipating or reflecting it so that sheltered, calm water is created behind them, allowing ships to moor safely and reducing coastal erosion. They are commonly built from rock, concrete armour units, or caissons, and may be attached to the shore or stand offshore as detached structures.

Types

Rubble-mound breakwaters consist of a core of graded stone covered by large rocks or interlocking concrete armour units such as tetrapods, which dissipate wave energy in the voids between them. Vertical (caisson) breakwaters are made of large concrete boxes sunk onto a prepared foundation and reflect wave energy rather than absorbing it. Other types include floating breakwaters, used in marinas where tidal range is large, and submerged breakwaters, which reduce wave height while remaining below the surface.

Design considerations

Engineers size a breakwater for a design wave height based on local wave climate and acceptable overtopping. Failure modes include displacement of armour units, overtopping in storms, toe erosion and undermining. Breakwaters also alter sediment transport, which can cause accretion on one side and erosion on the other, and they can provide habitat for marine life; artificial reef effects are sometimes incorporated deliberately.

Notable examples

Famous breakwaters include the Plymouth Breakwater in England, completed in the 19th century, and the long mole protecting the harbour of Alexandria, built on the ancient mole to Pharos island.
