Monophony

In music, monophony is the simplest of musical textures, consisting of a melody (or tune) without any accompanying harmony or chords. A single voice, or several voices singing the same melody in unison or at the octave, produces a monophonic texture. It contrasts with polyphony, in which multiple independent melodic lines sound simultaneously, and with homophony, in which a melody is supported by chordal accompaniment.

History

Monophony is the oldest and most widespread musical texture. Ancient Greek music, as far as it can be reconstructed, was predominantly monophonic, as are many folk traditions worldwide. In the Western tradition, the liturgical chant of the medieval church — Gregorian chant in the Latin West and Byzantine chant in the East — was monophonic, sung by a soloist or choir in unison.

The troubadours and trouvères of medieval France, and the Minnesänger of Germany, cultivated secular monophonic song, sometimes with improvised instrumental accompaniment that nonetheless followed the melody. From roughly the ninth century, the addition of a parallel voice in organum began the long development toward polyphony, but monophony persisted in liturgical chant, folk song, and ceremonial music such as bugle calls.

Monophony in world music

Most of the world's musical traditions are fundamentally monophonic or heterophonic, including much Middle Eastern, South Asian, and East Asian art music. A solo melodic instrument — for example the Japanese shakuhachi in honkyoku repertoire — exemplifies monophony in its purest form.
