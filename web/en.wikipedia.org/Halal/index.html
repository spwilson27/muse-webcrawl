Halal (Arabic: حلال, ḥalāl) is an Arabic word that translates to "permissible" or "lawful" in English. In the Quran, the term halal is contrasted with haram ("forbidden"). This binary opposition was elaborated into a more complex classification of five categories of human action in Islamic jurisprudence.

In general every object and action is considered permissible unless there is a prohibition in the Quran or Hadith. The term is also widely used in everyday language to describe food prepared in accordance with Islamic dietary laws.

Food and drink

Islamic dietary law prohibits, among other things, pork and its by-products, intoxicating alcohol, blood, carrion, and meat of animals not slaughtered in the prescribed manner. Several verses of the Quran set out these prohibitions, including Surah Al-Baqarah (2:173) and Surah Al-Ma'idah (5:3). Seafood is generally regarded as permissible, though schools of law differ over details such as shellfish.

Slaughter (dhabihah)

For meat to be halal, animals must be slaughtered according to the method known as dhabīḥah: a swift, deep incision with a sharp knife to the throat, cutting the windpipe, oesophagus and jugular veins while the name of God is invoked. The animal must be healthy at the time of slaughter and all blood drained from the carcass. Scholars and certifying bodies differ over questions such as pre-slaughter stunning and mechanical slaughter, which are debated in contemporary practice. Similarities and differences with Jewish kashrut rules are frequently noted; kosher meat is accepted by some Muslim authorities, though not all.

Certification and industry

Because supply chains are complex, a large international industry of halal certification bodies has developed to audit farms, abattoirs, processors, restaurants and manufacturers, and to license the use of halal logos. The global halal food market is measured in the trillions of US dollars, driven by a Muslim population of roughly two billion. Certification standards vary between countries and organisations — Malaysia, Indonesia, the Gulf states and others maintain their own regimes — and the absence of a single global standard is a recurring subject of industry discussion.

Beyond food

The concept also extends to halal finance (banking and investment structured to avoid interest, riba), cosmetics and pharmaceuticals free of prohibited ingredients, modest fashion, and tourism and hospitality services marketed as halal-friendly.
