Direct democracy

Direct democracy or pure democracy is a form of democracy in which the electorate decides on policy initiatives directly, without elected representatives as proxies. This differs from representative democracy, in which the electorate elects representatives who then decide policy. Depending on the system, direct democracy may entail citizens passing executive decisions, laws, electing or dismissing officials, and conducting trials.

History

The most famous historical example is Athenian democracy in the fifth century BC, in which adult male citizens voted directly in the assembly (ecclesia) on laws and policy. Direct democratic assemblies survive today in the Landsgemeinden of the Swiss cantons of Appenzell Innerrhoden and Glarus, and in New England town meetings in the United States.

Instruments

Within modern mostly-representative systems, direct democracy appears through specific devices: referendums, in which voters decide a question put by a government; popular initiatives, in which citizens who gather enough signatures can place a proposal on the ballot; and recalls, by which officials can be removed by vote before their term expires. Switzerland is the leading example, holding several national referendums and initiatives each year.

Arguments

Supporters argue direct democracy increases legitimacy, participation, and accountability. Critics point to the influence of money in campaigns, the risk of majority tyranny over minorities, voter fatigue, and the difficulty of deciding complex technical questions by simple votes. Recent experiments include deliberative citizens' assemblies and online participatory platforms.
