Seraphim

A seraph (plural seraphim; Hebrew: שָׂרָף, sārāp, "burning one") is a type of celestial or heavenly being in Judaism and Christianity. Seraphim are traditionally placed in the highest rank of the Christian angelic hierarchy and in the fifth rank of ten in the Jewish angelic hierarchy. They are the beings who attend God's throne, continually praising him.

In the Hebrew Bible

The term is used in the Book of Isaiah (chapter 6), where seraphim appear in the prophet's vision of the Temple: each has six wings — with two they cover their faces, with two their feet, and with two they fly — and they call to one another, "Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory." One seraph touches Isaiah's lips with a live coal from the altar, purging his sin. In Numbers and Deuteronomy, related Hebrew terms refer to fiery serpents, and Isaiah also mentions a "flying serpent" (saraph me'ofef).

In Christian theology

In the hierarchy systematized by Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, seraphim are the first of the nine orders of angels, closest to God, aflame with divine love, and charged with regulating the heavens. Thomas Aquinas held that seraphim excel in charity and contemplation. The six-winged seraph is closely associated with Saint Francis of Assisi, whose stigmata vision at La Verna featured a crucified seraph.

In art

Seraphim are a common motif in Byzantine, medieval, and Orthodox art, often depicted as faces surrounded by six red wings, surrounding the throne of God on church domes and iconostases.
