Fermentation in winemaking

The process of fermentation in winemaking turns grape juice into an alcoholic beverage. During fermentation, yeast interacts with sugars in the juice to create ethanol — commonly expressed as alcohol — and carbon dioxide as a by-product. The science of fermentation in wine is known as oenology, and its management is one of the principal ways a winemaker shapes the finished wine.

The fermentation process

Primary fermentation begins when yeast — either wild yeast present on the grapes or a cultured strain such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae — is introduced to the must. Wild fermentations start more slowly and less predictably but are prized by some producers for complexity; cultured yeast offers control and reliability. Fermentation is exothermic, and temperature control is critical: white wines are typically fermented cool (around 12–22 °C) to preserve aromatics, while reds ferment warmer (around 20–32 °C) to extract colour and tannin from the skins.

Red wine fermentation

Red wines ferment on their skins, forming a "cap" of solids that must be punched down, pumped over, or otherwise managed to extract phenolics and prevent spoilage. Total fermentation time ranges from a few days to several weeks.

Malolactic fermentation

Most red wines, and some whites such as Chardonnay, undergo a secondary malolactic fermentation, in which bacteria (chiefly Oenococcus oeni) convert sharp malic acid into softer lactic acid and carbon dioxide, rounding the wine's texture and adding flavours such as butter in Chardonnay.

Other fermentations

Sparkling wines such as Champagne undergo a second fermentation in the bottle to trap carbon dioxide. A "stuck" fermentation, in which yeast dies before consuming all the sugar, is a feared fault that can leave an unstable, sweet wine vulnerable to spoilage.
