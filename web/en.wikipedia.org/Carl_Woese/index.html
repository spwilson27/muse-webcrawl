Carl Woese (1928–2012)

Carl Richard Woese (15 July 1928 – 30 December 2012) was an American microbiologist and biophysicist. Woese is famous for defining the Archaea (a new domain of life) in 1977 by phylogenetic taxonomy of 16S ribosomal RNA, a technique he pioneered that revolutionized microbiology. He also originated the RNA world hypothesis in 1967, although it was not widely supported until later.

Early life and education

Woese was born in Syracuse, New York, in 1928. He attended Deerfield Academy in Massachusetts and graduated from Amherst College in 1950 with a bachelor's degree in mathematics and physics. He received his PhD in biophysics from Yale University in 1953, where he studied under Ernest Pollard. He worked as a biophysicist at General Electric's Knolls Atomic Power Laboratory from 1954 to 1960 and spent a year at the Pasteur Institute in Paris before joining the faculty of the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign in 1964.

Discovery of the archaea

In the 1970s, Woese began cataloguing the sequences of 16S rRNA oligonucleotides from diverse microorganisms, using the ribosome as a "molecular chronometer" of evolutionary relationships. In 1977, he and George Fox announced that a group of methane-producing organisms, previously assumed to be ordinary bacteria, were in fact as distantly related to bacteria as bacteria are to eukaryotes. This led Woese to propose, in his 1990 paper with Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis, the three-domain system of Bacteria, Archaea, and Eucarya, replacing the older prokaryote–eukaryote dichotomy and the five-kingdom classification.

The proposal was initially controversial and resisted by many biologists, but it is now widely accepted and foundational to modern evolutionary biology and taxonomy.

Later work and legacy

Woese's later research focused on the nature of the earliest life, horizontal gene transfer, and the "Darwinian threshold" — the point at which vertical inheritance became the dominant mode of genetic transmission. He criticized what he saw as an overly reductionist and gene-centered view of evolution. Woese received the Leeuwenhoek Medal (1992), the U.S. National Medal of Science (2000), and the Crafoord Prize (2003), sometimes described as the equivalent of a Nobel Prize for biosciences. He died in Urbana, Illinois, in 2012.
