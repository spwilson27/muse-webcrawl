Etruscan language

Etruscan was the language of the Etruscan civilisation of ancient Italy, in Etruria (modern Tuscany, western Umbria and northern Lazio), attested in more than 13,000 inscriptions dating from about 700 BC to the 1st century AD, when it was replaced by Latin. It is written in an alphabet derived from Euboean Greek, usually right to left.

Classification

Etruscan is not Indo-European. Most scholars group it with Raetic of the Alps and Lemnian of the Aegean in a small Tyrsenian language family. The alphabet can be read, and many words and grammatical features are understood from bilingual texts, funerary formulas and the long ritual Liber Linteus (the Zagreb mummy wrappings) and the Tabula Capuana, yet large-scale translation remains impossible for lack of extensive bilingual material and surviving literature.

Corpus and features

Most inscriptions are short funerary or votive texts. The language appears agglutinative in part, with genitive endings in -s and -l, a plural in -r, and numerals known from inscribed dice and texts (thu, zal, ci, śa, mach, huth). Latin borrowed some words possibly through Etruscan, and Etruscan influenced early Roman religion and custom.

Decline

As Roman political control spread, Latin displaced Etruscan; the last speakers likely died out around the turn of the era, though the Emperor Claudius still wrote a (lost) history of the Etruscans in the 1st century AD. See also Etruscan civilization, Liber Linteus and Tyrsenian languages.
