Constitutional Convention

A constitutional convention (also known as a constituent assembly, or, in the United States context, a convention of states) is a gathering for the purpose of writing a new constitution or revising an existing one. Members are often elected by popular vote, though some are appointed. The resulting text generally requires ratification by referendum or by state legislatures before taking effect.

Famous conventions

The best-known example is the Philadelphia Convention of 1787, which drafted the Constitution of the United States. Presided over by George Washington, it produced the federal system, the separation of powers, and the bicameral Congress that still structure American government. Other notable conventions include the French National Constituent Assembly of 1789 and the German Parlamentarischer Rat of 1948–49, which drafted the Basic Law.

Convention versus legislature

Unlike an ordinary legislature, a constitutional convention is usually convened for the single task of framing a constitution and dissolves once its work is done. Supporters argue that conventions let the people, rather than incumbent politicians, decide fundamental rules; critics worry about runaway conventions exceeding their mandate.

Modern usage

Many U.S. states periodically place the question of calling a constitutional convention before voters. Internationally, conventions and constituent assemblies have been used in South Africa (1994–96), Iceland (2011), and Chile (2021–23) to draft or attempt new constitutions.
