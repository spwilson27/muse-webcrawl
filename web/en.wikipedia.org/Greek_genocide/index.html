Greek genocide

Date1913–1923
LocationOttoman Empire (Anatolia and Pontus)
TargetOttoman Greeks
Estimated deathsSeveral hundred thousand (estimates vary widely)
PerpetratorsOttoman government; Turkish nationalist forces

The Greek genocide is the term used for the systematic killing, deportation and expulsion of the Greek Orthodox Christian population of the Ottoman Empire during and after the First World War, roughly between 1913 and 1923. It formed part of a wider campaign against the empire's Christian minorities, alongside the Armenian and Assyrian genocides.

Background

Greeks had lived in Anatolia and around the Black Sea (Pontus) since antiquity and numbered around two million in the Ottoman Empire on the eve of the war. The Committee of Union and Progress government's Turkish nationalist policies, setbacks in the Balkan Wars and the influx of Muslim refugees from lost territories created a climate in which Christian minorities were viewed as internal enemies.

Events

Beginning in 1913–1914, Greeks in eastern Thrace and the Aegean coast were subjected to boycotts, expulsions and massacres, and many fled to Greece. During the war, Greek men were conscripted into labour battalions (amele taburları) under lethal conditions, while communities in Pontus and the interior were deported on long marches with heavy mortality. Massacres accompanied the deportations.

After the Greek landing at Smyrna in 1919 and the ensuing Greco-Turkish War, violence escalated. The war ended with the Turkish recapture of Smyrna in September 1922 and the Great Fire of Smyrna, in which much of the Greek and Armenian quarters burned and many civilians died or fled by sea.

Population exchange and aftermath

The 1923 Treaty of Lausanne sanctioned a compulsory population exchange between Greece and Turkey: roughly 1.2–1.5 million Orthodox Christians were transferred to Greece and some 400,000 Muslims to Turkey, with exemptions for the Greeks of Constantinople and the Muslims of western Thrace. Death toll estimates for the genocide itself vary widely, from around 300,000 to over 900,000.

Recognition

Greece, Cyprus, Armenia and Sweden, as well as several US states and other legislatures, have recognised the events as genocide; the International Association of Genocide Scholars passed a resolution to that effect in 2007. Turkey rejects the genocide designation, characterising the deaths as part of wartime upheaval. In Greece, 19 May is observed as a day of remembrance for the Pontic Greeks.
