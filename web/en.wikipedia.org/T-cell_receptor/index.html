T-cell receptor

The T-cell receptor (TCR) is a protein complex found on the surface of T cells (T lymphocytes) that is responsible for recognising fragments of antigen as peptides bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. Each T cell expresses TCRs of a single specificity, generated by random gene rearrangement, giving the immune system an enormous repertoire of antigen specificities.

Structure

The TCR is a disulfide-linked heterodimer. In most T cells it consists of alpha (α) and beta (β) chains; a minority of T cells carry gamma (γ) and delta (δ) chains instead. Each chain has a variable region — containing hypervariable complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) that contact the peptide–MHC — and a constant region anchoring it in the membrane. Because the TCR itself has short cytoplasmic tails, signalling is performed by the associated CD3 complex, whose immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) are phosphorylated on engagement.

Generation of diversity

TCR genes are assembled during T-cell development in the thymus by V(D)J recombination, in which variable, diversity, and joining gene segments are randomly combined and junctional nucleotides added or removed. The theoretical repertoire exceeds 1015 distinct receptors, though an individual carries perhaps 107–108. Thymic selection then removes strongly self-reactive clones.

Signalling and function

TCR engagement, stabilised by the CD4 or CD8 co-receptor, activates kinases such as Lck and ZAP-70, triggering calcium flux and transcriptional programmes that drive T-cell proliferation and differentiation. Unlike antibodies, TCRs exist only as membrane-bound receptors and are never secreted. Engineered TCRs and chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are used in adoptive cell therapy for cancer.
