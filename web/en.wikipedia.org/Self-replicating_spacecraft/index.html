Self-replicating spacecraft

A self-replicating spacecraft is a hypothetical type of robotic spacecraft capable of constructing copies of itself. Such a craft might theoretically be sent to a distant star system, where it would seek out raw materials (e.g., from asteroids) to create copies of itself. These copies could then be sent to other star systems, repeating the process. In this way it would be possible to explore the whole galaxy in a relatively short time span, given a sufficiently fast reproduction cycle.

The concept was first proposed in 1948 by mathematician John von Neumann in his work on self-replicating automata, which is why such spacecraft are often called von Neumann probes.

History of the concept

Von Neumann proved mathematically that a machine could reproduce itself given a supply of parts and a suitable environment. The idea was extended to space exploration by various authors; a detailed engineering study was produced by NASA in 1980 (Advanced Automation for Space Missions), which envisioned a self-replicating lunar factory. In 1986, K. Eric Drexler discussed molecular assemblers in Engines of Creation.

Variants

Von Neumann probes

A von Neumann probe is a spacecraft capable of replicating itself fully. It combines the concept of a von Neumann universal constructor with mobility, allowing exponential exploration of the galaxy.

Berserkers

A malevolent variant, popularized by Fred Saberhagen's Berserker stories, is a self-replicating probe programmed to destroy life. The concept has been invoked in discussions of the Fermi paradox.

Seeding probes

A seeding probe carries embryos or genetic material to colonize other worlds, merging replication with directed panspermia.

Implications for the Fermi paradox

Because a single self-replicating probe launching civilization could, in principle, fill the galaxy with descendants in a few million years—short compared with the age of the Milky Way—the absence of observed probes is sometimes cited as sharpening the Fermi paradox, unless civilizations refrain from building them due to the risk of runaway replication (the "grey goo" concern in nanotechnology).
