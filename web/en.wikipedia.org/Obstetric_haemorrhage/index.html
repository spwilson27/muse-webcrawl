Obstetric haemorrhage

Obstetric haemorrhage is bleeding in pregnancy that occurs before, during, or after childbirth. Bleeding before childbirth is known as antepartum haemorrhage, while heavy bleeding after delivery is termed postpartum haemorrhage. Obstetric haemorrhage is one of the leading causes of maternal mortality worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings.

Causes

Causes are often summarised by the "four T's": Tone (uterine atony), Trauma (lacerations of the genital tract), Tissue (retained placenta or abnormal placentation) and Thrombin (coagulation disorders). Antepartum bleeding may arise from placenta praevia or placental abruption, while postpartum bleeding most commonly results from failure of the uterus to contract after delivery.

Management

Management involves rapid resuscitation, uterotonic drugs such as oxytocin, uterine massage, tranexamic acid, and, where bleeding continues, surgical measures including balloon tamponade, arterial ligation or embolisation, and hysterectomy as a last resort. Active management of the third stage of labour significantly reduces the incidence of postpartum haemorrhage.

Epidemiology

The World Health Organization estimates that haemorrhage accounts for roughly a quarter of maternal deaths globally. Most deaths occur within hours of delivery and are considered preventable with timely access to skilled care, blood transfusion and essential medicines.
