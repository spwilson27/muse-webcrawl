Ocean thermal energy conversion

Ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC) is a process or technology for producing energy by harnessing the temperature differences (thermal gradients) between ocean surface waters and deep ocean waters. Energy from the sun heats the surface water of the ocean. In tropical regions, surface water can be much warmer than deep water. This temperature difference can be used to produce electricity and to desalinate ocean water.

History

The concept dates to 1881, when French engineer Jacques Arsène d'Arsonval proposed tapping the thermal energy of the ocean. His student Georges Muse built the first known OTEC plant in Cuba in 1930, producing 22 kilowatts of electricity. In 1935, Muse constructed another plant aboard a 10,000-ton cargo vessel off the coast of Brazil. Weather and waves destroyed both plants before they became net energy producers. Jacques Cousteau later explored the concept in the book The Silent World.

Thermodynamic cycle types

Cold seawater is an integral part of each of the three types of OTEC systems: closed-cycle, open-cycle, and hybrid. To operate, the cold seawater must be brought to the surface. The three types differ mainly in how the working fluid and the cycle are arranged.

Closed-cycle

Closed-cycle systems use a working fluid such as ammonia or R-134a that is vaporized by warm surface water, drives a turbine, and is condensed again by cold deep water in a continuous loop.

Open-cycle

Open-cycle OTEC uses the seawater itself as the working fluid. Warm surface water is placed in a low-pressure container, causing it to boil; the expanding steam drives a low-pressure turbine. Because the steam leaves its salt behind, open-cycle systems can also produce desalinated fresh water.

Hybrid

Hybrid cycles combine features of both closed and open systems to maximize both electricity generation and fresh water production.

Applications

Beyond electricity, OTEC plants can support seawater desalination, air conditioning (using cold deep water directly), aquaculture, and agriculture in coastal areas. The world's largest operational OTEC plant for many years was the Makai Ocean Engineering facility at Kailua-Kona, Hawaii, connected to the grid in 2015 with a capacity of about 105 kilowatts.

Challenges

OTEC faces high capital costs, low thermodynamic efficiency (typically only a few percent, limited by the small temperature difference), and the engineering difficulty of pumping large volumes of cold water through long intake pipes several kilometres in length.
