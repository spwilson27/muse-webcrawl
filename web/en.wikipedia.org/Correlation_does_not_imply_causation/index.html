Correlation does not imply causation

Correlation ≠ causation
Latin formCum hoc ergo propter hoc ("with this, therefore because of this")
TypeStatistical fallacy / cautionary principle
Related conceptConfounding variable

Two variables may rise together because of a third, hidden factor — or by chance.

The phrase "correlation does not imply causation" expresses the principle that a statistical association between two variables is not, by itself, sufficient evidence that one causes the other. It is a standard warning in statistics and scientific reasoning against a particular logical error, known in its Latin form as cum hoc ergo propter hoc.

Why correlations mislead

An observed correlation between X and Y may arise for several reasons other than X causing Y. Y might cause X (reverse causation); a third variable, called a confounder or lurking variable, might cause both — as hot weather causes both ice-cream sales and drowning incidents, producing a correlation between them; or the correlation may be pure coincidence, which becomes common when many variables are compared at once. "Spurious correlations", such as the near-perfect historical correlation between some unrelated social statistics, are popular illustrations of the last point.

Establishing causation

Because correlation alone is insufficient, researchers use controlled experiments with random assignment, natural experiments, longitudinal designs, and causal inference methods such as the Bradford Hill criteria in epidemiology. Randomised controlled trials are considered the gold standard precisely because randomisation breaks the influence of confounders. Nonetheless, correlation remains a useful first sign of a possible causal relationship and often motivates further study; the principle warns against treating it as proof, not against taking it seriously.

History

The caution is as old as formal statistics: Karl Pearson, who developed the correlation coefficient, warned against inferring causation from it, and the idea underlies David Hume's earlier philosophical analysis of cause and effect. The phrase became a ubiquitous slogan of twentieth-century statistics teaching.
