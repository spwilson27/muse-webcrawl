Drilling fluid

In geotechnical engineering, drilling fluid, also known as drilling mud, is used to aid the drilling of boreholes into the earth, most commonly for oil and natural gas wells, exploration wells and water wells. It is pumped down the drill string, out through nozzles in the bit, and back up the annulus to the surface.

Functions

Drilling fluid carries rock cuttings to the surface, cools and lubricates the bit and drill string, balances formation pressures to prevent blowouts, and deposits a thin filter cake that seals permeable formations and stabilises the borehole wall. Its density ("mud weight") is carefully adjusted: too light risks an influx of formation fluids; too heavy can fracture the formation.

Types

Water-based muds are the most common, typically bentonite clay suspensions with additives such as barite for weighting, polymers and lignosulfonates. Oil-based and synthetic-based muds perform better in high temperatures and reactive shales but cost more and raise disposal concerns. Air, mist and foam are used in some hard-rock and underbalanced drilling.

Handling

At the surface, returned mud passes over shale shakers and through desanders, desilters and centrifuges to remove cuttings before recirculation. Engineers — "mud engineers" — continually test properties including viscosity, density and pH. Used mud and cuttings must be disposed of under environmental regulation.
