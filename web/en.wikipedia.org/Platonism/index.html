Platonism is the philosophy of Plato and the philosophical systems derived from it. Its central doctrine holds that abstract objects—most famously the Forms (or Ideas), such as the Form of the Good, Beauty itself, and Equality itself—exist independently of the physical world and of minds, and that sensible particulars derive their character by participating in or imitating these eternal, unchanging entities.

Theory of Forms

In dialogues such as the Phaedo, Republic, and Phaedrus, Plato presents the Forms as perfect exemplars: physical things are beautiful only by sharing in Beauty itself, and knowledge (epistēmē) properly concerns the Forms, whereas the sensible world yields only opinion (doxa). The allegory of the cave and the divided line in the Republic dramatize the relation between appearance and reality. Aristotle, Plato's student, criticized the theory—notably with the "third man" regress already raised in Plato's own Parmenides.

History

Platonism developed in Plato's Academy, founded in Athens around 387 BC. Under successors such as Speusippus and Xenocrates it turned toward mathematics and systematization; the later Academy became sceptical under Arcesilaus and Carneades. Middle Platonism (1st century BC–3rd century AD) revived dogmatic metaphysics, and Neoplatonism, inaugurated by Plotinus, organized reality into a hierarchy emanating from the One. Through figures such as Augustine, Platonism profoundly shaped Christian, and later Islamic and Jewish, theology. Renaissance humanists, the Florentine Academy of Ficino, and the Cambridge Platonists renewed Platonic thought in the early modern period.

Mathematical Platonism

In contemporary philosophy of mathematics, "Platonism" denotes the view that mathematical objects such as numbers and sets exist independently of human thought and language—a position associated with arguments by Gödel and with Quine and Putnam's indispensability argument. It stands opposed to nominalism, formalism, and intuitionism.

Criticism

Classical objections target the epistemological problem of how spatiotemporal minds can know non-spatiotemporal Forms (Benacerraf's problem for mathematics), the ontological cost of a doubled world, and the relation of participation. Nominalist and Aristotelian alternatives locate universals in things or in concepts.
