Subsidiarity

Subsidiarity is an organising principle holding that matters should be handled by the smallest, lowest, or least centralised authority capable of addressing them effectively. Decisions should be taken as closely as possible to the people they affect, and a larger or higher-level body should perform only those tasks that cannot be performed effectively at a more immediate or local level. The principle is central to the law of the European Union and to Catholic social teaching.

Subsidiarity assigns each task to the lowest level of organisation competent to perform it.

Origins

The concept has roots in the thought of early modern writers on federal polity such as Johannes Althusius and was developed within Catholic social doctrine. Pope Pius XI's encyclical Quadragesimo anno (1931), drafted largely by Oswald von Nell-Breuning, gave the classic formulation: it is "an injustice and at the same time a grave evil and disturbance of right order to assign to a greater and higher association what lesser and subordinate organizations can do." Later popes, including John Paul II in Centesimus annus (1991), reaffirmed the principle while stressing that the state retains a duty to intervene where lower levels genuinely cannot cope.

In the European Union

Subsidiarity was written into EU law by the Treaty of Maastricht (1992) and is now stated in Article 5 of the Treaty on European Union: in areas that do not fall within its exclusive competence, the Union shall act "only if and in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States... but can rather, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level." The Protocol on subsidiarity and proportionality gives national parliaments a monitoring role, including the "yellow card" procedure for objecting to draft legislation. Political debates continue over how strictly the test is applied, with critics arguing it is honoured unevenly in practice.

Other applications

Beyond the EU, subsidiarity informs the design of federal systems, the allocation of powers between central and devolved governments, and management theory, where it supports delegating decisions to those closest to the problem. It is often paired with the principle of proportionality, which limits any higher-level action to what is necessary to achieve its objective.

Criticism

Commentators note that the principle is indeterminate: whether a lower body "can" act effectively is itself a political judgement, so subsidiarity can be invoked both against centralisation and, where local capacity is judged inadequate, in favour of intervention. Its application therefore depends heavily on institutional and cultural context.
