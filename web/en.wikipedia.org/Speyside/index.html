Speyside is an area around the River Spey in Moray and Badenoch and Strathspey, in north-east Scotland. The region is famous as the home of the largest concentration of single malt Scotch whisky distilleries in Scotland, and "Speyside" is also the name of a recognised whisky-producing region under the Scotch Whisky Regulations.

Geography

The River Spey rises in the Monadhliath Mountains and flows some 172 kilometres (107 miles) north-east to the Moray Firth at Spey Bay. It is one of Scotland's fastest-flowing rivers, noted for its salmon fishing. The Speyside Way, a long-distance footpath, follows much of the river from Buckie to Aviemore.

The lower, fertile part of the valley around Rothes, Aberlour and Dufftown forms the heart of the whisky district, while the upper valley (Strathspey) extends south-west toward Grantown-on-Spey and Aviemore, on the edge of the Cairngorms.

Whisky

Speyside distilleries account for more than half of Scotland's malt whisky distilleries — over 50 in total. Well-known names include Glenfiddich, The Glenlivet, The Macallan, Aberlour, Glenfarclas and Balvenie. Dufftown styles itself "the malt whisky capital of the world", and the local saying runs "Rome was built on seven hills, Dufftown stands on seven stills".

Before the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, Speyside malts were often classified as a sub-type of Highland single malts; the regulations gave Speyside its own protected regional designation. Speyside whiskies are typically characterised as elegant, complex and often sherried, though styles range from light and grassy to rich and heavily sherried.

The Spirit of Speyside Whisky Festival, held each spring since 1999, draws visitors to distilleries, many of which are not normally open to the public.

History

Illicit distilling was widespread in the glens of Speyside in the 18th and early 19th centuries, thanks to the area's barley, peat, pure water and remoteness from excise officers ("gaugers"). The Excise Act 1823 made legal distilling economical, and George Smith of The Glenlivet took out the first licence in the area in 1824 — to the resentment of his illicit competitors. The coming of the railways in the 19th century, and the blending boom, turned Speyside into the powerhouse of the Scotch industry.
