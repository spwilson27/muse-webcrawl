Japanese cuisine

Japanese cuisine (washoku) encompasses the regional and traditional foods of Japan, developed through centuries of political, economic, and social change. It is based on the combination of a staple food — steamed rice or noodles — with soup and side dishes (okazu) flavoured with dashi, soy sauce, miso, and other seasonings. Emphasis is placed on seasonality, quality of ingredients, and presentation. In 2013, washoku, the traditional dietary culture of the Japanese, was added to UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list.

Characteristics

The traditional Japanese meal format, ichijū-sansai ("one soup, three dishes"), typically consists of a bowl of miso soup, three side dishes (often one simmered, one grilled, and one dressed or pickled), a bowl of white rice, and pickles (tsukemono). Umami — the savoury taste identified scientifically by Kikunae Ikeda in 1908 from kombu dashi — is central to the cuisine. Buddhist influence historically discouraged meat eating, and although the prohibition was never absolute, fish and seafood became the dominant proteins; meat consumption rose substantially only after the Meiji Restoration of 1868.

History

Rice cultivation arrived from the Asian mainland in the Yayoi period and became the foundation of the diet. Court cuisine in the Heian period, temple vegetarian cooking (shōjin ryōri) in the Kamakura period, and the elaborate kaiseki banquet tradition associated with the tea ceremony in the Muromachi and Edo periods successively refined Japanese food culture. During the Edo period (1603–1868), urban commoners developed fast-food culture — sushi, soba, and tempura sold from stalls in Edo (Tokyo) — while sankin-kōtai traffic spread regional specialities. After 1868, Western dishes were adapted as yōshoku (for example tonkatsu, curry rice, and omurice), and Chinese-influenced chūka dishes such as ramen became staples in the twentieth century.

Representative dishes

Rice dishes

Sushi — vinegared rice combined with seafood or other ingredients — is Japan's most internationally recognised dish, in forms including nigirizushi, makizushi, and chirashizushi. Other rice dishes include donburi (toppings over rice, such as katsudon and oyakodon), onigiri rice balls, ochazuke, and okayu rice porridge.

Noodles

Soba (buckwheat) and udon (thick wheat) noodles long predate ramen, which was adapted from Chinese noodle soups in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries and diversified into regional styles — Sapporo miso, Kitakata, Tokyo shōyu, and Hakata tonkotsu among them.

Other dishes

Tempura (batter-fried seafood and vegetables, introduced in technique by the Portuguese in the sixteenth century), sukiyaki and shabu-shabu (hot-pot beef dishes), yakitori (grilled chicken skewers), okonomiyaki (savoury pancakes), takoyaki, sashimi, and nabe hot pots are all widely eaten. Miso soup accompanies most traditional meals.

Regional cuisines

Japan's geography produces strong regional variation: Hokkaido is known for seafood, miso ramen, and jingisukan (lamb); the Kansai region around Osaka is famous for okonomiyaki, takoyaki, and kitsune udon; Okinawan cuisine uses pork, goya (bitter melon), and awamori liquor, reflecting the islands' distinct Ryukyuan heritage. Fermented foods such as natto, regional pickles, and local sake brewing further distinguish local foodways.

Etiquette and dining customs

Meals are traditionally eaten with chopsticks, seated at low tables or Western-style seating. The phrases itadakimasu (before eating) and gochisōsama deshita (after) express gratitude. Slurping noodles is acceptable and even customary; pouring one's own sake before serving companions is discouraged in formal settings. Seasonal presentation — serving vessels, garnishes such as shiso leaves, and the choice of ingredients — is considered integral to the meal.
