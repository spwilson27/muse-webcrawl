Huangjiu (simplified Chinese: 黄酒; traditional Chinese: 黃酒; pinyin: huángjiǔ), literally "yellow wine," is a type of traditional Chinese alcoholic beverage brewed directly from grains such as rice, sorghum, millet, or wheat using a qu starter culture. Unlike distilled spirits such as baijiu, huangjiu is not distilled and typically contains less than 20% alcohol.

History

Fermented grain wines have been produced in China for thousands of years; chemical residues in pottery from the Neolithic village of Jiahu suggest fermented beverages were made as early as around 7000 BC. Huangjiu production was well established by the Shang and Zhou dynasties, and the drink held an important place in ritual, banqueting, and cooking.

Production

The grains are cooked, cooled, mixed with qu — a cake of molds and yeasts — and water, and fermented. The mash is then pressed, clarified, pasteurized, and aged, often in ceramic jars. The characteristic amber colour deepens with age and results in part from heating and caramelization of sugars.

Varieties

Huangjiu is classified by sweetness, from dry (gan) through semi-dry, semi-sweet, and sweet. The most famous style is Shaoxing wine from Zhejiang province, made with glutinous rice and water from Jianhu lake; it is widely used in Chinese cooking as well as consumed as a beverage, often warmed. Other regional styles include mijiu (rice wine) and Fujian's red-yeast wines.

Uses

Huangjiu is drunk with meals, served warm in winter, and is a staple marinade and flavouring in Chinese cuisine, especially in braised and "drunken" dishes.
