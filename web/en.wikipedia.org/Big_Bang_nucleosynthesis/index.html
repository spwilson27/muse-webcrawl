Big Bang nucleosynthesis

Big Bang nucleosynthesis (also known as primordial nucleosynthesis, and abbreviated as BBN) refers to the production of nuclei other than those of the lightest isotope of hydrogen during the early phases of the universe. Most cosmologists believe it lasted only a short time, roughly from 10 seconds to about 20 minutes after the Big Bang.

Process

As the universe expanded and cooled, protons and neutrons formed and began to fuse. A bottleneck occurred because deuterium is weakly bound and was readily destroyed by photons until temperatures fell sufficiently. Once deuterium survived, nuclear reactions rapidly produced helium-4, along with trace amounts of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7. Heavier elements were essentially not formed because of the absence of stable nuclei with mass numbers 5 and 8.

Predictions and observations

BBN predicts abundances that depend chiefly on one parameter, the baryon-to-photon ratio, which also determines the baryon density of the universe. The measured abundance of helium-4 (about 25% by mass) and deuterium in distant gas clouds agree well with predictions using the baryon density measured independently from the cosmic microwave background — a major success of the Big Bang model.

The lithium problem

The predicted abundance of lithium-7 exceeds that observed in old metal-poor stars by a factor of about three, a discrepancy known as the cosmological lithium problem. Proposed solutions include stellar depletion of lithium and physics beyond the standard model; the problem remains unresolved.

History

The theory began with Ralph Alpher's 1948 dissertation work and the famous Alpher–Bethe–Gamow paper. Fred Hoyle and others later refined the calculations of light-element production.
