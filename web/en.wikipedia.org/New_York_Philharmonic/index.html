The New York Philharmonic is an American symphony orchestra based in New York City. Founded in 1842 by Ureli Corelli Hill as the Philharmonic Society of New York, it is one of the oldest musical institutions in the United States and one of the "Big Five" American orchestras. It performs primarily at David Geffen Hall (formerly Avery Fisher Hall) at Lincoln Center.

The orchestra has given more than 17,000 concerts by the time of its 175th anniversary season, more than any other orchestra in the world, and it played a famous 2008 concert in Pyongyang, North Korea — the first visit by an American orchestra to that country.

History

The Philharmonic's first concert, on 7 December 1842, opened with Beethoven's Fifth Symphony. It later premiered or gave the American premieres of major works including Dvořák's "New World" Symphony (1893), which it commissioned. Music directors have included Theodore Thomas, Gustav Mahler (1909–1911), Arturo Toscanini, and Leonard Bernstein (1958–1969), whose televised Young People's Concerts made him a cultural icon. Bernstein was succeeded by Pierre Boulez, Zubin Mehta, Kurt Masur, Lorin Maazel, Alan Gilbert, and Jaap van Zweden (from 2018).

Broadcasts and tours

The orchestra has broadcast on radio since 1922 and made many celebrated recordings. In February 2008, at the invitation of the North Korean government, it performed in Pyongyang in an event widely interpreted as cultural diplomacy. Its annual free concerts in Central Park are a New York summer institution.
