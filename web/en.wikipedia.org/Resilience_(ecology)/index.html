Resilience (ecology)

In ecology, resilience is the capacity of an ecosystem to absorb disturbance, reorganise, and retain essentially the same function, structure, identity, and feedbacks. The concept is associated especially with C. S. Holling's 1973 critique of equilibrium-centred ecology.

Ecological and engineering resilience

Engineering resilience measures how quickly a system returns to a single equilibrium after a disturbance. Ecological resilience instead asks how much disturbance a system can absorb before its controlling processes change and push it into a different regime. A lake, for example, may remain clear despite rising nutrient inputs until a threshold is crossed, after which algal dominance and internal nutrient recycling make the turbid state self-reinforcing.

Adaptive cycle and panarchy

Resilience theory describes change through an adaptive cycle of growth, conservation, release, and reorganisation. Systems are nested across scales in a panarchy: slow, large-scale processes constrain faster local dynamics, while local disturbances can occasionally cascade upward.

Determinants

Biodiversity can provide response diversity—species that perform similar functions but react differently to stress. Connectivity spreads resources and organisms but can also spread disease, fire, or invasive species. Genetic diversity, habitat heterogeneity, dormant life stages, and social learning in managed systems may all contribute to recovery.

Management

Managing for resilience means protecting functional diversity and slow variables, avoiding thresholds, maintaining modularity, and encouraging monitoring and adaptation rather than attempting to hold every variable constant. The idea has been extended to social-ecological systems, where institutions, livelihoods, knowledge, and ecosystems interact.
