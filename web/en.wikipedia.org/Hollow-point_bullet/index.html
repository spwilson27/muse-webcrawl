Hollow-point bullet

A hollow-point bullet is a type of expanding bullet which expands on impact, causing a more lethal hit and which decreases unintended injury to bystanders. The hollowed-out tip allows the bullet to deform upon striking flesh, opening into a mushroom-like shape that increases its diameter, creates a larger wound channel and transfers more of its kinetic energy to the target. Because the expanded bullet slows and typically stops within the target, the risk of over-penetration and of hitting bystanders is reduced.

History

Expanding bullets emerged in the 19th century for hunting large and dangerous game. Solid lead bullets for early breech-loading rifles were sometimes hollowed to improve expansion; Captain Bertie-Clay's "Dum-dum" bullet, produced at the Dum Dum arsenal in India in the 1890s by removing the jacket from the nose of the bullet to expose the soft lead core, became the most famous early design. Express rifles for hunting, which fired bullets at unusually high velocities, also employed hollow points to control expansion.

Mechanism

The hollow cavity at the tip admits fluid and tissue under pressure when the bullet strikes, forcing the jacket and core outward into petals — the characteristic "mushroom". For reliable expansion a minimum impact velocity is needed; modern designs with skived (pre-scored) jackets, bonded cores and polymer tips expand across wider velocity ranges. Hollow points also have a ballistic benefit: moving the centre of mass rearward can improve stability and accuracy, which is why many match-grade rifle bullets are open-tipped, although target designs are not intended to expand like hunting or defensive rounds.

Use

Hollow-point ammunition is standard for police and civilian self-defence handguns in many countries, because of its stopping effect and reduced over-penetration risk in urban settings. Hunters use expanding bullets (including soft-point and hollow-point designs) because they kill game more quickly and humanely; many jurisdictions require expanding ammunition for hunting deer and similar game. Frangible and hollow-point rounds are also used on indoor ranges to reduce ricochet risk against steel targets.

Legality and military use

The Hague Convention of 1899 (Declaration III) prohibits the use in warfare between signatory states of bullets "which expand or flatten easily in the human body". Modern military small-arms ammunition therefore uses full metal jacket bullets, though open-tip match ammunition — whose hollow point exists for manufacturing and accuracy reasons — has been accepted as lawful by several armed forces, including those of the United States. Civilian legality varies: some jurisdictions restrict hollow points (New Jersey, for example, limits their possession outside the home), while others permit them freely.

Terminology

"Dum-dum" remains a colloquial term for expanding bullets generally. Related designs include soft-point bullets, in which a soft lead tip is exposed, and jacketed hollow points (JHP), semi-jacketed hollow points (SJHP) and bonded hollow points, in which the jacket is chemically or mechanically fused to the core to prevent separation after impact.
