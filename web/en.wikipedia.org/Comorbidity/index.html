Comorbidity

In medicine, comorbidity is the presence of one or more additional conditions co-occurring with a primary condition. Comorbidities are distinguished from complications, which are consequences of the primary condition, although the boundary is not always sharp. The term was introduced by the epidemiologist Alvan Feinstein in 1970.

Significance

Comorbid conditions can interact with each other and with the primary disease, affecting prognosis, treatment choices, and outcomes. For example, diabetes commonly co-occurs with hypertension and cardiovascular disease; depression frequently accompanies chronic physical illnesses and worsens their course. The presence of multiple chronic conditions in one patient is often called multimorbidity, a term preferred when no single index condition is the focus.

Measurement

Several instruments quantify comorbidity for research and risk adjustment. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (1987) assigns weights to conditions such as myocardial infarction, dementia, and metastatic cancer to predict mortality. The Elixhauser system, based on administrative data, covers a wider set of conditions. In mental health, dual diagnosis refers to the comorbidity of a substance use disorder with another psychiatric disorder, a combination that complicates treatment and is a major focus of psychiatric epidemiology.

Patterns

Comorbidity may arise because conditions share risk factors (such as smoking causing both lung cancer and heart disease), because one condition causes another, because of shared genetic or biological pathways, or simply by chance, increasingly likely as people age. Recognising comorbidity is central to designing realistic clinical trials and treatment guidelines, which have traditionally focused on single diseases.
