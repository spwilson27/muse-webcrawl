Igigi

The Igigi are a group of deities in Mesopotamian mythology. The term appears in Sumerian and Akkadian texts and is generally used in contrast to the Anunnaki, another major collective of gods. The exact distinction between the two groups varied over time and between traditions: in some texts the Igigi are the great gods of heaven, while in others they are younger or lesser deities who performed labour for the senior gods.

Mythology

In the Babylonian epic Atrahasis, the Igigi are the junior gods who were made to work digging canals and maintaining the waterways of the world. After forty years of toil they rebelled, burned their tools, and surrounded the house of the chief god Enlil. Their revolt led the divine assembly to create humankind, moulded from clay mixed with the flesh and blood of a slain god, to take over the labour of the Igigi.

Relationship with the Anunnaki

In later traditions, particularly from the Old Babylonian period onward, the Igigi were associated with the heavens and the Anunnaki with the earth and the underworld, although the usage was never fully consistent. The number of the Igigi is sometimes given as 300, set against 600 Anunnaki. In the Epic of Gilgamesh and other works, both terms may function simply as collective names for "the gods" as a whole.
