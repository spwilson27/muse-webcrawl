Fluoroquinolone

Fluoroquinolones are a class of broad-spectrum antibiotics that play an important role in the treatment of serious bacterial infections, especially hospital-acquired infections and others in which resistance to older antibacterial classes is suspected. They are distinguished from the earlier quinolone antibiotics by the presence of a fluorine atom attached to the central ring system, typically at the C-6 position.

Mechanism of action

Fluoroquinolones act by inhibiting two bacterial enzymes essential for DNA replication: DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV. By stabilising enzyme–DNA cleavage complexes, they cause double-stranded breaks in bacterial DNA, leading to rapid, concentration-dependent bactericidal killing. Mammalian topoisomerases are far less sensitive, which accounts for the class's selective toxicity.

Generations

The class is commonly divided into generations based on spectrum and potency. First-generation agents such as nalidixic acid precede the fluorinated compounds. Second-generation drugs include ciprofloxacin and ofloxacin, notable for activity against Gram-negative aerobes and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Third-generation agents such as levofloxacin add expanded Gram-positive coverage, and fourth-generation drugs such as moxifloxacin add strong anaerobic activity.

Clinical use

Fluoroquinolones are used for urinary tract infections, respiratory tract infections, intra-abdominal infections, anthrax, and certain sexually transmitted infections. Because of their excellent oral bioavailability, many can be given by mouth with blood levels approaching those achieved intravenously.

Adverse effects

Serious adverse effects have led regulators to restrict their use for uncomplicated infections. Reported risks include tendonitis and tendon rupture, peripheral neuropathy, central nervous system effects, QT prolongation, aortic aneurysm or dissection, and dysglycaemia. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration requires boxed warnings describing these potentially disabling and irreversible reactions.

Resistance

Resistance arises through chromosomal mutations in the target enzymes, plasmid-mediated qnr genes, efflux pumps, and modifying enzymes. Rising resistance rates in Escherichia coli and other pathogens have prompted antimicrobial stewardship efforts to reserve fluoroquinolones for cases where no alternative exists.
