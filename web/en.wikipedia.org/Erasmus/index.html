Desiderius Erasmus Roterodamus (28 October c. 1466 – 12 July 1536), known as Erasmus of Rotterdam or simply Erasmus, was a Dutch Christian humanist and theologian who was the leading intellectual of his era. He was one of the most influential figures of the northern Renaissance, writing new Latin and Greek scholarly editions of the New Testament and the Church Fathers, and an important figure in patristics and classical studies.

Life

Born the illegitimate son of a priest in Rotterdam, Erasmus was educated at the University of Paris, where he learned scholasticism, which he later deplored. He took holy orders and lived as an independent scholar, supported by friends and patrons. Settling in England for periods (1509–1514), he befriended Thomas More, John Colet and Thomas Linacre, and wrote In Praise of Folly — a satire of clerical and scholarly folly — at More's house.

Works

His edition of the Greek New Testament (1516), with a new Latin translation, became the basis for Luther's German Bible and Tyndale's English Bible, and underlies the Textus Receptus. Adagia (1500, expanded repeatedly) was a hugely popular collection of classical proverbs. His works on education, such as De pueris instituendis, helped shape humanist schooling.

Religious position

Erasmus criticised the abuses of the Catholic Church and called for internal reform, but he never broke with Rome. His stance on predestination, against Luther's doctrine, drew a famous controversy (De libero arbitrio, 1524, answered by Luther's De servo arbitrio). Luther complained that "Erasmus laid the egg that Luther hatched" — while Erasmus sought reform through learning rather than schism.
