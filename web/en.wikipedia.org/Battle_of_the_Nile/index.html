The Battle of the Nile (also known as the Battle of Aboukir Bay) was a major naval battle fought between the British Royal Navy and the French Navy at Aboukir Bay on the Mediterranean coast of Egypt from 1 to 3 August 1798. Rear-Admiral Sir Horatio Nelson's fleet destroyed or captured eleven of the thirteen French ships of the line, a victory that established British naval supremacy in the Mediterranean and stranded Napoleon's army in Egypt. This 1798 naval battle is distinct from the land Battle of the Pyramids, fought by Napoleon's army near Cairo a few weeks earlier.

Background

Napoleon had invaded Egypt to threaten British interests and the route to India. Nelson, hunting the French expedition across the Mediterranean, finally found Admiral Brueys's fleet anchored in line in the shallow Aboukir Bay, its inshore flank supposedly protected by shoals and a battery on Aboukir Island.

The battle

Arriving late on 1 August, Nelson attacked immediately, before dark. Captain Foley in Goliath judged there was room to pass inside the French line and led several ships between the anchored French and the shore, so that the French van was caught between two fires and overwhelmed piecemeal before the rear could work up to help. At about 10 p.m. the fire aboard the French flagship, the great 120-gun L'Orient, reached her magazines; the colossal explosion—heard in Alexandria—killed Brueys (already wounded) and nearly a thousand men, and fighting paused in shock. By the morning of 2 August only two French ships of the line, including Villeneuve's Guillaume Tell, and two frigates escaped.

Aftermath

Nelson became a European celebrity, was made Baron Nelson of the Nile, and received honours and gifts from across the coalition; his captains, the "Band of Brothers," were celebrated. Strategically, the victory cut Napoleon's communications with France, encouraged the formation of the Second Coalition, and confirmed British command of the Mediterranean for the rest of the war.
