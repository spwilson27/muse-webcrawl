Boumediene v. Bush

Boumediene v. Bush, 553 U.S. 723 (2008), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States in which the Court held that detainees held at the Guantánamo Bay Naval Base have a constitutional privilege of habeas corpus under the Suspension Clause of Article I, and that the Military Commissions Act of 2006, which sought to strip federal courts of jurisdiction over their petitions, was an unconstitutional suspension of the writ.

Background

The case was brought by Lakhdar Boumediene, an Algerian-born citizen of Bosnia and Herzegovina arrested after the September 11 attacks and held at Guantánamo without charge. Earlier decisions, including Rasul v. Bush (2004) and Hamdan v. Rumsfeld (2006), had addressed statutory habeas rights and the legality of military commissions; Congress responded with the Detainee Treatment Act and the Military Commissions Act, attempting to remove habeas review for "enemy combatants."

Decision

Writing for a 5–4 majority, Justice Anthony Kennedy held that the constitutional habeas privilege extends to Guantánamo because the United States exercises complete and indefinite control over the base, even though formal sovereignty lies with Cuba. The Court applied a functional test considering the detainees' status, the nature of the site, and the practical obstacles to extending the writ, and found the Combatant Status Review Tribunals an inadequate substitute for habeas.

Significance

The decision was the third time the Court ruled against the executive branch's detention policies in the "war on terror," and it affirmed that the Constitution constrains the political branches even in wartime and in territory under de facto U.S. control.
