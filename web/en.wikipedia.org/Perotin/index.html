Pérotin (fl. c. 1200), also called Perotinus Magnus ("Pérotin the Great"), was a medieval European composer, probably French, who was among the first known composers of polyphonic music in more than two voices. He was associated with the Notre Dame school of polyphony in Paris and is credited by the theorist known as Anonymous IV with revising the Magnus liber organi ("Great Book of Organum") of his predecessor Léonin.

Works

Anonymous IV attributes to Pérotin the earliest surviving works of European polyphony for four voices (quadrupla): Viderunt omnes (1198) and Sederunt principes (1199), both organa on Gregorian chant tenors for the feasts of Christmas and St Stephen at Notre Dame Cathedral. His three-voice organa include Alleluia Posui adiutorium and Alleluia Nativitas. He also composed conductus and clausulae in two and three parts, among them Beata viscera and Dum sigillum summi Patris.

Style and significance

Pérotin's music is notable for its rhythmic coordination of voices using the rhythmic modes, its exchanges of voice parts (Stimmtausch), and its architectural sense of large-scale repetition and contrast. His expansion of organum from two voices to three and four transformed medieval music and laid foundations for the motet and for later Western polyphony. Almost nothing is known of his life beyond the testimony of Anonymous IV, writing several decades later.
