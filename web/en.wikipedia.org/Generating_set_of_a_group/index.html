Generating set of a group

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In abstract algebra, a generating set of a group is a subset of the group such that every element of the group can be expressed as a combination (under the group operation) of finitely many elements of the subset and their inverses. In other words, if S is a subset of a group G, then ⟨S⟩, the subgroup generated by S, is the smallest subgroup of G containing every element of S; equivalently, it is the intersection of all subgroups of G containing S. If ⟨S⟩ = G, then S is said to generate G, and the elements of S are called generators.

Examples

The integers under addition are generated by the single element 1 (or −1), so ℤ is a cyclic group. The symmetric group Sn is generated by a transposition and an n-cycle. The rotations by an irrational multiple of π generate a dense subgroup of the circle group. Many groups of interest, such as free groups, are defined by generators together with relations between them, leading to the notion of a group presentation.

Finitely generated groups

A group with a finite generating set is called finitely generated. Every finite group is finitely generated, but the converse is false: the additive group of rational numbers is not finitely generated, because any finite set of rationals has a common denominator and can produce only fractions with that denominator. Whether a finitely generated group is also finitely presented, and algorithms for working with generating sets (such as Schreier's lemma for subgroups of free groups), are central questions in combinatorial and geometric group theory. The Cayley graph of a group with respect to a generating set encodes the group's geometry and underlies the study of word metrics and growth.
