Seleucus I Nicator (c. 358 – September 281 BC) was a Macedonian Greek general, a Diadochus (successor) of Alexander the Great, and the founder of the Seleucid Empire. After Alexander's death in 323 BC, Seleucus received the satrapy of Babylonia in the Partition of Triparadisus (321 BC); driven out by Antigonus, he returned in 312 BC — the epoch of the Seleucid era — and over the following decades conquered most of Alexander's Asian empire, from Asia Minor to the Indus.

Rise to power

Seleucus had served as a commander of the elite hypaspists and married the Sogdian princess Apama at the mass weddings of Susa in 324 BC — a marriage he uniquely kept after Alexander's death. Allied with Ptolemy, Lysimachus, and Cassander against Antigonus, he secured the east: his treaty with the Mauryan emperor Chandragupta (c. 303 BC) exchanged eastern territories for 500 war elephants, which proved decisive at the Battle of Ipsus (301 BC), where Antigonus was killed.

Empire and foundations

Seleucus founded numerous cities, most famously Antioch on the Orontes — his western capital — and Seleucia on the Tigris. His empire was the largest of the Hellenistic kingdoms, stretching at his death from Thrace to Central Asia. In 281 BC he defeated Lysimachus at Corupedium, gaining Asia Minor and Macedonia's sphere, but was assassinated shortly afterwards by Ptolemy Keraunos while crossing into Thrace. He was succeeded by his son Antiochus I.

Legacy

Seleucus's empire endured for over two centuries, spreading Greek urban culture across western and central Asia, though it gradually lost its eastern and western territories to the Parthians, Pergamon, and Rome.
