A nuchal cord is when the umbilical cord becomes wrapped around the fetus's neck 360 degrees or more. Nuchal cords are not very common, with prevalence rates of 6% to 37%. About half of nuchal cords resolve before delivery.

A nuchal cord may be loosely or tightly wrapped. It may be classified as a "type A" pattern, which can undo itself, or a "type B" pattern, in which the cord forms a locked loop that cannot easily undo itself. Nuchal cords may also wrap around other parts of the body, such as an arm or leg.

Signs and symptoms

Most nuchal cords cause no symptoms before delivery. During labor, a tight nuchal cord may cause variable decelerations in the fetal heart rate, and in uncommon cases may contribute to hypoxia, meconium staining, or the need for operative delivery. Serious adverse outcomes are uncommon, and the great majority of babies born with a nuchal cord are healthy.

Causes

Risk factors include a long umbilical cord, excess amniotic fluid (polyhydramnios), and high levels of fetal movement. Fetal movements during pregnancy can cause the cord to loop around the neck, torso, or limbs.

Diagnosis

Nuchal cords can be detected before delivery with obstetric ultrasonography, particularly with color Doppler imaging. They may first be discovered at the time of delivery when the cord is seen around the newborn's neck after the head emerges.

Treatment

If detected during delivery, a loose nuchal cord can often be slipped over the baby's head. When the cord is too tight to reduce, it may be clamped and cut before delivery of the shoulders, or the baby may be delivered through the loop using the "somersault" maneuver. Management is generally supportive, and cesarean delivery is not routinely required solely because a nuchal cord is present.

Prognosis

Prognosis is generally excellent. Although occasional associations with adverse outcomes such as low Apgar scores have been reported, most studies find no significant long-term harm from nuchal cords detected and managed appropriately.
