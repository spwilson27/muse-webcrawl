Holding company

A holding company is a company whose primary business is holding a controlling interest in the securities of other companies. It is a type of financial organization that owns a controlling interest in other companies, which are called subsidiaries. The holding company can control the policies and oversee the management decisions of its subsidiaries but does not itself produce goods or services.

Purpose and structure

Holding companies are created to reduce risk for owners, to allow control of many companies with a relatively small investment, to gain tax advantages, and to separate ownership of assets (such as real estate or intellectual property) from operating businesses. A pure holding company does nothing but own stock; a mixed holding company also operates a business of its own. Famous examples include Berkshire Hathaway and Alphabet Inc., which became Google's holding company in a 2015 restructuring.

History and regulation

The structure became prominent in the United States after New Jersey liberalized its incorporation laws in the late 19th century; the great trusts reorganized as holding companies. Abuses in the utility industry led to the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935, largely repealed in 2005. Bank holding companies are separately regulated under the Bank Holding Company Act of 1956.
