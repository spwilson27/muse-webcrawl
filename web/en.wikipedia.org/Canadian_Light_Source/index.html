Canadian Light Source

The Canadian Light Source (CLS; French: Centre canadien de rayonnement synchrotron) is Canada's national synchrotron light source facility, located on the campus of the University of Saskatchewan in Saskatoon. Opened in 2004, it accelerates electrons to 2.9 GeV to produce intensely bright light — from infrared to hard X-rays — used by thousands of academic and industrial researchers in physics, materials science, biology, medicine, agriculture, and archaeology.

Facility

Electrons are generated by an electron gun, accelerated by a linear accelerator and a booster ring, and injected into a storage ring where bending magnets and insertion devices (wigglers and undulators) cause them to emit synchrotron radiation. Beamlines filter this light for experiments. The ring's 2.9 GeV energy places the CLS among mid-sized third-generation light sources worldwide.

Research

CLS techniques include X-ray crystallography of proteins, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, imaging, and microanalysis. Notable applications include imaging the brains of multiple-sclerosis patients, studying ancient artifacts such as the Antikythera mechanism's inscriptions (in comparative work), developing battery materials, mapping contaminants in mine tailings, and structural studies of viruses, including work relevant to COVID-19. The facility operates a dedicated medical-imaging beamline (BMIT).

History and funding

The CLS project was approved in 1999 as one of Canada's largest scientific investments, funded by federal, provincial, municipal, university, and industry partners, and built on the site of the former Saskatchewan Accelerator Laboratory. First light was achieved in 2003 and the facility was officially opened in October 2004. It remains Canada's only synchrotron.
