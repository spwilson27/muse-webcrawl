Regulatory capture

Regulatory capture is an economic concept in which a regulatory agency, created to act in the public interest, instead advances the commercial or political special interests of the industry or sector it is charged with regulating. It is a form of government failure and corruption, related to the phenomenon of rent-seeking.

Theory

The idea was developed by economists of the Chicago School, notably George Stigler, whose 1971 paper "The Theory of Economic Regulation" argued that regulation is "acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit". Marver Bernstein's 1955 Regulating Business by Independent Commission described a "life cycle" in which agencies grow dependent on the industries they oversee. Sam Peltzman generalised Stigler's theory, and the framework became central to public choice economics.

Mechanisms

Capture can occur through several channels: the revolving door, in which officials move between the regulator and the regulated industry; information asymmetry, because agencies depend on industry for technical data; cultural capture, in which regulators internalise industry's worldview; and outright bribery or political pressure. Concentrated industries also have stronger incentives to organise and lobby than the diffuse public.

Examples

Frequently cited examples include the U.S. Interstate Commerce Commission's protection of railroads and trucking, the close ties between the Minerals Management Service and offshore oil companies revealed after the Deepwater Horizon spill (2010), and the FAA's delegation of certification to Boeing, scrutinised after the 737 MAX crashes. Historical debates also surround financial regulation before the 2008 crisis.

Variants

Scholars distinguish materialist capture (driven by money and career incentives) from cognitive or cultural capture (driven by shared assumptions and status). Some also discuss "regulatory collusion", "agency capture" by non-industry groups, and capture of standard-setting bodies.

Countermeasures

Proposed remedies include transparency rules, cooling-off periods for the revolving door, independent funding, sunset clauses, cost–benefit review, and designing agencies with competing or overlapping jurisdictions.
