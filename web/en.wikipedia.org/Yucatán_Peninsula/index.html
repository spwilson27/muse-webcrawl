Yucatán Peninsula

Yucatán Peninsula
Countries: Mexico, Belize, Guatemala
Mexican states: Yucatán, Campeche, Quintana Roo
Largest city: Mérida
Features: Maya ruins, cenotes, Chicxulub crater

The Yucatán Peninsula (Spanish: Península de Yucatán) is a large peninsula in southeastern Mexico and northern Central America, separating the Gulf of Mexico from the Caribbean Sea. It comprises the Mexican states of Yucatán, Campeche, and Quintana Roo, plus Belize and Guatemala's Petén department.

Geography and geology

The peninsula is a flat limestone platform with almost no surface rivers; rain drains underground into natural sinkholes called cenotes, sacred wells to the Maya and now famous swimming sites. Its low scrub jungle gives way to rainforest in the south. Buried beneath the northwest coast lies the Chicxulub impact crater, some 180 km wide, formed by the asteroid that caused the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction 66 million years ago.

History

The region was the heartland of Maya civilisation, home to cities such as Chichen Itza, Uxmal, Calakmul, Palenque (on its western edge), and Tikal in Petén. Spanish conquest under the Montejo family in the 1520s–1540s was fiercely resisted; in the 19th century the Caste War of Yucatán (from 1847) saw Maya rebels hold much of the east for decades. Henequen (sisal) plantations made Mérida wealthy around 1900.

Today

Tourism dominates the Caribbean coast — Cancún, developed from 1970, Playa del Carmen, Tulum, and the "Riviera Maya" — while Mérida is a cultural and services hub. The Maya Train (Tren Maya) railway loop, opened in stages from 2023, circles the peninsula amid environmental controversy. Maya languages, especially Yucatec Maya, remain widely spoken.
