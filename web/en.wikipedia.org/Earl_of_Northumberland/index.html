Earl of Northumberland

Earl of Northumberland is a title in the peerage of England that has been created several times, most famously for the Percy family, who dominated the Anglo-Scottish border in the later Middle Ages. The earldom takes its name from Northumberland, the historic county north of the River Tyne (the older earldom of Northumbria had covered a wider territory).

Percy earls

Henry de Percy was created Earl of Northumberland in 1377 by Richard II. His son, Henry Percy — Shakespeare's "Hotspur" — was killed at the Battle of Shrewsbury in 1403 while in rebellion against Henry IV, and the first earl died in rebellion in 1408. The title was restored to Hotspur's son in 1416. The Percys were central figures in the Wars of the Roses; the third earl was killed at Towton (1461) and the earldom was forfeited and granted briefly to John Neville before being restored to the fourth earl. The sixth earl died without an heir in 1537 and the title lapsed; it was recreated in 1557 for Thomas Percy, whose involvement in the Rising of the North (1569) led to his execution in 1572. The ninth earl spent many years imprisoned in the Tower of London after the Gunpowder Plot. The Percy male line failed in 1670, and the earldom became extinct.

Later creations

The title was recreated in 1749 for Algernon Seymour, 7th Duke of Somerset, with remainder to his son-in-law Sir Hugh Smithson, who became the 2nd earl and was created 1st Duke of Northumberland in 1766. The earldom has since descended with the dukedom; the family seat is Alnwick Castle.
