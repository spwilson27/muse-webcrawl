Peripatric speciation

Peripatric speciation
FieldEvolutionary biology
ModeSpeciation in a small population isolated at the periphery of the ancestral range
Key mechanismsFounder effect, genetic drift, genetic bottleneck, novel selection pressures
Proposed byErnst Mayr (1954)
RelatedAllopatric speciation, parapatric speciation, sympatric speciation

Peripatric speciation is a mode of speciation in which a new species forms from a small population that becomes isolated at the edge (periphery) of the ancestral species' geographic range. It is regarded as a special case of allopatric speciation distinguished by the small size of the isolated population, which makes it especially susceptible to rapid genetic change through the founder effect and genetic drift.

A peripheral isolate diverges from the parent population

Concept

The model was developed by Ernst Mayr, who argued from the biogeography of birds on Pacific archipelagos that small founder populations — for example, a few individuals colonising an island — undergo a "genetic revolution": inbreeding and the loss of genetic variation reshuffle gene interactions, drift fixes new combinations, and selection in a novel environment drives divergence. If reproductive isolation evolves before contact with the parent population resumes, a new species has formed.

Evidence and examples

Frequently cited cases include the diversity of Drosophila fruit flies on the Hawaiian Islands, where each island hosts endemic species descended from rare colonists; island bird populations around New Guinea that Mayr himself studied; and the London Underground mosquito (Culex pipiens form molestus), sometimes offered as a rapid, human-made example of peripheral isolation, though its history is debated. Peripatric speciation remains influential but contested: critics note that founder-effect speciation is hard to demonstrate in laboratory experiments, and that ordinary geographic isolation over long periods may explain most cases equally well.
