Mercury(II) sulfide

Mercury(II) sulfide
Formula: HgS
Molar mass: 232.66 g/mol
Density: 8.10 g/cm³
Appearance: red (α) or black (β) solid

Mercury(II) sulfide, or mercuric sulfide, is the chemical compound composed of the elements mercury and sulfur with the formula HgS. It is virtually insoluble in water and occurs in nature as the minerals cinnabar (red, hexagonal α-HgS) and metacinnabar (black, cubic β-HgS).

Occurrence and extraction

Cinnabar is the most important ore of mercury. Deposits at Almadén in Spain, Idrija in Slovenia and in China were mined for millennia. Heating cinnabar in air roasts the sulfide to mercury vapour and sulfur dioxide, the vapour being condensed to liquid metal.

Vermilion pigment

Ground cinnabar, and later synthetic HgS made by heating mercury with sulfur, gave the brilliant scarlet pigment vermilion, prized in Roman wall painting, illuminated manuscripts, Chinese lacquer and seals, and European easel painting. Vermilion can darken over time through conversion to black metacinnabar, a notorious problem in conservation. Because of mercury's toxicity its artistic use has largely been replaced by cadmium and modern organic reds, though it remains culturally important, for example as sindoor.

Properties

Mercury(II) sulfide is extremely insoluble — one reason cinnabar passes through the body with relatively low bioavailability compared with other mercury compounds — though it is still toxic and environmentally hazardous. It is a semiconductor with applications studied in photodetection; the red α form has a band gap around 2 eV.
