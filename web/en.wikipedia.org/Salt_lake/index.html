Salt lake

A salt lake or saline lake is a landlocked body of water that has a concentration of salts (typically sodium chloride) and other dissolved minerals significantly higher than most lakes, often defined as at least three grams of salt per litre. In many cases a salt lake is saltier than seawater.

Formation

Salt lakes form in endorheic basins where water flows in but cannot flow out to the sea, and where evaporation exceeds inflow. As water evaporates, dissolved minerals carried in by rivers and groundwater are left behind and become concentrated over thousands of years. Most salt lakes occur in arid and semi-arid regions. When such a lake dries up completely it leaves a salt flat or playa.

Examples

The largest salt lake in the world is the Caspian Sea, although its salinity is only about a third that of the ocean. Other notable examples include the Great Salt Lake in Utah, the Dead Sea between Israel and Jordan, the highly alkaline Lake Natron in Tanzania, and Bolivia's Salar de Uyuni, the world's largest salt flat.

Biology and uses

High salinity excludes most fish, but salt lakes can support dense populations of brine shrimp, algae such as Dunaliella salina (which colours some lakes pink), and the flamingos and other birds that feed on them. Salt lakes are commercially important sources of table salt, lithium brines, potash and other minerals, and some, notably the Dead Sea, are destinations for health tourism.
