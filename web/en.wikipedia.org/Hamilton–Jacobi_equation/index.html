Hamilton–Jacobi equation

In physics, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, named after William Rowan Hamilton and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, is an alternative formulation of classical mechanics equivalent to Newton's laws, Lagrangian mechanics and Hamiltonian mechanics. It is a first-order nonlinear partial differential equation for Hamilton's principal function S.

Formulation

The equation takes the form H(q, ∂S/∂q, t) + ∂S/∂t = 0, where H is the Hamiltonian, q denotes generalised coordinates, and S is Hamilton's principal function. The momenta are given by p = ∂S/∂q, so solving this single PDE yields the full dynamics.

History and significance

Hamilton developed the theory in the 1830s from his work on optics, noticing the deep analogy between rays and particle trajectories. Jacobi clarified and extended the method. The equation is notable for its connection to wave mechanics: in the classical limit, the Schrödinger equation reduces to the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, with S playing the role of the phase of the wavefunction.

Applications

The method is used in celestial mechanics, optics, and the theory of integrable systems, and underlies action principles and semiclassical approximations such as WKB theory.
