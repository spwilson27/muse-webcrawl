Bubonic plague

A plague doctor's beaked mask, filled with aromatics, in a 17th-century engraving.

Bubonic plague is one of three types of plague caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. One to seven days after exposure, flu-like symptoms develop, including fever, headaches, and vomiting, together with swollen and painful lymph nodes — buboes — in the area nearest where the bacteria entered the skin. Bubonic plague is spread through exposure to the body fluids of a dead plague-infected animal or, classically, by the bites of infected fleas (especially the oriental rat flea, Xenopsylla cheopis) that live on rodents.

History

Bubonic plague caused the Black Death (1346–1353), which killed an estimated 30–60% of Europe's population, and earlier the Plague of Justinian (541–549, recurring for two centuries) in the Byzantine world. A third pandemic began in China in the 19th century and spread worldwide via steamships. Alexandre Yersin identified the bacillus in Hong Kong in 1894, and Paul-Louis Simond demonstrated flea transmission in 1898.

Signs, diagnosis and treatment

Besides buboes, acral necrosis of fingers, toes and nose — blackening that gave the "Black Death" its name — may occur. Diagnosis is by finding the bacterium in blood, sputum or a bubo aspirate. Untreated, bubonic plague has a mortality of roughly 40–70%; with prompt antibiotics (such as gentamicin or doxycycline) it falls dramatically. The other forms of plague are septicaemic and pneumonic, the latter transmissible person-to-person by droplets.

Modern occurrence

Plague remains enzootic in rodent populations in parts of Africa (notably Madagascar and the Democratic Republic of the Congo), Asia and the western United States, with a few thousand human cases reported globally in typical years. Its study informs modern conservation biology surveillance of wildlife disease and the ecology of biodiversity reservoirs.
