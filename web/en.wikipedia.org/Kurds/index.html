Kurds

Kurds, an Iranian ethnic group native to the Zagros mountain region.

The Kurds are an Iranian ethnic group native to Kurdistan, a mountainous region spanning southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran, northern Iraq, and northern Syria. They speak Kurdish, a group of related dialects belonging to the Iranian branch of the Indo-European languages. With an estimated population of 30 to 45 million, the Kurds are often described as one of the largest ethnic groups in the world without a state of their own.

History

The Kurds trace their ancestry to the peoples of the ancient Zagros region; scholars have debated links to the Medes and to the Karduchoi mentioned by Xenophon. Under the Islamic empires, Kurdish dynasties such as the Marwanids and Ayyubids — the latter founded by Saladin, himself of Kurdish origin — rose to prominence. The 1514 Battle of Chaldiran divided Kurdish-inhabited lands between the Ottoman and Safavid empires, a partition largely preserved in today's borders.

After the First World War, the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) raised the possibility of a Kurdish state, but the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) left the Kurds divided among Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria. The twentieth century saw repeated Kurdish uprisings and repression, including the Iraqi Anfal campaign of the late 1980s, which included the chemical attack on Halabja in 1988. Following the 1991 Gulf War, Iraqi Kurds established an autonomous region, formalized after 2003 as the Kurdistan Region of Iraq. In Syria, Kurdish-led forces established an autonomous administration in the north during the civil war, while in Turkey the conflict between the state and the PKK has continued since 1984.

Culture

Kurdish culture is marked by the Newroz new year festival, celebrated at the spring equinox; rich traditions of oral literature, including the epic Mem û Zîn; music played on instruments such as the tembûr; and distinctive dress. Most Kurds are Sunni Muslims of the Shafi'i school, with significant Alevi, Shia, Yazidi, and Christian minorities.
