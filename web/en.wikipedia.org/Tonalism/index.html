Tonalism

Tonalism
Years activec. 1880–1920
CountryUnited States
Major figuresGeorge Inness, James McNeill Whistler, Albert Pinkham Ryder, Dwight William Tryon

Tonalism was an artistic style that emerged in the 1880s in the United States when American artists began to paint landscape forms with an overall tone of colored atmosphere or mist. Between 1880 and 1915, dark, neutral hues such as gray, brown or blue often dominated compositions by artists associated with the style. During the late 1890s, American art critics began to use the term "tonal" to refer to these works, and the two leading exponents were George Inness and James McNeill Whistler.

Characteristics

Tonalist paintings are characterized by soft, indistinct forms, muted and closely related color harmonies, and an emphasis on mood and atmosphere over topographical detail. Landscapes were frequently depicted at dawn or dusk, in moonlight, or in mist and rain, so that the scene appears veiled in a unifying atmospheric tone. Compositions tend to be simple and meditative, often eliminating narrative detail in favour of a lyrical, contemplative effect.

Origins and development

The style drew on the French Barbizon school, on the paintings of Whistler — including his "Nocturnes" — and on the example of Albert Pinkham Ryder. George Inness, in his late work, developed a loose, vaporous manner informed by Swedenborgian ideas about the correspondence between nature and spirit. Other artists associated with Tonalism include Dwight William Tryon, Charles Warren Eaton, Edward Steichen in his early pictorialist photographs, and the California painters of the "California Tonalism" group such as Arthur Mathews and Xavier Martinez.

Decline and influence

Tonalism flourished until about 1915 and then declined as modernism gained ground after the Armory Show of 1913. It nonetheless influenced American modernists — the use of tonal harmonies can be traced in the work of Marsden Hartley and others — and its photography counterpart, Pictorialism, extended its aesthetics into the camera arts. Interest in Tonalism revived among scholars and collectors from the 1970s onward.
