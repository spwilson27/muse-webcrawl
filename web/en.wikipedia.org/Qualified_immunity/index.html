Qualified immunity

Qualified immunity
FieldUnited States constitutional law; civil rights
Key casesHarlow v. Fitzgerald (1982); Saucier v. Katz (2001); Pearson v. Callahan (2009)
StandardOfficials shielded unless they violate "clearly established" law

Qualified immunity is a legal doctrine in United States federal law that shields government officials from liability in civil lawsuits for money damages, unless their conduct violates "clearly established" statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known. The doctrine was developed by the Supreme Court of the United States, principally in cases brought under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and in Bivens actions against federal officials.

The U.S. Supreme Court developed the doctrine of qualified immunity

History

The doctrine grew out of common-law immunities available to officials when § 1983 was enacted in 1871 as part of the Civil Rights Act. In Pierson v. Ray (1967), the Supreme Court held that police officers sued for false arrest could raise a defence of good faith and probable cause. The modern test emerged in Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982), which replaced a subjective inquiry into an official's good faith with an objective standard: government officials performing discretionary functions are shielded from liability insofar as their conduct does not violate clearly established law.

In Saucier v. Katz (2001), the Court required courts to decide first whether the facts alleged show a constitutional violation, and second whether the right was clearly established. In Pearson v. Callahan (2009), the Court made that sequence optional, allowing courts to grant immunity based solely on the "clearly established" prong without ruling on the constitutional question.

The "clearly established" standard

For a right to be clearly established, existing precedent must have placed the statutory or constitutional question beyond debate, such that every reasonable official would understand that what he or she is doing violates the right. Courts generally require a prior case with facts materially similar to the case at hand; general propositions, such as the rule against excessive force, are usually insufficient at a high level of generality. Critics argue this creates a catch-22, in which the first victim of a novel form of misconduct cannot recover, and the right can never become established.

Criticism and reform efforts

Qualified immunity has been criticized by scholars and judges across the ideological spectrum. Critics contend that the doctrine lacks a basis in the text or history of § 1983, undermines accountability for misconduct — particularly by law enforcement officers — and denies compensation to victims of constitutional violations. Defenders argue that immunity protects officials from the burdens of litigation over split-second decisions and prevents personal liability from deterring public service.

Following the murder of George Floyd in 2020, legislative efforts to abolish or limit qualified immunity were introduced in Congress, including the George Floyd Justice in Policing Act, but did not become law. Some states, including Colorado and New Mexico, have enacted statutes creating state-law causes of action that do not permit a qualified immunity defence.

Related doctrines

Qualified immunity is distinct from absolute immunity, which fully protects certain officials — such as judges, prosecutors, and legislators — for acts within their official functions, and from sovereign immunity, which protects governments themselves from suit.
