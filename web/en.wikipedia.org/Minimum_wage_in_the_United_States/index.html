Minimum wage in the United States

In the United States, the minimum wage is set by U.S. labor law and a range of state and local laws. The first federal minimum wage was instituted in the National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933, signed into law by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, but later found to be unconstitutional. In 1938, the Fair Labor Standards Act established a minimum wage of 25 cents an hour, which was raised to $7.25 per hour in 2009, where the federal rate has remained.

History

The 1938 Act applied to workers engaged in interstate commerce and initially covered only a fifth of the labour force. Amendments in 1961 and 1966 extended coverage to retail, service and other workers. The real value of the federal minimum wage peaked in 1968, when it was worth considerably more in inflation-adjusted terms than the rate that has applied since 2009.

State and local laws

Employers generally must pay workers the highest applicable minimum wage — federal, state or local. A majority of states have set rates above the federal level; California, Washington and New York, among others, reached or exceeded $16 per hour in the mid-2020s, and many cities, including Seattle, San Francisco and New York City, mandate higher local rates. Five states have no state minimum wage law, leaving the federal rate to apply. A lower federal "tipped wage" of $2.13 per hour applies to employees who customarily receive tips, provided tips bring their earnings to the full minimum.

Debate

Supporters argue that minimum wages raise living standards, reduce poverty and stimulate consumer demand. Critics argue they reduce employment, particularly among teenagers and low-skilled workers, and raise business costs. Empirical research, beginning with Card and Krueger's 1994 study of fast-food employment, has found smaller disemployment effects than traditional models predicted, and the question remains actively debated among economists.
