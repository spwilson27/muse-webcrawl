Intrinsic value (ethics)

In ethics and moral philosophy, intrinsic value is a property of anything that is valuable in itself, for its own sake, as an end in itself. It contrasts with instrumental (extrinsic) value, which a thing has as a means to something else. Money is a classic example of purely instrumental value; happiness, knowledge, and human life are commonly held to have intrinsic value.

Philosophical discussion

G. E. Moore, in Principia Ethica (1903), proposed isolating a thing in thought to test whether its existence alone would be good. In environmental ethics, intrinsic value is attributed by some thinkers — notably in deep ecology — to nature, species, and ecosystems, grounding duties of preservation independent of human use. The concept underlies debates in value theory about monism versus pluralism among ultimate goods.
