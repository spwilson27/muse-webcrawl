History of the firearm


A bronze hand cannon of the Yuan dynasty

The history of the firearm begins in China, where gunpowder was invented by the 9th century and the earliest gunpowder weapons — fire lances, bombs and bronze hand cannons — appeared by the 12th–13th centuries. Firearms spread through the Islamic world to Europe, evolving from hand cannons and matchlock arquebuses to flintlocks, rifled arms, revolvers, repeating rifles and modern automatic weapons.

Chinese origins

Gunpowder formulae appear in Chinese texts by the 9th–11th centuries; the Wujing Zongyao (1044) gives military recipes. The fire lance — a bamboo or metal tube projecting flames and pellets — is depicted by the 10th–12th centuries. The oldest surviving firearm is a bronze hand cannon dated to about 1288 from Heilongjiang; metal-barrelled guns were used by the Song and Jin dynasties against the Mongols, and by the Yuan in campaigns across Asia.

Spread westward

Gunpowder knowledge travelled via the Mongol world and Islamic lands; Arab treatises described cannon by the late 13th century. European references to cannon appear from the 1320s (Florence, 1326). Hand cannons gave way to the matchlock mechanism in the 15th century, producing the arquebus; Ottoman musketeers and Spanish tercios made massed small-arms fire decisive in 16th-century warfare. The wheellock (early 1500s) enabled pistols and cavalry arms; the flintlock, perfected in France around 1610–30, remained standard for two centuries.

Rifling and industrialisation

Rifled barrels existed from the 16th century but loaded slowly; the Minié ball (1849), an expanding bullet, made rifled muskets practical for mass armies, with devastating effect in the Crimean War and American Civil War. Percussion ignition (from the 1820s–30s) replaced flint. Breech-loading designs — the Dreyse needle gun (1841), Sharps and others — increased rates of fire. Samuel Colt's revolvers (from 1836) and Oliver Winchester's lever-action repeaters (1860s) popularised multi-shot arms; Hiram Maxim's recoil-operated machine gun (1884) automated fire. Interchangeable-parts manufacturing, the "American system", underpinned mass production.

Smokeless powder and the world wars

Smokeless powder (Poudre B, 1884; cordite) enabled smaller, higher-velocity cartridges and magazine bolt-action rifles (Mauser, Lee–Enfield) that served through both world wars. The First World War combined machine guns, tanks and artillery; submachine guns (MP 18) and semi-automatic rifles (M1 Garand, 1936) appeared. The Second World War produced the first assault rifle, Germany's StG 44, and vast standardised production.

Modern firearms

Mikhail Kalashnikov's AK-47 (1947) became the most widely used military rifle in history; Eugene Stoner's AR-15/M16 family (1950s–60s) introduced widespread use of aluminium and polymers. Selective-fire intermediate-cartridge rifles dominate military service, while sporting, hunting and handgun designs proliferated in civilian markets. Debates over gun control, and technologies such as 3D printing of components, continue to shape the firearm's social history.
