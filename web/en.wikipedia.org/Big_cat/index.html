Big cat

The term big cat is typically used to refer to any of the five living members of the genus Panthera: the tiger, lion, jaguar, leopard, and snow leopard. In broader usage the term is also extended to other large felids such as the cougar (puma), cheetah, and Eurasian lynx, even though they are not members of Panthera. Big cats are apex predators native to the Americas, Africa, and Asia.

Characteristics

Members of Panthera share a distinctive morphology of the hyoid apparatus and larynx that, in the tiger, lion, jaguar, and leopard, enables roaring; the snow leopard cannot roar, a fact that once placed it in its own genus. Big cats have muscular bodies, powerful forelimbs, large canine teeth, and short, powerful jaws adapted for killing and consuming large prey. The tiger is the largest living felid, with large males exceeding 300 kg; the snow leopard is the smallest pantherine, typically 22–55 kg.

Distribution and habitat

Tigers and leopards range across Asia; leopards also inhabit much of sub-Saharan Africa, where lions are now largely confined. Jaguars live in the Americas from Mexico to northern Argentina, favouring wetlands and rainforest. Snow leopards occupy the mountain ranges of Central and South Asia. Cheetahs and cougars, often counted as big cats in the loose sense, are found in Africa and Iran, and throughout the Americas respectively.

Behaviour

Most big cats are solitary stalk-and-ambush hunters, active mainly at dawn, dusk, or night. The lion is the notable exception, living in social groups called prides. All are obligate carnivores requiring large home ranges, which brings them into conflict with humans over livestock and space.

Conservation

Every big cat species is threatened to some degree by habitat loss, prey depletion, poaching, and retaliatory killing. The tiger, lion, jaguar, leopard, and snow leopard are all listed on the IUCN Red List as Endangered, Vulnerable, or Near Threatened, and all are covered by CITES restrictions on international trade. Conservation efforts focus on protected areas, anti-poaching patrols, and corridors linking fragmented populations.
