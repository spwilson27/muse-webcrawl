Fearsome critters

Fearsome critters are a category of legendary creatures from North American folklore, particularly the tall tales told by lumberjacks working in logging camps across the United States and Canada during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. These imaginary animals were said to inhabit the deep forests of the Great Lakes region, the Pacific Northwest, and other logging frontiers, and stories about them served as entertainment, as a way of explaining strange sounds and occurrences in the woods, and as material for hazing new arrivals to the camps.

Background

The tradition grew up alongside the lumber industry. Isolated in remote camps for long winters, loggers developed a rich oral culture of exaggerated stories. Fearsome critters were typically described with deadpan seriousness to greenhorns, who were expected to believe in creatures such as the hodag or the splintercat. Many of the tales were later collected in print, notably in Henry Harrington Tryon's Fearsome Critters (1939), which catalogued dozens of the beasts with tongue-in-cheek natural-history descriptions, and in earlier works by lumberman and folklorist Charles E. Brown in Wisconsin.

Notable examples

  • Hodag — a fearsome creature of the Rhinelander, Wisconsin area, said to have the head of a frog, the grinning face of an elephant, thick legs ending in claws, and the back of a dinosaur.
  • Splintercat — a nocturnal cat that used its hard head to shatter trees in search of raccoons and honey.
  • Sidehill gouger — a grazing animal with legs shorter on one side, adapted to living on steep hillsides.
  • Jackalope — a jackrabbit with antelope horns, more associated with the American West.
  • Snallygaster — a dragon-like creature said to haunt Maryland.
  • Squonk — a sad, warty creature of the Pennsylvania hemlock forests that dissolves in its own tears when captured.
  • Teakettler — a dog-like creature that made a sound like a boiling teakettle and walked backwards.
  • Whirling whimpus — a gorilla-like creature that spun at high speed to bludgeon its prey.

Legacy

Fearsome critters remain a part of regional identity in the North Woods; the hodag, for example, is the mascot of Rhinelander, Wisconsin. The creatures have been compared to the "fearsome critters" of other logging and frontier traditions worldwide, and they continue to appear in children's literature, tourism, and modern collections of American folklore.
