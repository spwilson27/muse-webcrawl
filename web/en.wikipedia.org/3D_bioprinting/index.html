3D bioprinting

Three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting is the use of 3D printing-like techniques to deposit layers of living cells, growth factors, and biomaterials — collectively called bioinks — to fabricate structures resembling natural tissues. It is a central technology of tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, with the long-term goal of producing functional organs for transplantation.

Process

A typical workflow begins with imaging or designing the target structure, followed by the preparation of a bioink, commonly cells suspended in a hydrogel such as alginate, gelatin methacrylate, or collagen. The construct is deposited layer by layer using extrusion, inkjet, or laser-assisted printing, then matured in a bioreactor where the cells proliferate, remodel their surroundings, and develop tissue function. Major technical challenges include keeping cells alive during printing, achieving fine resolution, and forming the blood vessel networks (vascularisation) needed to sustain thick tissues.

Applications

Bioprinted skin, cartilage, and bone constructs have been demonstrated in laboratory settings, and simple tissues such as bladders built on printed or moulded scaffolds have been implanted in patients. Printed tissue models are also used commercially for drug testing and cosmetics testing, offering alternatives to animal experiments. Fully functional printed hearts, kidneys, and livers remain a long-term research objective.

Ethics and regulation

The prospect of printing human organs raises ethical questions about access, the commercialisation of body parts, and the enhancement of human capabilities, and regulators continue to develop frameworks for approving bioprinted products as medical devices or therapies.
