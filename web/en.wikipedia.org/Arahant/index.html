In Buddhism, an arahant (Pali) or arhat (Sanskrit) is a person who has attained enlightenment and achieved nibbāna (nirvana), having completely uprooted the defilements (kilesa) of greed, hatred, and delusion. The arahant is the fourth and highest of the four stages of awakening in early Buddhism and Theravada tradition, following stream-enterer, once-returner, and non-returner. Having completed the Noble Eightfold Path, an arahant is freed from the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra) and will not be reborn after death.

In Theravada Buddhism

The Pali Canon describes the arahant as one who has destroyed the ten fetters and the three poisons, and who possesses the knowledge of the destruction of the taints (āsavakkhaya-ñāṇa). The Buddha himself is described as an arahant in the canonical formula of homage — iti pi so bhagavā arahaṃ — and arahants such as Sāriputta and Moggallāna rank among his chief disciples. The canon distinguishes arahants liberated by wisdom from those who also possess the higher knowledges and psychic powers. After the Buddha's passing, the arahant ideal became central to the self-understanding of the Theravada tradition, exemplified by the five hundred arahants said to have convened the First Buddhist Council.

In Mahayana Buddhism

Mahayana texts regard the arahant's attainment as genuine but incomplete relative to the full Buddhahood pursued by the bodhisattva, and encourage practitioners to aim beyond arahantship. Mahayana sutras such as the Lotus Sutra teach that arahants will ultimately progress to Buddhahood. Historically, this contrast between the arahant and bodhisattva ideals was sometimes framed polemically in the term "Hīnayāna" ("Lesser Vehicle"), which Theravada traditions reject. In Chinese Buddhism the arhats (luohan) became popular devotional figures, especially the group of Eighteen Arhats, depicted as eccentric, wonder-working elders.

Etymology

The word derives from the Sanskrit root arh, "to be worthy", hence "the worthy one". A traditional folk etymology analyses it as ari (enemy) + han (to slay): "slayer of the foes", the foes being the defilements; this gloss is preserved in the Tibetan translation dgra bcom pa, "one who has conquered the enemy".
