Jin dynasty (266–420)

The Jin dynasty (Chinese: 晉朝; 266–420) was an imperial dynasty of China, founded by Sima Yan of the Sima clan. It is conventionally divided into the Western Jin (266–316), which briefly reunified China after the Three Kingdoms period, and the Eastern Jin (317–420), which ruled southern China after the loss of the north.


The Jin dynasty and its successors

Western Jin

Sima Yan, whose grandfather Sima Yi had dominated the Cao Wei court, forced the last Wei emperor to abdicate in 266 and reigned as Emperor Wu. In 280 Jin conquered Eastern Wu, reuniting China for the first time since the fall of Han. However, the enfeoffment of imperial princes with military commands led to the devastating War of the Eight Princes (291–306), which fatally weakened the dynasty. Xiongnu and other non-Han peoples rose in the north; Luoyang fell in 311 and Chang'an in 316, ending Western Jin.

Eastern Jin

The prince Sima Rui re-established the dynasty at Jiankang (modern Nanjing) in 317, ruling territory south of the Huai River in uneasy partnership with great refugee clans such as the Wang. In 383, Eastern Jin forces famously defeated the Former Qin army of Fu Jian at the Battle of Fei River, preventing northern conquest. The dynasty was nonetheless plagued by aristocratic factionalism and rebellions, and in 420 the general Liu Yu deposed the last emperor and founded the Liu Song dynasty, beginning the Northern and Southern dynasties period.

Culture

The Jin era saw the flourishing of calligraphy (Wang Xizhi, the "Sage of Calligraphy"), landscape poetry, and Neo-Daoist (xuanxue) philosophy, as well as the southward spread of Buddhism and the colonisation of the Yangtze delta.
