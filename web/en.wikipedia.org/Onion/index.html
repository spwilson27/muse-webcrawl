Onion

Scientific classification
Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Asparagales
Family: Amaryllidaceae
Genus: Allium
Species: A. cepa

The onion (Allium cepa L., from Latin cepa meaning "onion"), also known as the bulb onion or common onion, is a vegetable that is the most widely cultivated species of the genus Allium. The onion plant has been grown and selectively bred in cultivation for well over 7,000 years. It is a biennial plant but is usually grown as an annual. Modern varieties grow to 15 to 45 cm tall, with hollow blue-green leaves and a bulb of concentric fleshy scales that serves as a food store through the winter.

Cultivation and uses

Onions are cultivated worldwide. China and India are among the largest producers. Common onions are normally available in three colour varieties: yellow or brown onions, red (or purple) onions, and white onions. They have a pungent flavour and are used fresh or cooked in a wide range of savoury dishes; they can also be pickled or used as a base for sauces and chutneys. The pungency comes from sulfur compounds released when cells are cut, which also produce the vapour that makes eyes water when an onion is chopped.

History

The geographic origin of the onion is uncertain, though it is thought to come from Central Asia. Onions were depicted in ancient Egyptian art and were placed in tombs; they were also a staple in ancient Greek and Roman cooking. Because onions store well and are easy to transport, they became a dietary mainstay across Europe in the Middle Ages and were carried to the Americas by early European settlers.

Health and chemistry

Onions consist mostly of water, with carbohydrates, small amounts of protein and fat, and useful quantities of vitamin C and dietary fibre. They contain flavonoids such as quercetin. Cutting an onion releases syn-propanethial-S-oxide, a lachrymatory agent that irritates the eyes and stimulates tears.
