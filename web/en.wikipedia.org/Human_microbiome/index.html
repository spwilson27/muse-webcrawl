Human microbiome

Human microbiome
ComprisesBacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses
Major sitesGut, skin, mouth, vagina
Estimated cells~38 trillion bacteria
Key projectHuman Microbiome Project (2007–2016)

The human microbiome is the aggregate of all microorganisms — bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and other microbes — that live on and in the human body, together with their genetic material. The largest and best-studied community is the gut microbiota, which aids digestion, synthesises vitamins, trains the immune system and resists colonisation by pathogens.

Composition and development

Colonisation begins at birth and is shaped by delivery mode, feeding, antibiotics and environment; the community stabilises in early childhood but continues to vary between individuals far more than the human genome does. Different body sites host characteristic communities adapted to local conditions such as moisture, pH and oxygen.

Health and disease

Disturbances of the microbiome (dysbiosis) are associated with inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, allergies, colorectal cancer and recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection — the last being treatable by faecal microbiota transplantation. The NIH Human Microbiome Project mapped microbial diversity in healthy adults and spurred therapies including probiotics and live biotherapeutics, though many popular claims outrun the evidence.
