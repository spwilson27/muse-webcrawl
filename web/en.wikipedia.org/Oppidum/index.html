An oppidum (plural oppida) is a large fortified settlement of Iron Age Europe, associated especially with the Celtic La Tène culture of the last two centuries BC. The term is Latin: Julius Caesar used it in his Commentaries on the Gallic War for the fortified towns of Gaul, and archaeologists have adopted it for similar sites from Iberia and Britain to Hungary and Poland.

Characteristics

Oppida were far larger than earlier hillforts—from tens of hectares to more than 1,000 hectares at sites such as Manching in Bavaria or Ulaca in Spain. They occupied defensible positions, often hilltops or river bends, and were protected by massive ramparts. Caesar described the characteristic murus gallicus: a wall of stone facings and rubble fill laced together with horizontal timbers pinned by iron nails, resistant both to battering rams and to fire.

Within the walls, excavations reveal planned streets, houses, workshops for metalworking and coin production, temples, and storage pits—evidence that oppida functioned as centres of craft production, trade, administration, and religion for the tribal states (civitates) of late Iron Age Europe. Imported Mediterranean goods, including Roman wine amphorae, attest to far-reaching exchange networks.

History

Oppida flourished in the second and first centuries BC. Many were abandoned or destroyed during the Roman conquests—Alesia, where Vercingetorix surrendered in 52 BC, and Bibracte, the Aeduan capital whose population was later moved to Roman Augustodunum (Autun), are famous examples. Some oppida became the nuclei of Roman and medieval towns; others were deserted. In Britain, sites such as Colchester (Camulodunum) and St Albans (Verulamium) fit the pattern of large, lowland "territorial oppida."
