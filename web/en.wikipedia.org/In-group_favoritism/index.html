In-group favoritism

In-group favoritism
Also called: In-group bias
Field: Social psychology
Related paradigm: Minimal group paradigm

In-group favoritism, sometimes known as in-group–out-group bias, is a pattern of favouring members of one's in-group over out-group members. This can be expressed in evaluation of others, allocation of resources, and many other ways.

Research

The effect was demonstrated in the minimal group experiments of Henri Tajfel in the 1970s: participants divided by the flimsiest criteria still allocated more rewards to anonymous members of their own group. Subsequent research found the effect in children, across cultures, and even in implicit measures of attitude.

Explanations

Social identity theory attributes favoritism to the motive for positive self-esteem through group membership. Other accounts emphasise expectations of reciprocity from in-group members, perceived similarity, and the cognitive ease of categorisation. Research also distinguishes in-group love (favouring insiders) from out-group hate (actively harming outsiders), noting the two do not always occur together.

Real-world implications

In-group favoritism contributes to discrimination in workplaces and institutions, preferential treatment in politics, and sports fandom rivalries, but also underpins charitable giving and mutual aid within communities. See also In-group and out-group and Implicit bias.
