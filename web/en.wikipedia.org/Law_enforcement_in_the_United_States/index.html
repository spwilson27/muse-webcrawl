Law enforcement in the United States

Law enforcement in the United States is highly decentralized, carried out by roughly 18,000 federal, state, county, and local agencies employing more than 800,000 full-time sworn officers. Unlike many countries, the United States has no single national police force; policing is primarily a responsibility of local governments, a structure rooted in the federal system of government and the Tenth Amendment.

Organization

Local and county

The great majority of officers work for municipal police departments — ranging from the New York City Police Department, with tens of thousands of officers, to thousands of small departments with only a handful — and for county sheriffs' offices. Sheriffs are typically elected officials responsible for county law enforcement, operating jails, and serving court process. Most counties also maintain a county police or public safety department in larger metropolitan areas.

State

Every state maintains a statewide agency, usually a state police or highway patrol, responsible for traffic enforcement on state highways and providing investigative and support services statewide; some, like the Texas Rangers, have distinctive historical roles.

Federal

Federal law enforcement officers, numbering over 100,000, work for agencies including the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF), U.S. Marshals Service, Secret Service, Customs and Border Protection, and Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). Their jurisdiction is limited to federal offenses.

History

Formal policing began with colonial constables and night watches; Boston established one of the first modern police departments in 1838, modeled partly on London's Metropolitan Police (1829). The 19th century saw expansion intertwined in the South with slave patrols and in the West with posses and federal marshals. The 20th century brought professionalization, the automobile patrol, forensic science, and — after eras of corruption and political control — reform movements; the late 20th century saw the rise of community policing and, later, data-driven approaches.

Issues and oversight

American policing is the subject of continuing debate over use of force, racial disparities, qualified immunity, militarization, civilian oversight, and recruitment. Officer conduct is governed by constitutional law, notably the Fourth Amendment on searches and seizures, and landmark Supreme Court decisions such as Miranda v. Arizona (1966) and Terry v. Ohio (1968). The federal government can investigate local departments for patterns or practices of civil rights violations.
