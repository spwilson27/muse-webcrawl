Sputnik 1 (Russian: Спутник-1, "Satellite 1") was the first artificial Earth satellite. It was launched into an elliptical low Earth orbit by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957 as part of the Soviet space programme. It sent a radio signal back to Earth for three weeks until its transmitter batteries ran out, then fell into the atmosphere and burned up on 4 January 1958.

The satellite

Sputnik 1 was a polished metal sphere 58 cm (23 in) in diameter, filled with nitrogen, with four external radio antennae trailing behind. Its 1-watt transmitter broadcast distinctive "beep-beep" signals at 20.005 and 40.002 MHz, which were received by radio operators around the world; variations in the signal let scientists probe the density and temperature of the upper atmosphere and the properties of the ionosphere. The satellite was built under the direction of Sergei Korolev's OKB-1 bureau as a simplified substitute ("PS-1", Prosteishiy Sputnik — "Simplest Satellite") for a heavier scientific satellite that was not ready in time.

Impact

The launch came as a profound shock in the United States, where it triggered the "Sputnik crisis": fears of Soviet technological and military superiority (the R-7 launcher was also the first intercontinental ballistic missile). The political response included the creation of NASA and DARPA in 1958, greatly increased federal funding for science and engineering education (the National Defense Education Act), and the acceleration of the American satellite and missile programmes. Sputnik 1's launch is generally taken as the beginning of the Space Age and of the Space Race.

Aftermath

Sputnik 2, carrying the dog Laika, followed a month later (3 November 1957); the first American satellite, Explorer 1, reached orbit on 1 February 1958. Backup units and replicas of Sputnik 1 are displayed in museums worldwide, and its name — Russian for "fellow traveller" — entered many languages.
