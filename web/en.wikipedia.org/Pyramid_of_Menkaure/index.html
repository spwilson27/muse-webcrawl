Pyramid of Menkaure

The Pyramid of Menkaure is the smallest of the three main pyramids of the Giza pyramid complex in Egypt. It was built during the Fourth Dynasty to serve as the tomb of the pharaoh Menkaure (Mycerinus, reigned c. 2530 BC). Standing at the southern end of the Giza plateau alongside the larger pyramids of Khufu and Khafre, it is distinguished by its partly granite casing and by the fine statues of the king recovered from its valley temple.

Dimensions and construction

The pyramid was originally about 65 metres (213 feet) tall on a base of roughly 103 metres (338 feet) square — a fraction of the volume of Khufu's pyramid. Its lower courses, to about the sixteenth course, were cased in red Aswan granite, much of it left rough and unfinished, while the upper part was cased in fine white Tura limestone. The haste or incompleteness of the granite work, together with the completion of the mortuary temple in mudbrick and wood, suggests that Menkaure died before the complex was finished and that his successor Shepseskaf completed it in cheaper materials.

Interior

A descending corridor leads to chambers below the pyramid, including a burial chamber lined with granite. A basalt sarcophagus, richly carved with palace-façade panelling, was found there in the 1830s; it was lost at sea in 1838 when the ship Beatrice sank while transporting it to England. A wooden anthropoid coffin inscribed for Menkaure, now in the British Museum, proved to be a much later, Saite-period replacement burial, together with bones of the same late date.

Complex and finds

South of the main pyramid stand three subsidiary "queens' pyramids". Excavations by George Reisner in the early twentieth century recovered celebrated statues from the valley temple, including triads showing Menkaure flanked by the goddess Hathor and nome deities, and a superb greywacke dyad of the king and a queen, now divided between the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, and the Egyptian Museum in Cairo. In the late twelfth century, the Ayyubid sultan al-Aziz Uthman attempted to demolish the Giza pyramids, beginning with Menkaure's; his workmen's gash in the north face, still visible, was abandoned after eight months as too costly.
