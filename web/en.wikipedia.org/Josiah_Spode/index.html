Josiah Spode

Josiah Spode (23 March 1733 – 18 August 1797) was an English potter and the founder of the English pottery works that became Spode. He is credited with the development of bone china and with innovations in transfer printing on earthenware.

Life and work

Born in Stoke-on-Trent, Staffordshire, into a poor family, Spode was apprenticed to the potter Thomas Whieldon and later worked for William Banks. In 1770 he acquired the factory at Stoke where he had once worked, and built it into one of the largest potteries in the Staffordshire Potteries.

Spode perfected the process of underglaze blue transfer printing, enabling the mass production of decorated earthenware in patterns such as the famous "Blue Italian," introduced by the firm around 1816. In the 1790s, Spode (and his son Josiah Spode II) standardised the bone china body — a mixture of bone ash, china clay, and china stone — which was whiter, stronger, and more translucent than rival porcelains and became the standard English porcelain body.

Legacy

The Spode business continued under his son and later owners; the Spode Museum Trust preserves the factory's pattern books and collections. Josiah Spode is regarded alongside Josiah Wedgwood as one of the great figures of the Staffordshire pottery industry.
