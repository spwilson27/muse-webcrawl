Thermocline

A thermocline is the transition layer in a body of water where temperature changes more rapidly with depth than it does in the layers above or below.

A thermocline (also known as the thermal layer or the metalimnion in lakes) is a distinct layer based on temperature within a large body of fluid (e.g. water or air) with a high gradient of distinct temperature differences associated with depth. In the ocean, the thermocline divides the upper mixed layer from the calm deep water below.

Depending largely on season, latitude and turbulent mixing by wind, thermoclines may be a semi-permanent feature of the body of water in which they occur, or they may form temporarily in response to phenomena such as the radiative heating or cooling of surface water during the day or night. Factors that affect the depth and thickness of a thermocline include seasonal weather variations, latitude and local environmental conditions, such as tides and currents.

In the ocean

Most of the temperature change with depth occurs in the transition zone between the surface zone and the deep zone. This transition zone is called the thermocline. The deep zone, which contains about 90 percent of the ocean's volume, is dark and cold with an average temperature of around 2 °C (35.6 °F). The surface zone receives the most light and therefore has the highest temperature; the density of water increases as the temperature decreases, until it reaches its freezing point.

In the thermocline, temperature decreases rapidly from the mixed upper layer of the ocean (called the epipelagic zone) to much colder deep water in the thermocline (mesopelagic zone). Below 3,300 feet to a depth of about 13,100 feet, water temperature remains constant. At depths below 13,100 feet, the temperature ranges from near freezing to just above the freezing point of water as depth increases.

In lakes

In lakes, thermoclines are often observed as a result of seasonal stratification. During the summer, warm surface water (the epilimnion) sits atop the cold deep water (the hypolimnion), separated by the metalimnion, where temperature falls steeply. This stratification can limit the mixing of oxygen and nutrients between layers. Twice a year, typically in spring and autumn, surface cooling or warming equalises temperatures and the water column mixes in an event called lake turnover.

Other applications

Thermoclines are important in scuba diving, where a diver may feel cold water as they descend through the layer, and in submarine warfare, because a thermocline can refract sonar signals and conceal a submarine from detection. Thermocline-based energy generation, such as ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC), exploits the temperature difference between warm surface water and cold deep water to drive a heat engine.
