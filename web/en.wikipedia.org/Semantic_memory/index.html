Semantic memory

This article is about the type of long-term memory. For other uses, see Episodic memory.


Networks of associated concepts are a common model of semantic memory.

Semantic memory refers to general world knowledge that humans have accumulated throughout their lives. This general knowledge — interwoven in experience and dependent on culture — includes facts, ideas, meaning, and concepts. Semantic memory is one of the two main types of explicit or declarative memory, the other being episodic memory, which is memory of personal experiences and specific events.

Semantic memory appears to be supported largely by perirhinal structures in the brain. Unlike episodic memory, semantic memory is conscious knowledge about the world that is not tied to the time or place of learning; for example, knowing that Paris is the capital of France, or that a dog has four legs, without remembering when that information was acquired.

History

The concept of semantic memory was first introduced following a conference in 1972 by Warrington and Endel Tulving. Tulving proposed a distinction within declarative memory between semantic and episodic memory in his book Elements of Episodic Memory (1983). The idea was influenced by earlier work in linguistics and by the computer metaphors of cognitive psychology.

Models

Early models, such as the semantic network model of Collins and Quillian (1969), represented concepts as nodes in a hierarchical network connected by links, with retrieval explained by spreading activation. Later feature-comparison models, such as that of Smith, Shoben and Rips, represented concepts as sets of defining and characteristic features. Modern connectionist and distributed models propose that semantic knowledge emerges from patterns of activation distributed across neural networks rather than from discrete stored symbols.

Neural correlates

Neuropsychological evidence indicates that damage to the anterior temporal lobes impairs semantic knowledge, as in semantic dementia, whereas damage to the hippocampus and medial temporal structures more severely impairs episodic memory. Semantic memory is also studied through the N400 component of event-related potentials, an electrophysiological response sensitive to semantic incongruity.
