Heterophony

Heterophonic texture in a traditional ensemble

In music, heterophony is a type of texture characterised by the simultaneous variation of a single melodic line. A single melody is performed by two or more voices, each of which embellishes, simplifies or otherwise varies the line in its own way while all remain recognisably versions of the same tune.

Characteristics

The term was introduced by Plato and revived by musicologists to describe textures common in many non-Western traditions. Heterophony contrasts with monophony (a single unvaried line), homophony (melody with chordal accompaniment) and polyphony (independent lines). Because the variations are often improvised according to each instrument's idiom, the resulting texture is fluid and can be difficult to notate in Western staff notation.

Examples

Heterophony is fundamental to Thai classical music, Indonesian gamelan, Japanese gagaku, Middle Eastern ensemble music and much traditional music of China, Korea and Vietnam. It also appears in folk traditions such as Gaelic psalm singing in the Hebrides, some New Orleans jazz, and in passages of Western art music by composers such as Benjamin Britten, who used heterophonic textures deliberately in works including Curlew River and the War Requiem.
