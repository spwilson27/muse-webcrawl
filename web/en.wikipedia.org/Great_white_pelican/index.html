Great white pelican

The great white pelican (Pelecanus onocrotalus), also known as the eastern white pelican, rosy pelican or white pelican, is a bird in the pelican family. It breeds from southeastern Europe through Asia and Africa in swamps and shallow lakes. Carl Linnaeus described the species in 1758.

Description

The great white pelican is one of the largest flying birds. Its plumage is predominantly white, with black primaries visible in flight. The huge bill is yellow-orange with a large throat pouch used to scoop up fish; during the breeding season adults develop a pinkish tinge to the plumage and a yellow-orange patch on the breast. Males are larger than females and have longer bills. Juveniles are grey-brown and become whiter with age.

Distribution and habitat

The species breeds colonially on islands in shallow lakes, lagoons and deltas, notably including the Danube Delta, lakes of the African Rift Valley and wetlands across central Asia. Northern populations are migratory, wintering in Africa, India and southeast Asia; African populations are largely resident. Key habitats include the Banc d'Arguin in Mauritania, Lake Turkana, and deltas of major rivers.

Behaviour

Great white pelicans fish cooperatively: groups form a line or semicircle, beating the water with their wings to drive fish into shallow water, then scooping them up with their pouches. They do not plunge-dive like the brown pelican. Breeding colonies may number thousands of pairs; the nest is a heap of vegetation on the ground, and two eggs are usually laid, though typically only one chick survives — often the stronger kills or outcompetes its sibling. Chicks are fed regurgitated fish and gather in crèches.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the species as Least Concern, with a global population estimated at several hundred thousand individuals. Threats include wetland drainage, disturbance at colonies, collision with power lines, and persecution where pelicans are blamed for competing with fisheries.
