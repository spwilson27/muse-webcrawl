Indian removal was the United States government policy of forced displacement of self-governing Native American peoples from their ancestral homelands in the eastern United States to lands west of the Mississippi River — specifically, to a designated Indian Territory (roughly, present-day Oklahoma). The policy was embodied in the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and carried out principally during the 1830s.

Origins

Proposals to exchange eastern tribal lands for western territory dated to the Jefferson administration, and treaties ceding Native land had been concluded since the early republic. After the War of 1812 and the Creek War, land hunger in the cotton-growing South, the discovery of gold in Georgia in 1828, and state laws extending jurisdiction over tribal lands (notably Georgia's over the Cherokee) sharpened the conflict. Southern states demanded that the federal government extinguish Native title within their borders.

The removals

Between 1830 and about 1850, more than 60,000 Native Americans — principally the Choctaw (1831–34), Muscogee (Creek) (1834–37), Chickasaw (1837–47), Cherokee (1838–39) and Seminole (1832–58) — were removed to Indian Territory, some in chains and nearly all under military supervision. Thousands died en route from disease, starvation and exposure; the Cherokee route became known as the Trail of Tears (Nunna daul Tsuny, "the trail where they cried"). Northern peoples, including the Shawnee, Ottawa, Potawatomi, Sauk and Fox, were also removed to Kansas and later Oklahoma; the Ponca removal of 1877 and the Nez Perce flight of the same year extended the pattern to the Great Plains.

Resistance and law

Tribes resisted through diplomacy, petitions, litigation and war. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831) and Worcester v. Georgia (1832), the Supreme Court recognised tribes as political communities whose lands state law could not reach, but the rulings were not enforced against Georgia. Armed resistance included the Black Hawk War (1832) and the Second Seminole War (1835–1842), the costliest Indian war in U.S. history.

Consequences

Removal opened some 25 million acres in the Southeast to white settlement and cotton slavery, devastated the removed nations, and reconstituted them in Indian Territory, where they rebuilt governments, schools and newspapers — only to face further land losses through allotment and the dissolution of tribal governments around Oklahoma statehood (1907). Modern scholarship and tribal nations regard removal as ethnic cleansing; the United States formally apologised to Native peoples in 2009.
