Stem cell

Stem cells are cells that can differentiate into other types of cells and can also divide in a self-renewing process to produce more of the same type of stem cell. In mammals, there are two broad categories of stem cells: embryonic stem cells, which are isolated from the inner cell mass of blastocysts, and adult stem cells, which are found in various tissues. Advanced methods also allow scientists to reprogram adult cells into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs).

Properties

Stem cells are defined by two properties: self-renewal (the ability to divide and produce more stem cells indefinitely) and potency (the ability to differentiate into specialized cell types). Potency ranges from totipotent (able to form an entire organism, like the zygote) through pluripotent (able to form all cell types of the body, like embryonic stem cells) to multipotent adult stem cells such as hematopoietic stem cells of the bone marrow, which produce all blood cell types.

Medical applications

Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (bone marrow transplant) has been used for decades to treat leukemias, lymphomas, and immune disorders. Stem cell therapies are under active investigation for conditions including spinal cord injury, macular degeneration, diabetes, and heart disease. In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka showed that four transcription factors could reprogram adult mouse cells into pluripotent stem cells, a breakthrough that earned a share of the 2012 Nobel Prize and reduced reliance on embryos.

Controversy

The use of human embryonic stem cells raised ethical debates because deriving cell lines destroyed embryos; these concerns varied by country and religion and led to differing regulations and funding restrictions. Induced pluripotent stem cells largely avoid this controversy, though debates continue over cloning, chimeras, and germline editing.
