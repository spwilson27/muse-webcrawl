Planet Nine

StatusHypothetical (undiscovered)
Proposed byKonstantin Batygin and Mike Brown (2016)
Predicted massRoughly 5–10 Earth masses
Predicted orbitSemi-major axis ~500–700 AU; period ~10,000–20,000 years

Planet Nine is a hypothetical giant planet in the outer Solar System, proposed to explain the unusual clustering of the orbits of a group of extreme trans-Neptunian objects (ETNOs). The hypothesis was set out in 2016 by Caltech astronomers Konstantin Batygin and Michael E. Brown, who showed that the clustered orbits of distant Kuiper belt objects would be unlikely to arise by chance and could be shepherded by a planet of roughly five to ten Earth masses on a wide, eccentric, inclined orbit.

Evidence and predictions

The principal evidence is the alignment in physical space of the perihelia and orbital planes of the most distant known Kuiper belt objects, the existence of objects with orbits nearly perpendicular to the Solar System's plane, and the tilt they would collectively explain in the Sun's spin axis. The hypothesised planet would have a semi-major axis of several hundred astronomical units and never come closer to the Sun than about 200 AU, making it extremely faint — far beyond Pluto's average distance of 39 AU. Later analyses have debated both the statistical significance of the clustering (some surveys argue it is an observational selection effect) and the required planet parameters, with updated fits favouring a mass near 6 Earth masses.

Searches

No direct detection has been confirmed. Searches have used the Subaru Telescope's Hyper Suprime-Cam, Pan-STARRS, the Dark Energy Survey and infrared sky surveys such as WISE, which rule out a Saturn-sized body over much of the sky. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time is expected to either detect Planet Nine or discover enough new distant objects to test the clustering decisively.

Alternative explanations

Proposed alternatives include the combined gravity of a massive, self-gravitating debris disk in the distant Solar System, a close stellar flyby in the Sun's birth cluster, and modifications of gravity. The hypothesis continues a long history of predicted outer planets, from Percival Lowell's "Planet X" — which led to Pluto's discovery in 1930 — to earlier claims about the orbits of Uranus that led to Neptune's discovery in 1846.
