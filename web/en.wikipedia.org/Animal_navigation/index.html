Animal navigation

Animal navigation is the ability of many animals to find their way accurately without maps or instruments. Birds such as the Arctic tern, insects such as the monarch butterfly, and fish such as the salmon regularly migrate thousands of miles to and from their breeding grounds, and many other species navigate effectively over shorter distances.

History of research

Charles Darwin proposed in 1873 that animals might navigate by dead reckoning, accumulating information about their outward journey. In the twentieth century, Gustav Kramer showed that starlings use the sun as a compass, and later work by the Kramers' successors demonstrated that birds can also use the stars and the Earth's magnetic field. Karl von Frisch decoded the waggle dance of honey bees, showing that bees communicate the direction and distance of food sources relative to the sun.

Mechanisms

Several mechanisms are used, often in combination. Path integration (dead reckoning) keeps a running vector back to a starting point. Celestial cues include the sun, the pattern of polarised light in the sky, and star patterns at night. Many animals detect the Earth's magnetic field through magnetoreception, possibly involving cryptochrome proteins in the retina or magnetite particles. Landmarks, odours, sounds including infrasound, and memory of routes all contribute.

Examples

Homing pigeons navigate home over unfamiliar terrain, apparently using an olfactory map as well as magnetic and visual cues. Sea turtles return to the beaches where they hatched. Wildebeest, monarch butterflies, and eels undertake migrations of extraordinary precision, some spanning entire ocean basins.
