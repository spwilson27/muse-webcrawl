Coping

Coping refers to the conscious, voluntary efforts — cognitive, emotional and behavioural — that people make to manage, tolerate or reduce stress and conflict. In the influential transactional model of Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman (1984), coping is the process by which a person appraises a demand as exceeding their resources and responds to manage the problem or the emotions it arouses.

Types of coping

Problem-focused coping aims to change the stressful situation itself: planning, problem-solving, seeking instrumental support or removing the stressor. Emotion-focused coping aims to regulate the feelings the situation produces: seeking emotional support, reframing, relaxation, humour, prayer or acceptance. A third, less adaptive group of strategies — avoidant coping, including denial, distraction and substance use — can reduce distress briefly but often worsens outcomes when used habitually.

Appraisal and resources

Lazarus distinguished primary appraisal (is this a threat, harm or challenge?) from secondary appraisal (what can I do about it?). Coping effectiveness depends on the fit between strategy and situation: problem-focused coping helps most when the stressor is controllable, while emotion-focused strategies may be preferable for uncontrollable stressors such as bereavement. Resources include health, social support, self-efficacy and material means.

Measurement and health outcomes

Coping is measured with instruments such as the Ways of Coping Questionnaire and the COPE inventory of Carver and colleagues. Research links flexible, approach-oriented coping with better psychological adjustment and physical health, and chronic avoidant or ruminative coping with anxiety, depression and poorer immune and cardiovascular outcomes.
