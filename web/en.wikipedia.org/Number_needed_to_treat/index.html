Number needed to treat

FieldEpidemiology, evidence-based medicine
FormulaNNT = 1 / ARR
Introduced1988 (Laupacis, Sackett & Roberts)

The number needed to treat (NNT) is an epidemiological measure used to communicate the effectiveness of a health-care intervention, typically a medicine. It is the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction (ARR): the average number of patients who must be treated for one additional patient to benefit (or for one fewer to experience a harmful outcome) compared with a control treatment over a specified period.

Interpretation

An NNT of 1 means every treated patient benefits; the ideal NNT is therefore 1, and larger values indicate a less effective intervention. For example, if a drug reduces the risk of an event from 8% to 5%, the ARR is 3% and the NNT is about 33 — thirty-three patients must be treated for one event to be prevented. The NNT always depends on the outcome measured, the baseline risk, and the time horizon, so values are only comparable across treatments when those conditions match. The analogous measure for harm is the number needed to harm (NNH).

History and use

The measure was introduced by Laupacis, Sackett, and Roberts in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1988 and has become a standard summary in clinical trials, systematic reviews, and patient decision aids because it expresses treatment effects in natural frequencies that clinicians and patients find easier to grasp than relative risks.

Criticism

Critics note that confidence intervals for the NNT behave awkwardly when the ARR is near zero (the interval passes through infinity), and that the NNT can mislead when baseline risks differ between a trial population and an individual patient.
