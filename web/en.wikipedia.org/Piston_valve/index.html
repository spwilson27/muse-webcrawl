Piston valve

A piston valve is a device used to control the motion of a fluid along a tube or pipe by means of a piston that slides inside a chamber. Piston valves are used in a variety of applications, most notably in steam engines, where they admit steam into, and exhaust it from, the cylinder in alternation, and in brass musical instruments, where they change the effective length of the tubing and hence the pitch.

Steam engines

In steam locomotives, piston valves replaced the earlier slide valve because they are inherently better balanced against steam pressure and suffer less wear, especially with high-pressure superheated steam. Each piston valve consists of two piston heads, joined by a rod, sliding in a cylindrical steam chest above or beside the cylinder. The valve is driven by the valve gear (such as Walschaerts or Stephenson gear) and its travel determines the admission, cut-off, release, and compression events of the engine's cycle.

Brass instruments

On trumpets, cornets, French horns (in some designs), euphoniums, and tubas, piston valves are operated by spring-loaded fingers keys. Pressing a piston diverts the air column through additional loops of tubing, lowering the pitch by a specific interval — typically a whole tone for the first valve, a semitone for the second, and a minor third for the third. Piston valves were patented in several forms in the 1830s and 1840s, notably by François Périnet, whose design of 1839 became the standard.

Other uses

Piston valves are also used in some hydraulic and pneumatic control systems, and in reciprocating pumps, where their low leakage and ability to handle abrasive or dirty fluids are advantages.
