Celestial pole

The north and south celestial poles are the two points in the sky where Earth's axis of rotation, indefinitely extended, intersects the celestial sphere. The north celestial pole currently lies near the star Polaris, and the south celestial pole lies in the faint constellation Octans, near the star Sigma Octantis.

Observation

Because Earth rotates about its axis, the stars appear to circle the celestial poles once per sidereal day. The altitude of the visible celestial pole above the horizon equals the observer's latitude. Long-exposure photographs taken facing the pole show concentric star trails centred on the pole.

Precession

Due to axial precession, the celestial poles trace circles of radius about 23.5° on the celestial sphere over a period of roughly 26,000 years. Polaris has not always been the pole star: in ancient Egyptian times Thuban held that role, and in about 12,000 years Vega will lie near the north celestial pole.

Navigation

The north celestial pole's fixed position made Polaris a key navigational reference in the Northern Hemisphere for determining latitude.
