Fitness (biology)

Fitness
FieldEvolutionary biology, population genetics
Symbolw
MeasuresRelative contribution of a genotype to the next generation

In evolutionary biology, fitness is the quantitative representation of an individual's reproductive success — roughly, how many of its genes are passed on to subsequent generations. A genotype's fitness is the average number of offspring (weighted by their own expected success) contributed by individuals carrying that genotype relative to others in the population. Fitness is the central currency of natural selection: genotypes with higher fitness tend to increase in frequency over generations.

Definitions

Absolute fitness is the ratio of the number of individuals with a genotype after selection to the number before selection. Relative fitness normalises these values against the most successful genotype, which is conventionally assigned a fitness of 1; the shortfall of other genotypes is expressed by the selection coefficient s, where w = 1 − s. Fitness combines survival to reproductive age, mating success, and fecundity, and is always defined relative to a particular environment — a trait that is advantageous in one setting may be neutral or harmful in another.

Inclusive fitness

W. D. Hamilton's theory of inclusive fitness (1964) extends the concept beyond an individual's own offspring to include effects on the reproduction of genetic relatives, weighted by relatedness. It explains the evolution of altruistic behaviour, such as sterile worker castes in social insects, through kin selection: a gene can spread by helping copies of itself in other bodies.

Fitness landscapes

Sewall Wright's metaphor of the fitness landscape pictures genotypes as points on a surface whose height is fitness. Populations climb toward peaks by selection, but may become stranded on local optima, separated from higher peaks by valleys of low fitness that selection alone cannot cross; genetic drift and mutation help populations explore the landscape.

Measurement and complications

Empirically, fitness is estimated from components such as survival rates, clutch size, and lifetime reproductive success, often in laboratory populations of fast-breeding organisms. Frequency-dependent selection, in which a genotype's fitness depends on how common it is, and density-dependent effects complicate simple models. Fitness is a predictive, statistical property of genotypes, not a measure of health, strength, or complexity — a point often lost in the phrase "survival of the fittest", coined by Herbert Spencer and adopted by Darwin.
