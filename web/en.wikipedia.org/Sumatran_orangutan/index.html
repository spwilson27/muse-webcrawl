The Sumatran orangutan (Pongo abelii) is one of three species of orangutan, found only in the north of the Indonesian island of Sumatra. It is rarer than the Bornean orangutan but more common than the Tapanuli orangutan, described as a separate species in 2017. It is listed as Critically Endangered.

Description

Males stand about 1.4 m tall and weigh around 50–90 kg; fully mature "flanged" males develop prominent cheek pads and long hair. Females are smaller, around 30–50 kg, with shaggy reddish fur. Compared with the Bornean species, Sumatran orangutans are thinner-faced, with longer, paler red hair.

Behaviour and ecology

Sumatran orangutans are highly arboreal, spending nearly all their lives in the rainforest canopy, and are more social than their Bornean relatives, partly because mass fruiting of figs allows groups to gather. They are primarily frugivorous and are known for tool use, including using sticks to extract seeds and insects. Females have the longest interbirth interval of any land mammal, about nine years.

Conservation

The population is estimated at roughly 14,000 individuals and is threatened by deforestation for oil palm plantations and logging, road building and hunting. Much of its remaining habitat lies in the Leuser Ecosystem of Aceh and North Sumatra.
