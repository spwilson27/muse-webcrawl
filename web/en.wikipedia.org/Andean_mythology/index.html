Andean mythology

Andean mythology encompasses the beliefs and ritual traditions of the indigenous peoples of the central Andes—among them the Quechua, Aymara, and earlier cultures such as Wari and Tiwanaku—culminating in the religion of the Inca Empire and surviving, transformed, in present-day folk practice in Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and neighbouring regions.

Deities and cosmology

The creator god Viracocha was said to have fashioned the sun, moon, stars, and humanity before journeying across the world and departing over the Pacific. Inti, the sun god, was the divine ancestor of the Inca emperors; his consort was the moon goddess Mama Killa. Pachamama, the earth mother, received offerings for fertility and harvest, while mountain lords called apus guarded local communities. The Inca cosmos was divided into the upper world (Hanan Pacha), this world (Kay Pacha), and the inner world (Ukhu Pacha).

Sacred landscape

Andean religion was inscribed on the landscape: shrines called huacas included springs, stones, mummies, and mountain peaks, linked around Cuzco by the radiating ceque lines. Pilgrimage, sacrifice (including, rarely, the capacocha child sacrifices on high summits), divination, and calendrical festivals such as Inti Raymi sustained reciprocity—ayni—between humans, gods, and nature.

After the conquest

Spanish extirpation campaigns suppressed public cult, yet Andean beliefs fused with Catholicism in syncretic festivals, the cult of saints, and continuing offerings to Pachamama, notably each August. Oral traditions recorded in the Huarochirí Manuscript preserve pre-conquest myths of floods, culture heroes, and local deities.
