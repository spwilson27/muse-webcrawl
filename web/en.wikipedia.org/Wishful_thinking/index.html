Wishful thinking

Wishful thinking is the formation of beliefs based on what it is pleasing to imagine, rather than on evidence, rationality, or reality. It is a cognitive bias in which desires directly influence the appraisal of facts: people tend to interpret ambiguous information in line with the outcome they want and to overestimate the probability of positive events.

Psychology

Psychologists describe wishful thinking as a form of motivated reasoning, related to the optimism bias and the desirability effect. Experiments show that participants who stand to gain from an outcome rate it as more likely, and that the effect strengthens when outcomes feel controllable or are perceived at a distance. Mechanisms proposed include selective attention to favorable evidence, easier recall of positive instances, and biased interpretation of statistics.

As a fallacy

In logic and rhetoric, appealing to wishful thinking is an informal fallacy — arguing that something is true because one hopes it is, or false because one fears it. It is closely related to the appeal to emotion and to "thinking with your heart." Conversely, worst-case reasoning driven by fear is sometimes called "wishful thinking in reverse" or fearful thinking.

Effects and countermeasures

Wishful thinking can distort medical decisions, financial forecasting, political expectations, and risk assessment, though mild optimism may also sustain motivation and mental health. Techniques to counter it include considering the opposite, base-rate forecasting, pre-mortem analysis, and seeking disconfirming evidence.
