Hagakure (Japanese: 葉隠, "Hidden in the Leaves" or "Hidden Leaves") is a practical and spiritual guide for a warrior, drawn from the recorded conversations of the samurai Yamamoto Tsunetomo (1659–1719) with his attendant Tashiro Tsuramoto between 1709 and 1716. A former retainer of Nabeshima Mitsushige, the daimyo of Saga, Tsunetomo became a Buddhist priest after his lord's death and dictated the work in retirement.

Content

The book comprises some 1,300 short anecdotes, aphorisms and reflections arranged in eleven volumes. Its most famous declaration opens the first volume: "The way of the warrior is found in death." Hagakure exhorts the samurai to constant readiness to die in his lord's service, to value action and loyalty over calculation, and to cultivate resolve through daily meditation on mortality. Alongside its stern martial ethos it contains observations on etiquette, governance, tea ceremony, and the decline of warrior vigour in an age of peace.

Historical context

Hagakure was written during the long peace of the Tokugawa shogunate, when the samurai had become administrators rather than battlefield fighters. Tsunetomo lamented what he saw as the softening of the warrior class and idealised the sterner customs of an earlier generation. The text circulated only within the Nabeshima clan for over a century and was little known outside Saga until the late Edo and Meiji periods.

Reception and influence

In the 1930s and during the Second World War, Hagakure was widely promoted in Japan as an expression of the national spirit, and it was associated with the ideology informing kamikaze pilots. After the war it fell under a cloud, but it was later championed by the writer Yukio Mishima, whose 1967 commentary introduced it to a new readership. Today it is read around the world as a classic of bushido literature, though scholars caution that it reflects the particular customs of one domain rather than a universal samurai code.
