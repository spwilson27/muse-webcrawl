Cartagena (Spanish: [kaɾtaˈxena]) is a Spanish city in the Region of Murcia on the Mediterranean coast of southeastern Spain. It has one of the finest natural harbours in the western Mediterranean and has been an important naval port for more than two thousand years.

History

The site was settled early and became the Iberian town of Mastia. In about 227 BC the Carthaginian general Hasdrubal the Fair refounded it as Qart Hadast ("New City"), the same name as Carthage itself, making it the Punic capital in Iberia. Scipio Africanus captured it for Rome in 209 BC during the Second Punic War, and as Carthago Nova it became one of the richest cities of Roman Hispania, exporting silver, esparto grass, and garum. After periods of Vandal, Byzantine, and Visigothic rule it passed to Muslim control and then to Castile in the thirteenth century.

Under the Bourbons in the eighteenth century Cartagena became a principal base of the Spanish Navy in the Mediterranean, with an arsenal and shipyards. It was the centre of the cantonal rebellion of 1873 during the First Spanish Republic and suffered heavily in the Spanish Civil War, when it remained a Republican naval base until near the end of the conflict.

Main sights

  • The Roman Theatre, rediscovered in 1988 and now restored with a museum designed by Rafael Moneo.
  • The National Museum of Underwater Archaeology (ARQVA).
  • Castillo de la Concepción and the panoramic lift overlooking the harbour.
  • Modernist buildings in the old town, including the Gran Hotel and the Palacio Consistorial.

Economy

The port handles bulk cargo, containers, and cruise ships; the Escombreras valley hosts a refinery and petrochemical and energy installations. The Spanish Navy maintains a major presence, including its submarine base.
