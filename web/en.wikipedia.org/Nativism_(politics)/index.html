Nativism (politics)

Nativism is the political policy of promoting or protecting the interests of native-born or long-established inhabitants over those of immigrants, including support for restrictions on immigration. The term originated in the United States, where it described movements from the 1830s onward.

United States history

American nativism surged with Irish and German Catholic immigration in the 1840s–50s, producing the Know Nothing (American) Party. Later waves targeted Chinese immigrants (Chinese Exclusion Act, 1882), southern and eastern Europeans (culminating in the Immigration Act of 1924 and its national-origins quotas), Japanese immigrants, and, in later decades, Latin American and Muslim immigrants. John Higham's classic study Strangers in the Land (1955) identified recurring strains of anti-Catholic, anti-radical, and Anglo-Saxon racial nativism.

Elsewhere

Analogous politics appear worldwide: anti-immigrant parties in Europe, "sons of the soil" movements in parts of Africa and Asia, and majoritarian movements defining the nation by ethnicity or religion. Scholars debate how nativism differs from, and overlaps with, nationalism, xenophobia, and populism.
