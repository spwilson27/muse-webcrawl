Virginia opossum

The Virginia opossum (Didelphis virginiana), also known as the North American opossum, is the only marsupial found in North America north of Mexico. It is a solitary, nocturnal animal about the size of a domestic cat, and is a successful opportunist. The Virginia opossum is familiar to many North Americans as it frequents areas inhabited by humans and often raids garbage cans and other containers.

Description

Adults weigh 4 to 14 pounds (1.8–6.4 kg) and measure 13–37 inches long from snout to base of tail, with the tail adding another 8.5–19 inches. The coat is dull grayish brown with a white face, opposable thumbs on the hind feet, and a long, hairless, prehensile tail. The opossum is known for its defensive behaviour of feigning death — "playing possum" — an involuntary response to danger in which the animal becomes rigid, its lips draw back, and a foul-smelling fluid may be secreted.

Range and habitat

The species ranges from Central America northward through Mexico and the eastern and central United States into southern Canada. Originally native to the eastern United States, it was introduced to the West Coast during the Great Depression, likely as a food source. It adapts readily to forests, farmland, and suburban environments, denning in burrows, hollow trees, and brush piles.

Diet and reproduction

Virginia opossums are omnivores, feeding on insects, snails, rodents, carrion, eggs, fruits, and grains. Their diet of ticks is notable: studies suggest a single opossum may consume thousands of ticks per season. Females give birth to as many as 20 tiny young, which crawl to the mother's pouch, where they remain for roughly two months; surviving young then ride on her back for another month or two. The lifespan is short — typically only two to four years in the wild.
