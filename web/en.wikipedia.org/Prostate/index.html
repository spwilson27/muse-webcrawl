The prostate is an accessory gland of the male reproductive system found in most mammals. It surrounds the urethra just below the bladder and produces prostatic fluid, an alkaline, milky secretion that makes up roughly a third of the volume of semen and helps nourish and protect sperm. In a young adult man the gland is about the size of a walnut and weighs around 20 grams, divided anatomically into zones — peripheral, central and transition — a distinction important because different diseases arise in different zones: most cancers in the peripheral zone, most benign enlargement in the transition zone.

Function

Prostatic fluid contains enzymes (including prostate-specific antigen, PSA, which liquefies semen after ejaculation), citrate, zinc and acid phosphatase. The prostate's muscular tissue helps expel semen during ejaculation and, by surrounding the urethra, the gland also contributes to urinary control. Its growth and function are regulated by androgens, principally dihydrotestosterone (DHT), converted from testosterone by the enzyme 5-alpha-reductase.

Clinical significance

Three conditions account for most prostate disease. Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a non-cancerous enlargement common with ageing — present in about half of men in their sixties — which can compress the urethra and obstruct urination; it is treated with alpha-blockers, 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, or surgery such as transurethral resection (TURP). Prostatitis is inflammation of the gland, sometimes bacterial, causing pelvic pain and urinary symptoms. Prostate cancer is among the most common cancers in men worldwide; screening with PSA blood tests and digital rectal examination remains debated because of overdiagnosis of slow-growing tumours, and diagnosis is increasingly guided by MRI before biopsy.
