Gram stain

The Gram stain (also Gram staining or the Gram method) is a method of differentiating bacterial species into two large groups: Gram-positive bacteria and Gram-negative bacteria. It was devised in 1884 by the Danish bacteriologist Hans Christian Gram and remains one of the most frequently performed procedures in microbiology, providing rapid preliminary information about pathogens in clinical specimens.

Procedure

  1. A heat-fixed smear of bacteria is stained with crystal violet, the primary stain.
  2. Gram's iodine is added as a mordant, forming a complex with the crystal violet.
  3. A decolouriser such as ethanol or acetone is applied briefly.
  4. The smear is counterstained with safranin (or basic fuchsine).

Gram-positive cells retain the purple complex because of their thick peptidoglycan wall; Gram-negative cells, with a thin wall and an outer membrane dissolved by the decolouriser, lose the purple stain and take up the pink counterstain.

Medical use

Gram staining of sputum, cerebrospinal fluid, wound swabs, and other specimens guides empirical antibiotic therapy hours or days before culture results are available: for example, Gram-positive cocci in chains suggest streptococci, while Gram-negative diplococci in cerebrospinal fluid suggest meningococcal meningitis. The test is less reliable on old cultures or after antibiotics have been started, and some organisms—such as Mycobacterium species with waxy cell walls—stain weakly or variably.

History

Gram developed the technique in Berlin while working in the laboratory of Carl Friedländer, originally seeking a way to make bacteria in lung tissue sections easier to see. He published the method in 1884, modestly noting its imperfections. The stain's empirical basis was explained only later, once the differing cell-wall structures of the two groups were understood.
