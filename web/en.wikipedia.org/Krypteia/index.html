Krypteia

The krypteia, also rendered crypteia (Greek: κρυπτεία, from κρυπτός kryptós, "hidden, secret"), was an institution of the ancient Greek city-state of Sparta. It formed part of the agoge, the rigorous upbringing and training regimen mandated for all male Spartan citizens. Select youths — the kryptai (κρύπται, singular kryptēs) — were sent into the countryside, where they were expected to survive by stealth and, according to ancient sources, to terrorize and kill the enslaved helot population.

Accounts

The fullest description comes from Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus. According to Plutarch, the young men judged most promising were equipped only with a dagger and essential supplies, dispersed at night to hide in the countryside by day, and at night killed any helot they caught, also plundering food from fields. Plato, in the Laws, describes the krypteia more neutrally as a form of endurance training involving going barefoot in winter, sleeping without bedding, and attending to oneself without servants.

Purpose

Scholars debate the krypteia's function. Interpretations include a rite of passage or initiation into Spartan manhood; a security institution designed to suppress the vastly more numerous helots and deter revolt; military training in reconnaissance, stealth, and irregular warfare; and a means of culling the helot population. Thucydides recounts that the Spartans at one point invited helots who claimed to have rendered the most service to come forward, then made some two thousand of them disappear — an episode sometimes associated with the krypteia, though Thucydides does not name it.

Modern interpretations

Modern historians caution that the sources are late, hostile, or both, and that the krypteia may have been less a systematic murder program than an occasional practice magnified by the "Spartan mirage" of later writing. Nonetheless, the institution remains one of the most discussed features of Spartan society.
