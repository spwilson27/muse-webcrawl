Lethe

In Greek mythology, Lethe (/ˈliːθiː/; Ancient Greek: Λήθη, "forgetfulness", "oblivion") is one of the rivers of the Greek underworld, also known as the Ameles potamos (river of unmindfulness). The shades of the dead were required to drink the waters of the Lethe in order to forget their earthly life. In Classical Greek, the word lethe literally means "oblivion", "forgetfulness", or "concealment". It is also the name of the Greek spirit of forgetfulness and oblivion, with whom the river is often identified.

Mythology

Lethe was one of the five rivers of Hades, alongside the Styx, Acheron, Phlegethon and Cocytus. In some traditions, drinking from Lethe induced complete forgetfulness, preparing souls for reincarnation. Plato, in the Myth of Er at the end of the Republic, tells how souls journeyed to the plain of Lethe and drank from the river Ameles, losing memory of their previous lives before being reborn. Virgil similarly describes in the Aeneid how Aeneas saw souls destined for rebirth drinking the waters of Lethe. According to Hesiod, Lethe was the daughter of Eris (Strife) and sister of other personified afflictions such as Ponos (Hardship) and Limos (Starvation).

Contrasting traditions

Not all traditions held that the dead forgot: certain initiates in mystery religions, as attested by gold tablets placed in graves, were instructed to avoid Lethe and instead drink from the spring of Mnemosyne (Memory), declaring "I am a child of Earth and starry Heaven." Pausanias reported a real spring and oracle of Trophonius in Boeotia where visitors drank from two springs named Lethe and Mnemosyne.

Later references

The river became a stock image for oblivion in European literature: Dante places a Lethe in Earthly Paradise, whose waters wash away the memory of sin; Shakespeare, Goethe and Baudelaire ("Le Léthé") all invoke it. The word survives in English derivatives such as "lethal" (by folk association) and the scientific term aletheia ("un-forgetting", Greek for truth), much discussed by philosophers.
