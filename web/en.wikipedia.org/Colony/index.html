Colony

DefinitionA territory under the political control of a foreign state (metropole)
Related conceptsColonialism, imperialism, protectorate, dependency
EtymologyLatin colonia, from colonus, farmer/settler

A colony is a territory subject to a form of foreign rule. Though dominated by the foreign colonizers, the rule remains separate to the original country of the colonizers, the metropolitan state (or "mother country"), within the shared imperial state. In a narrower, older sense, a colony is also a settlement of citizens of one state in a new territory — the meaning preserved in the word's origin, the Roman colonia, a settlement of veteran soldiers granted land in conquered territory.


European colonial empires at their height controlled most of the world's land.

History

Colonies in the settlement sense are ancient: Phoenician cities founded colonies such as Carthage around the Mediterranean, and Greek city-states planted hundreds of apoikiai from Spain to the Black Sea. Rome used colonies to secure conquered land. The modern age of colonialism began with Portugal and Spain in the 15th and 16th centuries, followed by the Netherlands, France, and Britain, creating overseas empires in the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Oceania.

Types of colony

  • Settler colonies — large-scale immigration intended to replicate the metropole's society, as in North America, Australia, and Algeria; often involving the displacement of Indigenous peoples.
  • Exploitation (extractive) colonies — administered to extract resources and labour with a small expatriate presence, as in much of tropical Africa and Asia.
  • Plantation colonies — organised around export crops worked by imported, often enslaved, labour, as in the Caribbean.
  • Trading posts and strategic colonies — ports and coaling stations such as Singapore, Aden, and Hong Kong.
  • Penal colonies — settlements for transported convicts, such as early Australia and French Guiana.

Decolonization

The 20th century saw the dismantling of the colonial empires: the independence of the United States (1776–83) and of Spanish America (1810s–1820s) began the process; after 1945, independence movements and the United Nations' 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples accelerated it, producing dozens of new states in Asia and Africa. A small number of non-self-governing territories remain on the UN list.

Modern usage

The term is also used by analogy for biological colonies — groups of organisms of the same species living together, such as ant colonies, bacterial colonies, and colonial animals like corals — and figuratively for communities such as artists' colonies and nudist colonies.
