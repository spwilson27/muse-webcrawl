Pinhole camera

A pinhole camera is a simple camera without a lens but with a tiny aperture — the pinhole — effectively a light-proof box with a small hole in one side. Light from a scene passes through the aperture and projects an inverted image on the opposite side of the box. The device is also known as a camera obscura when used at room scale for viewing.

Light rays pass through the pinhole and form an inverted image on the screen.

Principle

Because light travels in straight lines, each point of the scene maps to a single small spot on the image plane, producing a sharp image with nearly infinite depth of field. The trade-off is dimness: the tiny aperture admits little light, so exposure times range from seconds to hours. If the hole is too small, diffraction blurs the image; an optimum pinhole diameter balances geometric sharpness against diffraction.

History

The pinhole phenomenon was described by the Chinese philosopher Mozi in the 5th century BC and studied by Aristotle and Ibn al-Haytham. Artists used the camera obscura as a drawing aid for centuries before the invention of photography. In the 19th century, photographic plates placed in pinhole cameras produced some of the earliest photographs.

Modern use

Pinhole photography remains popular with hobbyists, who build cameras from cans, boxes, and even rooms; images are often characterised by soft focus, vignetting, and extreme depth of field. Long-exposure solargraphs, made with pinhole cameras left for months, trace the sun's path across the sky. Pinhole cameras are also used to view solar eclipses safely.
