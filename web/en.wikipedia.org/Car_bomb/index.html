Car bomb

A car bomb, or vehicle-borne improvised explosive device (VBIED), is an improvised explosive device placed in a car or other vehicle and then exploded. It is a common tactic of insurgents, terrorists, and organized crime groups because a vehicle can carry a large quantity of explosives inconspicuously close to a target.

History

Precursors include the horse-drawn wagon bomb that struck Wall Street in 1920. Car bombs became widespread from the 1940s onward — in the Palestine insurgency, the Vietnam War, the Northern Ireland conflict, and the Lebanese Civil War, where truck bombs destroyed the U.S. embassy and Marine barracks in Beirut in 1983. They were used extensively by the IRA, ETA, Colombian cartels, Al-Qaeda, and the Islamic State, including against civilian targets.

Characteristics and countermeasures

Devices range from small charges meant to kill a vehicle's occupants to truck bombs carrying tonnes of explosives. Defensive measures include standoff barriers, checkpoints, blast-resistant design, intelligence, and regulation of precursor materials such as ammonium nitrate fertilizer.
