Scottish mythology

Scottish mythology is the collection of myths that have emerged throughout the history of Scotland, sometimes being elaborated upon by successive generations, and at other times being rejected and replaced by other explanatory narratives. It draws on Celtic, Norse, and local folk traditions.

Nature myths

Much Scottish mythology concerns the powers of nature, personified in figures such as the Cailleach, the hag goddess of winter who shapes the landscape, and Beira, queen of winter. The storm kelpies and the spirits of lochs and rivers reflect the importance and danger of water in Scottish life.

Supernatural beings

Scottish folklore is rich in supernatural creatures: the kelpie, a shape-shifting water horse said to lure the unwary to their deaths; the selkie, who lives as a seal in the sea but sheds its skin to become human on land; the brownie, a helpful household spirit; and the Bean Nighe, a washerwoman seen as an omen of death. The islands of the north added Norse-influenced beings such as the trow.

Heroic traditions

Through Gaelic tradition Scotland shares the Ulster and Fenian cycles with Ireland, including tales of Fionn mac Cumhaill. The poems attributed to Ossian, published by James Macpherson in the 1760s, presented themselves as translations of ancient Gaelic epics and had an enormous influence on European Romanticism, though their authenticity was disputed from the start.
