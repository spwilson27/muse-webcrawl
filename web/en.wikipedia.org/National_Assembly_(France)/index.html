The National Assembly (French: Assemblée nationale, [asɑ̃ble nɑsjɔnal]) is the lower house of the bicameral French Parliament, the upper house being the Senate. It has 577 deputies (députés), elected for five-year terms in single-member constituencies by a two-round system. The Assembly sits in the Palais Bourbon on the left bank of the Seine in Paris.

History

The name recalls the National Assembly formed during the French Revolution in 1789, when the deputies of the Third Estate proclaimed themselves the representatives of the nation. Under the Fifth Republic, established in 1958, the Assembly's powers were deliberately constrained relative to the executive, though it remains the house in which confidence in the government is tested: the government is responsible to the Assembly, which can dismiss it by a motion of censure.

Powers

The National Assembly shares legislative power with the Senate, but in the event of persistent disagreement over a bill, the government may give the final word to the Assembly. It also has a leading role in voting the state budget. Since a 2002 reform, legislative elections follow immediately after the presidential election, a sequence that has usually given the president a working majority in the Assembly.

Composition

Of the 577 constituencies, 539 are in metropolitan France, 27 in overseas departments and territories, and 11 represent French citizens living abroad. To be elected in the first round, a candidate must win an absolute majority of votes cast with at least 25% of registered voters; otherwise a second round decides by plurality among candidates who cleared a threshold.
