The Emerald Necklace is a 1,100-acre (445-hectare) chain of parks linked by parkways and waterways in Boston and Brookline, Massachusetts. It is named for the way the planned landscape appears to hang from the "neck" of the Boston peninsula. The system was designed by the landscape architect Frederick Law Olmsted and represents a milestone in the movement for large public parks in American cities.

Design

Olmsted planned the Necklace as a continuous "linear park" of contrasting landscape experiences, from the manicured Boston Common (1634) and Public Garden (1837), through the urban wild of the Back Bay Fens, the Riverway, Olmsted Park and Jamaica Pond, to the more naturalistic Arnold Arboretum and Franklin Park. A crucial innovation was the separation of through-traffic parkways from pleasure drives and pedestrian paths, allowing safe public access throughout the system.

Components

The parks, from Boston Common southward, are: Boston Common, Public Garden, Commonwealth Avenue Mall, Back Bay Fens, Riverway, Olmsted Park, Jamaica Pond, Arnold Arboretum and Franklin Park. Several are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The Muddy River, which runs through much of the system, was restored in the early 2000s after decades of pollution.

Legacy

The Emerald Necklace was one of Olmsted's last great projects and helped define the profession of landscape architecture in the United States. Its model of connected urban green space influenced the Boston parkway system, the park systems of many other American cities, and the modern concept of green infrastructure.
