Landscape architecture

Central Park, New York — a landmark of landscape architecture by Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux
Landscape architecture
DisciplineDesign of outdoor areas, landmarks and structures
Term coinedGilbert Laing Meason (1828); popularised by Frederick Law Olmsted
Professional bodyInternational Federation of Landscape Architects (IFLA, founded 1948); ASLA in the U.S.
ScaleFrom small gardens to regional planning and green infrastructure

Landscape architecture is the design of outdoor areas, landmarks, and structures to achieve environmental, social-behavioural, or aesthetic outcomes. It involves the systematic design and general engineering of various structures for construction and human use, the investigation of existing social, ecological, and soil conditions in the landscape, and the design of interventions. The professional practitioner is a landscape architect, a licensed title in many jurisdictions.

History

Designed landscapes are as old as the gardens of Persia, Egypt and Rome, and include the formal gardens of Islamic Spain, Mughal India and Baroque France — André Le Nôtre's gardens at Versailles being the supreme example — and the naturalistic English landscape garden of Capability Brown and Humphry Repton. The modern profession is usually dated to Frederick Law Olmsted and Calvert Vaux's design for Central Park in New York (from 1858); Olmsted went on to design park systems, suburbs and campuses across North America and to champion landscape as public health infrastructure. The American Society of Landscape Architects was founded in 1899.

Scope of practice

  • Site design and planning for parks, campuses, housing, commercial and civic spaces.
  • Urban design and public realm, including streetscapes, plazas and waterfronts.
  • Environmental restoration — wetlands, brownfields, reclaimed mines and river corridors.
  • Green infrastructure for stormwater management, urban cooling and climate adaptation.
  • Heritage conservation of historic gardens and cultural landscapes.
  • Landscape planning and assessment at regional and national scales.

In the later twentieth century the field broadened from beautification toward ecology, influenced by Ian McHarg's Design with Nature (1969), and toward the reclamation of industrial sites — exemplified by projects such as the Landschaftspark Duisburg-Nord and New York's High Line, a linear park on a disused elevated railway.
