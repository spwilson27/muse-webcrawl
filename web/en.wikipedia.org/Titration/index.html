Titration

Titration (also known as titrimetry and volumetric analysis) is a common laboratory method of quantitative chemical analysis used to determine the concentration of an identified analyte. A reagent of known concentration, called the titrant or standard solution, is added gradually from a burette to a measured volume of the analyte solution until the reaction between them is just complete. The volume of titrant used, together with the stoichiometry of the reaction, gives the amount — and hence the concentration — of the analyte.

Procedure and endpoint

The completion of the reaction is signalled by the endpoint, ideally coinciding with the theoretical equivalence point. In manual titrations an indicator is added to the analyte that changes colour at or near the endpoint — phenolphthalein, for example, turns from colourless to pink in acid–base work. Instrumental methods detect the endpoint by monitoring pH (with a pH meter), electrical conductance, potential, or light absorption, and automatic titrators record whole titration curves whose inflection points mark the equivalence volume. In a back titration, a known excess of reagent is added and the surplus is titrated, a trick used when the analyte reacts slowly or is insoluble.

Types

  • Acid–base titrations rely on neutralisation, used to assay vinegar, antacids and water alkalinity.
  • Redox titrations use oxidation–reduction reactions, as in iodometry and permanganate or dichromate determinations of iron.
  • Complexometric titrations, usually with EDTA, measure metal ions such as the calcium and magnesium of water hardness.
  • Precipitation titrations, such as argentometric determination of chloride with silver nitrate.
  • Karl Fischer titration measures trace water content.

History and applications

Titrimetry developed in the late 18th and 19th centuries in the French textile and chemical industries and was systematised by chemists such as Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac and Karl Friedrich Mohr, who introduced the burette and standard apparatus. It remains a reference method in pharmacopeias, food and water analysis, and industrial quality control because of its high precision, typically better than other routine techniques.
