German idealism

German idealism is a philosophical movement that emerged in Germany in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, beginning with the work of Immanuel Kant in the 1780s and culminating in Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel in the 1820s–30s. Between them stand Johann Gottlieb Fichte and Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling. The movement developed in reaction to Kant's critical philosophy and is characterised by the claims that reality is fundamentally mental or spirit-like in structure, and that the mind actively constitutes the world it knows.

Kant and the critical turn

Kant's Critique of Pure Reason (1781) argued that the mind imposes forms of intuition (space and time) and categories upon experience — a "transcendental idealism" that limits knowledge to phenomena while leaving things in themselves unknowable. Younger thinkers regarded the thing-in-itself as an incoherent remnant to be eliminated.

Fichte, Schelling, Hegel

Fichte's Wissenschaftslehre began from the self-positing I, deriving both subject and object from its activity. Schelling developed a philosophy of nature and identity, treating nature and spirit as two expressions of one absolute. Hegel's system — set out in the Phenomenology of Spirit (1807) and the Science of Logic — presented reality as the self-unfolding of Spirit (Geist) through dialectical stages, a vision encompassing logic, nature, history, art, religion, and philosophy itself.

Influence

German idealism reshaped theology, historiography, aesthetics, and political thought; it provoked reactions from Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard, and Marx, and was revived by later movements from British idealism to contemporary scholarship on Kant and Hegel. Its central texts remain among the most difficult and debated in Western philosophy.
