Hematoma

A hematoma (also spelled haematoma) is a localised collection of blood outside blood vessels, usually caused by haemorrhage following injury to a vessel wall. Unlike a simple bruise (contusion), in which blood spreads diffusely through tissues, a hematoma forms a distinct, often palpable swelling of clotted or partially clotted blood. Hematomas can occur almost anywhere in the body and range from trivial to life-threatening depending on their size and location.

Causes

Most hematomas result from trauma, including blows, surgery and invasive procedures such as injections or blood draws. They are more likely, larger and slower to resolve in people taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication and in those with bleeding disorders such as haemophilia or thrombocytopenia. Spontaneous hematomas can occur with aneurysm rupture, certain tumours and vascular malformations.

Types

Hematomas are commonly named by their anatomical site. Important examples include subdural and epidural (extradural) hematomas inside the skull, which can compress the brain and constitute neurosurgical emergencies; subungual hematoma beneath a nail, classically after a crush injury; periorbital hematoma (black eye); auricular hematoma, which untreated can produce cauliflower ear; rectus sheath hematoma in the abdominal wall; and retroperitoneal hematoma. A cephalohematoma is a collection of blood under the periosteum of a newborn's skull following birth.

Symptoms and course

Typical features are pain, swelling and discoloration that evolve as the blood is broken down, passing from red or purple through blue-green to yellow-brown as haemoglobin is converted to biliverdin and bilirubin. Small superficial hematomas are reabsorbed over days to weeks. Risks include infection, continued bleeding, pressure on adjacent structures (compartment syndrome in limbs), and, within the skull, raised intracranial pressure.

Treatment

Management of minor hematomas is conservative: rest, ice, compression and elevation (the RICE protocol) and analgesia. Large, expanding, infected or strategically located hematomas may require needle aspiration or surgical drainage and evacuation; intracranial hematomas with mass effect require urgent neurosurgical intervention. The underlying cause — such as excessive anticoagulation — must also be addressed.
