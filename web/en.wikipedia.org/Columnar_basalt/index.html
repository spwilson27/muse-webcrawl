Columnar basalt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Columnar basalt is a volcanic rock formation produced by the contraction of a thick lava flow as it cools, fracturing into regular, typically hexagonal, vertical pillars known as columnar joints. The columns form perpendicular to the cooling surface and can range from a few centimetres to several metres across.

Formation

As a body of basaltic lava cools and solidifies, it shrinks. Because the cooling proceeds from the top and bottom surfaces inward, contraction cracks propagate and organise themselves into a polygonal network — most often five- to seven-sided, with hexagons predominating — minimising the energy of the fracture pattern. Slow, uniform cooling yields tall, regular columns; rapid or irregular cooling produces thinner, curved, or chaotic colonnades (entablature). The same process, called columnar jointing, can occur in other volcanic rocks and even in non-volcanic materials such as dried starch or mud.

Famous examples

  • Giant's Causeway, County Antrim, Northern Ireland — some 40,000 interlocking columns formed by a Palaeogene lava flow; in Irish mythology built by the giant Fionn mac Cumhaill.
  • Fingal's Cave, Staffa, Scotland — a sea cave in columnar basalt facing the Giant's Causeway across the sea.
  • Devils Tower, Wyoming, USA — a laccolithic butte displaying dramatic columnar jointing in phonolite porphyry.
  • Devils Postpile, California, USA — a notably regular basalt colonnade exposed by glacial erosion.
  • Garni Gorge, Armenia — cliffs of polygonal basalt columns sometimes called the "Symphony of the Stones".
