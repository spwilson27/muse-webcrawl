Sea

A sea is a large body of salt water wholly or partly surrounded by land, or a large subdivision of an ocean. Seas are generally smaller and shallower than oceans and are often partly enclosed by coastlines — the Mediterranean Sea, the Caribbean Sea and the South China Sea are prominent examples — though the term is also used for some large inland salt lakes such as the Caspian Sea, and for open ocean regions like the Sargasso Sea. Collectively, the Earth's seas and oceans form the world ocean, covering about 71 per cent of the planet's surface.

Definition and naming

There is no single authoritative definition distinguishing seas from oceans. The International Hydrographic Organization delimits the world's oceans and seas for charting purposes. Marginal seas lie along continental margins, partly enclosed by islands or peninsulas; enclosed or inland seas, like the Black Sea and the Baltic Sea, connect to the ocean through narrow straits. Some large lakes are called seas for historical reasons — the Caspian and the Dead Sea among them.

Properties

Seawater has an average salinity of about 35 grams of dissolved salts per litre, dominated by sodium chloride. Temperature, salinity and pressure govern density and drive thermohaline circulation. Seas experience tides caused principally by the gravitational pull of the Moon, wind-driven waves and currents, and support rich ecosystems from sunlit surface waters to the deep sea floor.

Human use

Humans have used the sea for transport, trade, fishing and warfare since prehistory; maritime routes connected civilisations across the Mediterranean, Indian Ocean and Pacific. Today the law of the sea is codified in the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), defining territorial seas, exclusive economic zones and rights of passage, while pollution, overfishing and climate-driven sea level rise are major concerns.
