The Chemical History of a Candle


Title page of the 1861 edition, edited by William Crookes.

The Chemical History of a Candle is a series of six lectures on the chemistry and physics of flames given by Michael Faraday at the Royal Institution in London in 1848 and again, in the form in which they became famous, as the Christmas Lectures of 1860–61. The lectures were published in book form in 1861, edited by William Crookes, and have remained in print ever since — one of the most successful science popularizations of the nineteenth century.

Contents

Beginning from the humble candle, Faraday explores the nature of combustion: the melting and capillary rise of the fuel, the vaporisation of wax, the structure and luminosity of the flame, the presence of hydrogen and carbon in it, the production of water from burning hydrogen, the role of oxygen and the composition of air, and the formation of carbon dioxide, which he connects to respiration and the carbon cycle. Faraday's celebrated dictum was that "there is no better, there is no more open door by which you can enter into the study of natural philosophy than by considering the physical phenomena of a candle."

Legacy

The lectures were designed for a young audience and are noted for their clarity, showmanship, and insistence on observation and experiment over speculation. They have been translated into many languages, including a Japanese edition that was widely used in science education, and are still recommended as an introduction to chemistry. Faraday himself considered them among his finest work as a lecturer.
