CITES

CITES (the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora, also known as the Washington Convention) is a multilateral treaty to protect endangered plants and animals from the threats of international trade. It was drafted as a result of a resolution adopted in 1963 at a meeting of members of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), signed in Washington, D.C., in 1973, and entered into force on 1 July 1975. More than 180 states are parties, and the convention protects over 40,000 species.

How it works

CITES works by subjecting international trade in specimens of selected species to certain controls. Species are listed in three appendices according to the degree of protection needed. Appendix I includes species threatened with extinction; trade in them is permitted only in exceptional circumstances. Appendix II includes species not necessarily threatened with extinction, but whose trade must be controlled to avoid utilization incompatible with their survival. Appendix III lists species protected in at least one country that has asked other parties for assistance in controlling the trade.

Permits and enforcement

Trade in listed species requires export and, for Appendix I, import permits issued by national management authorities, supported by scientific advice that the trade will not be detrimental to the species' survival. The parties meet every two to three years in a Conference of the Parties to review listings and policy; the CITES Secretariat, administered by the United Nations Environment Programme, is based in Geneva.

Criticism and debates

Debates have focused on high-profile species such as African elephants and the ivory trade, rhinoceros horn, pangolins (uplisted to Appendix I in 2016), and the balance between strict prohibition and regulated sustainable use, as well as the convention's expansion into commercially harvested fish and timber species such as sharks and rosewoods.
