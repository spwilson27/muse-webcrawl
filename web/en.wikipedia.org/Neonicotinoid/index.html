Neonicotinoids (sometimes shortened to neonics) are a class of neuro-active insecticides chemically similar to nicotine. Developed from the 1980s by companies including Bayer and Syngenta, they became the most widely used class of insecticides in the world in the 2000s, applied as seed coatings, soil treatments, and sprays on crops, and in veterinary and household products. Because they are systemic — absorbed and distributed throughout the plant — they protect growing tissue, but the same properties have raised major concerns about harm to pollinators and other non-target wildlife.

Mode of action

Neonicotinoids act on the central nervous system of insects, binding to postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. Their much higher affinity for insect than mammalian receptors underlies their relatively low acute toxicity to mammals. Binding is effectively irreversible, causing excitation, paralysis, and death of the insect. Major compounds include imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam, acetamiprid, thiacloprid, dinotefuran, and nitenpyram.

Environmental effects

Neonicotinoids are water-soluble and persistent, leaching into soil and waterways and appearing in pollen and nectar at low concentrations. A large body of research links field-realistic exposure to impaired foraging, learning, reproduction, and colony performance in honey bees and wild bees, and to declines of aquatic invertebrates and possibly insect-eating birds. Their role in bee declines — alongside parasites such as Varroa, disease, and habitat loss — is scientifically and politically contested, with manufacturers disputing the significance of laboratory findings at field doses.

Regulation

In 2013 the European Union restricted the use of clothianidin, thiamethoxam, and imidacloprid on bee-attractive crops, and in 2018 extended the ban to essentially all outdoor uses of the three compounds; approvals for others have also lapsed or been withdrawn. Several other jurisdictions, including some US states and Canadian provinces, have imposed partial restrictions, while the compounds remain widely used elsewhere.
