Qasida

The qaṣīda (also spelled qasida; plural qaṣāʾid) is a form of lyric poetry that originated in pre-Islamic Arabia and later spread throughout the Islamic world. It is an ode of usually 15 to 100 lines, written in a single metre with a monorhyme sustained throughout the poem. The qasida was the most prestigious genre of classical Arabic poetry and was adopted into Persian, Turkish, Urdu and other literary traditions.

Structure

The classical qasida typically follows a tripartite structure. It opens with the nasīb, a nostalgic evocation of a lost beloved and an abandoned campsite, in which the poet halts at the traces of the encampment. This is followed by the raḥīl, a journey section describing the poet's travels through the desert, often praising the endurance of his camel or horse. The poem concludes with its main purpose: praise (madīḥ) of a patron or tribe, satire of enemies, or moral reflection. Famous examples include the seven celebrated odes known as the Muʿallaqāt, attributed to poets such as Imruʾ al-Qays and Labid.

Spread and influence

With the expansion of Islam, the qasida became a central form in Persian poetry, where it was used for panegyric and religious verse, and in Urdu literature, where masters such as Mir and Ghalib composed qasidas alongside the ghazal. In Arabic, the form remained a vehicle for praise of the Prophet Muhammad, notably al-Busiri's Qaṣīdat al-Burda ("Poem of the Mantle"). Variants also developed in Swahili, Hausa and other languages of Muslim Africa, and in the Iberian Peninsula under Muslim rule. The qasida continues to be composed and performed in the Arab world today, including in religious and ceremonial contexts.
