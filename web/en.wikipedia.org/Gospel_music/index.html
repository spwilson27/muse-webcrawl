Gospel music

Gospel music is a traditional genre of Christian music and a mainstay of Christian media. Created by African Americans in the southern United States, it is characterised by dominant vocals (often with strong use of harmony), lyrics expressing Christian faith, and roots in the oral traditions of the Black church: spirituals, hymns, and sacred songs blended with elements of blues and jazz. Gospel's influence reaches far beyond its genre — it shaped soul, rhythm and blues, and rock and roll.

History

The genre coalesced in the 1920s and 1930s, when composer Thomas A. Dorsey — "the father of gospel music" — fused blues structures with sacred lyrics, writing standards such as "Precious Lord, Take My Hand" (1932). In Chicago, Dorsey's publishing house and the National Convention of Gospel Choirs and Choruses spread the style nationally. Mahalia Jackson, the "Queen of Gospel", brought the sound to concert halls and a mass recording audience from the 1940s onward.

Traditions

Black gospel features choirs, call-and-response, hand-clapping, and expressive soloists; the quartet tradition produced groups such as the Golden Gate Quartet and the Soul Stirrers (whose young Sam Cooke carried gospel phrasing into soul music). Southern gospel, associated with white Southern quartets and publishing houses such as Stamps-Baxter, is centred on four-part harmony and piano accompaniment. From the 1970s, artists including Andraé Crouch and later Kirk Franklin fused gospel with pop, R&B, and hip-hop, creating contemporary urban gospel.

Influence

Gospel vocal technique — melisma, improvisation, testifying delivery — underpins soul and R&B singing, and many major popular singers, from Aretha Franklin to Whitney Houston, were formed in gospel choirs. The genre is recognised by the Grammy Awards and the Gospel Music Hall of Fame, maintained by the Gospel Music Association, which also presents the Dove Awards.
