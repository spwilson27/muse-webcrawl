Miniature (illuminated manuscript)

Miniature
MediumTempera, gold and silver leaf on parchment or paper
ContextIlluminated manuscripts
FlourishedEarly medieval period – 16th century

A miniature is a small illustration used to decorate an illuminated manuscript. Despite the modern sense of the word, a miniature is defined not primarily by small size — full-page miniatures exist — but by its technique and function as a picture accompanying a text. The word derives not from Latin minutus ("small") but from miniare, to paint with minium, the red lead pigment used for rubrication and early illustrations.

History

Small-scale painting in books descends from the illustrations of late antique codices such as the Vatican Virgil and the Vienna Dioscurides. In the early Middle Ages, insular manuscripts like the Lindisfarne Gospels and the Book of Kells combined interlace ornament with evangelist portraits, while Carolingian and Ottonian schools produced grand figural miniatures. The Romanesque period (11th–12th centuries) favoured historiated initials — letters containing narrative scenes — and the Gothic era (13th–15th centuries) made the miniature the dominant glory of the manuscript: psalters, breviaries, and above all books of hours, such as the Très Riches Heures of the Limbourg brothers, filled with calendar scenes, marginal drolleries, and increasingly naturalistic detail.

The tradition flourished outside Latin Europe as well: Byzantine manuscripts, Armenian Gospel books, and the Persian, Mughal, and Ottoman miniature schools produced sophisticated painting in books — Persian miniatures, though usually bound in albums or manuscripts, became an art form of their own. Hebrew manuscripts of medieval Spain and Italy also feature notable miniature painting.

Technique

Miniatures were painted in water-based media (egg tempera or gum-bound pigments) on prepared parchment or, later, paper. The artist (the miniator or limner) drew the composition in lead point or ink, laid down gesso and gold leaf for burnished gold backgrounds, and applied colour in fine layers with small brushes. A single manuscript might pass between scribe, rubricator, flourisher, and one or more illuminators, often working in urban workshops by the later Middle Ages.

Decline

The spread of printing in the later 15th century gradually displaced the hand-painted miniature, though deluxe manuscripts continued to be produced for wealthy patrons into the 16th century, and printed books were often hand-illuminated. The word "miniature" later transferred to small independent portraits — portrait miniatures — painted in the same delicate technique.
