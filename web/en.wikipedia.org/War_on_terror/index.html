War on terror

The war on terror, officially the Global War on Terrorism (GWOT), is a global military campaign launched by the United States government following the September 11 attacks in 2001, and continued for two decades. The principal aims were to counter al-Qaeda and affiliated militant networks and to prevent further terrorist attacks on the United States and its allies.

Origins

On 20 September 2001, President George W. Bush addressed Congress, declaring that the United States’ war on terror “begins with al Qaeda, but it does not end there”. Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF), which successive administrations have used to justify operations against groups beyond those responsible for 9/11.

Major campaigns

The campaign opened with the invasion of Afghanistan in October 2001, which overthrew the Taliban government that had sheltered al-Qaeda, beginning the Afghanistan War, which lasted until the US withdrawal and Taliban return to power in 2021. In 2003 the United States and a coalition invaded Iraq, overthrowing Saddam Hussein; the Iraq War and the insurgency that followed killed hundreds of thousands and reshaped the region. Further theatres included drone campaigns in Pakistan and Yemen, operations in Somalia, and, from 2014, the war against the Islamic State in Iraq and Syria, which had proclaimed a caliphate after exploiting the Syrian civil war.

Domestic and legal measures

At home, the campaign brought the USA PATRIOT Act, the creation of the Department of Homeland Security, mass surveillance programmes later revealed by Edward Snowden, the detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, and the use of “enhanced interrogation” techniques widely condemned as torture. In May 2011, US special forces killed al-Qaeda leader Osama bin Laden in Abbottabad, Pakistan.

Costs and criticism

The Watson Institute’s Costs of War project has estimated the campaign’s total cost at roughly $8 trillion and direct war deaths at around 900,000, with far higher indirect deaths and some 38 million people displaced. Critics argue the phrase “war on terror” defined an unwinnable struggle against a tactic, justified invasions and abuses, and at times fuelled the extremism it aimed to defeat. The Obama administration retired the term in favour of “overseas contingency operations”, though the underlying authorities and many operations continued.
