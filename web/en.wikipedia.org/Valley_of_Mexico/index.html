The Valley of Mexico (Spanish: Valle de México) is a highlands plateau in central Mexico, an endorheic basin surrounded by mountain ranges and volcanoes. It contains Mexico City and parts of the states of Mexico, Hidalgo, Tlaxcala and Puebla, and is home to more than 20 million people.

Pre-Hispanic history

The valley was one of the cradles of Mesoamerican civilisation: Teotihuacan rose in its northeast in the first centuries AD, and the Mexica founded Tenochtitlan in 1325 on an island in Lake Texcoco, building a hydraulic system of dikes, causeways and chinampa gardens around a network of five shallow lakes.

Drainage and environment

Colonial and later governments drained the lakes over four centuries to control flooding, leaving Mexico City built on soft lakebed clays. The city consequently suffers severe subsidence — in places up to 9 metres over the past century — as groundwater is pumped from the aquifer, together with chronic flooding and air pollution trapped by the surrounding mountains.

Geography

The basin is bounded by the Sierra de Guadalupe to the north, the Sierra Nevada (including Popocatépetl and Iztaccíhuatl) to the east, and the Sierra de las Cruces to the west. Since the 20th century, artificial outlets such as the Emisor Central tunnel drain stormwater and sewage out of the closed basin.
