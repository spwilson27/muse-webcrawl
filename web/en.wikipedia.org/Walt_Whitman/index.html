Walter Whitman Jr. (May 31, 1819 – March 26, 1892) was an American poet, essayist, and journalist. Considered one of the most influential poets in the American canon, his work is often classified alongside that of Emily Dickinson as foundational to American poetry. Whitman is often called the father of free verse, and his magnum opus, Leaves of Grass, was first published in 1855 and revised and expanded throughout his life.

"I celebrate myself, and sing myself, / And what I assume you shall assume, / For every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you." — "Song of Myself"

Life

Whitman was born on Long Island and worked as a printer, schoolteacher, and journalist in Brooklyn and New York. He founded the Long Islander newspaper in 1838 and edited the Brooklyn Eagle. The first edition of Leaves of Grass, printed largely at his own expense, contained twelve poems including "Song of Myself"; Ralph Waldo Emerson praised it in a famous private letter that Whitman publicized. During the American Civil War, Whitman worked in Washington, D.C., hospitals tending wounded soldiers, an experience reflected in Drum-Taps (1865) and his elegy for Abraham Lincoln, "When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd." After a stroke in 1873 he settled in Camden, New Jersey, where he died in 1892.

Style and themes

Whitman's poetry broke with traditional meter and rhyme in favor of long, cadenced free-verse lines, catalogues of everyday life, and an expansive first-person voice embracing the body, democracy, nature, and death. His work was controversial in his lifetime for its frank treatment of sexuality, and his sexuality has long been debated by scholars. Leaves of Grass is now regarded as a landmark of American literature.
