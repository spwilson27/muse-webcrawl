Rugby union in New Zealand

Rugby union is the national sport of New Zealand and a central part of the country's culture and identity. The game is administered by New Zealand Rugby (NZR), founded in 1892, and the national team — the All Blacks — is the most successful in the history of the sport, with a winning record against every opponent and three Rugby World Cup titles (1987, 2011 and 2015).

History

Rugby was introduced to New Zealand by Charles Monro, who had learned the game at school in England; the first recorded match took place in Nelson in 1870. Provincial unions were formed through the 1870s and 1880s, and a New Zealand team toured Australia in 1884. The celebrated "Originals" tour of Britain in 1905–06, which lost only one of 35 matches, established New Zealand as a rugby power and fixed the All Blacks name in popular use.

Structure

Below the national team sit five Super Rugby franchises and 26 provincial unions competing in the National Provincial Championship and the Heartland Championship. The Māori All Blacks and the women's national team, the Black Ferns — six-time World Cup winners — hold prominent places in the game. Rugby faces growing competition from other codes and sports, and participation and governance questions, including the place of Māori and Pasifika players, are ongoing themes.

Cultural significance

The All Blacks' haka, their black jersey with the silver fern, and figures such as Jonah Lomu have made New Zealand rugby a global symbol of the small nation punching above its weight. Matches against South Africa's Springboks, the British and Irish Lions and Australia (for the Bledisloe Cup) are major national occasions, and the 1981 Springbok tour protests divided the country and remain a landmark in its social history.
