Snap election

A snap election is an election that is called earlier than the one that has been scheduled. Generally, a snap election in a parliamentary system is called to capitalise on a favourable political situation, to resolve a deadlock, or to gain a stronger mandate; it also frequently backfires on the government that calls it.

Characteristics

Because the timing is chosen by the incumbent, opposition parties may be caught unprepared, with little time to campaign or raise funds. Snap elections are common in systems where the head of government can request a dissolution of the legislature, and rare where terms are fixed. Notable examples include the British general election of 2017 and several Japanese elections called by prime ministers dissolving the House of Representatives.

Advantages and risks

A governing party may call a snap election when riding high in opinion polls, hoping to extend its majority or renew its term. However, voters may punish perceived opportunism, and campaigns can develop dynamics the government cannot control, sometimes resulting in lost majorities.
