H II region

TypeEmission nebula of ionised hydrogen
Temperature~10,000 K
SizesFrom under 1 to hundreds of light-years
Ionising sourceHot, massive young O and B stars
Famous examplesOrion Nebula, Eagle Nebula, Tarantula Nebula

An H II region is a region of interstellar space in which hydrogen gas is ionised (H II denotes singly ionised hydrogen) by the intense ultraviolet radiation of young, hot, massive stars. These glowing clouds of plasma, tens to hundreds of light-years across, are the sites of recent star formation and appear as emission nebulae, glowing characteristically in the red light of the hydrogen-alpha line at 656.3 nm.

Physics

Ultraviolet photons from O and early B stars strip electrons from hydrogen atoms within a roughly spherical "Strömgren sphere". Recombination of electrons with protons produces emission lines; forbidden lines of ionised oxygen and nitrogen give many H II regions their greenish-blue hues in images. Typical electron densities range from a few to thousands of particles per cubic centimetre, and temperatures are around 10,000 K.

Life cycle and significance

H II regions are short-lived by astronomical standards — a few million years — because the massive stars that ionise them live fast and because stellar winds and supernovae disperse the gas. Giant H II regions in spiral arms are important tracers of star formation in galaxies, and their luminosity is used to measure galactic star-formation rates. The Tarantula Nebula in the Large Magellanic Cloud is the most massive and luminous known example in the Local Group.

Observation

The Orion Nebula, visible to the naked eye, is the nearest massive H II region, about 1,344 light-years away. Edwin Hubble established the extragalactic nature and classification of many such nebulae in the 1920s.
