Stereographic projection

Stereographic projection maps points on a sphere (except the projection point) onto a plane.

In mathematics, a stereographic projection is a perspective projection of a sphere onto a plane through a point on the sphere, in which each point of the sphere (other than the projection point itself) is mapped to the intersection with the plane of the line joining it to the projection point. It is a smooth, bijective, conformal mapping, known since antiquity.

Properties

Stereographic projection preserves angles (it is conformal) but not distances or areas. Circles on the sphere map to circles or straight lines on the plane, and the mapping extends to a homeomorphism between the sphere and the one-point compactification of the plane — the basis of the Riemann sphere in complex analysis.

History

The projection was known to Hipparchus and Ptolemy, who used it in the planispheric astrolabe. It was called the planisphere projection for centuries; the name "stereographic" was introduced in the 17th century.

Applications

The projection underlies the Wulff net in crystallography and structural geology, the polar stereographic map projection used for polar regions, fisheye and "little planet" photography, and visualisations of spherical data. Because it is conformal, local shapes are rendered faithfully, making it valuable wherever directional relationships matter.
