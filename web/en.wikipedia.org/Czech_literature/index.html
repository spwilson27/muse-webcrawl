Czech literature

Czech literature is the literature written in the Czech language, produced in Bohemia, Moravia, and Czech Silesia from the Middle Ages to the present. It encompasses medieval chronicles and religious writing, the humanist scholarship of the Hussite era and Comenius, the nineteenth-century National Revival, and the internationally celebrated modern fiction of Jaroslav Hašek, Karel Čapek, Milan Kundera, and Bohumil Hrabal.

Medieval and early modern periods

The earliest writing in the Czech lands was in Old Church Slavonic and Latin; the oldest Czech-language texts, including glosses and hymns, date from the late thirteenth century, followed by the rhymed Dalimil's Chronicle. Jan Hus's religious writings and orthographic reforms shaped the written language. After the Battle of White Mountain (1620), Czech letters declined under Germanisation, though the exiled educator John Amos Comenius achieved European fame.

National Revival

In the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, scholars such as Josef Dobrovský and Josef Jungmann codified the language, while poets and novelists — Karel Hynek Mácha, Božena Němcová (author of The Grandmother, 1855), and Jan Neruda — built a modern national literature.

Twentieth century

Hašek's satirical novel The Good Soldier Švejk and Karel Čapek's plays — R.U.R. introduced the word "robot" — won worldwide audiences. The poet Jaroslav Seifert received the Nobel Prize in 1984. Under communism, writers such as Václav Havel, Milan Kundera, Ivan Klíma, and Bohumil Hrabal published in samizdat or exile; Havel, a leading dissident playwright, became president after the Velvet Revolution of 1989.
