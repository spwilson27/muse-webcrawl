The Hardy–Littlewood conjectures are a pair of influential conjectures in analytic number theory proposed by G. H. Hardy and John Edensor Littlewood in their 1923 paper "Some Problems of 'Partitio Numerorum'". The first Hardy–Littlewood conjecture (the prime k-tuple conjecture) predicts the asymptotic density of patterns of prime numbers, such as twin primes, prime triplets, and prime quadruplets. The second Hardy–Littlewood conjecture concerns the concavity of the prime-counting function.

First conjecture

For an admissible pattern of integers, the conjecture gives an asymptotic formula for the number of integers n such that all the shifted values n + ai are prime. The formula involves a "singular series" constant that measures local congruence obstructions — for twin primes it yields the famous twin-prime constant, about 0.66016. The conjecture implies that there are infinitely many twin primes, a special case of the unsolved twin prime conjecture, and extensive computation supports its accuracy.

Second conjecture

The second conjecture asserts that π(x + y) ≤ π(x) + π(y) for x, y ≥ 2 — that an interval of length y never contains more primes than the initial interval of the same length. Although plausible for small values, it is now widely believed to be false and, remarkably, incompatible with the first conjecture: in 1974 Ian Richards and others showed the two cannot both hold, and a counterexample to the second is expected at very large values, though none has been found.
