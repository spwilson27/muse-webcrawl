Urban geography is the subdiscipline of geography that derives from a study of cities and urban processes. Urban geographers and urbanists work to understand how and why cities are distributed as they are, how they are internally organized, and how urban life and environments change over time.

Scope

The field addresses two broad themes: the geography of cities — their location, distribution, and the systems and networks that connect them — and geography within cities — the spatial patterns of land use, housing, employment, transport, segregation, and social life inside urban areas.

Models of urban structure

Classical models of internal city structure were developed by the Chicago School of sociology in the 1920s–1940s: Ernest Burgess's concentric zone model (1925), Homer Hoyt's sector model (1939), and Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman's multiple nuclei model (1945). Walter Christaller's central place theory (1933) explained the size and spacing of settlements in an urban hierarchy. Later work incorporated bid-rent theory, quantitative and GIS methods, and critical perspectives on capitalism, race, gender and the state in producing urban space.

Contemporary themes

Modern urban geography examines suburbanization and sprawl, gentrification and displacement, global and world cities, informal settlements, smart cities, urban sustainability and resilience, and the effects of globalization and financialization on housing and labour markets. With more than half of humanity now living in urban areas, the field is central to understanding contemporary social and environmental change.
