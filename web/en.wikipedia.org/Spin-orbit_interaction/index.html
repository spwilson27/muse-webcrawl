In quantum physics, the spin–orbit interaction (also called spin–orbit effect or spin–orbit coupling) is a relativistic interaction of a particle's spin with its motion inside a potential. A key example is the spin–orbit interaction leading to shifts in an electron's atomic energy levels, due to electromagnetic interaction between the electron's magnetic dipole, its orbital motion, and the electrostatic field of the positively charged nucleus.

Explanation

In the electron's rest frame, the nucleus moves around it, and its electric field is partly transformed into a magnetic field that acts on the electron's spin magnetic moment. Llewellyn Thomas showed in 1926 that relativistic precession (Thomas precession) halves the naive result, giving agreement with experiment. The interaction energy is proportional to L·S, so states with total angular momentum J = L + S and J = |L − S| have different energies.

Fine structure

Together with the relativistic correction to the kinetic energy and the Darwin term, spin–orbit coupling explains the fine structure of atomic spectra — for example, the splitting of the sodium D lines into a close doublet. It grows rapidly with atomic number (roughly as Z4 for hydrogen-like atoms), so it is far stronger in heavy elements, influencing their chemistry and producing effects such as the inert pair effect and the colour of gold.

In solids

In condensed matter physics, spin–orbit coupling (including the Rashba and Dresselhaus effects, arising where inversion symmetry is broken) underlies spintronics, the spin Hall effect, and much of the physics of topological insulators. In astronomy and celestial mechanics, a related gravitational phenomenon is called spin–orbit coupling — for instance the Moon's 1:1 spin–orbit resonance (tidal locking), and Mercury's 3:2 resonance.
