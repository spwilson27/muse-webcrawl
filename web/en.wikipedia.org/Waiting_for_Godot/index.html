Waiting for Godot

Waiting for Godot is a play by Irish playwright Samuel Beckett, written in French as En attendant Godot between 1948 and 1949 and first performed in Paris on 5 January 1953 at the Théâtre de Babylone. Beckett's own English translation followed in 1954. Considered the archetypal work of the Theatre of the Absurd, it is frequently described as the most significant English-language play of the twentieth century.

Plot

Two tramps, Vladimir (Didi) and Estragon (Gogo), wait by a bare tree for a figure called Godot, who never arrives. To pass the time they talk, bicker, eat, sleep, contemplate suicide, and perform comic routines with their hats and boots. Twice they encounter the imperious Pozzo and his slave Lucky, the second time with Pozzo blind and Lucky dumb. At the end of each act a boy arrives to announce that Godot will come "tomorrow". The play closes with the famous exchange: "Well? Shall we go?" — "Yes, let's go." They do not move.

Themes and interpretation

Beckett, who resisted explaining the play, allowed interpretations based on existentialism, religion (Godot as a punning echo of "God"), the aftermath of the Second World War, and the rituals by which people structure empty time. The play's minimal plot — in Vivian Mercier's phrase, "a play in which nothing happens, twice" — its music-hall comedy rhythms, and its cycles of hope and disappointment made it a foundational text for modern drama.

Reception and legacy

Early productions divided audiences, but landmark stagings — including Peter Hall's 1955 London production and performances in prisons from San Quentin (1957) onward, where inmates famously identified with the waiting — established its reputation. It has been translated and performed worldwide, and "waiting for Godot" has passed into the language as a phrase for futile, indefinite expectation.
