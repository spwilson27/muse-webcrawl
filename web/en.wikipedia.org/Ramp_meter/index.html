Ramp meter

PurposeRegulate flow of vehicles entering a freeway
First installed1963, Eisenhower Expressway, Chicago
Control methodsFixed-time, local responsive, system-wide adaptive (e.g. ALINEA, SWARM)

A ramp meter — also called a ramp signal or metering light — is a traffic signal installed on a freeway on-ramp to control the rate at which vehicles enter the mainline traffic stream. By breaking up platoons of entering vehicles, ramp metering delays the onset of congestion, reduces merge collisions, and can improve overall freeway throughput.

Operation

Meters are usually active only during peak periods. Signals cycle quickly, often showing green for just long enough to release one vehicle per lane ("one car per green"). Metering rates are set from detector measurements of mainline occupancy and ramp queues, using algorithms ranging from simple fixed-time plans to adaptive system-wide strategies.

Benefits and criticism

Studies in the Minneapolis–Saint Paul region, where the system was controversially switched off for a legislatively mandated experiment in 2000, found that metering improved travel speeds and reliability and reduced crashes; a similar body of evidence supports systems in cities across North America, Europe, Australia and New Zealand. Criticisms include ramp queues spilling onto local streets, perceived inequity between suburbs, and diversion of traffic onto surface roads.

Priority lanes

Many ramps provide bypass lanes for buses, carpools (HOV) and emergency vehicles so that metering does not penalise high-occupancy travel.
