Climate sensitivity

Estimates of equilibrium climate sensitivity have centred near 3 °C for a doubling of CO2

Climate sensitivity is a measure of how much the Earth's surface temperature will warm in response to a change in radiative forcing. Usually, climate sensitivity is the equilibrium temperature change in response to a doubling of the atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration.

Definitions

The transient climate response (TCR) is the warming at the time of CO2 doubling under a scenario in which concentrations increase by 1% per year. The equilibrium climate sensitivity (ECS) is the warming after the system has fully equilibrated, which takes centuries to millennia because of the thermal inertia of the oceans. A related measure, the Earth system sensitivity, includes very long-term feedbacks such as ice-sheet changes.

Estimates

Assessment reports of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change have long placed the likely ECS range between about 1.5 °C and 4.5 °C. The Sixth Assessment Report narrowed the likely range to 2.5–4.0 °C, with a best estimate of 3 °C. Estimates draw on four lines of evidence: the instrumental temperature record, satellite and process studies of feedbacks, palaeoclimate reconstructions (for example of the Last Glacial Maximum), and climate model ensembles, along with studies of climate sensitivity per unit of forcing.

Significance

Climate sensitivity determines how much warming is committed by any given emissions pathway, and thus shapes mitigation policy. Because the fast feedbacks (water vapour, lapse rate, albedo and clouds) are net positive, the sensitivity is substantially larger than the roughly 1.2 °C warming expected from the Planck response alone.
