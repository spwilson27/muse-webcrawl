Conservation paleobiology

Conservation paleobiology is a field of paleontology that applies the fossil and historical record of past ecosystems to problems in conservation biology and ecosystem management. It uses geohistorical data — from decades to millions of years old — to establish baselines, understand long-term ecological change and anticipate responses to future environmental change.

Rationale

Instrumental and ecological monitoring records are short compared with the timescales of climate change, species invasions and ecosystem transformation. Fossils, subfossil remains, sedimentary ancient DNA and other archives extend the record, revealing former species ranges, past community structures and how ecosystems responded to earlier warming events and human impacts.

Applications

Researchers in the field reconstruct pre-industrial baselines for fisheries and oyster reefs, identify extinction debts and range shifts, assess the novelty of present-day ecosystems, and inform restoration targets and rewilding decisions. A related "near-time" branch studies death assemblages — shells and bones accumulating on modern landscapes — which record recent ecological history.

Development

The field coalesced in the early 2000s, promoted by researchers such as Karl Flessa and Gregory Dietl, and is supported by working groups and syntheses connecting the geological and biological sciences.
