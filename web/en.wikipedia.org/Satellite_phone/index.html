Satellite phone

A satellite phone, or satphone, is a type of mobile phone that connects to other phones or the telephone network by radio link through orbiting communications satellites instead of through terrestrial cellular base stations. Satellite phones can therefore function in most geographic locations on Earth, including oceans, polar regions, and remote deserts and mountains where cellular coverage is absent. They are widely used by mariners, explorers, military forces, disaster-relief workers, journalists, and remote industries such as mining and offshore energy.

A handheld satellite phone; the prominent antenna must have line of sight to the sky.

Networks and operation

Systems use either geostationary satellites — such as Inmarsat and Thuraya, whose single satellites serve large regions from high orbit but introduce noticeable voice delay and poor high-latitude coverage — or low Earth orbit constellations such as Iridium and Globalstar, which provide truly global coverage including the poles. Modern satphones resemble ordinary mobile handsets, though they require a clear view of the sky and generally do not work reliably indoors. Most offer voice calling, text messaging, and low-speed data; newer services provide broadband terminals for ships and field offices.

History and regulation

The first handheld satellite phone services appeared in the 1990s with the launch of the Iridium and Globalstar constellations; both companies passed through bankruptcy before finding sustainable markets in government and industry. Because satphones bypass national telephone infrastructure, their possession or use is restricted or banned in some countries, including India and North Korea, and they are subject to licensing in others. Since 2022, some ordinary smartphones have gained limited satellite messaging capability for emergency use, blurring the line between satphones and cellular handsets.
