Minoru Yamasaki

Minoru Yamasaki (December 1, 1912 – February 6, 1986) was an American architect, best known for designing the original World Trade Center in New York City and several other large-scale projects. Yamasaki was one of the most prominent architects of the 20th century. Along with Henry N. Cobb, he is considered a leader of the New Formalism movement, which rejected the stark functionalism of the International Style in favour of ornamented, human-scaled modernism drawing on classical and Gothic motifs.

Early life and career

Yamasaki was born in Seattle to Japanese immigrant parents and grew up in poverty. After studying architecture at the University of Washington and New York University, he worked for firms including Githens and Keally and Raymond Loewy before moving to Detroit, where he joined Smith, Hinchman & Grylls and later founded his own firm (eventually Yamasaki, Inc.) in Troy, Michigan.

Major works

His St. Louis Lambert Airport terminal (1956) brought national attention. Yamasaki designed the Pruitt–Igoe public housing project in St. Louis (completed 1955), whose televised demolition beginning in 1972 became, for critic Charles Jencks, a symbol of modernism's failure — a verdict Yamasaki himself disputed, arguing the design had been compromised by budget cuts.

His best-known commission was the World Trade Center (1966–1973): twin 110-storey towers with narrow Gothic-arched windows and a vast plaza, destroyed in the September 11 attacks of 2001. Other works include the Rainier Tower in Seattle (on a distinctive tapered pedestal), Century Plaza Towers in Los Angeles, the Dhahran International Airport terminal in Saudi Arabia (whose minaret-like tower appeared on Saudi currency), university buildings including the McGregor Memorial Conference Center at Wayne State, and the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond.

Style

Yamasaki sought what he called "serenity, surprise, and delight" in architecture. His New Formalist buildings used slender columns, pointed arches, screens, reflecting pools, and delicate ornament to humanise modern construction — an approach critics alternately praised as elegant and dismissed as decorative.
