Akira Suzuki (chemist)

Akira Suzuki (鈴木 章, born 12 September 1930) is a Japanese chemist and professor emeritus at Hokkaido University, best known for the discovery of the reaction named after him, the Suzuki reaction (Suzuki–Miyaura coupling), a widely used organometallic cross-coupling reaction. In 2010 he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Richard F. Heck and Ei-ichi Negishi "for palladium-catalyzed cross couplings in organic synthesis".

Early life and education

Suzuki was born in Mukawa, Hokkaido, Japan. He studied at Hokkaido University, where he received his PhD in 1959 and became an assistant professor. From 1963 to 1965 he worked as a postdoctoral researcher with Herbert C. Brown at Purdue University in the United States, where he learned organoborane chemistry that would later underpin his most famous discovery.

Career and research

Returning to Hokkaido University, Suzuki became a full professor in 1973. In 1979, together with Norio Miyaura, he reported that organoboron compounds could be coupled with organic halides in the presence of a palladium catalyst and a base. The Suzuki reaction proved exceptionally mild, tolerant of many functional groups, and reliant on relatively non-toxic, air-stable boron reagents, and it became one of the most important carbon–carbon bond-forming methods in pharmaceutical and materials synthesis.

Honours

Suzuki received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2010. He was also named a Person of Cultural Merit and awarded the Order of Culture in Japan the same year. He has received numerous other awards, including the Japan Academy Prize.
