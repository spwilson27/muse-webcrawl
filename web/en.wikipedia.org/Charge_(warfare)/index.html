Charge (warfare)

A cavalry charge, the classic form of the manoeuvre.
TypeOffensive manoeuvre
Performing armsCavalry; infantry; armoured forces
EffectShock action, psychological and physical

A charge is an offensive manoeuvre in battle in which combatants advance toward their enemy at their best speed in an attempt to engage in decisive close combat. Historically most associated with cavalry, the charge relies on speed, momentum, and shock — frequently psychological as much as physical — to break the enemy's formation and morale before or upon contact.

Cavalry charges

The charge was the decisive tactic of heavy cavalry from ancient shock cavalry such as the Macedonian Companions, through medieval knights fighting with the couched lance, to early-modern cuirassiers and Napoleonic cavalry. A well-timed charge against disordered or flanked infantry could rout whole formations; against steady infantry in square, however, Napoleonic-era cavalry rarely succeeded. Famous examples include the Polish Winged Hussars' charge at the Battle of Vienna (1683), often described as the largest cavalry charge in history, and the Charge of the Light Brigade at Balaclava (1854), remembered as a gallant but disastrous attack on prepared artillery.

Infantry charges

Infantry has also charged, from the Greek phalanx's advance at the run and the Roman pilum volley followed by a sword charge, to the bayonet charges of the 18th and 19th centuries. Commanders such as Frederick the Great and Napoleon timed infantry charges — often in column — to exploit shaken defenders. "Cold steel" was frequently more a threat than an event: defenders who stood firm usually stopped charges short, while charges that succeeded often found the defenders already fleeing.

Decline

The rise of rifled muskets, breech-loading artillery, machine guns, and barbed wire in the later 19th and early 20th centuries made frontal charges against prepared positions extremely costly, as shown in the American Civil War (Pickett's Charge, 1863) and the First World War. Nevertheless, charges did not disappear: the Australian Light Horse charge at Beersheba (1917) succeeded against trench defences, and Polish cavalry charged German positions in 1939 in limited actions. The banzai charges of Imperial Japan in the Second World War were large-scale infantry charges against modern firepower, usually with catastrophic losses.

Modern descendants

The conceptual successor of the cavalry charge is the armoured thrust: massed tanks advancing rapidly to rupture a line and exploit into the rear, a core element of blitzkrieg and later manoeuvre warfare. In riot and crowd-control contexts, police still employ controlled baton charges.
