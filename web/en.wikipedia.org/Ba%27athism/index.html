Ba'athism

Ba'athism (Arabic: البعثية, romanized: al-Baʿthiyya, from بعث, baʿth, meaning "resurrection" or "renaissance") is a political ideology based on the writings of Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and Zaki al-Arsuzi. It is the ideology of the Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party and promotes the creation of a single, unified Arab state through Arab nationalism, socialism, and secularism, summarised by the slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism".

Ba'athist governments came to power in Syria (1963–2024) and Iraq (1968–2003), where in practice the ideology coexisted with authoritarian one-party rule and, increasingly, the personal dominance of ruling families.

Principles

Aflaq's writings define the Arab nation as a single cultural and historical entity destined for rebirth (ba'ath). "Unity" refers to the unification of the Arab lands in one state; "Freedom" to liberation from colonial domination and freedom within that state; and "Socialism" to an Arab form of socialism distinct from Marxist communism, promising social justice and the redistribution of wealth while retaining a role for private property. Ba'athism is officially secular, though Aflaq, a Greek Orthodox Christian, regarded Islam as a defining spiritual achievement of the Arab nation.

In power and criticism

In Syria and Iraq, Ba'athist regimes built extensive party-state apparatuses, pursued state-led development and land reform, and suppressed opposition. Critics, including former insiders such as Syrian vice-president Abdul Halim Khaddam and theorist Elias Farah in his later years, argued that practice diverged sharply from Aflaq's ideals, degenerating into military dictatorship and personality cults around Hafez al-Assad and Saddam Hussein. After the 1966 party split, the Syrian and Iraqi branches each claimed sole legitimacy and revised doctrine in rival directions.
