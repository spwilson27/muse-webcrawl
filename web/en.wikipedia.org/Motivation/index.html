Motivation

[Image: Maslow's hierarchy of needs, a classic model of motivation]

Motivation is an internal state that propels individuals to engage in goal-directed behaviours. It explains why people initiate actions, which goals they pursue, how much effort they invest, and how long they persist. The concept is studied in psychology, neuroscience, organizational behaviour and education, and derives from the Latin movere, "to move."

Theories

Early theories emphasized instincts and drives: drive theory holds that behaviour is motivated to reduce deficits such as hunger, maintaining homeostasis. Abraham Maslow's hierarchy of needs (1943) arranged motives from physiological needs and safety through belonging, esteem and self-actualization. Two-factor theory in the workplace (Herzberg), expectancy theory (Vroom), goal-setting theory (Locke) and reinforcement theory explain effort in organizational settings. Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan) distinguishes intrinsic motivation - acting for inherent interest - from extrinsic motivation by rewards or punishments, and identifies needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness; research shows expected tangible rewards can sometimes undermine intrinsic motivation (the overjustification effect).

Biology and applications

Neuroscience links motivation to dopaminergic circuits, especially the mesolimbic pathway, and to hypothalamic regulation of hunger and thirst. In practice, motivation research informs education (fostering mastery goals), health behaviour, management, addiction treatment and sport psychology. Disturbances of motivation feature in conditions such as depression, apathy and addiction.
