Miso

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Miso (Japanese: 味噌) is a traditional Japanese seasoning produced by fermenting soybeans with salt and kōji (the mould Aspergillus oryzae), often together with rice, barley, or other grains. The result is a thick paste with a rich, salty, umami flavour used for sauces and spreads, pickling vegetables and fish, and above all for miso soup, a staple of Japanese cuisine.

Types and production

Miso is classified by its fermenting grain and colour. White miso (shiromiso), fermented briefly with a high proportion of rice kōji, is mild and slightly sweet; red miso (akamiso), aged longer, is darker and saltier; barley miso (mugimiso) and pure soybean miso (mamemiso, such as hatchō miso) are other major types. Fermentation ranges from a few weeks to several years. Miso is believed to have been introduced to Japan from the Asian mainland by the seventh century, developing from earlier fermented soybean pastes.

Uses and nutrition

Beyond soup, miso is used in marinades (as in miso-glazed black cod), dressings, glazes, and noodle broths. It is high in protein and rich in vitamins and minerals, though also high in sodium. Unpasteurized miso contains living cultures and is valued in macrobiotic and health-food traditions.
