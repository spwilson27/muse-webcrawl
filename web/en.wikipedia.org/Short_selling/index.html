Short selling


Short sellers profit when the price of the borrowed security falls.

In finance, short selling or shorting is the practice of selling a security that the seller does not own, in the expectation of buying it back later at a lower price and thereby profiting from the decline. Typically the short seller borrows the shares from a broker, sells them in the market, and must eventually return them by repurchasing ("covering") the position. Because a share price can in principle rise without limit, the potential loss on a short position is theoretically unlimited, while the maximum gain is capped at the sale price.

Mechanics

To sell short, an investor opens a margin account and borrows shares, usually from the broker's inventory or another client's account, paying interest and compensating the lender for any dividends. The proceeds are held as collateral along with additional margin. If the price rises enough, the broker issues a margin call and may forcibly close the position. Short interest — the proportion of a company's float sold short — is published regularly and is watched as a sentiment indicator.

Purposes and criticism

Short selling serves several economic functions: it allows investors to hedge long positions, contributes to price discovery by impounding negative information, and provides market liquidity. Activist short sellers publish research alleging fraud or overvaluation, and shorts have exposed major accounting scandals. Critics argue that short selling can drive down the prices of healthy companies, amplify panic during crises, and reward manipulation; regulators therefore impose rules such as the U.S. uptick rule (1938–2007), circuit-breaker restrictions, and temporary bans — imposed by several countries during the 2008 financial crisis and the 2011 euro-area crisis.

Short squeezes

When a heavily shorted stock rises sharply, covering purchases can push the price higher still in a self-reinforcing short squeeze. Famous examples include the Volkswagen squeeze of October 2008, briefly making Volkswagen the world's most valuable company, and the GameStop short squeeze of January 2021, in which retail buying inflicted billions in losses on short-selling hedge funds.
