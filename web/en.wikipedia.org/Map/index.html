Map

Field: Cartography
Elements: Scale, projection, symbols, legend
Types: Topographic, political, thematic, nautical

A map is a symbolic depiction emphasising relationships between elements of some space, such as objects, regions or themes. Most maps are drawn to represent geography — the surface of the Earth or another celestial body — but maps can represent any space, real or imagined, from star charts to diagrams of the mind.

History

Maps are among the oldest forms of human communication: Babylonian clay tablets and Egyptian papyri survive from antiquity, Greek scholars such as Ptolemy systematised projection and coordinates, and medieval mappaemundi blended geography with theology. The age of exploration, the printing press and scientific surveying produced increasingly accurate national maps, while the 20th century added aerial photography, satellites and geographic information systems (GIS), and the 21st brought interactive web mapping to everyday use.

Cartographic principles

Because a flat map cannot represent a curved surface without distortion, cartographers choose map projections that trade off area, shape, distance and direction. Maps select and generalise features, encode them with symbols and colour, and state a scale relating map distances to real ones. Orientation is conventionally with north at the top.

Types

General-reference maps show coastlines, settlements and relief; topographic maps add contour lines; political maps show boundaries; and thematic maps display a single variable such as population density, climate or disease. Nautical and aeronautical charts serve navigation, while cadastral plans record property.
