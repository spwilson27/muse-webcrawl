Social War (91–88 BC)

The Social War (from Latin bellum sociale, "war of the allies"), also called the Italian War or the Marsic War, was fought from 91 to 88 BC between the Roman Republic and several of its Italian allies (socii), who had supplied Rome's armies for centuries but were denied Roman citizenship and its political rights. Although Rome won militarily, the war ended with the extension of citizenship to virtually all Italians south of the Po, one of the most consequential political settlements in Roman history.

Causes

Italian allies furnished roughly half the manpower of Roman armies yet received an unequal share of land distributions, booty, and voting power. The tribune Marcus Livius Drusus proposed in 91 BC to enfranchise the Italians, but his assassination that year destroyed the hopes of a peaceful settlement. When Roman officials threatened the town of Asculum, its inhabitants massacred the Roman residents, and the revolt spread rapidly among the Marsi, Samnites, and other peoples of central and southern Italy.

The Italian confederation

The rebels organized a rival state, Italia, with its capital at Corfinium (renamed Italica), a senate of 500, two consuls, and coinage depicting the Italian bull goring the Roman wolf. The confederation fielded armies under commanders such as Quintus Poppaedius Silo and Gaius Papius Mutilus, and early in the war inflicted severe defeats on Rome, killing the consul Publius Rutilius Lupus at the river Tolenus in 90 BC.

Roman concessions and victory

Rome responded with a combination of force and concession. The Lex Julia of 90 BC granted citizenship to loyal and surrendering communities; the Lex Plautia Papiria of 89 BC extended the offer to individuals; and the Lex Pompeia granted Latin rights north of the Po. These measures detached the more moderate allies, isolating the Samnites and Lucanians, who fought on. Roman generals, including Lucius Cornelius Sulla and Gnaeus Pompeius Strabo, gradually subdued the strongholds; Asculum fell in 89 BC. Residual Samnite resistance merged into the civil wars of the 80s BC.

Consequences

Roman citizenship was extended to nearly the whole free population of peninsular Italy, though Romans initially confined the new citizens to a small number of voting tribes to limit their influence — a grievance that fed the civil conflicts of the following decade. In the long run, the war completed the Romanization of Italy and transformed the Republic's citizen body.
