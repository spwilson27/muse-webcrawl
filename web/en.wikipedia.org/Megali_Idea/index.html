Megali Idea

The Megali Idea (Greek: Μεγάλη Ιδέα, "Great Idea") is an irredentist concept that expressed the goal of restoring the Byzantine Empire, by establishing a Greek state that would encompass the large Greek populations that were still under Ottoman rule after the foundation of the Kingdom of Greece (1830) and all the regions that traditionally belonged to the Greek world. The term appeared in 1844 in a speech by Ioannis Kolettis, who became prime minister of Greece.

The Megali Idea implied that Greece would grow at the expense of the Ottoman Empire, recovering lands such as Epirus, Thessaly, Macedonia, Thrace, Crete, the Aegean islands, Cyprus and, most symbolically, Constantinople, which irredentists wished to make the Greek capital. It dominated Greek politics for nearly a century, from the Crimean War era through the aftermath of the Greco-Turkish War of 1919–1922.

History

Greece gained partial independence in 1830, but the majority of Greek speakers still lived outside its borders. Kolettis declared in 1844 that a Greek was not only someone living within the kingdom but anyone in any land historically associated with Greek history or race. During the nineteenth century Greece expanded gradually: the Ionian Islands were ceded by Britain in 1864, Thessaly was annexed in 1881, and the Balkan Wars of 1912–13 brought Epirus, Macedonia, Crete and the eastern Aegean islands into the kingdom, roughly doubling its territory.

The catastrophic defeat of the Greek army in Asia Minor in 1922, the burning of Smyrna, and the population exchange of 1923 between Greece and Turkey shattered the Megali Idea as a practical policy. The Treaty of Lausanne fixed the modern borders. Although the rhetoric of the Great Idea survived in diminished form, notably in connection with Cyprus, after 1922 Greek policy increasingly focused on the existing state rather than territorial expansion.
