Dante Alighieri (c. 1265 – 14 September 1321), most often known simply as Dante, was an Italian poet, writer and philosopher from Florence. His Divine Comedy, originally called Comedìa and later christened Divina by Giovanni Boccaccio, is widely considered one of the most important poems of the Middle Ages and the greatest literary work in the Italian language.

Life

Dante was born in Florence into a family of minor nobility aligned with the Guelphs. He fell in love with Beatrice Portinari, whom he first met as a child and who became the inspiration for La Vita Nuova and his guide in Paradise. He fought at the Battle of Campaldino (1289) and entered Florentine politics, serving as one of the city's priors in 1300. When the Black Guelphs seized Florence with the backing of Pope Boniface VIII, Dante was exiled in 1302 under sentence of death should he return. He spent the rest of his life in exile, chiefly at Verona and Ravenna, where he died in 1321.

Works

The Divine Comedy, written in exile, describes Dante's journey through Hell (Inferno), Purgatory (Purgatorio) and Paradise (Paradiso), guided first by the Roman poet Virgil and then by Beatrice. Written in the Tuscan vernacular in terza rima, it profoundly influenced the standardisation of Italian. His other works include the Convivio, the Latin treatise De Monarchia arguing for a universal monarchy separate from papal authority, and De vulgari eloquentia on vernacular literature.

Legacy

Dante is often called the "Father of the Italian language" and il Sommo Poeta ("the Supreme Poet"). His tomb is in Ravenna; Florence, which repeatedly sought the return of his remains, holds an empty cenotaph in Santa Croce.
