Information society

An information society is a society or economy in which the creation, distribution, use, and manipulation of information is a significant activity, and in which information technology and knowledge work occupy a central role. The concept is closely associated with ideas of a post-industrial society.

Definitions and theory

Economist Fritz Machlup measured the production and distribution of knowledge in the United States in the 1960s, and sociologist Daniel Bell's The Coming of Post-Industrial Society (1973) argued that services and theoretical knowledge would supplant manufacturing as the organizing principle of advanced economies. Thinkers such as Yoneji Masuda in Japan and Manuel Castells, with his theory of the "network society", further developed the concept.

Characteristics

Information societies are typically marked by high penetration of telecommunications and computing, a large share of employment in information occupations, and the treatment of information as a commodity. The term was prominent in policy discussions, including the World Summit on the Information Society (2003 and 2005).

Criticism

Critics argue that the concept can be technologically determinist, that information work is hard to measure, and that talk of a new kind of society may obscure continuities with industrial capitalism.
