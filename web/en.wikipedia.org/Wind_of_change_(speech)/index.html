Wind of change (speech)

The "wind of change" speech is a famous address made by British Prime Minister Harold Macmillan to the Parliament of South Africa on 3 February 1960 in Cape Town. In it, Macmillan acknowledged the growth of African national consciousness and signalled that the British government could no longer support the policies of the apartheid-era South African government.

"The wind of change is blowing through this continent, and whether we like it or not, this growth of national consciousness is a political fact. We must all accept it as a fact, and our national policies must take account of it."

Context

Macmillan delivered versions of the speech during a tour of Africa in early 1960, first in Accra, Ghana, on 10 January. The Cape Town version, addressed to an audience including Prime Minister Hendrik Verwoerd, was the most pointed, as South Africa was then tightening apartheid and moving toward becoming a republic outside the Commonwealth, which it did the following year.

Significance

The speech is regarded as a landmark in the decolonisation of Africa. It reflected the acceleration of independence movements across the continent — 1960 became known as the "Year of Africa" as seventeen nations gained independence — and marked Britain's public acceptance that its remaining African colonies would move toward majority rule. Verwoerd replied that justice for the white man in Africa was also required, but the speech deepened the rift between London and Pretoria.
