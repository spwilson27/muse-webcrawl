Mycelium

Mycelium (plural mycelia) is the vegetative part of a fungus, consisting of a mass of branching, thread-like filaments called hyphae. It is the main body of the fungus — the mushroom is merely its fruiting structure — and it grows through soil, wood, and other substrates, secreting enzymes that digest food externally before absorbing the nutrients.

Function

Through decomposition, fungal mycelia are among the planet's most important recyclers of organic matter, breaking down lignin and cellulose in wood and returning nutrients to the soil. Many mycelia form mycorrhizal networks with plant roots, exchanging nutrients and, some researchers argue, signalling compounds between plants — popularly dubbed the "wood wide web".

Size and age

Mycelial networks can be enormous. A single genetic individual of Armillaria ostoyae in Oregon's Malheur National Forest covers nearly 10 km² and is estimated to be thousands of years old, making it one of the largest and oldest living organisms on Earth.

Uses

Mycelium is cultivated commercially to produce mushrooms, fermented foods such as tempeh and koji, enzymes, and antibiotics. Newer applications include mycelium-based leather substitutes, packaging materials, and building composites, as well as bioremediation of polluted soils ("mycoremediation").
