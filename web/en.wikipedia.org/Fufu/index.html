Fufu

Staple dough of West and Central African cuisine

Fufu (also spelled foufou, fufuo or sakora) is a dough-like staple food of West and Central African cuisine, made by pounding or stirring starchy ingredients — traditionally cassava, yams, plantains or cocoyam — in hot water until they form a smooth, elastic mass. It is eaten by pinching off a piece, shaping it into a scoop, and using it to carry soups and stews such as light soup, groundnut (peanut) soup, palm nut soup and okra soup.

Preparation

Traditionally, boiled cassava and plantain are pounded alternately in a large wooden mortar with a long pestle, one person pounding while another turns the dough by hand and adds water. The result is a stretchy, slightly sour ball of dough. Today, dried fufu flour (from cassava or yam) can be stirred into boiling water as a quicker substitute, and fufu is also made in the African diaspora, notably in the Caribbean.

Cultural significance

Fufu is a daily staple in countries including Ghana, Nigeria, Cameroon, Togo, Benin, Sierra Leone, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Angola, with regional variants such as Congolese luku and Cameroonian achu accompaniments. It is eaten communally, traditionally with the right hand and without utensils, and swallowing without chewing is customary for the well-formed bite.
