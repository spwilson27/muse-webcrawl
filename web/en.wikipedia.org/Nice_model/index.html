The Nice model (pronounced "niece") is a scenario for the dynamical evolution of the Solar System, named for the location of the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur in Nice, France, where it was initially developed in 2005. It proposes that the giant planets migrated from an initial compact configuration into their present positions long after the dissipation of the primordial gas disk.

The scenario

In the model, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune originally orbited in a tight, near-resonant configuration just inside a massive disk of leftover icy planetesimals (roughly 15–35 AU). Slow scattering of planetesimals drew the giants gradually apart for hundreds of millions of years, until Jupiter and Saturn crossed their mutual 1:2 mean-motion resonance. This destabilised the whole system: Uranus and Neptune were flung outward into the planetesimal disk, which was violently scattered — sending a shower of comets and asteroids into the inner Solar System (offered as the cause of the Late Heavy Bombardment about 4 billion years ago) and leaving behind the Kuiper belt, scattered disk, and the captured Trojan populations of Jupiter and Neptune.

Evidence and variants

The model accounts for many otherwise puzzling features: the eccentric orbits of the giant planets, the structure of the Kuiper belt, the existence of Jupiter's Trojans, and the capture of irregular moons. Later research has explored variants, notably a version with a fifth ice-giant planet that was ejected from the Solar System entirely, and "jumping Jupiter" scenarios that better reproduce the terrestrial planets' orbits.
