Forced disappearance

Relatives of the disappeared display photographs of missing loved ones at marches worldwide.

A forced disappearance (also enforced disappearance) is the secret abduction or imprisonment of a person by a state or political organization, or by a third party acting with their authorization, support, or acquiescence, followed by a refusal to acknowledge the person's fate and whereabouts, thereby placing the victim outside the protection of the law. Victims are frequently tortured and murdered, their bodies concealed, and their families left without information — the uncertainty itself being a form of suffering that human rights bodies recognize as cruel treatment of relatives.

History

The practice is associated with Adolf Hitler's Night and Fog (Nacht und Nebel) decree of 1941, under which opponents in occupied Europe were seized and vanished without trace. After the Second World War, forced disappearance became a hallmark of repressive regimes, notably in Latin America: in Guatemala from the 1960s, in Chile after the 1973 coup of Augusto Pinochet, and in Argentina's "Dirty War" (1976–1983), in which up to 30,000 people were "disappeared" (desaparecidos) according to human rights organizations. The Mothers and Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo became emblematic campaigners for truth and accountability. Enforced disappearances have since been documented in dozens of countries on every continent.

International law

The 1992 UN Declaration on the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance and the International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance, adopted in 2006 and in force since 2010, define the offence, oblige states to criminalize and investigate it, and establish the UN Committee on Enforced Disappearances. Under the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court, enforced disappearance committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population is a crime against humanity. Courts such as the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, beginning with Velásquez Rodríguez v. Honduras (1988), have held states responsible for disappearances and for failing to investigate them.

Investigations and legacy

Families' organizations, forensic anthropology teams such as the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team (founded 1984), and truth commissions have worked to locate remains, identify victims, and document cases. The International Day of the Victims of Enforced Disappearances is observed on 30 August.
