Musical temperament

In musical tuning, a temperament is a tuning system that slightly compromises the pure intervals of just intonation to meet other requirements. Most modern Western musical instruments are tuned in the equal temperament system. Tempering is the process of altering the size of an interval by making it narrower or wider than pure. A temperament can be described as the set of adjustments used to tune an instrument.

Just intonation and the need for temperament

In just intonation, intervals are tuned to simple whole-number frequency ratios: the octave 2:1, the perfect fifth 3:2, the major third 5:4. However, these pure ratios are mutually inconsistent: twelve pure fifths exceed seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma (about 23.46 cents). On fixed-pitch instruments such as keyboards, a tuning must distribute these discrepancies — "temper" them — so that music can modulate between keys.

Historical systems

Pythagorean tuning tunes fifths purely and leaves thirds wide. Meantone temperament, dominant in the Renaissance and Baroque eras, narrows fifths to make major thirds pure, at the cost of a severely out-of-tune "wolf" interval in remote keys. Various well temperaments of the 18th century distributed the comma unevenly so that all keys were usable but retained distinct characters.

Equal temperament

In twelve-tone equal temperament, the octave is divided into twelve equal semitones of 100 cents each; every interval except the octave is slightly impure, but all keys sound identical and modulation is unrestricted. It became the standard for Western instruments during the 19th and 20th centuries, although performers on flexible-pitch instruments often adjust intonation expressively toward just intervals.
