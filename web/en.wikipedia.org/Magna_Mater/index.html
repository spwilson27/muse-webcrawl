Magna Mater

Other namesCybele; Magna Deum Mater Idaea
OriginPhrygia (Anatolia)
Cult introduced to Rome204 BC, Second Punic War
TemplePalatine Hill, Rome (dedicated 191 BC)
FestivalMegalesia (4–10 April); March rites of Attis
SymbolsMural crown, lions, tympanum

"Magna Mater" (Latin for "Great Mother") was a title of the Anatolian mother goddess Cybele in her official Roman cult, formally Magna Deum Mater Idaea, "Great Idaean Mother of the Gods." Her cult was officially brought to Rome from Pessinus in Phrygia in 204 BC, during the crisis of the Second Punic War, and became one of the most prominent imported cults of the Roman state.

Arrival in Rome

According to Livy, prodigies and consultation of the Sibylline Books during Hannibal's war prompted Rome to fetch the goddess's sacred emblem—a black meteoric stone—from her sanctuary at Pessinus, with the blessing of King Attalus I of Pergamum. The stone was received by Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica, judged the "best man" in Rome, and by a procession of matrons including Claudia Quinta, whose legendary role in freeing the goddess's grounded ship became a moral exemplum. A temple was vowed on the Palatine and dedicated in 191 BC; its anniversary festival, the Megalesia, opened with games that became a fixture of the Roman calendar and the occasion of many of Plautus's plays.

Cult and priesthood

The Roman cult balanced official acceptance with official unease at its Phrygian character. Roman citizens were long forbidden to serve as her eunuch priests, the galli, whose ecstatic music, dancing, and ritual self-castration—commemorating the fate of the goddess's consort Attis—were regarded as un-Roman. Her priests included the Phrygian-born archigallus, admitted under Claudius, when the March festival cycle (the "Holy Week" of Attis, 15–27 March) was added to the state calendar. In the later empire her cult was linked to the taurobolium, a bull sacrifice performed for the wellbeing of the emperor and, later, the initiate.

Iconography

Magna Mater is depicted as a stately matron wearing a mural (turreted) crown, seated on a throne flanked by lions or in a lion-drawn chariot, holding a tympanum (hand drum) and patera. Her Augustan-era temple images connected her with Rome's Trojan ancestry, since Mount Ida, her home, overlooked Troy.
