Ice core

An ice core is a core sample removed from an ice sheet or high mountain glacier, usually drilled vertically as a cylinder of ice. Because ice forms from the incremental build-up of annual layers of snow, lower layers are older than upper ones, and an ice core preserves a continuous record of past climate — including temperature, atmospheric composition, volcanic activity and snowfall — stretching back hundreds of thousands of years. Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica are among the most important archives in paleoclimatology.

What ice cores record

Bubbles trapped in the ice are samples of ancient air, allowing direct measurement of past concentrations of carbon dioxide and methane. Isotopic ratios of oxygen and hydrogen in the ice itself act as a proxy thermometer: colder conditions produce ice depleted in heavy isotopes. Volcanic eruptions appear as spikes of sulphate and ash; dust, sea salt, pollen and even industrial lead pollution are preserved. Annual layers can be counted like tree rings in the upper parts of a core, while deeper ice is dated by flow modelling and by matching volcanic horizons to eruptions known from history.

Major results

Greenland cores revealed the Dansgaard–Oeschger events — abrupt warmings of as much as 10 °C within decades during the last ice age — and the sharp cold snap of the Younger Dryas. The Antarctic EPICA Dome C core, completed in 2004, extended the record through eight glacial cycles and showed that atmospheric carbon dioxide and Antarctic temperature rose and fell together across ice ages. Comparisons of cores from both poles established that pre-industrial carbon dioxide levels had not exceeded about 300 ppm for at least 800,000 years, far below present values above 420 ppm.

Drilling projects

Major drilling efforts include Camp Century and GISP2 in Greenland, Byrd Station and WAIS Divide in West Antarctica, and the Russian-French Vostok station core, which reached 3,769 metres. Current projects seek "oldest ice" — continuous records beyond one million years — at sites such as Little Dome C in East Antarctica.
