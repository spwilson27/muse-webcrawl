History of New York City

The history of New York City has been shaped by immigration, commerce and its position as one of the world's great natural harbours. The area was inhabited for thousands of years by the Lenape people before European exploration. In 1524 the Florentine explorer Giovanni da Verrazzano, sailing for France, became the first European known to enter the harbour, and in 1609 Henry Hudson, sailing for the Dutch East India Company, explored the river that now bears his name.

Native American era

The Lenape, an Algonquian-speaking people, lived in the region in semi-permanent villages, farming maize, beans and squash and fishing the rich estuary. Manhattan Island, which they called Mannahatta ("island of many hills"), was one of their settlements.

New Netherland

Dutch colonisation began in the 1620s. In 1626 Peter Minuit, director of New Netherland, famously "purchased" Manhattan from the Lenape for goods valued at 60 guilders. The settlement of New Amsterdam grew at the island's southern tip, protected by Fort Amsterdam and a defensive wall along the line of present-day Wall Street. The colony tolerated substantial diversity, including a small Jewish community from 1654, but was also a centre of the Dutch slave trade.

English and revolutionary periods

In 1664 an English fleet seized the colony and renamed it New York after the Duke of York. During the American Revolutionary War, New York was occupied by the British from 1776 until Evacuation Day in 1783, and suffered two devastating fires. After independence, New York served as the first capital of the United States (1785–1790): George Washington took the oath of office at Federal Hall on Wall Street in 1789.

19th century growth

The Commissioners' Plan of 1811 imposed the famous street grid on Manhattan. The opening of the Erie Canal in 1825 made New York the chief port linking the Atlantic to the Great Lakes and the interior, cementing its commercial dominance. Immigration surged, especially after the Irish Famine and the German revolutions of 1848; by 1860 the city had over 800,000 people. The Civil War brought the Draft Riots of July 1863, among the deadliest civil disturbances in American history. Central Park opened in 1857, the Brooklyn Bridge in 1883, and the Statue of Liberty in 1886.

Consolidation and the skyscraper age

In 1898 the five boroughs — Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and Staten Island — were consolidated into the modern city. The subway opened in 1904. The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire of 1911 killed 146 garment workers and spurred labour reform. Ellis Island processed millions of immigrants until 1924. The Harlem Renaissance of the 1920s made the city a centre of African American culture, and the Empire State Building, completed in 1931, defined the skyscraper age.

Great Depression and postwar years

The Wall Street Crash of 1929 ushered in the Depression; Mayor Fiorello La Guardia and parks commissioner Robert Moses reshaped the city with public works. After 1945 the city hosted the headquarters of the United Nations, but suburbanisation and deindustrialisation drained population and manufacturing jobs through the 1960s.

Fiscal crisis and revival

In 1975 New York City came close to bankruptcy and was rescued by state intervention and federal loans after the famous Daily News headline "Ford to City: Drop Dead". Crime peaked around 1990, then fell dramatically during the 1990s. Finance, media and tourism drove a revival, and the population surpassed eight million.

21st century

On 11 September 2001, terrorists destroyed the World Trade Center, killing nearly 3,000 people; the site was rebuilt with One World Trade Center, opened in 2014. Hurricane Sandy in 2012 caused severe flooding. The city remained the financial capital of the United States and one of the world's most visited destinations, while grappling with housing affordability and inequality.
