Cloud classification

Cloud classification is the system by which clouds are named and grouped according to their appearance, altitude, and physical structure. The modern scheme rests on the Latin-based nomenclature proposed by the English amateur meteorologist Luke Howard in 1802–1803, and is maintained today by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) in the International Cloud Atlas. Under this system every cloud belongs to one of ten basic genera, which may be subdivided into species and varieties.

Howard's nomenclature

Howard distinguished three fundamental forms: cirrus (fibrous, like a tuft of hair), stratus (layered, like a sheet), and cumulus (heaped, like a pile). Combinations of these forms yielded compound types such as cirro-stratus and cumulo-stratus, and a fifth class, nimbus, denoted rain-bearing clouds. Howard's Latin terms were adopted internationally and were popularised in art and literature as well as in science, influencing the cloud studies of John Constable and Goethe's meteorological writings.

The ten genera

Clouds are classified by the altitude of their bases into high, middle, and low étages. High clouds (prefix cirro-) comprise cirrus, cirrocumulus, and cirrostratus. Middle clouds (prefix alto-) comprise altocumulus and altostratus, together with nimbostratus, whose base may extend downward. Low clouds comprise stratus and stratocumulus, while cumulus and cumulonimbus are clouds of vertical development that may span several étages. Each genus is further divided into species — describing shape and internal structure, such as fibratus, castellanus, or lenticularis — and varieties describing transparency and arrangement, such as translucidus or undulatus.

Extensions and special clouds

The 2017 edition of the International Cloud Atlas formally recognised supplementary features and accessory clouds, including the wall cloud (murus) and the arcus, as well as new classifications for clouds formed by human activity, such as contrails persisting as cirrus homogenitus, and clouds arising from waterfalls, fires, and volcanoes. Special clouds outside the standard étages include noctilucent clouds in the mesosphere and polar stratospheric (nacreous) clouds.
