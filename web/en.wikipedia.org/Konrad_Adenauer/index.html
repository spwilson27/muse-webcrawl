Konrad Adenauer (1876–1967) was a German statesman who served as the first Chancellor of the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) from 1949 to 1963. A lawyer and longtime mayor of Cologne, he led his country's recovery from the Second World War, anchoring it in the Western alliance and in European integration.

Early career

Adenauer, a devout Catholic and Centre Party politician, became mayor of Cologne in 1917 and president of the Prussian State Council. The Nazi regime removed and briefly imprisoned him; after an assassination plot against Hitler he was again arrested in 1944. The American authorities reinstated him as mayor in 1945, and the British dismissed him months later.

Chancellorship

Co-founder of the Christian Democratic Union (CDU), Adenauer chaired the Parliamentary Council that drafted the Basic Law and became Chancellor in 1949 at the age of 73, winning by a single vote — his own. His era saw the "economic miracle", rearmament within NATO (joined 1955), reconciliation with France, reparations agreements with Israel, and the Treaties of Rome (1957) founding the European Economic Community. The Élysée Treaty with Charles de Gaulle in 1963 sealed Franco-German friendship.

Later life and legacy

Pressure over the Spiegel affair and his succession led Adenauer to resign in 1963 in favour of Ludwig Erhard; he remained CDU chairman until 1966 and died in 1967. Germans have voted him the greatest German of all time in polls, and he is regarded as a founding father of the European Union.
