Mixed marriage

Mixed marriage is a marriage between spouses of differing background, most commonly used for unions that cross lines of race, ethnicity, religion, nationality, caste, or social class. Specific terms include interracial marriage, interfaith marriage (interreligious marriage), interethnic marriage, and cross-cultural marriage; the general term for marrying outside one's own group is exogamy, the opposite of endogamy.

History

Historically, many societies restricted marriage across group boundaries through law, custom, or religious rule. In the United States, anti-miscegenation laws in many states prohibited interracial marriage until the Supreme Court's 1967 decision in Loving v. Virginia struck them down nationwide. South Africa's Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949) banned marriages between Europeans and non-Europeans during apartheid and was repealed in 1985. Nazi Germany's Nuremberg Laws (1935) forbade marriages between Jews and "citizens of German or kindred blood."

Religious perspectives

Religions vary in their treatment of interfaith marriage. Roman Catholic canon law historically required a dispensation for "mixed religion" marriages; Judaism traditionally discourages intermarriage, though practice varies widely by movement; Islamic jurisprudence permits Muslim men to marry women from the "People of the Book" while restricting Muslim women's marriages to non-Muslim men. In India, marriages across caste lines face particular social pressures, and "honour" violence has been reported against some inter-caste couples.

Demographic trends

Mixed marriages have become more common in many countries with immigration and urbanization. In the United States, the share of newlyweds married to someone of a different race or ethnicity rose from about 3% in 1967 to roughly one in six by the mid-2010s, according to Pew Research Center analyses, with rates varying by group and education level.

Children of mixed marriages

Children of mixed marriages may identify with one, both, or neither parent's group, and classification systems (such as the historical "one-drop rule" in the United States) have varied sharply across societies.
