William Morris Davis (February 12, 1850 – February 5, 1934) was an American geographer, geologist, geomorphologist and meteorologist, often called the "father of American geography". He founded the science of geomorphology, the study of landforms.

Career

Davis graduated from Harvard in 1869 and worked at the Argentine National Observatory in Córdoba before returning to Harvard, where he taught for over three decades. He helped professionalise American geography, founding the Association of American Geographers in 1904, and wrote more than 500 works, including Elementary Meteorology (1894) and Geographical Essays (1909).

Cycle of erosion

Davis's most famous idea, first set out in "The Rivers and Valleys of Pennsylvania" (1889), is the cycle of erosion: uplifted land is progressively worn down by rivers through stages he likened to youth, maturity and old age, ending in a low, featureless plain called a peneplain. Landforms, in his scheme, are a function of structure, process and stage. The model dominated geomorphology for half a century; although later criticised as overly qualitative and superseded by process-based and plate-tectonic approaches, it remains a landmark in the history of the earth sciences.
