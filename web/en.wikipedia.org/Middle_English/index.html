Middle English

Middle English (abbreviated to ME) was a form of the English language spoken after the Norman Conquest (1066) until the late 15th century. The period saw dramatic changes to English grammar, pronunciation, and vocabulary: inflections largely disappeared, and the language absorbed thousands of words from Norman French and Latin. Middle English literature includes Geoffrey Chaucer's The Canterbury Tales, the anonymous Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, and William Langland's Piers Plowman.

Historical background

After 1066, French became the language of England's ruling class and administration, while English survived as the spoken language of the majority, gradually losing the complex case system of Old English — partly through contact with Old Norse speakers in the Danelaw, whose similar but differently inflected language encouraged simplification. When English re-emerged in writing in the 13th and 14th centuries, it differed markedly from the Old English of Anglo-Saxon manuscripts.

Grammar and vocabulary

Middle English replaced grammatical gender and most case endings with word order and prepositions. The plural ending -es and third-person -eth (later -s) became standard in the London dialect. French loanwords — perhaps 10,000 entered the language — enriched fields of law, government, cuisine, fashion, and art, while Latin supplied learned vocabulary. Pronouns such as they/them/their were borrowed from Old Norse.

Literature

The 14th century is the great age of Middle English literature. Chaucer (c. 1343–1400), writing in the London dialect, produced Troilus and Criseyde as well as the Canterbury Tales. His contemporary, the anonymous Gawain-poet of the North-West Midlands, used dense alliterative verse and regional vocabulary. John Wycliffe's followers produced the first complete English Bible translation. In the 15th century, Caxton's printing press (1476) helped standardise the London-East Midlands form that became the basis of Modern English.

Transition to Early Modern English

The Great Vowel Shift, beginning around 1400, gradually transformed long vowels, while printing and administrative standardisation (Chancery Standard) fixed spelling. By about 1500 the language is conventionally classed as Early Modern English.
