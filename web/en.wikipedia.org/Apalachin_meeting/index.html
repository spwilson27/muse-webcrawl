Apalachin meeting

The Apalachin meeting exposed the American Mafia to public scrutiny

The Apalachin meeting was a historic summit of the American Mafia held at the home of Joseph "Joe the Barber" Barbara in Apalachin, New York, on 14 November 1957. Roughly 100 Mafiosi from the United States, Italy, and Cuba gathered to discuss matters including the heroin trade, garment industry rackets, and the leadership disputes following the shooting of Frank Costello and the murder of Albert Anastasia.

The raid

Suspicious of the expensive cars and out-of-state plates converging on the small town, New York State Police Sergeant Edgar Croswell—who had Barbara's estate under surveillance—set up roadblocks with federal agents. Dozens of mobsters fled into the surrounding woods; about 60 were detained and identified, many in expensive suits and silk shirts, though most were eventually released for lack of evidence of a crime.

Consequences

Twenty attendees were convicted of conspiracy to obstruct justice in 1959, but the convictions were overturned on appeal in 1960. Regardless, the publicity forced the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, who had long downplayed the existence of a national crime syndicate, to acknowledge the Mafia and launch the Top Hoodlum Program. The meeting is widely regarded as a turning point that exposed La Cosa Nostra to American public and law-enforcement attention, and it raised the profile of attendees such as Vito Genovese, who is believed to have called the meeting partly to legitimize his position.
