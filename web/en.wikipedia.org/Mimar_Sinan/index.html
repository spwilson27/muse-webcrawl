Mimar Sinan (c. 1488/1490 – 17 July 1588), in full Mimar Koca Sinan ("Sinan the Great Architect"), was the chief Ottoman royal architect and civil engineer for sultans Suleiman the Magnificent, Selim II, and Murad III. Over a career of some fifty years he was responsible for the design and construction of more than 300 major structures — mosques, palaces, bridges, aqueducts, baths, and caravanserais — and is regarded as the supreme master of classical Ottoman architecture, often compared with his near-contemporary Michelangelo.

Life

Sinan was born in the village of Ağırnas near Kayseri in central Anatolia, probably into a Christian (Greek or Armenian) family. He was recruited through the devşirme system, converted to Islam, and trained in Istanbul, becoming an officer in the Janissary corps. As a military engineer he campaigned with Suleiman in the Balkans, Persia, and the Mediterranean, building bridges — including a celebrated bridge over the Prut during the Moldavian campaign — and ships. In 1538/1539 he was appointed chief royal architect (mimarbaşı), a post he held until his death half a century later.

Major works

Sinan himself described three works as the stages of his mastery. The Şehzade Mosque in Istanbul (1543–1548), built for Suleiman's deceased son Şehzade Mehmed, he called his apprentice work. The Süleymaniye Mosque (1550–1557), the vast imperial complex crowning Istanbul's Third Hill, was his journeyman work. The Selimiye Mosque in Edirne (1568/69–1574/75), built for Selim II, he considered his masterpiece: its dome, about 31.3 metres in internal diameter, rests on an elegant eight-pier system, and the interior is regarded as one of the supreme achievements of Islamic architecture. The Selimiye was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2011.

Other notable works include the Rüstem Pasha Mosque, famed for its İznik tiles; the Mihrimah Sultan mosques; the Kılıç Ali Pasha complex; the Mağlova Aqueduct and the Kırkçeşme water supply system for Istanbul; and, in Bosnia, the Mehmed Paša Sokolović Bridge at Višegrad, also a World Heritage Site.

Style and influence

Sinan's architecture is characterized by the rational integration of structure and space: cascades of half-domes resolving the thrust of great central domes, unified and luminous interiors, and complexes (külliye) that combined mosques with schools, hospitals, soup kitchens, and tombs. He trained generations of architects in the royal office (Hassa Mimarlar Ocağı); his pupil Sedefkâr Mehmed Ağa later designed the Sultan Ahmed (Blue) Mosque.

Death and legacy

Sinan died in 1588 and was buried in a modest tomb of his own design in a corner of the Süleymaniye complex. His works define the skyline of Istanbul to this day, and his name is borne by universities, mosques, and the fine-arts university in Istanbul.
