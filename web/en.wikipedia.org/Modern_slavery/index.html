Modern slavery

Modern slavery is an umbrella term for situations of exploitation that a person cannot refuse or leave because of threats, violence, coercion, deception, or abuse of power. It encompasses human trafficking, forced labour, debt bondage, forced marriage, and the worst forms of child labour. Although slavery is illegal in every country, the International Labour Organization and Walk Free estimated in 2022 that some 50 million people were living in modern slavery on any given day in 2021 — about 28 million in forced labour and 22 million in forced marriage.

Forms

  • Forced labour — work performed involuntarily under menace of penalty, prevalent in agriculture, construction, domestic work, fishing, and manufacturing supply chains.
  • Human trafficking — recruitment or movement of people by coercion or deception for exploitation, including sexual exploitation.
  • Debt bondage — labour pledged against a loan whose terms make repayment effectively impossible.
  • Forced marriage — marriage without full and free consent, disproportionately affecting women and girls.
  • Descent-based and state-imposed slavery — including hereditary servitude in parts of the Sahel and state-imposed forced labour programmes.

Causes

Poverty, discrimination, conflict, displacement, weak labour inspection, and demand for cheap goods and services create vulnerability. Victims are often migrants or members of marginalised groups, controlled through confiscated documents, withheld wages, isolation, and threats against family members.

Responses

International law prohibits slavery under the 1926 Slavery Convention and ILO Conventions Nos. 29 and 105; the UN's Sustainable Development Goal 8.7 commits states to ending modern slavery. National measures include the United Kingdom's Modern Slavery Act 2015, which requires large companies to report on supply-chain risks, and similar due-diligence laws elsewhere. Critics debate the "modern slavery" framing, arguing it can conflate distinct abuses and obscure labour-rights remedies.
