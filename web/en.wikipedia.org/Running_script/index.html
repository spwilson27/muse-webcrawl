Running script

Running script is one of the major categories of Chinese calligraphy, a semi-cursive style in which strokes are connected and characters are written more fluidly than in the regular script (kaishu), yet remain more legible and structured than in the fully cursive grass script (caoshu). Its Chinese name, xingshu (行書), means "walking" or "running" script, reflecting a middle pace between the slow, deliberate standard form and the rapid cursive.

Running script balances speed of writing with legibility.

History

Running script emerged toward the end of the Han dynasty, traditionally attributed to the calligrapher Liu Desheng (1st century CE), and was refined by Zhong Yao and later masters. Its most celebrated exponent is Wang Xizhi (303–361) of the Jin dynasty, whose Preface to the Poems Composed at the Orchid Pavilion (353 CE) is regarded as the greatest masterpiece of running script in Chinese history. Later masters such as Su Shi, Mi Fu, and Zhao Mengfu developed distinctive running-script styles during the Song and Yuan dynasties.

Characteristics

In running script, brush strokes are abbreviated and linked within and sometimes between characters; radicals are simplified, but character structure remains recognisable. It is one of several styles of Chinese characters alongside seal script, clerical script, regular script, and grass script, and it is the style most often used for everyday handwriting and personal letters in China, Taiwan, and Japan (where it is called gyōsho).
