Fornix (neuroanatomy)

Fornix
SystemLimbic system
OriginHippocampus
TargetsMammillary bodies, septal nuclei, nucleus accumbens

The fornix (Latin for "arch") is a C-shaped bundle of white matter in the brain that carries signals from the hippocampus to the mammillary bodies and other structures. It is the major output tract of the hippocampus and a central component of the limbic system circuit described by James Papez in 1937.

Anatomy

Fibres arise from the hippocampus as the alveus and fimbria, sweep upward as the paired crura, join beneath the corpus callosum as the body of the fornix, then divide into columns that descend in front of the interventricular foramina. Most fibres terminate in the mammillary bodies of the posterior hypothalamus (the postcommissural fornix); others pass to the septal area and ventral striatum (the precommissural fornix).

Function and clinical significance

The fornix is essential for episodic memory. Damage — from tumours, surgery, trauma or the thiamine deficiency of Korsakoff syndrome — produces anterograde amnesia similar to that seen after hippocampal injury. The fornix is also a target of deep brain stimulation trials in Alzheimer's disease.
