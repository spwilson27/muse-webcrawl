The hippocampus is a major component of the brain of humans and other mammals. It belongs to the limbic system and plays a central role in consolidating information from short-term memory to long-term memory and in spatial memory that enables navigation. Its name derives from the Greek hippokampos ("seahorse"), which its curved shape resembles.

Anatomy

Each hippocampus lies beneath the cerebral cortex in the medial temporal lobe. It consists of tightly folded layers of densely packed neurons, divided into subfields CA1–CA4, together with the dentate gyrus and subiculum. Its principal output travels through the fornix to the mammillary bodies, forming part of the Papez circuit.

Function

The case of patient H.M., who lost the ability to form new episodic memories after bilateral hippocampal removal in 1953, established the structure's role in memory. Place cells in the hippocampus fire when an animal occupies a particular location, forming a cognitive map of the environment; grid cells in the neighbouring entorhinal cortex complement this system. The hippocampus is among the first regions damaged in Alzheimer's disease, and it is vulnerable to the stress hormone cortisol and to oxygen deprivation.
