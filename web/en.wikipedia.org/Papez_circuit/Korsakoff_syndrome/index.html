Korsakoff syndrome (also called Korsakoff psychosis) is a chronic memory disorder caused by severe deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1), most commonly resulting from alcohol use disorder, but also seen after bariatric surgery, hyperemesis or malnutrition. It is frequently the sequel of Wernicke encephalopathy, and the two are often combined as Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

Symptoms

The hallmark is a profound anterograde amnesia — an inability to form new memories — together with gaps in past memory. Patients characteristically fill those gaps with confabulation: sincere but fabricated accounts of events that never happened. Working memory, procedural learning and general intelligence are comparatively spared.

Neuropathology

Damage centres on the mammillary bodies, the mediodorsal nucleus of the thalamus and other structures along the circuit linking the hippocampus to the diencephalon — the same circuit carried by the fornix. The syndrome, described by the Russian neuropsychiatrist Sergei Korsakoff in the 1880s, demonstrates that memory depends on an extended network within the limbic system, not on the hippocampus alone.

Treatment

Early high-dose thiamine can prevent progression; established Korsakoff syndrome is only partly reversible, and many patients require long-term supervised care and memory rehabilitation.
