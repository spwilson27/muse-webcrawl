Unicameralism

Unicameralism is a type of legislature consisting of one house or assembly that legislates and votes as one. Roughly half of the world's sovereign states are presently unicameral, including China, New Zealand, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, and many smaller countries; examples of subnational unicameral legislatures include those of most Canadian provinces, German Länder, and the U.S. state of Nebraska.

Arguments in favour

Supporters argue that a single chamber is more efficient, avoids duplication and deadlock between houses, makes lawmakers more accountable, and reduces cost. The British utilitarian Jeremy Bentham and the French revolutionary thinker Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès were early advocates; Sieyès argued the law expresses the will of the people, which cannot be divided against itself.

Arguments against

Critics argue that a second chamber provides checks and balances, revises hasty legislation, and can represent regions or other constituencies differently. Some unicameral systems compensate with strong committee systems or constitutional courts.

History

Many countries abolished upper houses during the twentieth century, including Denmark (1953), Sweden (1970), and New Zealand (1951). Nebraska's legislature became unicameral in 1937 under the advocacy of Senator George W. Norris. Unicameralism is most common in unitary states, while federations usually retain a second chamber representing their constituent units.
