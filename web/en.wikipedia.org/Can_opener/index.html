Can opener

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A can opener (North American and Australian English) or tin opener (British English) is a mechanical device used to open metal cans. Although preservation of food using tin cans had been practiced since at least 1772 in the Netherlands, the first can openers were not patented until 1855 in England and 1870 in the United States. These early openers were essentially variations of a knife, and the basic lever-type design—still in use—dates to this period.

History

The first tin cans, introduced in the early 19th century, were heavy containers that required ingenuity to open; instructions on early cans advised opening them with a hammer and chisel. The first dedicated can opener was patented in 1855 by Robert Yeates, a cutlery maker in London. In 1870, William Lyman of Meriden, Connecticut, patented an opener with a rotating cutting wheel that rolled around the rim of the can. A serrated wheel that gripped the rim was added by the Star Can Company of San Francisco in 1925, producing the familiar butterfly opener form.

The standard military issue opener, the P-38, was developed in the United States in 1942 and distributed with canned field rations. Its compact folding design was later supplemented by the larger P-51.

Mechanisms

Most manual can openers use a cutting wheel that severs the top of the can just inside the rim while a traction wheel grips the rim's edge; turning a butterfly key advances the cutter around the can. Side-cutting ("safety") openers cut below the rim so that the lid separates without a sharp edge. Electric can openers, first marketed in the 1930s and popularized in the 1950s, automate the cutting wheel with a motor.

Alternatives

Many modern cans feature a pull-tab or easy-open end that eliminates the need for an opener entirely; such ends were popularized after the invention of the ring-pull by Ermal Fraze in the 1960s.
