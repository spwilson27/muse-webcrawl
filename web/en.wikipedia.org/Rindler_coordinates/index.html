Rindler coordinates are a coordinate chart in Minkowski spacetime adapted to observers who undergo constant proper acceleration. Introduced in the context of relativistic physics by Wolfgang Rindler, the chart describes the reference frame of a family of accelerating observers ("Rindler observers") who maintain fixed distances from one another, analogous to a rigid accelerating rocket in special relativity. Although the spacetime itself is flat, the metric in Rindler coordinates resembles that of a gravitational field, making the chart a standard illustration of the equivalence principle.

The Rindler chart

A single Rindler observer with proper acceleration α follows a hyperbolic world line, asymptotically approaching the speed of light without ever reaching it. The congruence of such observers fills only one wedge of Minkowski spacetime, bounded by null planes called the Rindler horizon: events beyond the horizon can never send signals to the accelerating observers, an effect closely analogous to a black hole event horizon.

The horizon and Unruh effect

The Rindler horizon is the simplest example of an observer-dependent horizon. In quantum field theory, an accelerated observer perceives the Minkowski vacuum as a thermal bath of particles at the Unruh temperature, proportional to the acceleration — a result derived in the 1970s that parallels Hawking radiation from black holes. Rindler coordinates are therefore central to discussions of black hole thermodynamics and the nature of horizons in quantum gravity.
