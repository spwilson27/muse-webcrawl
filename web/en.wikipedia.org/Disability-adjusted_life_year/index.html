Disability-adjusted life year

The disability-adjusted life year (DALY) is a measure of overall disease burden, expressed as the number of years lost due to ill-health, disability, or early death. Originally developed by the World Health Organization, Harvard University, and the World Bank in the 1990s for the Global Burden of Disease study, it remains the primary metric used by the WHO to assess the global burden of disease.

Calculation

One DALY represents the loss of the equivalent of one full year of health. For a given disease or health condition in a population, DALYs are calculated as the sum of two components: the years of life lost (YLL) due to premature mortality and the years lived with disability (YLD), the latter obtained by multiplying the number of prevalent cases by a disability weight reflecting the severity of the condition on a scale from 0 (perfect health) to 1 (equivalent to death).

Methodological choices

The original formulation discounted future years and applied age weights valuing years of young adult life more highly; these were dropped in the Global Burden of Disease 2010 study, which also switched YLD estimation from an incidence to a prevalence basis. Critics have raised ethical and technical objections to disability weights and to the assumptions embedded in the measure.

Uses

DALYs guide priority-setting and cost-effectiveness analysis of health interventions, and are reported country by country by the WHO and the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation.
