Balance of trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The balance of trade is the difference between the monetary value of a nation's exports and imports of goods over a certain time period. Sometimes a distinction is made between the trade balance for goods and for services ("commercial balance"). When exports exceed imports, the country has a trade surplus; when imports exceed exports, a trade deficit. The balance of trade is the largest component of a country's balance of payments current account.

Measurement

Statistics are compiled by customs authorities from declarations on cross-border goods movements, with adjustments for valuation (exports usually FOB, imports often CIF) and coverage. Because services, investment income and transfers are excluded, the trade balance differs from the current account balance: a country can run a goods deficit and a current-account surplus if it earns enough from services and investments, as the United Kingdom and the United States illustrate in part. The United States has run persistent goods deficits since the 1970s, while China, Germany, and Japan have been prominent surplus economies.

Economic interpretation

Mercantilist doctrine treated a surplus as inherently desirable — a view criticised by Adam Smith and later economists, who noted that imports raise living standards and that bilateral deficits are economically uninformative. In national accounting, the current account balance equals national saving minus investment, so persistent deficits reflect low saving relative to investment rather than trade practices alone. Deficits are financed by capital inflows — sales of assets or borrowing — while surpluses correspond to lending abroad. Debates over "unfair" trade, currency manipulation, tariffs, and global imbalances keep the balance of trade politically salient even where economists regard the bilateral figures as poor policy guides.
