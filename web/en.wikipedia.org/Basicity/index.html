Basicity

In chemistry, basicity is the quality of being a base (as opposed to an acid). The term is used in two main senses. First, for an acid, the basicity of the corresponding concept in older literature — sometimes called the acid's "basicity" — refers to the number of replaceable hydrogen atoms in one molecule of an acid: acetic acid is monobasic, sulfuric acid is dibasic, and phosphoric acid is tribasic. Second, and more commonly today, basicity describes the strength or proton-accepting tendency of a base, i.e. its ability to accept a proton (H+) or donate an electron pair.

Base strength is commonly expressed through the pKb value.

Measurement

The basicity of a base B in water is quantified by its base dissociation constant Kb for the equilibrium B + H2O ⇌ BH+ + OH−, and by the corresponding pKb = −log10 Kb; a smaller pKb indicates a stronger base. Equivalently, chemists often use the pKa of the conjugate acid BH+: the higher that pKa, the stronger the base. In the Brønsted–Lowry view basicity is proton affinity; in the Lewis view it is the tendency to donate an electron pair to an acid. Basicity is influenced by factors such as electronegativity, resonance stabilisation, inductive effects, hybridisation, and solvent.
