Borderline personality disorder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Borderline personality disorder (BPD), also known as emotionally unstable personality disorder (EUPD), is a personality disorder characterized by a long-term pattern of intense and unstable interpersonal relationships, distorted sense of self, and strong emotional reactions. Those affected often engage in self-harm and other dangerous behaviors, often due to their difficulty with returning their emotional level to a healthy or normal baseline. They may also struggle with a feeling of emptiness, fear of abandonment, and detachment from reality.

Signs and symptoms

The nine diagnostic criteria in the DSM-5 include frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment; unstable and intense relationships alternating between idealization and devaluation; identity disturbance; impulsivity; recurrent suicidal behavior or self-mutilation; affective instability; chronic feelings of emptiness; intense, inappropriate anger; and transient stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociation. Symptoms most commonly begin in adolescence or early adulthood.

Causes

The causes of BPD are not fully understood. Twin studies suggest a substantial genetic contribution, and neuroimaging research has found differences in brain regions involved in emotion regulation, including the amygdala and prefrontal cortex. Adverse childhood experiences such as abuse, neglect, and unstable family environments are reported at high rates among people with the disorder, though BPD also occurs in their absence.

Treatment

Psychotherapy is the mainstay of treatment. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), developed by Marsha Linehan specifically for BPD, teaches skills in mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness, and has the strongest evidence base. Mentalization-based therapy and transference-focused psychotherapy are also used. No medication is approved specifically for BPD, though drugs are sometimes prescribed for co-occurring symptoms such as depression and anxiety.

Prognosis

Long-term studies show that many people with BPD improve substantially over time; a majority no longer meet diagnostic criteria after a decade, though social and occupational functioning may recover more slowly.
