Rehabilitation Act of 1973

Activists' 1977 sit-ins forced the signing of Section 504 regulations.

The Rehabilitation Act of 1973 is a United States federal law, codified at 29 U.S.C. § 701 et seq., that authorises funding for vocational rehabilitation services, supported employment, independent living and client assistance, and established civil-rights protections for people with disabilities in connection with federal programmes and employment. President Richard Nixon signed the Act on 26 September 1973 after vetoing two earlier versions over cost concerns.

Key sections

Section 501 prohibits disability discrimination in federal employment and requires affirmative action plans. Section 503 imposes similar obligations on federal contractors. Section 504 — the most famous provision — provides that no otherwise qualified individual with a disability shall, solely by reason of disability, be excluded from participation in, denied the benefits of, or subjected to discrimination under any programme or activity receiving federal financial assistance. Section 508, added in 1986 and strengthened in 1998, requires federal electronic and information technology to be accessible to people with disabilities.

Implementation

Section 504's implementing regulations were delayed for four years, provoking nationwide protests in April 1977, including a 28-day sit-in at the San Francisco federal building — among the longest occupations of a federal building in U.S. history. Secretary Joseph Califano signed the regulations on 28 April 1977. The Act laid the conceptual groundwork for the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, which extended similar protections to the private sector.
