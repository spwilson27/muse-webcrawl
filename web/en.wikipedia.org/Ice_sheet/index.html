Ice sheet

Ice sheet
[Image: Aerial view of the margin of the Greenland ice sheet]
DefinitionMass of glacial land ice extending over more than 50,000 km²
Present examplesAntarctic ice sheet, Greenland ice sheet

An ice sheet, also known as a continental glacier, is a mass of glacial ice that covers surrounding terrain and is greater than 50,000 km² (19,000 sq mi) in area. Smaller masses of land ice are called ice caps or ice fields. Today the only ice sheets on Earth are those covering Antarctica and Greenland; together they hold about 99% of the fresh water stored in ice on the planet.

Characteristics

Ice sheets form where snow accumulates faster than it melts or calves away over many thousands of years. The weight of new snow compresses older layers into dense glacial ice, which flows outward under its own weight. The Antarctic ice sheet is up to about 4.8 km thick and covers some 14 million km²; if it melted entirely, global sea level would rise by roughly 58 metres. The Greenland ice sheet, about 1.7 million km² in area, would raise sea level by about 7.4 metres if fully melted.

Dynamics and climate

Ice sheets lose mass by surface melting, sublimation, and the calving of icebergs from outlet glaciers and ice shelves. Satellite measurements since the 1990s show both the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets losing mass overall, contributing measurably to global sea-level rise. During the Last Glacial Maximum, much larger ice sheets covered North America (the Laurentide and Cordilleran sheets) and northern Europe (the Fennoscandian sheet); their growth and retreat were central to ice-age climate cycles. Ice cores drilled through the sheets preserve a record of past atmospheres stretching back hundreds of thousands of years.
