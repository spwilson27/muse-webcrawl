Quanah Parker (Comanche: Kwana, "smell, odor"; c. 1845 – February 23, 1911) was a war leader of the Kwahadi ("Antelope") band of the Comanche Nation. The son of the Comanche chief Peta Nocona and Cynthia Ann Parker, an Anglo-American woman who had been captured as a child and assimilated into the tribe, Quanah became the principal chief of the Comanche in the years after the Red River War and a successful rancher and investor in the reservation era.

Early life

Quanah was born into the Nokoni band of Comanches, most likely in the late 1840s or early 1850s. His mother, Cynthia Ann Parker, had been taken captive at age nine in the 1836 Fort Parker massacre and lived among the Comanche for 24 years, marrying Peta Nocona. In 1860, Texas Rangers attacked a Comanche camp at the Battle of Pease River and recaptured Cynthia Ann, who never readjusted to Anglo society and died a few years later. Quanah escaped and joined the Kwahadi band, rising to become a war leader.

War leader

Quanah led raids against settlers and the United States Army across the Texas frontier. In June 1874, he was one of the leaders of the attack on the buffalo hunters' outpost at the Second Battle of Adobe Walls, an engagement that helped precipitate the Red River War of 1874–1875. The U.S. Army's campaign destroyed the free-roaming Southern Plains tribes' food supply and horse herds. Facing starvation, Quanah led the last band of free Comanches into surrender at Fort Sill on June 2, 1875.

Reservation leader

On the reservation in Indian Territory, Quanah embraced an accommodationist strategy, encouraging the Comanche to take up ranching and farming while defending their tribal landholdings and cultural practices. He became a prosperous cattleman, built the Star House at Cache, Oklahoma, invested in railroads, and served as a deputy sheriff and as a judge on the Court of Indian Offenses. He became close friends with President Theodore Roosevelt, who visited him in 1905.

Quanah also played a central role in spreading the peyote religion among Plains tribes, which was incorporated in 1918 as the Native American Church. He is often described as the last chief of the Comanches. He died in 1911 of rheumatism complicated by heart failure and was buried beside his mother, whose remains he had reinterred near his home.

Legacy

The town of Quanah, Texas, is named for him. His life story, and that of his mother, inspired extensive historical writing and influenced the novel and film The Searchers.
