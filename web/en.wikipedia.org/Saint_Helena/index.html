Saint Helena is a volcanic island in the South Atlantic Ocean and one of the most remote inhabited islands in the world. It is a British Overseas Territory — part of the territory of Saint Helena, Ascension and Tristan da Cunha — lying some 1,950 kilometres west of the coast of south-western Africa. Saint Helena is best known as the place of Napoleon Bonaparte's second exile and death.

History

The uninhabited island was sighted by the Portuguese navigator João da Nova in 1502 and named for Saint Helena of Constantinople. The English East India Company took possession in 1659, building Jamestown and using the island as a provisioning stop for ships between Europe and Asia; it became briefly a Crown colony in 1673–1815 and again after the Napoleonic period. Tens of thousands of ships called during the age of sail, but the opening of the Suez Canal (1869) and the shift to steam sharply reduced the island's importance.

Napoleon's exile

After his defeat at Waterloo, Napoleon was exiled to Saint Helena in October 1815 and held at Longwood House under the governorship of Sir Hudson Lowe. He died on the island on 5 May 1821. His body was returned to France in 1840 (the retour des cendres), but his empty tomb remains in the Valley of the Tomb. Longwood House and the Briars are now maintained as museums by France.

Geography and economy

The island is rugged and volcanic, with a central ridge rising to Diana's Peak (818 m). Its isolation produced many endemic species, including the Saint Helena plover or "wirebird". Until the opening of Saint Helena Airport to scheduled flights in 2017, the island was reached only by sea — a five-day voyage from Cape Town on the RMS St Helena. The economy depends heavily on British aid, fishing, and a growing tourism trade centred on the island's Napoleonic heritage and nature.
