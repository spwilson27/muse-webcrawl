The University of Königsberg (German: Albertus-Universität Königsberg), commonly known as the Albertina, was the university of Königsberg in East Prussia. Founded in 1544 by Duke Albert of Prussia, it was the second Protestant academy after the University of Marburg and became a celebrated centre of learning, especially in philosophy and mathematics.

History

Modeled partly on the University of Kraków, the Albertina opened with faculties of theology, law, medicine, and philosophy. Its most famous figure was Immanuel Kant, who studied there, taught logic and metaphysics for decades, and served as rector; Kant never traveled far from Königsberg yet transformed philosophy through works such as the Critique of Pure Reason (1781). Other notable scholars included the mathematician David Hilbert and the philologist and philosopher Johann Georg Hamann and Johann Gottfried Herder as students.

The university marked its 400th anniversary in 1944, months before the city was devastated by Allied bombing and captured by the Red Army. After 1945 Königsberg became Soviet Kaliningrad, the German population was expelled, and the Albertina ceased to exist. Its site and traditions are today partly continued by the Immanuel Kant Baltic Federal University, founded under a new institution in 1947.

Legacy

The university's gardens, library, and observatory were renowned; astronomer Friedrich Bessel directed its observatory and made the first reliable measurement of stellar parallax. The famous Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem, solved by Leonhard Euler, grew from the city's geography and launched graph theory.
