Animal locomotion

ModesSwimming; walking/running; flying; gliding; burrowing; crawling
MediaWater; land; air; substrates
StudyBiomechanics; comparative physiology

Animal locomotion, in ethology, is any of a variety of methods that animals use to move from one place to another. Some modes of locomotion are (initially) self-propelled, e.g., running, swimming, jumping, flying, hopping, soaring and gliding.

Modes

Aquatic animals swim by undulating the body or appendages, by jet propulsion (as in squid and jellyfish), or by rowing with fins and flippers. Terrestrial animals walk, run, hop, crawl or slither, evolving gaits suited to their limb number and habitat — from the lateral-sequence walk of quadrupeds to bipedal running in humans and birds. Aerial locomotion includes powered flight in insects, birds and bats, and gliding in animals such as flying squirrels and some lizards.

Energetics and mechanics

The cost of transport differs greatly by medium: swimming is generally cheapest per unit distance, flying intermediate, and running most expensive, reflecting the physics of drag, gravity and lift. Muscles acting on skeletons (or hydrostatic skeletons in soft-bodied animals) convert chemical energy into mechanical work, and elastic structures such as tendons recycle energy with each stride or wingbeat.

Why animals move

Locomotion serves foraging, escaping predators, finding mates, migration and dispersal. Many animals move between media during their lives — eels, salmon, amphibians and sea turtles among them — and some change modes as they grow.
