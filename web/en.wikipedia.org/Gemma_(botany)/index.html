Gemma (botany)

A gemma (plural gemmae) is a single cell, or a mass of cells, or a modified bud of tissue, that detaches from a parent plant and develops into a new individual. Gemmae are a means of asexual reproduction found in many mosses, liverworts and hornworts, as well as in some fungi and algae. The term comes from the Latin gemma, "bud" or "jewel".

Occurrence and dispersal

In liverworts such as Marchantia, disc-shaped gemmae are produced in splash cups (gemma cups) on the upper surface of the thallus; raindrops splash the gemmae out and disperse them, whereupon each can grow into a new plant genetically identical to the parent. In many leafy liverworts and mosses, gemmae form on leaves, stems or specialised branches, and in some mosses they are produced on rhizoids or in leaf axils.

Because gemmae are produced without fertilisation, they allow rapid colonisation of suitable damp habitats, complementing the sexual reproductive cycle of the parent plant.
