The Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (CPPCG), or Genocide Convention, is an international treaty adopted unanimously by the United Nations General Assembly on 9 December 1948. It was the first human rights treaty adopted by the UN and codified the crime of genocide, a term coined by the Polish lawyer Raphael Lemkin, who campaigned tirelessly for the convention.

Definition

Article II defines genocide as acts committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group", including killing members of the group, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to destroy the group, imposing measures to prevent births, and forcibly transferring children. Political groups were excluded from the definition, a compromise criticised ever since. Article III makes punishable genocide itself, conspiracy, incitement, attempt and complicity.

Enforcement

States parties undertake to prevent and punish genocide, to enact domestic legislation, and to try offenders in their own courts or before an international tribunal. Disputes may be referred to the International Court of Justice — invoked, for example, in cases brought by Bosnia and Herzegovina against Serbia, and by The Gambia against Myanmar. The ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda and the International Criminal Court have prosecuted genocide under definitions drawn from the convention.

Criticism

Scholars and activists have criticised the convention's narrow group definition, the difficulty of proving specific intent, and states' reluctance to act on the duty to prevent, notably during the Rwandan genocide of 1994. Despite these limits, its definition remains the authoritative legal standard.
