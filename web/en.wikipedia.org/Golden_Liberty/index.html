Polish nameZłota Wolność
StatePolish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

Key featuresElective monarchy, liberum veto, noble privileges

Golden Liberty (Polish: Złota Wolność) was the political system of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, under which the nobility (szlachta) — some 8–10 per cent of the population — enjoyed extensive privileges, elected the king, and governed through the Sejm (parliament). Its adherents proudly described the Commonwealth as a republic under the presidency of the king.

Principles

The system's foundations were laid by the Henrician Articles of 1573, signed by every elected king: free royal elections, religious tolerance guaranteed by the Warsaw Confederation (1573), regular Sejms, and the right of the nobility to resist (rokosz) a king who broke the law. Laws such as Nihil novi (1505) and the privilege of personal inviolability made Poland-Lithuania, for the nobility, one of the freest states in early modern Europe.

Liberum veto and decline

The requirement of unanimity in the Sejm hardened into the liberum veto, by which a single deputy could dissolve the assembly and nullify its work. First used notoriously in 1652, it was increasingly exploited — often with foreign bribery — paralysing taxation and reform. Historians debate whether this paralysis, combined with magnate power and serfdom for the peasantry, doomed the Commonwealth, which disappeared in the Partitions of Poland (1772–1795). The Constitution of 3 May 1791 attempted to abolish the veto and reform the system, too late to save the state.
