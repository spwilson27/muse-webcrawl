Dye-sublimation printing

Dye-sublimation printing (or dye-sub printing) is a digital printing technology that uses heat to transfer dye onto materials such as plastic, card, paper, or fabric. The name derives from the sublimation process, in which the solid dye converts directly into a gas under heat and pressure without passing through a liquid phase, then bonds with the substrate as it cools.

Process

In a typical dye-sub printer, a ribbon carries panels of cyan, magenta, yellow, and sometimes a clear protective overcoat. A thermal print head with hundreds of heating elements vaporizes varying amounts of each dye in proportion to the heat applied, allowing true continuous-tone images with smoothly blended colors — unlike inkjet printing, which simulates tones with discrete dots. The dyes diffuse into a polymer coating on the receiving medium, producing photographic-quality output.

Applications

Dye-sublimation is used for photo printing in kiosks and studios, ID cards, and — in a related transfer process — for decorating polyester fabrics, sportswear, mugs, phone cases, and other polymer-coated items. In textile transfer printing, the image is first printed with disperse dyes onto transfer paper, then heat-pressed onto the fabric, where the dye sublimates and permanently colors the fibers.

Advantages and limitations

Prints are durable, water-resistant, and do not fade or peel easily because the dye is embedded within the substrate. However, the process requires specially coated media or polyester materials, and it cannot easily produce sharp text or pure black-and-white linework compared with some other technologies.
