Traditional animation

Drawing frames for a hand-drawn animated film

Traditional animation (also known as cel animation or hand-drawn animation) is an animation technique in which each frame is drawn by hand. The technique was the dominant form of animation in cinema and television until the advent of computer animation, and it remains in use in many productions, particularly Japanese anime.

Process

In the classic workflow, a storyboard sets out the story in drawings; voice tracks are recorded first and analysed so animators can match mouth movements. Key animators draw the major poses of a scene, assistants and inbetweeners supply the intermediate frames, and the drawings are tested in a pencil test. Final drawings are transferred or drawn onto transparent celluloid sheets called cels, painted on the reverse side, and photographed one at a time on a rostrum camera over painted backgrounds — each frame typically exposed twice ("on twos") for a rate of twelve drawings per second. Multiplane cameras, pioneered at the Walt Disney studio, added parallax depth; from the late 1980s, systems such as Disney's CAPS digitised ink-and-paint and compositing.

History

Pioneers such as Émile Cohl, Winsor McCay and the Fleischer and Disney studios developed the craft in the 1910s–1930s, and hand-drawn feature animation flourished from Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) through the Disney Renaissance of the 1990s. Although computer-generated imagery largely displaced cel animation in American feature films during the 2000s, traditional drawing continues in television animation, anime, independent film and hybrid productions, now usually executed with digital ink-and-paint tools rather than physical cels.
