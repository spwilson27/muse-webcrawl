Gaels

Gaels (Irish: Na Gaeil; Scottish Gaelic: Na Gàidheil)
Regions: Ireland, Scotland (esp. Highlands and Islands), Isle of Man
Languages: Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Manx (Goidelic languages)
Related groups: Celts; Brythonic peoples

The Gaels (Irish: Na Gaeil [n̪ˠə ˈɡeːlʲ]; Scottish Gaelic: Na Gàidheil [nə ˈkɛː.əl]; Manx: Ny Gaeil) are an ethnolinguistic group native to Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man. They are associated with the Gaelic languages, a branch of the Celtic languages comprising Irish, Manx and Scottish Gaelic.

Historically, Gaelic society was organized into clans and kin groups (fine), ruled by kings and chiefs, with a learned class of poets, jurists, and physicians; in Ireland law was codified as Brehon law. Gaelic culture and language spread from Ireland to western Scotland (Dál Riata) and the Isle of Man in the early Middle Ages, and Gaels later settled as far afield as Iceland and, through emigration, North America and Australasia.

History

The Gaels trace their legendary origins, in medieval texts such as the Lebor Gabála Érenn, to the Milesians and ultimately to Scythia and Spain. In the early medieval period, Ireland was a patchwork of kingdoms (túatha) under provincial kings and a high king. From the 5th century, Gaels from Ireland colonized Argyll, founding Dál Riata, and Gaelic later spread across most of Scotland, merging with the Picts under Kenneth MacAlpin in the 9th century. Viking and then Norman invasions reshaped Gaelic Ireland, and the English and Scottish states progressively conquered the Gaelic world between the 16th and 18th centuries — in Ireland through plantations and the Flight of the Earls (1607), and in Scotland after the Jacobite risings, through the Highland Clearances.

Language and culture

The Goidelic languages descend from Old Irish. Today native Gaelic-speaking regions are the Gaeltacht of Ireland and the Gàidhealtachd of Scotland, with smaller Manx revival communities. Revival efforts include Gaelic-medium education, broadcasting (TG4, BBC Alba), literature, and traditional music; Gaelic games are organized by the GAA in Ireland. The Gaelic diaspora, driven by famine and clearance emigration, remains culturally prominent in Canada (notably Nova Scotia), the United States, and Australia.
