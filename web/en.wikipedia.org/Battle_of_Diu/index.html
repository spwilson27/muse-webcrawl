Battle of Diu

Date3 February 1509
LocationDiu harbour, Gujarat, India (Arabian Sea)
ResultDecisive Portuguese victory
BelligerentsPortuguese Empire vs. Mamluk Sultanate, Gujarat Sultanate, Zamorin of Calicut, with Ottoman support
CommandersFrancisco de Almeida vs. Amir Husain Al-Kurdi ("Mirocem"), Malik Ayyaz

The Battle of Diu was a naval battle fought on 3 February 1509 in the harbour of Diu, India, between the Portuguese Empire and a joint fleet of the Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt, the Gujarat Sultanate, and the Zamorin of Calicut, assisted by the Republic of Venice and the Ottoman Empire. The Portuguese victory is often ranked among the most consequential naval battles in history, because it marked the beginning of European naval dominance in the Indian Ocean and the decline of the traditional spice trade routes through Egypt and Venice.

Background

After Vasco da Gama's arrival in India in 1498, Portuguese fleets began diverting the spice trade around the Cape of Good Hope, ruining the Mamluk and Venetian merchants who had controlled the Red Sea route. The Mamluks, supplied with timber and guns through Venetian and Ottoman channels, built a fleet at Suez which, under Amir Husain Al-Kurdi, sailed to India and, with Gujarati allies, defeated a Portuguese squadron at the Battle of Chaul in March 1508, killing Lourenço de Almeida, son of the Portuguese viceroy Francisco de Almeida. Almeida, refusing to hand over power to his successor until he had avenged his son, sailed for Diu, where Husain's fleet lay under the protection of Malik Ayyaz, governor of Diu.

Battle

Almeida's fleet of some 18–19 ships and about 1,300 Portuguese (with Malabar auxiliaries) entered Diu harbour on 2 February and attacked the next day. The allied fleet of around 100 vessels, mostly smaller craft, was anchored in the harbour. Portuguese heavy carracks, using superior artillery in a concentrated line, bombarded the Mamluk flagship and boarded enemy vessels in succession. The Mamluk and allied fleet was annihilated: its large ships were sunk or captured, while Portuguese losses were light.

Consequences

The battle broke Muslim naval power in the Indian Ocean for a generation. Portugal imposed the cartaz licensing system on Asian shipping, exacted tribute from Gujarat, and went on under Afonso de Albuquerque to seize Goa (1510), Malacca (1511) and Hormuz (1515), creating the framework of the Estado da Índia. The spice trade's centre of gravity shifted from the Mediterranean to Lisbon and later to northern Europe.
