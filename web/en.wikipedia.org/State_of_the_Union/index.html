State of the Union

The annual State of the Union address in the U.S. Capitol.

The State of the Union address (sometimes abbreviated SOTU) is an annual message delivered by the president of the United States to a joint session of the United States Congress near the beginning of each calendar year on the current condition of the nation. It fulfils the constitutional requirement that the president "shall from time to time give to the Congress Information of the State of the Union, and recommend to their Consideration such measures as he shall judge necessary and expedient."

History

George Washington delivered the first annual message in person in 1790. Thomas Jefferson discontinued the practice, sending a written message instead — a custom followed until Woodrow Wilson revived personal delivery in 1913. The address has been broadcast on radio since the 1920s and on television since 1947. Calvin Coolidge's 1923 address was the first broadcast on radio, and Lyndon B. Johnson's 1965 speech was the first televised in the evening.

Format and customs

The speech typically reports on the economy, the budget, and the president's legislative agenda and priorities for the coming year. Customs include the attendance of the cabinet, the Supreme Court, and the Joint Chiefs of Staff, with one cabinet member designated the "designated survivor" who stays away to preserve continuity of government. Since 1966 the opposition party has delivered a televised response following the address.
