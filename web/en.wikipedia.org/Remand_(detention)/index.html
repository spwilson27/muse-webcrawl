Remand (detention)

Remand, also known as pre-trial detention, preventive detention, or provisional detention, is the process of detaining a person until their trial after they have been arrested and charged with an offence. A person who is on remand is held in a prison or detention centre and has not been convicted of the charge against them.

Grounds and procedure

Courts typically order remand where there are substantial grounds for believing the defendant would abscond, commit further offences, interfere with witnesses, or obstruct justice if released. Alternatives include release on bail, with or without conditions such as sureties, residence requirements, curfews, electronic monitoring, or surrender of passport. In England and Wales remand decisions are governed primarily by the Bail Act 1976, which establishes a general presumption in favour of bail subject to exceptions.

Duration and custody time limits

Many jurisdictions impose custody time limits on how long a defendant may be held awaiting trial, and time spent on remand is usually deducted from any custodial sentence later imposed. In numerous countries remand prisoners form a large fraction of the prison population, and concerns are frequently raised about lengthy pre-trial detention, the presumption of innocence, and the effect on employment, housing, and families of people ultimately acquitted.

Terminology

In the United States the comparable practice is pre-trial detention following denial of bail or inability to post it. In Scotland and some Commonwealth jurisdictions, remand procedures follow similar principles with locally defined time limits.
