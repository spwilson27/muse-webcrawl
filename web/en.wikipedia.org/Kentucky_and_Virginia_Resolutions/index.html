Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions

The Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions were political statements drafted in 1798 and 1799 in which the Kentucky and Virginia legislatures took the position that the federal Alien and Sedition Acts were unconstitutional. The resolutions argued that the states had the right and duty to declare unconstitutional those acts of Congress that were not authorised by the Constitution. In doing so, they argued for states' rights and a strict construction of the Constitution.

Background

In 1798 the Federalist-controlled Congress, amid the Quasi-War with France, passed the Alien and Sedition Acts, which restricted immigration and criminalised false or malicious criticism of the government. Democratic-Republicans regarded the Sedition Act in particular as an unconstitutional assault on free speech and free press aimed at their party's newspapers.

The resolutions

Vice President Thomas Jefferson secretly drafted the Kentucky Resolutions, introduced by John Breckinridge; the Kentucky legislature adopted them in November 1798, and a second, stronger set in 1799. James Madison drafted the Virginia Resolutions, adopted in December 1798. Both rested on the compact theory: the Constitution was a compact among the states, and the federal government possessed only delegated powers. Kentucky's 1799 resolutions went furthest, declaring "nullification" the "rightful remedy" for unauthorised federal acts. Madison's Virginia text spoke more cautiously of states' duty to "interpose". Madison's Report of 1800 defended the resolutions against Federalist criticism and clarified that they asserted a right of protest and political action, not a legal veto by a single state.

Response and legacy

No other state endorsed the resolutions; several Federalist legislatures formally rejected them, replying that the federal judiciary, not state legislatures, judged constitutionality. The principles resurfaced in John C. Calhoun's nullification doctrine during the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33, in Southern arguments before the Civil War, and in 20th-century "massive resistance" to desegregation. Madison himself, in old age, repudiated the use of his resolutions to justify nullification and secession.
