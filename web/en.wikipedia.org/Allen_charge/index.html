Allen charge

AreaCriminal procedure, jury instructions
OriginAllen v. United States, 164 U.S. 492 (1896)
Also known as"Dynamite charge", "shotgun instruction"

In criminal law, an Allen charge is an instruction given by a trial court to a hung jury, urging jurors in the minority to reconsider their views in light of the majority's opinion, while also reminding jurors not to surrender their honest convictions. The name derives from the United States Supreme Court's approval of such an instruction in Allen v. United States (1896).

Because of the pressure it can exert on holdout jurors, the Allen charge has been nicknamed the "dynamite charge" — said to be able to "blast loose" a deadlocked jury — and the "shotgun instruction". Many jurisdictions have restricted, modified, or forbidden its use, while federal courts continue to permit modified versions.

Form

The original instruction approved in Allen told jurors, in substance, that although the verdict must be the verdict of each individual juror and not a mere acquiescence in the conclusions of others, jurors should examine the questions submitted with candour and with a proper regard and deference to the opinions of each other; that it was their duty to decide the case if they could conscientiously do so; and that jurors in the minority should consider whether their doubt was reasonable when most of their peers, equally honest and intelligent, had reached a different conclusion.

Criticism and restrictions

Critics argue that the charge is inherently coercive, pressuring minority jurors to change their votes merely to reach a verdict, and that it risks convicting defendants whose guilt is not accepted by every juror's independent judgment. Several states, including Arizona, California, and Montana, have prohibited the classic Allen charge in their courts, substituting neutral instructions that urge continued deliberation without singling out the minority. The American Bar Association's standards recommend a more balanced instruction, given before deliberations begin, rather than the traditional Allen formulation.

Federal practice

United States courts of appeals generally uphold modified Allen charges that include safeguards: the instruction must remind jurors not to give up honestly held beliefs, must be addressed to both the majority and the minority, and must not impose a deadline. The Supreme Court declined to condemn the practice in Lowenfield v. Phelps (1988), though it has never required the charge's use.
