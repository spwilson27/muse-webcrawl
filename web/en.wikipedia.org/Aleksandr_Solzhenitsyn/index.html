Aleksandr Isayevich Solzhenitsyn (11 December 1918 – 3 August 2008) was a Russian writer, historian and dissident whose works exposed the Soviet Union's system of forced labour camps, the Gulag, to the world. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1970, expelled from the Soviet Union in 1974, and returned to Russia in 1994 after the USSR's collapse.

Life and imprisonment

Solzhenitsyn studied mathematics at Rostov State University and served as a Red Army artillery officer in the Second World War, twice decorated for bravery. In 1945 he was arrested for criticising Stalin in private letters and sentenced to eight years in labour camps, followed by internal exile in Kazakhstan. His experiences — the camps of the Gulag, a sharashka research prison, cancer treatment in Tashkent — became the raw material of his fiction.

Major works

During Khrushchev's thaw, One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich (1962) was published in Novy Mir — the first account of Stalinist repression to appear legally in the USSR. After Khrushchev's fall his novels Cancer Ward and The First Circle were suppressed at home and published abroad. The Gulag Archipelago (1973), a monumental documentary history of the camp system based on hundreds of testimonies, was published in Paris; Solzhenitsyn was arrested, charged with treason and deported to West Germany in 1974. He settled in Vermont, USA, where he completed his historical cycle The Red Wheel.

Views and return

A fierce critic of both Soviet communism and Western materialism — as in his 1978 Harvard commencement address — Solzhenitsyn returned to Russia in 1994, travelling across the country by train. He remained a prominent, sometimes controversial nationalist voice, critical of the Yeltsin era and cautiously supportive of Vladimir Putin's restoration of Russian state power. He died in Moscow in 2008 and received a state funeral.
