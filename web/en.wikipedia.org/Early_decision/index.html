Early decision

Early decision (ED) is an admission plan used by many colleges and universities in the United States in which applicants apply earlier than the regular deadline — typically by November — and receive a decision by December. Unlike early action, early decision is binding: a student admitted under the plan agrees to enroll and to withdraw all other applications.

How it works

Applicants submit the same materials required for regular admission — transcripts, test scores where required, essays and recommendations — but on an accelerated schedule. Many schools offer two rounds, ED I (November) and ED II (January). Admitted students are expected to commit, although most schools release students whose financial aid offers make attendance genuinely unaffordable, and some release students for other compelling reasons.

Advantages and criticism

Admission rates in early decision pools are often considerably higher than in regular pools, partly because the pool is self-selected and because colleges value the guaranteed enrollment (yield). Critics argue the system favors affluent applicants who can commit without comparing financial aid offers, and pressure students to choose a first-choice school before they are ready. A number of institutions have abolished early decision at times, while others have adopted non-binding alternatives such as single-choice early action or restrictive early action.

Early decision and aid

Because the commitment is made before aid packages from competing schools can be compared, counselors usually advise students with significant financial need to weigh the trade-off carefully. Colleges state that admitted early decision students receive aid under the same formulas as regular admits, typically meeting demonstrated need for domestic students.

Related plans

Related admission plans include early action, restrictive early action, rolling admission and regular decision.
