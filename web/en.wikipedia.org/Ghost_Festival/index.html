Ghost Festival

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ghost Festival (traditional Chinese: 中元節; simplified Chinese: 中元节), also known as the Hungry Ghost Festival, the Zhongyuan Festival in Taoism, and the Yulanpen Festival in Buddhism, is a traditional festival held in many East and Southeast Asian countries. According to the Chinese calendar (a lunisolar calendar), it falls on the 15th night of the seventh month; celebrations in some regions extend throughout the whole seventh month, the "Ghost Month."

Origins

The festival combines Taoist and Buddhist sources. In Taoism, the Zhongyuan festival is the birthday of the Official of Earth, who pardons sins on that day. In Buddhism, the festival derives from the Yulanpen Sutra, in which the monk Maudgalyayana (Mulian) seeks to relieve his mother's suffering as a hungry ghost; the Buddha instructs him to make offerings to the assembled sangha on the 15th day of the seventh month, whose merit can liberate suffering ancestors going back seven generations. The story made the festival a central expression of filial piety toward the dead.

Observances

During the seventh month, the gates of the underworld are believed to open and ghosts roam the world of the living. Families honour their ancestors with offerings of food, incense, and joss paper ("hell money" and paper replicas of houses, cars, and goods, which are burned for the dead to use). On the festival day, elaborate feasts are laid out — sometimes with empty seats for the deceased — and monks and nuns perform rituals to release souls from suffering. Common practices include floating river lanterns and paper boats to guide lost spirits back to the afterlife, and staging operas and getai concerts (in Singapore and Malaysia) at which the front row of seats is left empty for ghostly guests.

Taboos

Ghost Month is considered an inauspicious time: traditional cautions advise against swimming (lest water ghosts drag one down), whistling at night, hanging laundry out overnight, moving house, getting married, or starting a business during the month. These beliefs remain culturally prominent across China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and the Chinese diaspora, and parallels exist with Japan's Obon festival and Vietnam's Tết Trung Nguyên.
