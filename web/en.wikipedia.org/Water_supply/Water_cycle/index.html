Water cycle

The water cycle, or hydrologic cycle, is the continuous circulation of water between the Earth's surface and the atmosphere, driven by solar energy. It describes how water evaporates from the surface of the Earth, rises into the atmosphere, cools and condenses into rain or snow in clouds, and falls again as precipitation.

Description

The sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers, turning it to vapour; plants add moisture through transpiration. Water vapour rises, cools, and condenses around dust particles to form clouds, which winds transport around the globe. Precipitation returns water to the surface, where it collects in bodies of water, soaks into soil and aquifers, or freezes into snowpack and glaciers.

Why it matters

The cycle continually purifies and redistributes freshwater — evaporation leaves salts and most contaminants behind — making it the ultimate source of every community's water supply. Understanding its timing and storage (snowpack, reservoirs, groundwater) underlies flood forecasting, drought planning, and water resource management.

Scientific history

Although ancient thinkers speculated about the origin of springs and rain, the quantitative water balance was established in the 17th century by Pierre Perrault and Edme Mariotte, who showed that rainfall on the Seine basin sufficed to supply its rivers, and by Edmond Halley, who measured evaporation from the Mediterranean.
