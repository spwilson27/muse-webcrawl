Water scarcity

Water scarcity describes a situation in which the demand for water exceeds the available supply, or in which poor quality restricts its use. It is one of the defining resource challenges of the 21st century: the United Nations estimates that global water demand continues to rise while climate change makes precipitation more erratic, and by 2030 the world could face a large gap between demand and reliable supply.

Drivers

Key drivers are population and economic growth, dietary shifts toward water-intensive foods, energy production, aquifer depletion, and pollution. Because most water use is agricultural, regional scarcity often reflects irrigation of thirsty crops in dry climates, from the American Southwest to Central Asia and the Middle East–North Africa region, the world's most water-stressed.

Supply consequences

When supplies fall short, utilities impose restrictions, rationing, and hose-pipe bans; farmers fallow land or switch crops; and cities invest in new sources such as desalination plants and recycled water. Day Zero in Cape Town (2018), when the city nearly shut off municipal taps, became a global symbol of urban water crisis.

Outlook

Integrated water resources management — matching supply systems to realistic demand, protecting source quality, and pricing water sustainably — is widely seen as the path to avoiding scarcity even as climates change.
