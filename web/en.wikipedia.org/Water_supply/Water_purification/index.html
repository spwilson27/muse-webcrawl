Water purification

Water purification is the removal of contaminants from raw water to produce water that is pure enough for its intended use — principally human consumption, but also medical, industrial, and laboratory purposes. Purification combines physical, chemical, and biological operations, and is the essential middle step in a public water supply between abstraction from a source and distribution to consumers.

The treatment train

Conventional purification proceeds through: (1) screening to remove debris; (2) coagulation and flocculation, in which chemicals such as alum cause fine particles to aggregate; (3) sedimentation, allowing flocs to settle; (4) filtration through sand, anthracite, or membranes; and (5) disinfection, most commonly with chlorine, which also leaves a protective residual in the distribution network.

Quality control

Utilities test source and finished water continuously for turbidity, pH, disinfectant residual, and microbial and chemical contaminants, under frameworks such as the WHO Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality, the EU Drinking Water Directive, and the US Safe Drinking Water Act.

Other applications

Beyond drinking water, purification includes producing ultrapure water for pharmaceuticals and microelectronics (via deionization and reverse osmosis), treating boiler feedwater, and purifying water at point of use with jugs, filters, boiling, or portable devices for travellers and emergency relief.
