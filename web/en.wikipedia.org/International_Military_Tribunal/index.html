The International Military Tribunal (IMT) was a court convened by the Allied powers after World War II to prosecute senior leaders of Nazi Germany. Sitting at Nuremberg from 20 November 1945 to 1 October 1946, it tried 22 major war criminals on charges of conspiracy, crimes against peace, war crimes and crimes against humanity, under the London Charter of 8 August 1945. Its proceedings and judgments — the Nuremberg trials in the strict sense — founded modern international criminal law.

Proceedings

The tribunal consisted of one judge and one alternate from each of the four Allied powers, with chief prosecutors including Robert H. Jackson (United States), Sir Hartley Shawcross (United Kingdom), Roman Rudenko (Soviet Union) and François de Menthon (France). The prosecution relied heavily on the Nazis' own documents and on concentration-camp film evidence; the trial introduced simultaneous interpretation in four languages. Defendants were represented by German counsel and could testify and call witnesses.

Judgment and sentences

Of the 22 tried (Robert Ley had committed suicide and Gustav Krupp was unfit; Martin Bormann was tried in absentia), 12 were sentenced to death — including Göring (who cheated the gallows by suicide), Ribbentrop, Keitel, Jodl, Rosenberg, Frank, Frick, Streicher, Sauckel, Seyss-Inquart and Kaltenbrunner; ten were hanged on 16 October 1946. Hess, Funk and Raeder received life imprisonment; others received terms of 10–20 years, and three (Schacht, von Papen, Fritzsche) were acquitted. The tribunal also declared the SS, Gestapo, SD and the Nazi Party's leadership corps criminal organisations.

Legacy

The IMT established core principles — individual criminal responsibility under international law, the rejection of "superior orders" and official position as defences, and the categories of crimes against peace and against humanity — later codified as the Nuremberg Principles and embodied in successor courts from the Tokyo tribunal to the International Criminal Court. Critics, then and since, have raised concerns about victors' justice and retroactive law (nullum crimen sine lege).
