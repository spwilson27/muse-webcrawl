Quantifier (linguistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics and formal semantics, a quantifier is an expression that indicates the quantity of objects referred to by a following nominal expression. Typical quantifiers in English include all, some, many, few, most, no, and the numerals such as three. Quantifier phrases combine with a common noun to form a quantified noun phrase, as in "every student" or "no more than two dogs".

Generalized quantifier theory

The dominant modern framework, generalized quantifier theory, was developed from the 1970s by Richard Montague and Jon Barwise and Robin Cooper, building on predicate logic. In this tradition a quantifier denotes a relation between two sets: the set denoted by the restrictor noun and the set denoted by the scope (usually a verb phrase). "Every student passed" is true when the set of students is a subset of the set of those who passed.

Scope ambiguity

Sentences containing more than one quantifier are often ambiguous in scope: "Every boy kissed a girl" can mean that each boy kissed some (possibly different) girl, or that there is one girl whom every boy kissed. Such readings correspond to different orderings of the quantifiers in logical form, and they motivate mechanisms such as quantifier raising in generative grammar.

Properties

Analyses of quantifiers often test for properties such as conservativity (a quantifier only depends on the intersection of its two arguments), monotonicity (whether truth is preserved under supersets or subsets), and symmetry. Most natural-language determiner quantifiers are conservative, a proposed linguistic universal.
