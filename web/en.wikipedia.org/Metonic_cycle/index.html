Metonic cycle

The Metonic cycle or enneadecaeteris is a period of approximately 19 years after which the phases of the Moon recur at the same times of the solar year. The recurrence is not exact, but the cycle is very close: 19 tropical years equal 6,939.60 days, while 235 synodic months (lunar phases) equal 6,939.69 days — a difference of only about two hours.

History

The cycle is named after Meton of Athens, a Greek astronomer of the 5th century BC, who introduced it in 432 BC. In fact, similar cycles were known earlier in Babylonian astronomy, and the cycle was independently discovered in Chinese astronomy, where it is called the zhang cycle.

Application

The Metonic cycle forms the basis of lunisolar calendars, including the Hebrew calendar and the ecclesiastical computations (computus) used to determine the date of Easter in the Christian tradition. In the Hebrew calendar, leap years occur 7 times in each 19-year cycle, in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19.

Numbers

The cycle comprises 235 lunar months: 110 "hollow" months of 29 days and 125 "full" months of 30 days, giving 6,940 days in the traditional formulation. The golden number used in computus is derived from a year's position within the Metonic cycle.
