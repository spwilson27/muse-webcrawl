Tonic water (or Indian tonic water) is a carbonated soft drink in which quinine is dissolved. Originally used as a prophylactic against malaria, tonic water usually now has a significantly lower quinine content and is consumed for its distinctive bitter flavour, often as a mixer in cocktails such as the gin and tonic.

History

The bitter bark of the South American cinchona tree, and its active alkaloid quinine, were used from the 17th century to treat malaria. In the 19th century, British officers and officials stationed in India and other tropical colonies took quinine daily as a preventive, commonly dissolved in soda water with sugar to mask the bitterness; gin was often added, giving rise to the gin and tonic. The first commercial tonic water was produced in 1858, and brands such as Schweppes — which introduced its "Indian Tonic Water" in the 1870s — made the drink a standard mixer.

Quinine content

Medicinal tonic preparations historically contained far more quinine than modern soft-drink versions. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration limits quinine in tonic water to 83 parts per million (about 83 mg per litre); typical commercial tonics contain roughly half that amount. The quinine in the drink remains readily detectable: tonic water fluoresces bright blue under ultraviolet light because of quinine's fluorescence.

Uses

Tonic water is most often used as a mixer with gin, vodka or other spirits, and appears in drinks such as the gin and tonic and the espresso tonic. It is also drunk on its own as a bitter soft drink. Diet and flavoured varieties — including Mediterranean, elderflower and aromatic styles — are marketed by major producers such as Schweppes, Canada Dry and Fever-Tree.

Health considerations

Although the quinine content is low, tonic water can still cause adverse reactions in sensitive individuals and interacts with certain medications. Regulatory agencies have warned against using tonic water to treat leg cramps, a common folk use, because of the risk of serious quinine side effects at effective doses.
