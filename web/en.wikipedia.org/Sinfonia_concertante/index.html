Sinfonia concertante

Sinfonia concertante (also called symphonie concertante) is an orchestral work, normally in several movements, in which one or more solo instruments contrast with the full orchestra. It emerged as a musical form during the Classical period of Western music from the Baroque concerto grosso, where the concertino group of solo instruments stood out against the ripieno.

The form was especially popular in Paris in the 1770s and in the Mannheim school. Its name distinguishes it from the sinfonia (symphony) and the concerto: whereas the concerto features a single soloist, the sinfonia concertante characteristically has two or more soloists, often from different instrument families, and gives them all prominent, virtuosic parts within a symphonic framework.

Classical period

The most famous example is Mozart's Sinfonia Concertante for Violin, Viola and Orchestra in E-flat major, K. 364 (320d), composed in 1779, in which the viola part was written in D major with the instrument tuned a semitone sharp (scordatura) to give it greater brilliance. Mozart also began a sinfonia concertante for flute, oboe, horn and bassoon, K. 297B, which survives in a possibly inauthentic version. Haydn's Sinfonia Concertante in B-flat major, Hob. I:105 (1792), for violin, cello, oboe and bassoon, was written for his London concerts.

Later examples

Later composers experimented with the genre, including Beethoven, whose Triple Concerto (1804) is closely related, and in the twentieth century composers such as William Walton (Sinfonia Concertante for piano and orchestra, 1927), Malcolm Arnold and others revived the idea of multiple soloists in dialogue with the orchestra.
