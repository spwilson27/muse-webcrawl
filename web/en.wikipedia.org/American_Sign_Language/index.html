American Sign Language (ASL) is a natural language that serves as the predominant sign language of Deaf communities in the United States and most of Anglophone Canada. ASL is a complete, organised visual language expressed by movements of the hands combined with facial expressions and postures of the body. It is distinct from English and is not a manual representation of it.

History

ASL emerged in the early 19th century at the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut (founded 1817), where Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet and the French deaf teacher Laurent Clerc combined French Sign Language with local village sign languages, notably that of Martha's Vineyard. As a result, ASL shares substantial vocabulary with French Sign Language but is largely mutually unintelligible with British Sign Language.

Linguistics

ASL signs are formed from phonological parameters: handshape, location, movement, palm orientation, and non-manual markers (facial expressions and head/body movements) that carry grammatical information such as questions and negation. ASL grammar makes extensive use of signing space, classifiers, and topicalisation. Research by William Stokoe in the 1960s established that ASL is a full natural language, transforming deaf education and linguistics.

Usage and recognition

ASL is widely taught as a second language, ranking among the most-studied languages in U.S. higher education. A number of U.S. states and Canadian provinces recognise ASL officially. Varieties include Black American Sign Language (BASL), which developed in segregated deaf schools in the American South.
