Freedom of the press

Freedom of the press is the principle that communication and expression through the media — print, broadcast and digital — should be exercised freely, without interference or censorship by the state. It is closely related to freedom of speech and is protected by Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and, in the United States, by the First Amendment.

History

Sweden adopted the world's first freedom of the press act in 1766; England's system of licensing printers had lapsed in 1695, the context for Milton's earlier Areopagitica (1644). In the United States the 1735 trial of John Peter Zenger established truth as a defence against libel, and the First Amendment (1791) forbade Congress from abridging press freedom.

Concepts

Press freedom encompasses protection against prior restraint, the right to gather news and protect sources, and freedom from discriminatory taxation or licensing. It is balanced against laws on defamation, privacy, incitement and national security — as in New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), the Pentagon Papers case.

Global state

Reporters Without Borders publishes an annual World Press Freedom Index ranking some 180 countries; Nordic countries typically lead, while journalists in many states face imprisonment, violence and state control of media. UNESCO marks 3 May as World Press Freedom Day.
