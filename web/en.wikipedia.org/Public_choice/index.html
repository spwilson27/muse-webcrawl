Public choice

Public choice, or public choice theory, is "the use of economic tools to deal with traditional problems of political science". It applies the methods and assumptions of economics — in particular methodological individualism and rational self-interest — to voters, politicians, and bureaucrats, treating them as maximising agents in a political "marketplace". Key founders include James M. Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, whose The Calculus of Consent (1962) launched constitutional political economy.

Main ideas

Public choice explains phenomena such as rational ignorance (voters have little incentive to become informed), the success of concentrated interest groups over diffuse taxpayers (Olson's The Logic of Collective Action, 1965), rent-seeking (Tullock), budget-maximising bureaucracy (Niskanen), and vote trading (logrolling). Buchanan received the 1986 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his contributions. The field parallels the notion of market failure with "government failure", arguing that policy outcomes reflect the incentives of political actors rather than a disinterested pursuit of the public interest.

Criticism

Critics contend that the self-interest assumption understates altruism, ideology, and civic duty in politics, and that some public choice claims are empirically weak or ideologically motivated; supporters reply that assuming self-interest is a modelling device, not a psychological claim, and a prudent basis for constitutional design.
