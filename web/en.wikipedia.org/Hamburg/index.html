Hamburg (German: [ˈhambʊʁk]), officially the Free and Hanseatic City of Hamburg, is the second-largest city in Germany after Berlin and is a federated city-state. Straddling the tidal Elbe some 110 kilometres from the North Sea, it is built around one of Europe's largest ports and a network of canals and two artificial lakes, the Inner and Outer Alster.

History

Hamburg originated in a 9th-century Saxon fort, the Hammaburg, and grew as a founding member of the Hanseatic League, becoming a free imperial city and a great entrepôt of northern European trade. It retained its independence through the German Confederation and Empire; its merchants pioneered transatlantic emigration lines, and in the late 19th century millions of emigrants passed through the port for the Americas. The city was devastated by British and American bombing in July 1943 (Operation Gomorrah), which killed tens of thousands and created a firestorm. In 1962 a North Sea flood inundated the city, prompting then-Senator Helmut Schmidt's national coming to prominence.

The city today

The Port of Hamburg, the "Gateway to the World," remains central to the economy alongside aviation (Airbus), media, and logistics. Landmarks include the Neo-Renaissance Rathaus, the Speicherstadt warehouse district and Kontorhaus district (UNESCO World Heritage since 2015), the Chilehaus, and the Elbphilharmonie concert hall (opened 2017), built atop a former warehouse. The Reeperbahn in St. Pauli is famous as a nightlife and red-light district; it was in Hamburg's clubs in 1960–62 that the Beatles honed their craft. Hamburg has more bridges than Venice and Amsterdam combined and one of Europe's highest qualities of life, alongside a strong tradition of civic independence expressed in its "Free and Hanseatic" title.
