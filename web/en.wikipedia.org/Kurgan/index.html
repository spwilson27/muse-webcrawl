Kurgan

Kurgan
TypeBurial mound (tumulus)
DistributionEurasian steppe: Eastern Europe to Central Asia and Siberia
Period4th millennium BCE – early medieval period
Associated culturesYamnaya, Scythians, Sarmatians, and later steppe peoples
EtymologyFrom Turkic kurğan, "mound, fortress"

A kurgan is a type of tumulus — a mound of earth and stones raised over a grave — characteristic of the Eurasian steppe. Kurgans were built by a succession of nomadic cultures from the Eneolithic period onward, and they preserve some of the richest archaeological evidence for early Indo-European-speaking societies.

Construction and burials

A typical kurgan covers a burial pit or timber chamber containing the deceased, often accompanied by weapons, jewellery, pottery, horse sacrifices and sometimes retainers or wheeled vehicles. The earliest monumental examples, associated with cultures such as the Yamnaya (c. 3300–2600 BCE), contained ochre-stained burials. Later Scythian "royal" kurgans of the 1st millennium BCE, such as those at Arzhan and Pazyryk, preserved elaborate goldwork and, in permafrost, tattooed bodies and textiles.

The Kurgan hypothesis

The archaeologist Marija Gimbutas proposed the "Kurgan hypothesis" (1956), identifying the kurgan-building peoples of the Pontic-Caspian steppe as speakers of Proto-Indo-European who expanded into Europe and Asia in the 4th–3rd millennia BCE. Ancient DNA studies since 2015 have confirmed a major steppe migration into Europe, lending strong support to a version of this model.
