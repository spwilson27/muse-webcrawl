Domus

In ancient Rome, the domus (plural domūs, genitive domūs or domī) was the type of house occupied by the upper classes and some wealthy freedmen during the Republican and Imperial eras. The word comes from the Latin domus, itself derived from the idea of a roofed dwelling. In contrast to the crowded insulae where most urban Romans lived, the domus was a private, inward-facing family residence, usually of a single storey.

Layout

The domus was typically built around one or more open courtyards. Entering from the street through the fauces (entrance corridor), a visitor reached the atrium, a large open hall with a central opening in the roof (the compluvium) under which a shallow pool (the impluvium) collected rainwater. Around the atrium were small rooms (cubicula), the master's study (tablinum), and dining rooms (triclinia).

Larger houses added a second courtyard, the peristylium, a colonnaded garden influenced by Greek architecture, around which the family's private quarters were arranged. The street frontage of the domus was often given over to shops (tabernae) that opened onto the street and had no connection to the house itself.

Social function

The domus was the centre of the Roman family's public and private life. Each morning the patron received his clients in the atrium in the ceremony of the salutatio. Ancestral portraits (imagines) were displayed in the atrium, reinforcing the family's status and lineage.

Examples

Well-preserved examples survive at Pompeii and Herculaneum, buried by the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD, including the House of the Faun, the House of the Vettii, and the House of Menander. The Golden House of Nero (Domus Aurea) was an extravagant imperial variant.
