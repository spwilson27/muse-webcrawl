Jacobitism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For the later movement of a similar name, see Jacobite.

Jacobitism was a political movement that supported the restoration of the senior line of the House of Stuart to the thrones of England, Scotland, and Ireland. It arose after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 deposed the Catholic James II & VII (Latin: Jacobus) in favour of his Protestant daughter Mary II and her husband William III.

Risings

Jacobites attempted restoration by force in a series of risings: in 1689 under Viscount Dundee (killed at Killiecrankie); in 1715 ("the Fifteen") for James Francis Edward Stuart, the "Old Pretender", which faltered after Preston and Sheriffmuir; a small Spanish-backed attempt in 1719; and most famously in 1745 ("the Forty-Five"), when Charles Edward Stuart — Bonnie Prince Charlie — landed in Scotland, took Edinburgh, won at Prestonpans, and marched as far south as Derby before retreating. The rising ended in catastrophe at the Battle of Culloden in April 1746, the last pitched battle on British soil.

Support and suppression

Jacobite support was strongest among Scottish Highland clans, Episcopalians, Catholics, and some English Tories, and was subsidised intermittently by France and Spain. After 1746 the government disarmed the Highlands, banned tartan and the bagpipes' military use, and abolished heritable jurisdictions, dismantling the clan system. The direct Stuart line died out with Henry Benedict Stuart in 1807, after which Jacobitism survived chiefly as romantic nostalgia, celebrated in song and later in the novels of Walter Scott.
