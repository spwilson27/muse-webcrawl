Greek wrestling

Greek wrestling (Greek: πάλη, palē) was the most popular organised sport in ancient Greece. A point was scored when one player touched the ground with his back, hip or shoulder, or conceded defeat, or was forced out of the wrestling area. Three points had to be scored to win a match. Wrestling was the first competition to be added to the Olympic Games that was not a foot race, being introduced in 708 BC, and it formed the concluding event of the pentathlon.

Rules and technique

Competitors wrestled naked, their bodies oiled and dusted with fine sand in the skamma, the dug-up pit within the palaestra. Unlike modern Greco-Roman wrestling, holds below the waist were permitted, as were leg trips and a wide range of throws. Biting and gouging were prohibited, and deliberately striking an opponent was forbidden, though the style remained rough: broken fingers were a recognised method of forcing a submission. There were no weight classes; wrestlers were divided only into boys and men.

Famous wrestlers

The most celebrated wrestler of antiquity was Milo of Croton, who won six Olympic victories in the 6th century BC and became the subject of many legends about his strength. Wrestling was also practised as military training and as part of a young man's general education, and it could be combined with boxing in the brutal pankration.
