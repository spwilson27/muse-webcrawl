Index card

An index card (also called a record card in British English) is a small, stiff piece of paper used to record and store individual pieces of information, one item per card, so that a collection can be filed, sorted, and searched. Index cards were the basis of library card catalogues, Rolodexes, and personal filing systems for more than a century before digital databases.

Sizes and format

Cards are cut from heavy stock, either blank or ruled. Common sizes are 3 × 5 inches (the most familiar), 4 × 6 inches, and 5 × 8 inches; the metric A7 size (74 × 105 mm) is also standard. Sets are stored in card boxes, drawers, or rotary files, often with tabbed divider cards.

Uses

Carl Linnaeus is credited with pioneering the use of loose paper slips for organizing information in the 1760s, a precursor of the modern card index. Libraries adopted standard catalogue cards — the 3 × 5 inch format promoted by Melvil Dewey's Library Bureau in the late 19th century. Index cards remain popular as flashcards for study, for speech notes, for recipes, and in personal knowledge management methods such as the Zettelkasten slip-box. Writers have used them to structure scenes and plots, and software interfaces (such as HyperCard and digital "card" layouts) consciously imitate them. In computing, the punched card was the index card's industrial cousin.
