Concerto

A concerto (plural concertos or concerti) is a musical composition generally composed of three movements, in which usually one solo instrument (for instance a piano, violin, cello or flute) is accompanied by an orchestra or concert band. It is accepted in current practice as the standard form for a soloist with orchestra.

History

Baroque

The form grew out of the concerto grosso, in which a small group of soloists (the concertino) contrasts with the full ensemble (the ripieno). Arcangelo Corelli established the model; Antonio Vivaldi, in some 500 concertos including The Four Seasons, codified the fast–slow–fast three-movement plan and ritornello form. J. S. Bach's Brandenburg Concertos and his harpsichord concertos were foundational.

Classical

Mozart raised the concerto to a dramatic dialogue between soloist and orchestra, composing 27 piano concertos as well as concertos for violin, horn, clarinet and other instruments. The first movement adopted sonata form with a double exposition, and the cadenza — an unaccompanied virtuoso passage — became a focal point near the end of the movement.

Romantic and modern

Beethoven, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, Mendelssohn, Chopin, Liszt, Rachmaninoff and Sibelius expanded the form's scale and emotional range, with the solo part demanding ever greater virtuosity. In the 20th century, Bartók, Prokofiev, Stravinsky, Shostakovich and many others continued the tradition; Bartók's Concerto for Orchestra treats the whole ensemble as soloist.

Structure

The standard concerto has three movements: a fast opening movement in sonata form, a slow lyrical movement, and a fast finale, often a rondo. Concertos for multiple soloists include the double concerto (e.g., Brahms's for violin and cello) and triple concerto (Beethoven's for violin, cello and piano).
