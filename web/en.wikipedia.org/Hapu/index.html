Hapū

In Māori society of New Zealand, a hapū is a subtribe — a kinship group of extended families (whānau) descended from a common ancestor, forming the basic political and economic unit of traditional Māori life. Several hapū sharing a more distant ancestor make up an iwi (tribe).

Organisation and functions

Historically, hapū ranged from under a hundred to several hundred members. They controlled land and resources in their rohe (territory), organised food production, fishing, and warfare, and gathered at a marae under chiefs (rangatira). Membership flows from whakapapa (genealogy), and individuals often affiliate with several hapū through both parents. Loyalty in war and politics was primarily to the hapū rather than to the larger iwi, which typically united only for major occasions.

Modern role

Since colonisation and the Treaty of Waitangi settlements process, iwi have often been the recognised negotiating bodies, but hapū retain enduring significance: many claims and mandates assert hapū rangatiratanga (authority), and hapū continue to manage marae, fisheries, and cultural affairs, and are recognised in resource-management law.
