The names of God in Judaism are numerous and carry great sanctity in Jewish tradition. The most important and most sacred is the Tetragrammaton, the four-letter name יהוה (transliterated YHWH), which appears over 6,800 times in the Hebrew Bible. Out of reverence, Jews ceased pronouncing it aloud in the Second Temple period, and its original pronunciation is no longer known with certainty; the scholarly reconstruction "Yahweh" is used in academic contexts, while the vocalisation "Jehovah" arose from a medieval combination of the consonants with the vowels of Adonai.

Principal names

YHWH is traditionally associated with God's self-revelation to Moses at the burning bush and with the name Ehyeh-asher-Ehyeh ("I Am that I Am", Exodus 3:14). Elohim, grammatically plural in form but used with singular verbs for God, is the name used in the first chapter of Genesis. Adonai ("my Lord") is read aloud in prayer wherever the Tetragrammaton appears in the text. El Shaddai is often rendered "God Almighty". Rabbinic tradition also uses descriptive epithets such as HaKadosh Baruch Hu ("the Holy One, Blessed be He"), HaMakom ("the Place", i.e., the Omnipresent), and HaRachaman ("the Merciful One").

Rules of reverence

The Third Commandment prohibits taking God's name in vain. Written names of God may not be erased or destroyed once written; worn-out sacred texts are stored in a genizah and later buried. In everyday speech, observant Jews substitute HaShem ("the Name") for Adonai, and many write "G-d" in English to avoid writing a divine name in a discardable medium.

In mysticism

Kabbalah elaborates further names, including the 42-letter and 72-letter names derived from biblical verses, and associates divine names with the sefirot, the emanations through which God interacts with the world.
