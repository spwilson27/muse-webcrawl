Aboriginal Australians

Aboriginal Australians are the various Indigenous peoples of the Australian mainland and many of its islands, excluding the Torres Strait Islands, whose peoples are culturally and genetically distinct. Aboriginal Australians descend from the first inhabitants of Australia, who arrived at least 50,000–65,000 years ago, and comprise hundreds of distinct groups, each with its own languages, territories, and laws.

Origins and prehistory

Archaeological evidence, including the Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land, indicates human occupation of Australia more than 50,000 years ago, making Aboriginal cultures among the oldest continuous cultures on Earth. Before colonisation there were perhaps 250–400 distinct languages. Societies were organised by kinship, with complex systems of law, ceremony, and land management, including fire-stick farming; cosmology centres on the Dreaming (Tjukurpa and related concepts), linking ancestors, land, and living people. The Gunditjmara people built the Budj Bim eel aquaculture system, now World Heritage listed.

Colonisation and its effects

British colonisation began in 1788. The Aboriginal population, estimated between 300,000 and over a million, fell sharply through disease, dispossession, and frontier violence. Terra nullius — the legal doctrine that the land belonged to no one — was overturned only in the 1992 Mabo decision. In the 20th century, protection and assimilation policies forcibly removed many Aboriginal children from their families, the "Stolen Generations", formally apologised for by the Australian government in 2008.

Modern era

Aboriginal Australians gained full voting rights in the 1960s and were counted in the census after the 1967 referendum. Land rights legislation, beginning with the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976, and the Native Title Act 1993 recognised forms of Indigenous land ownership. Aboriginal people number around 800,000–1,000,000 (about 3–4% of the population) but continue to face significant disparities in health, life expectancy, and incarceration, which "Closing the Gap" policies seek to address.
