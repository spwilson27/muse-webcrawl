Neidan

Neidan (Chinese: 內丹, "internal alchemy" or "internal elixir"), often contrasted with waidan ("external alchemy"), is an esoteric doctrine in Chinese Taoism and a body of physical, mental and spiritual practices intended to prolong life and eventually attain immortality. Instead of compounding elixirs from minerals in a laboratory vessel, the neidan adept treats the human body itself as the crucible in which the Three Treasures — jing (essence), qi (vital energy) and shen (spirit) — are refined.

Practice

The classic stages of refinement are summarised in the formula "refining essence into qi, refining qi into spirit, refining spirit and returning to the void". Techniques include breath control, meditation, visualisation of inner deities and landscapes, dietetics and, in some schools, sexual yoga. The process is mapped in charts such as the Neijing Tu (Chart of the Inner Landscape), which depicts the torso as a sacred geography of mountains, rivers and officials.

Texts and history

Neidan crystallised during the Tang and Song dynasties, drawing on the earlier Cantong qi (c. 2nd century CE), long read as the foundational alchemy classic. The Song-dynasty master Zhang Boduan's Wuzhen pian (Awakening to Reality, 1075) became the movement's central text. Neidan lineages — notably the Northern and Southern schools and later the Wu–Liu tradition — remain active within Quanzhen and other Taoist orders.

Symbolism

Neidan literature deliberately retains the vocabulary of laboratory alchemy — lead and mercury, dragon and tiger, furnace and tripod — as coded metaphors for processes within the body. This shared language long caused scholars to confuse the two traditions; modern research distinguishes them while recognising their historical entanglement.
