Nuclear fission

Nuclear fission is a reaction in which the nucleus of an atom splits into two or more smaller nuclei, often producing free neutrons and gamma rays, and releasing a large amount of energy. Fission of heavy elements such as uranium-235 and plutonium-239 powers nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons.

Discovery

Fission was discovered in December 1938 by chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann in Berlin, who found barium among the products of neutron-bombarded uranium. Physicists Lise Meitner and her nephew Otto Robert Frisch explained the phenomenon theoretically in January 1939 and Frisch named it "fission" by analogy with the division of living cells. The energy released arises because the binding energy per nucleon of the products is greater than that of the original nucleus, the difference appearing as kinetic energy of the fragments.

Chain reactions

Because fission of a fissile nucleus typically releases two or three neutrons, those neutrons can induce further fissions, producing a self-sustaining chain reaction. In a nuclear reactor the reaction rate is controlled with neutron-absorbing control rods and a moderator that slows neutrons to thermal energies; in a weapon, a supercritical mass is assembled so the reaction grows explosively before the assembly disassembles.

Applications and products

Fission provides roughly a tenth of the world's electricity. The fission products are highly radioactive, and managing spent fuel and its decay heat is a major engineering and political challenge. Uncontrolled releases of fission heat can lead to a nuclear meltdown.
