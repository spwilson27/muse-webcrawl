Baron Augustin-Louis Cauchy, founder of rigorous analysis.

Baron Augustin-Louis Cauchy (21 August 1789 – 23 May 1857) was a French mathematician, engineer, and physicist who made pioneering contributions to several branches of mathematics, including mathematical analysis, continuum mechanics, and mathematical physics. He was one of the first to state and rigorously prove theorems of calculus, rejecting the earlier algebraic approach of Euler and Lagrange. His criterion for the convergence of infinite series, his differential equations, and his early development of group theory all mark him as one of the founders of modern mathematics.

Contributions to analysis

Cauchy's Cours d'analyse (1821) put calculus on a firm logical basis, introducing precise definitions of limit, continuity, and the derivative, and an epsilon-style rigour that displaced infinitesimals as the foundation of the subject. He developed the theory of functions of a complex variable almost single-handedly: the Cauchy integral theorem and integral formula, the theory of residues, and the Cauchy–Riemann equations all originate with him.

Other work

Trained as an engineer, Cauchy worked on the fortifications of Cherbourg before turning to mathematics. In mechanics he founded the mathematical theory of elasticity, introducing the concept of stress. He was extraordinarily prolific, publishing some 800 papers; his output was so great that the Academy of Sciences limited the length of articles in its journal. A devout Catholic and legitimist royalist, he followed Charles X into exile after the July Revolution of 1830, returning to Paris in 1838.

Legacy

Cauchy's name is attached to dozens of concepts — the Cauchy sequence, Cauchy distribution, Cauchy horizon, and many theorems — and it appears among the 72 names on the Eiffel Tower.
