Inflation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time, equivalently a decline in the purchasing power of currency. When the price level rises, each unit of money buys fewer goods and services. The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price index — most commonly the consumer price index (CPI) — over a period, usually a year.

The opposite of inflation is deflation, a sustained fall in the price level; a slowing of inflation is called disinflation. Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation — commonly around 2 per cent per year.

Causes

Economists distinguish several mechanisms:

  • Demand-pull inflation — aggregate demand outruns supply ("too much money chasing too few goods").
  • Cost-push inflation — rising production costs, such as wages or imported energy, are passed into prices.
  • Monetary expansion — excessive growth of the money supply, emphasised by monetarist economists; in extreme cases this produces hyperinflation, as in Weimar Germany (1923) and Zimbabwe (2008).
  • Expectations — if workers and firms expect inflation, they build it into wage claims and prices, making it self-sustaining.

Effects

Moderate inflation redistributes wealth between lenders and borrowers, discourages hoarding cash, and gives central banks room to cut real interest rates. High and unpredictable inflation distorts price signals, erodes savings and fixed incomes, shortens contracts, and can destabilise governments. Unexpected inflation benefits borrowers with fixed-rate debts at creditors' expense.

Anti-inflation policy

Central banks control inflation mainly by raising policy interest rates, which dampens demand for credit and slows spending; fiscal measures and supply-side reforms may assist. Since the 1990s many countries have adopted explicit inflation targeting frameworks.
