Chapter 7

Statute11 U.S.C. §§ 701–784
TypeLiquidation bankruptcy
Administered byChapter 7 trustee
Eligibility gateMeans test (BAPCPA 2005)

Chapter 7 of the United States Bankruptcy Code governs the process of liquidation bankruptcy, under which a debtor's non-exempt assets are sold by a court-appointed trustee and the proceeds distributed to creditors, with most remaining debts discharged. It is the most common form of bankruptcy in the United States, used by both individuals and businesses.

Process

A case begins with the filing of a petition, schedules of assets and liabilities, and a statement of financial affairs. Filing imposes an automatic stay that halts most collection activity. A trustee is appointed to review the debtor's property, liquidate non-exempt assets, and distribute the proceeds according to statutory priorities. Most individual debtors have no non-exempt assets — so-called "no-asset cases" — and receive a discharge within a few months. Secured creditors generally retain their liens, and certain debts, including most taxes, child support, and most student loans, are not dischargeable.

Eligibility

Since the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005, individual filers with primarily consumer debts must pass a "means test": if their income exceeds thresholds suggesting they can fund a repayment plan, their Chapter 7 case may be dismissed or converted to Chapter 13. Debtors must also complete credit counseling before filing and a financial management course before discharge.

Business liquidation

For corporations and partnerships, Chapter 7 means the end of operations: the trustee winds up the business and sells its assets, and no discharge is granted. It contrasts with Chapter 11, under which a business may reorganize and continue operating.
