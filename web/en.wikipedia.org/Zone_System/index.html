Zone System

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Zone System is a photographic technique for determining optimal film exposure and development, formulated by Ansel Adams and Fred Archer in the late 1930s and early 1940s. It provides photographers with a systematic method of precisely defining the relationship between the way they visualize the photographic subject and the final results. Although it originated with black-and-white sheet film, the Zone System is also applicable to roll film, colour photography and digital photography.

The zones

The system divides the tonal range of a scene into eleven zones, numbered 0 through X, from pure black to pure white. Each zone differs from its neighbours by exactly one stop (a factor of two in exposure). Zone V is middle grey, reflecting 18% of incident light — the value to which reflected-light meters are calibrated. Zones I–III contain shadow detail, zones VI–VIII hold highlight detail, zone IX is near-white without texture, and zone X is pure paper white.

Method

The photographer meters key parts of the scene and "places" an important shadow value on a chosen low zone (commonly zone III), letting the highlights "fall" where they will. Development time is then adjusted (the classic "N−1, N, N+1" scheme) to expand or contract contrast so the negative's tonal range fits the printing paper. Adams summarised the practical rule as "expose for the shadows, develop for the highlights".

Influence

The Zone System became a foundation of fine-art photography education and influenced later metering practice, including spot metering and digital "expose to the right" workflows. Adams popularised it in his books The Camera, The Negative and The Print.
