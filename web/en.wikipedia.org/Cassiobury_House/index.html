Cassiobury House

Cassiobury House was a country house in Cassiobury Park, Watford, Hertfordshire, England. It was the seat of the Earls of Essex and one of the most celebrated English country houses of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The house was largely demolished in 1927, and its site now forms part of a public park.

History

The estate was originally part of the manor of Cashio, granted by Henry VIII to Richard Morrison in the 1540s. The first substantial house on the site was built by Sir Richard Morrison in the 1540s and 1550s. In 1627 the estate passed by marriage to Arthur Capell, later 1st Earl of Essex, who rebuilt the house on a grand scale between about 1677 and 1680.

The rebuilding was designed by the architect Hugh May, one of the pioneers of the classical style in England. The interiors were decorated with carvings by Grinling Gibbons and paintings by Antonio Verrio, making Cassiobury a showpiece of Restoration craftsmanship. The gardens were laid out and later remodelled, with contributions associated with the landscape tradition that culminated in the work of Humphry Repton.

Decline and demolition

By the early twentieth century the costs of maintaining the house had become prohibitive. In 1922 the contents were sold at auction, and in 1927 the house itself was demolished; the building materials, including panelling and the famous staircase, were sold off. The grand staircase was acquired for museum display abroad. The parkland was purchased by Watford Borough Council and opened to the public.

Legacy

Cassiobury Park remains one of the largest public parks in Hertfordshire. The surviving gatehouse and stable buildings recall the scale of the lost house, and the name survives locally in Cassiobury Park Avenue and the Cassiobury area of Watford. The estate is often cited alongside other demolished English country houses as an emblem of the decline of the aristocratic country house after the First World War.
