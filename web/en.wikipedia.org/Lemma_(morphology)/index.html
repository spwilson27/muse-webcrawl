Lemma (morphology)

In morphology and lexicography, a lemma is the canonical form of a set of words. For example, in English the forms run, runs, ran, and running are all forms of the same lexeme, whose lemma is conventionally written RUN or simply "run" — the infinitive/dictionary form. A lemma occurs as the headword in a dictionary.

Lemma versus stem

The lemma differs from the word stem: the stem is the part of a word that never changes (strictly, the part remaining after removing affixes), whereas the lemma is a full citable word form chosen by convention. For instance, the lemma of the Latin verb forms amō, amās, amat is amō ("I love"), while its stem is am-. Lemmas also differ from lexemes, which are the abstract units that lemmas represent.

Conventions across languages

The choice of lemma form depends on the language's morphology: nouns are often cited in the nominative singular (but genitive singular in some traditions), verbs by the infinitive in many European languages, by the third-person masculine perfect in Semitic lexicography, and by a root consonantal skeleton in some others. In agglutinative languages the citation form is typically the bare stem.

Computational use

Lemmatisation — mapping inflected forms to their lemmas — is a standard preprocessing step in natural language processing, used in information retrieval and corpus linguistics; lemmatised corpora allow all forms of a word to be counted together.
