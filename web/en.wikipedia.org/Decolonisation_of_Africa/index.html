Decolonisation of Africa

The decolonisation of Africa took place in the mid-to-late 20th century, when European colonial powers withdrew from the continent in the decades after the Second World War. Between 1951 (Libya) and 1975 (the Portuguese colonies), nearly all African territories became independent states, transforming the United Nations and the politics of the continent. The process was swift in much of West Africa, negotiated in some regions, and achieved only through armed struggle in Algeria, Kenya and the Portuguese colonies.

Background

By 1945 almost all of Africa was ruled by Britain, France, Belgium, Portugal, Spain and Italy, their territories carved out in the "Scramble for Africa" of the 1880s–1900s. The war weakened the European powers, and a generation of African leaders — many educated abroad and inflamed by the Atlantic Charter's talk of self-determination, pan-African congresses (Manchester, 1945), and the example of Indian independence in 1947 — demanded an end to colonial rule. The United States and the Soviet Union both, for their own reasons, pressed for decolonisation.

The wave of independence

Libya became independent in 1951, Sudan, Morocco and Tunisia in 1956. In 1957 Kwame Nkrumah led the Gold Coast to independence as Ghana — the first sub-Saharan colony to break free, an electrifying precedent. Guinea followed in 1958. In 1960, the "Year of Africa", seventeen nations gained independence, including Nigeria, Senegal, Mali, Madagascar and the Congo — whose chaotic Belgian exit brought the Congo Crisis and the murder of Patrice Lumumba. Algeria won independence from France in 1962 after an eight-year war. Kenya (1963, after the Mau Mau uprising), Tanzania, Zambia and Malawi followed; Britain's remaining southern African possessions mostly became independent in the later 1960s, though white-ruled Rhodesia declared its own unilateral independence in 1965 and only became Zimbabwe in 1980. Portugal clung to Angola and Mozambique until its 1974 revolution, granting independence in 1975 amid civil wars. Namibia followed in 1990, and apartheid South Africa held its first universal-suffrage election in 1994.

Legacy

Independence was framed by the Organisation of African Unity (founded 1963), whose members pledged to respect inherited colonial borders — a decision that spared border wars but entrenched arbitrary boundaries. New states faced weak institutions, economies geared to raw-material exports, and Cold War proxy interference, and many fell to military coups within a decade. Nevertheless, decolonisation ended a century of formal European empire and created more than fifty African states whose leaders, from Nkrumah to Mandela, reshaped world politics.
