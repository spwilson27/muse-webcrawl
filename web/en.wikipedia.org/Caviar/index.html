Caviar

Caviar is a food consisting of salt-cured roe (fish eggs) of the family Acipenseridae — the sturgeons. Traditionally, the term refers to roe from wild sturgeon of the Caspian Sea and Black Sea, particularly beluga, ossetra (Russian sturgeon), and sevruga, which command the highest prices.

Types

Beluga caviar, from the beluga sturgeon (Huso huso), has the largest, softest eggs and is the most expensive. Ossetra caviar is medium-sized and nutty in flavour, ranging from golden to brown. Sevruga eggs are small and grey. Roe from other fish — salmon, trout, lumpfish, or paddlefish — is often sold as caviar in a broader sense, though purists reserve the name for sturgeon.

Production and conservation

Overfishing and habitat loss devastated Caspian sturgeon stocks in the late 20th century; sturgeon are now protected under CITES, and most legal caviar comes from aquaculture in countries including China, Italy, France, and the United States. Caviar is graded, lightly salted ("malossol"), and packed in tins; it is served chilled, traditionally on blini or toast points with crème fraîche.

History

Salted sturgeon roe has been prized since antiquity in Persia — the word caviar derives from the Persian khāviyār — and became a luxury of Russian and European courts in the 19th century.
