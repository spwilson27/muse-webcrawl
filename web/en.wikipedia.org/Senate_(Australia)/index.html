The Australian Senate is the upper house of the bicameral Parliament of Australia, the lower house being the House of Representatives. The Senate consists of 76 senators: twelve for each of the six states, regardless of population, and two each for the Australian Capital Territory and the Northern Territory.

Composition and election

Senators for the states serve six-year terms, with half of each state's senators facing election every three years in a normal rotation, so that Senate elections usually coincide with those for the House of Representatives. Territory senators serve terms tied to the House. Senators are elected by proportional representation using the single transferable vote, a system adopted in 1949 that has allowed minor parties and independents to win seats and often hold the balance of power. Voting above the line on group voting tickets was reformed in 2016 to give voters control over preferences.

Powers

The Constitution gives the Senate nearly equal legislative power with the House: it may amend or reject almost any bill, though it may not originate or amend money bills (appropriation and taxation bills), which it may nevertheless reject outright — a power whose use contributed to the 1975 constitutional crisis. The Senate is noted for its committee system, which scrutinises legislation, estimates of expenditure and government administration. Unlike the House, the Senate is not the house from which the government is formed, and the Prime Minister sits in the House of Representatives.

Origins

The Senate was designed on the model of a states' house, reflecting the federal bargain at Federation in 1901 that gave the less populous states equal representation. Its proportional electoral system has since made it a house of review and of minority representation as much as a states' house.
