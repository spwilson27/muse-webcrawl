Biopsychosocial model

The biopsychosocial model is a broad view that attributes disease outcome to the intricate, variable interaction of biological factors (genetic, biochemical, etc.), psychological factors (mood, personality, behaviour, etc.), and social factors (cultural, familial, socioeconomic, medical, etc.). It contrasts with the traditional biomedical model, which focuses principally on biological causes of disease.

History

The model was proposed by psychiatrist George L. Engel in a 1977 paper in Science, "The need for a new medical model: a challenge for biomedicine". Engel argued that the purely biomedical approach left no room within its framework for the social, psychological and behavioural dimensions of illness, and he drew on general systems theory to describe interacting levels from the molecule to society.

Applications and criticism

The model is influential in psychiatry, clinical psychology, pain medicine and rehabilitation, and underpins biopsychosocial assessment and formulation in clinical practice. Critics note that it can be vague about how the three domains should be weighted or integrated in particular cases, and that in practice it is sometimes applied eclectically rather than systematically.
