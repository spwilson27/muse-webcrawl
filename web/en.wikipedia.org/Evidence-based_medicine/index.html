Evidence-based medicine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Evidence-based medicine (EBM) is "the conscientious, explicit and judicious use of current best evidence in making decisions about the care of individual patients." It aims to apply the best available evidence gained from the scientific method to clinical decision-making, combining clinical expertise, the best external evidence from systematic research, and patient values and preferences.

History

The term was coined in the early 1990s by a group led by Gordon Guyatt at McMaster University in Canada, including David Sackett, whose definition became canonical. EBM grew out of clinical epidemiology and from the work of Archie Cochrane, whose 1972 book Effectiveness and Efficiency argued for evaluating healthcare interventions through randomised controlled trials; the Cochrane Collaboration (now Cochrane), founded in 1993, produces systematic reviews of healthcare evidence.

Method

Practising EBM typically follows five steps: formulating an answerable clinical question (often in PICO format — Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome); searching for the best evidence; critically appraising it for validity and applicability; applying it to the patient; and evaluating one's performance. Evidence is conventionally ranked in hierarchies, with systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised controlled trials at the top and expert opinion at the bottom. Appraisal systems such as GRADE rate the certainty of evidence and the strength of recommendations in clinical practice guidelines.

Criticism

Critics argue that EBM can undervalue clinical judgment, pathophysiological reasoning, and individual patient circumstances; that published trials are distorted by publication bias and industry funding; and that "evidence-based" has sometimes been misapplied as a marketing label or a rationing tool. Proponents respond that EBM explicitly integrates evidence with expertise and patient values, and that appraisal methods have become steadily more sophisticated.
