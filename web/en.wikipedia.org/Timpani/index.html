Timpani

Timpani (Italian, singular timpano) or kettledrums are musical instruments in the percussion family: a membrane (drumhead) stretched over a large bowl-shaped resonator, traditionally of copper. Unlike most drums, timpani are tuned to specific pitches, and a single timpanist typically plays a set of two to five drums of different sizes.

Construction and tuning

Modern timpani are pedal tuned: a balanced-action or Dresden-style pedal changes the tension of the head, and with it the pitch, across a range of about a perfect fifth per drum. Historically, drums were hand-tuned with T-handles ("machine drums"), and earlier still tuned only by overall tension. Heads were once calfskin and are now usually plastic. Players use mallets with felt, cork or wooden heads to vary timbre.

History and use

Kettledrums entered European music from the Ottoman world, first in cavalry and ceremonial music and, from the 17th century, in the orchestra, where they were long paired with trumpets. Beethoven greatly expanded their role, tuning them to intervals beyond the traditional tonic–dominant. Twentieth-century composers such as Bartók and Stravinsky demanded glissando effects and unusual techniques, and the timpani became a virtuoso solo instrument in works like concertos written for timpanists.
