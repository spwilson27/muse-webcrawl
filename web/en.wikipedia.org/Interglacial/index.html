Interglacial

An interglacial period (or alternatively interglacial, interglaciation) is a geological interval of warmer global average temperature lasting thousands of years that separates consecutive glacial periods within an ice age. The present Holocene interglacial, which began about 11,700 years ago, follows the Last Glacial Period. During the Pleistocene (beginning 2.58 million years ago), Earth has cycled repeatedly between glacial and interglacial states, at intervals governed largely by Milankovitch cycles in Earth's orbit and axial tilt — dominated by periods of about 41,000 years early in the Pleistocene and about 100,000 years over the past million years.

Characteristics

During an interglacial, continental ice sheets retreat to high latitudes, global sea level rises (in the last interglacial, the Eemian around 130,000–115,000 years ago, sea level stood several metres above the present), forests expand poleward, and ocean circulation and biomes reorganize. Interglacials are identified in the marine record as odd-numbered marine isotope stages (MIS); the Holocene is MIS 1, and the Eemian corresponds to MIS 5e. MIS 11, about 400,000 years ago, is often considered an analogue for the current interglacial because of similar orbital configuration.

Duration and the current interglacial

Recent interglacials have typically lasted roughly 10,000 to 30,000 years. Because the present orbital configuration produces weak variation in Northern Hemisphere summer insolation, some studies suggest the Holocene might naturally last unusually long, and that anthropogenic greenhouse-gas emissions could postpone the onset of the next glaciation by tens of thousands of years or more.
