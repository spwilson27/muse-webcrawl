Gentianaceae

Gentianaceae
Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Gentianales
Family: Gentianaceae
Genera: about 103
Species: about 1,750

Gentianaceae is a family of flowering plants of the order Gentianales, containing roughly 103 genera and about 1,750 species. Members of the family — known as the gentian family — are distributed worldwide, with the greatest diversity in temperate and mountainous regions. The family takes its name from the type genus Gentiana, itself named for Gentius, a second-century BC king of Illyria who is said to have discovered the medicinal value of gentian roots.

Description

Most Gentianaceae are herbs, though the family also includes shrubs and a few small trees, as well as some achlorophyllous mycoheterotrophs such as Voyria that obtain nutrients from fungi rather than photosynthesis. The leaves are typically simple, opposite, and without stipules. The flowers are usually showy, radially symmetric, and bisexual, with a tubular or bell-shaped corolla of four or five fused petals; colors range from the intense blues for which alpine gentians are famous to pink, yellow, and white.

Taxonomy

In the APG IV system the family is placed in the order Gentianales alongside Apocynaceae, Rubiaceae, Loganiaceae, and Gelsemiaceae. Internally it is divided into several tribes, including Chironieae, Exaceae, Gentianeae, Helieae, Potalieae, and Saccifolieae. Well-known genera include Gentiana (gentians), Gentianella, Swertia, Centaurium (centauries), Eustoma (prairie gentians, including lisianthus), Sabatia, and Exacum.

Uses

Gentians have a long history in herbal medicine. The root of Gentiana lutea (great yellow gentian) contains intensely bitter secoiridoid glycosides such as amarogentin — among the most bitter natural substances known — and has been used for centuries as a digestive tonic and flavoring in aperitifs and bitters. Several species are prized ornamentals in rock gardens, and Eustoma grandiflorum is widely grown as a cut flower. Some species, notably alpine gentians, are protected because of over-collection and habitat loss.
