The International Labour Organization (ILO) is a United Nations agency whose mandate is to advance social and economic justice by setting international labour standards. Founded in October 1919 under the League of Nations, it survived the League's dissolution to become the first specialised agency of the UN in 1946. The ILO has 187 member states and is headquartered in Geneva, Switzerland.

Structure

The ILO is unique among UN agencies for its tripartite structure: its governing organs include representatives of governments, employers and workers, each with a voice in decision-making. The International Labour Conference meets annually in Geneva to adopt conventions and recommendations; a Governing Body serves as the executive council; and the International Labour Office — its secretariat, directed by a Director-General (Gilbert Houngbo since 2022) — conducts research and operations.

Standards and work

The ILO has adopted some 190 conventions covering freedom of association and collective bargaining, the abolition of forced labour and child labour, non-discrimination, occupational safety and health, wages, working time and social security. Its 1998 Declaration on Fundamental Principles and Rights at Work identifies core standards binding on all members; in 2022 a safe and healthy working environment was added as a fifth category of fundamental rights. The 1944 Declaration of Philadelphia restated its aims, famously declaring that "labour is not a commodity" and that poverty anywhere constitutes a danger to prosperity everywhere. The ILO received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1969, and its Decent Work Agenda (from 1999) frames employment, rights, social protection and social dialogue as its strategic objectives.
