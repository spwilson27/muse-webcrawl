The Jōmon people (Japanese: 縄文人, Jōmon-jin) were the inhabitants of the Japanese archipelago during the Jōmon period, a span of prehistory lasting roughly from 14,000 BCE to about 300 BCE. They are named for the distinctive cord-marked (jōmon) pottery they produced, among the oldest ceramic traditions in the world.

Origins and genetics

The ancestors of the Jōmon people arrived in Japan during the Upper Paleolithic, probably from northeast Asia via land bridges that existed during the last glacial maximum. Genetic studies show that the Jōmon lineage diverged early from other East Asian populations and remained relatively isolated for millennia. Modern Japanese people derive a minority of their ancestry — often estimated around 10–20 percent — from the Jōmon, with higher proportions among the Ainu of Hokkaido and the Ryukyuan people of Okinawa, who retain more Jōmon-related ancestry and distinctive cultural traits.

Way of life

Unlike many contemporaries, the Jōmon were sedentary or semi-sedentary hunter-gatherer-fishers. They lived in pit dwellings, often clustered in substantial villages such as Sannai-Maruyama in Aomori Prefecture. Their diet was rich and varied: acorns, chestnuts and other nuts (processed with grinding stones and leached to remove bitterness), deer and wild boar, salmon and other fish, shellfish from extensive coastal middens, and a wide range of plants. Some scholars argue they practiced a form of arboriculture, managing chestnut groves.

Culture

Jōmon material culture included elaborately decorated pottery, stone tools, lacquered objects, jade and obsidian ornaments, and clay figurines known as dogū, many depicting stylized female forms whose purpose remains debated. Ritual sites with stone circles, such as Ōyu in Akita Prefecture, suggest a developed ceremonial life.

Transition

Around the first millennium BCE, migrants from the Korean Peninsula — the ancestors of the Yayoi people — brought wet-rice agriculture, bronze and iron metallurgy, and new social structures. The Jōmon population interbred with and was largely absorbed by these newcomers, particularly in Honshu, while Jōmon-descended cultures persisted longer in Hokkaido (the Epi-Jōmon and later Satsumon cultures, ancestors of the Ainu) and in the Ryukyu Islands.
