Polysaccharide

Polysaccharides are long-chain polymeric carbohydrates composed of monosaccharide units bound together by glycosidic linkages. They range in structure from linear to highly branched and may consist of a single kind of repeating unit (homopolysaccharides) or several (heteropolysaccharides). Polysaccharides serve two broad biological roles: energy storage, as in starch and glycogen, and structure, as in cellulose and chitin. On hydrolysis they yield their constituent monosaccharides or oligosaccharides.

Storage polysaccharides

Starch, the storage form of glucose in plants, consists of amylose (mostly linear α-1,4 chains) and amylopectin (branched via α-1,6 bonds). Glycogen, the animal counterpart stored in liver and muscle, is even more highly branched, allowing rapid mobilisation of glucose. Because these polymers are osmotically inactive, cells can stockpile large amounts of sugar safely.

Structural polysaccharides

Cellulose, the most abundant organic polymer on Earth, is a linear chain of β-1,4-linked glucose whose straight chains hydrogen-bond into strong microfibrils — the principal component of plant cell walls and of wood, cotton, and paper. Humans lack cellulase and cannot digest it, though it serves as dietary fibre. Chitin, built from N-acetylglucosamine, forms arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls. In animal extracellular matrix, sulfated polysaccharides called glycosaminoglycans (such as heparan sulfate and chondroitin sulfate) form the carbohydrate part of proteoglycans.

Properties and uses

Polysaccharides are generally tasteless, insoluble or colloidal in water, and do not form crystals. Industrially important examples include agar and alginate (food gels), pectin (jam setting), dextran (plasma expander), and bacterial capsular polysaccharides used as vaccine antigens.
