James Lind (4 October 1716 – 13 July 1794) was a Scottish physician. He was a pioneer of naval hygiene in the Royal Navy. By conducting one of the first ever clinical trials, he developed the theory that citrus fruits cured scurvy. Lind argued for the health benefits of better ventilation aboard naval ships, the improved nutrition of seamen, and the introduction of surgical and medical training for naval surgeons.

The scurvy experiment

In 1747, aboard HMS Salisbury, Lind conducted an experiment often described as the first controlled clinical trial. He divided twelve scorbutic sailors into six pairs, each receiving a different remedy: cider, dilute sulphuric acid, vinegar, seawater, a medicinal paste, or two oranges and one lemon daily. The citrus pair recovered rapidly and were fit for duty within about a week. Lind published his findings in A Treatise of the Scurvy (1753). The deficiency he was unknowingly treating was of vitamin C, which the body needs to synthesize collagen; without it, connective tissue breaks down, producing the bleeding gums, wounds reopening, and debility of scurvy. Despite Lind's evidence, the Royal Navy did not mandate lemon juice until 1795, decades later — a delay partly caused by Lind's own later doubts and by the use of boiled, less potent juice.

Legacy

Lind later served as physician at the Royal Naval Hospital Haslar, where he promoted cleanliness, fumigation, and uniforms for sailors. His work on hygiene and diet is credited with saving many lives, and the British sailors' practice of carrying lime juice gave rise to the nickname "limeys."
