Gelatin

Gelatin (or gelatine) is a translucent, colourless, flavourless food ingredient, commonly derived from collagen taken from animal body parts. It is produced by the partial hydrolysis of collagen extracted from the skin, bones, and connective tissues of animals such as cattle and pigs. When dry it is brittle; when moist it becomes gummy. Gelatin forms thermo-reversible gels: a warm solution sets on cooling and melts again on warming, melting near body temperature — a property behind its characteristic "melt-in-the-mouth" texture.

Production

Raw materials are pre-treated with acid (type A gelatin, typically from pigskin) or alkali (type B, typically from cattle hides and bones) to open the collagen structure, then extracted with hot water in successive batches, filtered, concentrated, and dried. Gel strength is graded by the Bloom test. Fish gelatin, which gels at lower temperatures, serves markets avoiding mammalian products; plant-based gelling agents such as agar and pectin are alternatives but are not gelatin.

Uses

Gelatin is a gelling agent in gummy candies, marshmallows, desserts, aspics, and yoghurts; a stabiliser and thickener in many foods; and the film-former of pharmaceutical capsules (both hard and soft). Historically it was crucial to photography as the emulsion binder of film and paper, and it is used in cosmetics, paper sizing, matches, and as a clarifying fining agent for wine and juice. Collagen hydrolysate ("collagen peptides") is a further-processed form that does not gel.

Composition and properties

Like its parent protein, gelatin is rich in glycine, proline, and hydroxyproline but lacks tryptophan, making it an incomplete protein. Its gel melts around 35 °C, and its setting and melting behaviour, clarity, and low flavour make it prized in fine patisserie and molecular gastronomy.
