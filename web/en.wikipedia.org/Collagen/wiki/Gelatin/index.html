Gelatin or gelatine (from Latin gelatus, meaning "stiff" or "frozen") is a translucent, colourless, flavourless food ingredient commonly derived from collagen taken from animal body parts. It is brittle when dry and rubbery when moist. It may also be referred to as hydrolyzed collagen, collagen hydrolysate, gelatine hydrolysate, hydrolyzed gelatine, and collagen peptides after it has undergone hydrolysis.

Gelatin is an irreversibly hydrolyzed form of collagen, wherein the hydrolysis reduces protein fibrils into smaller peptides; depending on the physical and chemical methods of denaturation, the molecular weight of the peptides falls within a broad range. It dissolves in hot water and sets to a gel on cooling, melting again when reheated — behaviour that makes it melt in the mouth at body temperature.

Production

On a commercial scale, gelatin is made from by-products of the meat and leather industries, chiefly pig skin, cattle hides, and bones, which are treated with acid (type A gelatin) or alkali (type B) and then extracted with hot water. Worldwide production is roughly 400,000 tonnes per year. Fish gelatin is produced for markets avoiding mammalian products.

Uses

Gelatin is a gelling agent in food (gummy candies, marshmallows, desserts, aspic), a stabiliser and thickener in dairy products and cosmetics, the binder in pharmaceutical capsules, and historically the emulsion base of photographic film and paper. Gel strength is graded by "Bloom" value. Because it is derived from animals, it is not suitable for vegans, and some consumers avoid it for religious reasons; plant-based alternatives include agar, pectin, and carrageenan.
