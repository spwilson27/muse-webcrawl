Scurvy is a disease resulting from a lack of vitamin C (ascorbic acid). Early symptoms of deficiency include weakness, fatigue, and sore arms and legs. Without treatment, decreased red blood cells, gum disease, changes to hair, and bleeding from the skin may occur. As scurvy worsens, there can be poor wound healing, personality changes, and finally death from infection or bleeding.

Vitamin C is required for the synthesis of collagen in humans: it is a cofactor for the enzymes prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase, which stabilise the collagen molecule. Without it, connective tissue weakens throughout the body. It takes at least a month of little to no vitamin C intake before symptoms develop.

History

Scurvy was the scourge of long sea voyages and sieges; it is estimated to have killed millions of sailors between 1500 and 1800, more than storms, shipwreck, and combat combined. In 1747, the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind conducted one of the first controlled clinical experiments, showing that citrus fruit cured the disease. The Royal Navy eventually issued lemon (later lime) juice rations — hence the nickname "limeys" for British sailors — and Captain James Cook demonstrated scurvy prevention on his Pacific voyages. The antiscorbutic factor, ascorbic acid, was isolated and identified as vitamin C in 1932.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is typically based on physical signs, X-rays, and improvement with treatment rather than on laboratory testing. Treatment is with vitamin C supplements and a diet that contains fresh fruit and vegetables; symptoms often resolve within days to weeks. Modern scurvy is rare but still occurs in people with severely restricted diets, malabsorption, or alcohol dependence.
