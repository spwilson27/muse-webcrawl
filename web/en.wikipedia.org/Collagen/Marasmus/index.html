Marasmus is a form of severe malnutrition characterised by energy deficiency. A child with marasmus looks emaciated: body weight may be reduced to less than 62 percent of the expected weight for the age. It is the non-oedematous counterpart to kwashiorkor, and the two can coexist in an intermediate form called marasmic kwashiorkor.

Signs and symptoms

Children with marasmus show severe wasting of muscle and subcutaneous fat, giving prominent ribs, loose folds of skin over the buttocks ("baggy pants" appearance), and thin, stick-like limbs. Unlike kwashiorkor there is no oedema and no fatty liver enlargement; the abdomen is typically flat or sunken rather than distended. Affected children are often hungry and alert rather than apathetic, though they are vulnerable to hypothermia, bradycardia, and infection.

Causes

Marasmus results from a grossly insufficient intake of energy and protein — effectively starvation, usually in the setting of famine, extreme poverty, or severely restricted feeding in infancy. The body adapts by breaking down its own fat and muscle to meet energy needs, slowing growth and reducing metabolic rate. It most commonly affects infants and young children in low-income regions, and can also occur in older patients with chronic disease or severe malabsorption.

Treatment

As with other severe acute malnutrition, treatment proceeds cautiously: stabilisation with small, frequent feeds of a low-osmolarity therapeutic milk (F-75), correction of hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, dehydration and infection, then gradual rehabilitation with energy-dense food (F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food) for catch-up growth. Overly rapid refeeding risks refeeding syndrome, a dangerous shift in fluids and electrolytes.

Prognosis

With timely treatment many children recover fully, although prolonged or severe episodes in early life can leave lasting effects on growth and cognitive development. Untreated, marasmus is frequently fatal, usually through infection or hypothermia.
