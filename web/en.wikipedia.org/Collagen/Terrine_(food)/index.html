A terrine (French pronunciation: [tɛ.ʁin]) is a French forcemeat loaf similar to a pâté, made with more coarsely chopped ingredients. Terrines are usually served cold or at room temperature. The dish is cooked in a covered pottery mould — also called a terrine — in a bain-marie, and takes its name from the vessel.

Preparation

Modern terrines do not necessarily contain meat or animal fat; they may instead be made of mushrooms, vegetables, seafood, or cheese and other ingredients, formed into a loaf and baked or pressed. Typical meat terrines combine minced or diced meat (pork, game, poultry, or liver) with fat, aromatics, and sometimes pistachios or other inlays that create an attractive mosaic when sliced. After gentle cooking in a water bath, the terrine is weighted and chilled so it sets firmly, then turned out and sliced.

Terrines are commonly served as a first course with toast, cornichons, and chutney or mustard. Because they keep well for several days under refrigeration, they were historically a practical way for charcutiers to use and preserve varied cuts of meat.

Related dishes

The terrine is closely related to pâté, from which it is conventionally distinguished by texture: pâtés are usually smoother, while terrines are coarser. A galantine is a related preparation of deboned, stuffed meat. Aspic may be used to glaze or set some terrines, especially vegetable and seafood versions.
