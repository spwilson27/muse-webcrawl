Agar plate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An agar plate is a Petri dish containing a growth medium composed of agar and nutrients, used to culture microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi. Agar, a gelatinous polysaccharide extracted from red algae, melts at about 95 °C and solidifies below about 40 °C, remaining firm at incubation temperatures where gelatin would liquefy — the property that made it revolutionary in microbiology.

History

The use of agar in microbiology was suggested by Fanny Hesse, who worked with her husband Walther Hesse in Robert Koch's laboratory and knew agar from making jams and jellies. Introduced in the early 1880s, agar plates quickly became the standard tool for isolating pure cultures; the shallow glass dish with an overhanging lid was designed by Julius Richard Petri in 1887.

Types

  • General-purpose media, such as nutrient agar and tryptic soy agar, support a wide range of organisms.
  • Selective media contain agents that inhibit some organisms (e.g. MacConkey agar selects for Gram-negative bacteria).
  • Differential media reveal metabolic traits through indicator dyes (e.g. lactose fermenters turn pink on MacConkey agar).
  • Blood agar, enriched with sheep blood, differentiates haemolytic bacteria.

Use

Plates are inoculated by streaking a sample across the surface with a sterile loop so that individual cells are deposited apart and grow into isolated colonies, enabling counting (colony-forming units), isolation of pure strains, and tests such as antibiotic susceptibility (the Kirby–Bauer disc-diffusion test). Plates are incubated upside down to prevent condensation from dripping onto the culture.
