Collagen

Collagen (from Greek κόλλα kólla, "glue", and -γέν -gen, "giving birth to") is the main structural protein in the extracellular matrix of the various connective tissues in the body. As the main component of connective tissue, it is the most abundant protein in mammals, making up from 25% to 35% of the whole-body protein content. Collagen consists of amino acids bound together to form a triple helix of elongated fibrils known as a collagen helix. It is mostly found in cartilage, bones, tendons, ligaments, and skin.

Vitamin C is vital for collagen synthesis, while vitamin E improves its production and maintenance. Depending upon the degree of mineralization, collagen tissues may be rigid (bone) or compliant (tendon) or have a gradient from rigid to compliant (cartilage). Collagen is also abundant in corneas, blood vessels, the gut, intervertebral discs, and the dentin in teeth. In muscle tissue, it serves as a major component of the endomysium. Collagen constitutes 1% to 2% of muscle tissue and accounts for 6% of the weight of strong, tendinous muscles.

Structure

The collagen molecule, or tropocollagen, is a triple helix about 300 nm long formed by three polypeptide chains (alpha chains), each with the repeating sequence Gly–X–Y, where X and Y are frequently proline and hydroxyproline. Glycine at every third position allows the chains to pack tightly. Fibril-forming collagens assemble into staggered arrays that produce the characteristic banded appearance of collagen fibres under the electron microscope and give tendons their tensile strength.

Types

At least 28 types of collagen have been identified. Over 90% of the collagen in the human body is type I, with types II, III and IV also widespread:

  • Type I — skin, tendon, bone, ligaments, dentin, cornea;
  • Type II — cartilage and the vitreous body of the eye;
  • Type III — reticular fibres in skin, blood vessels, and internal organs;
  • Type IV — basement membranes, forming networks rather than fibrils.

Synthesis and disorders

Collagen is synthesised by fibroblasts (and by osteoblasts, chondrocytes and other cells) as procollagen, processed extracellularly, and cross-linked by the enzyme lysyl oxidase. Genetic defects in collagen genes cause disorders including osteogenesis imperfecta (brittle bone disease), several forms of Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, and Alport syndrome. Scurvy results from vitamin C deficiency impairing the hydroxylation of proline and hence the stability of the triple helix.

Uses

Gelatin, used in food and photography, is produced by partial hydrolysis of collagen. Collagen and collagen-derived materials are used in medicine for wound dressings, tissue engineering scaffolds, sutures and cosmetic fillers, and dietary collagen supplements are widely marketed, though evidence for many claimed benefits is limited.
