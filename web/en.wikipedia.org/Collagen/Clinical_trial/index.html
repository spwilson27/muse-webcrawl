Clinical trial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clinical trials are prospective biomedical or behavioural research studies on human participants designed to answer specific questions about biomedical or behavioural interventions, including new treatments (such as novel vaccines, drugs, dietary choices and medical devices) and known interventions that warrant further study and comparison. Clinical trials generate data on dosage, safety and efficacy, and are conducted only after information collected in preclinical research (in vitro and animal studies) justifies testing in humans.

Design

Rigorous trials use randomisation to allocate participants to treatment or control groups, blinding (single-, double- or triple-blind) to prevent bias, and pre-registered protocols and statistical analysis plans. Control groups receive a placebo, standard care or an active comparator. Trials are monitored by data safety monitoring boards and must receive ethics approval (institutional review boards) and the informed consent of participants, in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

History and regulation

James Lind's 1747 scurvy experiment aboard HMS Salisbury is often cited as an early controlled trial. Modern regulatory frameworks followed disasters and reforms of the 20th century — the Nuremberg Code (1947), the Kefauver–Harris Amendments (1962) requiring proof of efficacy in the United States, and mandatory trial registration (e.g. ClinicalTrials.gov, launched 2000). Depending on the country, trials are regulated by authorities such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA).
