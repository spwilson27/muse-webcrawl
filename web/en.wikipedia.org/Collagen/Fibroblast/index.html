Fibroblast

A fibroblast is a type of biological cell typically with a spindle shape that synthesizes the extracellular matrix and collagen, produces the structural framework (stroma) for animal tissues, and plays a critical role in wound healing. Fibroblasts are the most common cells of connective tissue in animals. They secrete collagen proteins that are used to maintain a structural framework for many tissues, and they also play an important role in healing wounds.

Structure and function

Fibroblasts are large, flat, elongated cells with processes extending from the cell body. Active fibroblasts have abundant rough endoplasmic reticulum reflecting their protein-secreting role; quiescent cells, sometimes called fibrocytes, are smaller and less metabolically active. Fibroblasts produce collagen types I and III, glycosaminoglycans, glycoproteins such as fibronectin, and reticular fibres.

Roles in health and disease

During wound healing, fibroblasts migrate into the wound, deposit new matrix, and some differentiate into contractile myofibroblasts that close the wound. Excess or persistent fibroblast activity causes fibrosis and hypertrophic scarring. In cancer, cancer-associated fibroblasts shape the tumour microenvironment. Fibroblasts are also a workhorse cell type in laboratory culture and were the first adult cells reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells by Shinya Yamanaka's group (2006–2007).
