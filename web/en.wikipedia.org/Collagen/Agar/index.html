Agar
TypePolysaccharide (hydrocolloid)
SourceRed algae (Gelidium, Gracilaria)
ComponentsAgarose and agaropectin
Gelling temperature~32–40 °C; melts ~85 °C

Agar, or agar-agar, is a jelly-like substance consisting of polysaccharides obtained from the cell walls of some species of red algae, primarily from ogonori (Gracilaria) and tengusa (Gelidiaceae). Agar is the dried hydrophilic, colloidal substance extracted from various species of red algae. It is a mixture of two components: the linear polysaccharide agarose and a heterogeneous mixture of smaller molecules called agaropectin.

Uses

Agar is best known as a solid growth medium for culturing microorganisms in Petri dishes, an application introduced into bacteriology by Fanny Hesse, who worked with Robert Koch's laboratory in the early 1880s; agar replaced gelatin because it remains solid at body temperature and is not degraded by most bacteria. In cooking it is used as a vegetarian gelling agent in desserts, aspics, and the Japanese sweet kanten; it is also used in food processing, dentistry (impression materials), and biotechnology (agarose gel electrophoresis).

Properties

Agar exhibits gel hysteresis: it sets at about 32–40 °C but does not melt until about 85 °C. This property makes it ideal for microbiological media that must remain solid during incubation at 37 °C. Agarose gels are widely used to separate DNA fragments by size.
