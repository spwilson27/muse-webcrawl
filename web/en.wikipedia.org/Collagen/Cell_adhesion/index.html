Cell adhesion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cell adhesion is the process by which cells interact and attach to neighbouring cells or to the extracellular matrix (ECM) through specialised molecules of the cell surface called cell adhesion molecules (CAMs). It is fundamental to the formation and maintenance of tissues and organs in multicellular organisms, and it also underlies cell migration, immune responses, wound healing and embryonic development.

Mechanisms

Adhesion occurs through protein complexes at the membrane. In cell–cell adhesion, major structures include:

  • Adherens junctions, built on cadherins, calcium-dependent proteins linking adjacent cells to the actin cytoskeleton via catenins;
  • Desmosomes, which anchor intermediate filaments and give tissues mechanical strength;
  • Tight junctions, which seal epithelial sheets and control paracellular transport;
  • Gap junctions, channels (connexons) allowing direct exchange of ions and small molecules.

In cell–matrix adhesion, integrins bind ECM proteins such as fibronectin, laminin and collagen, assembling focal adhesions and hemidesmosomes that transmit force and signals between the matrix and cytoskeleton. Other CAM families include the immunoglobulin superfamily and selectins, which mediate the rolling of leukocytes on endothelium.

Signalling and function

Adhesion is not merely structural: adhesion receptors signal bidirectionally, regulating survival (its loss triggering anoikis, a form of programmed cell death), proliferation and differentiation. Differential adhesion helps sort cells during development, as proposed by Malcolm Steinberg's differential adhesion hypothesis.

Medical relevance

Defective adhesion causes blistering diseases (pemphigus, epidermolysis bullosa), leukocyte adhesion deficiency, and contributes to cancer: loss of E-cadherin-mediated adhesion is a hallmark of invasion and metastasis. Pathogens and toxins also exploit adhesion molecules to enter cells.
