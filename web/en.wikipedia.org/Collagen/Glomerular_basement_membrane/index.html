The glomerular basement membrane (GBM) of the kidney is the middle layer of the glomerular filtration barrier. The barrier consists of fenestrated endothelial cells, the GBM, and the foot processes (pedicels) of podocytes. Together these layers filter blood in the glomerulus, allowing water and small solutes to pass into Bowman's capsule while retaining cells and most plasma proteins.

Structure and composition

The GBM is a fusion of the basement membranes produced by endothelial cells and podocytes. Under the electron microscope it shows three layers: the pale lamina rara externa (next to the podocytes), the dark central lamina densa, and the lamina rara interna (next to the endothelium). Its meshwork is built principally of type IV collagen — a network dominated in maturity by the α3α4α5(IV) heterotrimer — together with laminin (especially laminin-521), nidogen, and negatively charged heparan sulfate proteoglycans such as agrin and perlecan, which contribute charge-selective filtration.

Function

The GBM acts as both a size-selective and charge-selective filter. Its collagen network provides mechanical strength, while its negative charge repels negatively charged plasma proteins such as albumin. The membrane also provides attachment and signalling cues that maintain podocyte and endothelial architecture.

Clinical significance

  • Anti-GBM disease (Goodpasture syndrome) — autoantibodies against the α3 chain of type IV collagen produce rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, often with lung haemorrhage.
  • Alport syndrome — mutations in COL4A3, COL4A4, or COL4A5 cause a thinned, splitting GBM with progressive kidney failure, hearing loss, and eye abnormalities.
  • Thin basement membrane nephropathy — diffuse GBM thinning, usually presenting with isolated microscopic haematuria.
  • Diabetic nephropathy — progressive GBM thickening is an early structural change of diabetic kidney disease.
