Dietary fiber

Dietary fiber (spelled fibre in Commonwealth English), also known as roughage, consists of the parts of plant foods that the human body cannot digest or absorb completely. Unlike other carbohydrates, fiber passes relatively intact through the stomach and small intestine into the colon, where it is fully or partially fermented by the gut microbiota. Fiber is found naturally in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts and seeds.

Types

Fiber is commonly classified by solubility:

  • Soluble fiber dissolves in water to form a gel (pectin, beta-glucans, gums, inulin). It is readily fermented by colonic bacteria, producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, and it slows glucose absorption and can lower LDL cholesterol. Sources include oats, barley, beans, apples and citrus.
  • Insoluble fiber (cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin) does not dissolve; it adds bulk to stool and speeds intestinal transit. Sources include wheat bran, whole grains and vegetable skins.

Modern classifications also consider fermentability and viscosity, which better predict physiological effects.

Health effects

High fiber intake is consistently associated with lower risks of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer and obesity. Mechanisms include delayed gastric emptying and glucose absorption, bile-acid binding, stool bulking, and production of beneficial fermentation products that nourish colonocytes and modulate inflammation. Health authorities generally recommend adults consume around 25–30 grams of fiber daily, though average intakes in Western countries are well below this (often ~15 g/day). Sudden increases can cause bloating and flatulence; guidelines advise gradual increases with adequate fluid.

History

The "fiber hypothesis" was popularised in the 1970s by Denis Burkitt and Hugh Trowell, who observed low rates of bowel diseases in rural African populations eating unrefined diets. The chemist Eben Hipsley had coined the term "dietary fibre" in 1953.
