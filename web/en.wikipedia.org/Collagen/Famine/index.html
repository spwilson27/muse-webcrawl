A famine is a widespread scarcity of food, caused by several factors including war, natural disasters, crop failure, widespread poverty, an economic catastrophe or government policies. This phenomenon is usually accompanied or followed by regional malnutrition, starvation, epidemics, and increased mortality.

Nearly every continent in the world has experienced a period of famine throughout history. Some countries, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, continue to endure extreme cases of famine, and since 2010 global famines, while less severe than in past decades, remain a recurring humanitarian emergency.

Causes

Famines have a variety of causes. Modern famines are generally the product of human action rather than pure natural scarcity: wars and blockades disrupt food distribution, while droughts and crop failures reduce production. Economist Amartya Sen argued that many famines result not from an absolute lack of food but from failures of entitlement — the inability of people to access food that exists. Climate change, soil degradation, and loss of agricultural labour compound these risks.

Classification

The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) grades food insecurity from Phase 1 (minimal) to Phase 5 (famine). A famine is formally declared when at least 20 percent of households face extreme food shortages, acute malnutrition exceeds 30 percent, and death rates rise above two per 10,000 people per day.

Notable famines

  • Great Famine of Ireland (1845–1852) — potato blight destroyed the staple crop; about one million people died and another million emigrated.
  • Holodomor (1932–1933) — a man-made famine in Soviet Ukraine in which several million people starved.
  • Bengal famine of 1943 — some two to three million deaths in British India amid wartime inflation and distribution failures.
  • Great Chinese Famine (1959–1961) — associated with the Great Leap Forward; tens of millions of deaths by many estimates.
  • Ethiopian famine (1983–1985) — drought and civil war; widely publicised and a catalyst for modern humanitarian fundraising.

Effects and relief

Famine produces wasting and stunting in children, suppresses immune function (increasing deaths from disease as much as from literal starvation), and drives mass migration. Relief responses include emergency food aid, therapeutic feeding programmes, cash transfers, and, increasingly, anticipatory action triggered by early-warning systems such as FEWS NET. Long-term prevention focuses on resilient agriculture, social protection, and conflict resolution.
