Scurvy

Scurvy is a disease resulting from a lack of vitamin C (ascorbic acid). Early symptoms include weakness, fatigue, and sore arms and legs; without treatment, decreased red blood cells, gum disease, changes to hair, and bleeding from the skin may occur. Vitamin C is required for the synthesis of collagen — specifically the hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues that stabilises the collagen triple helix — so deficiency weakens connective tissue, blood vessels, and wound healing, and old scars may reopen.

Mechanism

Ascorbate is a cofactor for prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, enzymes that modify collagen after translation. Without hydroxyproline, collagen molecules are unstable and degrade, so tissues with high collagen turnover — gums, skin, blood vessels, scars — fail first. Humans, unlike most animals, cannot synthesise vitamin C because the gene for L-gulonolactone oxidase is a non-functional pseudogene; guinea pigs share this trait, which made them the classic experimental model.

History

Scurvy devastated long sea voyages: it probably killed more sailors than storms, combat, and all other diseases combined. Vasco da Gama lost most of his crew to it, and Anson's circumnavigation (1740–44) lost some 1,300 of 1,900 men. In 1747 the Scottish naval surgeon James Lind conducted one of the first controlled clinical trials, showing oranges and lemons cured the disease; his Treatise of the Scurvy appeared in 1753. The Royal Navy eventually issued lemon (later lime) juice rations — hence "limeys" — and scurvy receded at sea, though it recurred on polar expeditions and in sieges and famines. The chemical identity of the antiscorbutic factor, ascorbic acid, was established in the early 1930s by Albert Szent-Györgyi, Norman Haworth, and others.

Modern occurrence

Scurvy is now rare where diets are varied, but cases occur among people with highly restricted diets, alcohol dependence, malabsorption, or in refugee and famine settings. Recovery on vitamin C supplementation is rapid.
