Kwashiorkor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kwashiorkor
TypeSevere acute malnutrition (oedematous form)
CauseDiet severely deficient in protein relative to energy needs
HallmarksPitting oedema, dermatosis, hair depigmentation, distended abdomen
Typical ageChildren around weaning, roughly 1–3 years
ContrastMarasmus (non-oedematous wasting)

Kwashiorkor is a form of severe acute malnutrition characterised by oedema — swelling caused by excess fluid in body tissues — particularly in the legs, feet, and abdomen. It typically affects young children whose diet, often after weaning onto starchy staples, provides enough calories but far too little protein. The name, introduced into medical literature by the Jamaican paediatrician Cicely Williams in the 1930s, is often said to derive from a Ga phrase meaning roughly "the disease the older child gets when the new baby comes".

Signs and symptoms

Children with kwashiorkor develop bilateral pitting oedema that can mask severe wasting beneath. Other features include a characteristic "flaky paint" dermatosis, loss of hair pigment (hair turning reddish or pale), an enlarged fatty liver producing a protruding belly, irritability and apathy, loss of appetite, and growth failure. Low serum albumin reduces plasma oncotic pressure, contributing to the fluid retention, although oxidative stress, infection, and gut microbiome disruption are also implicated in the condition's development.

Causes

The primary cause is a diet inadequate in protein — classically when a toddler is weaned from breast milk onto a low-protein staple such as cassava, maize, or plantain. Infections (such as measles or diarrhoeal disease) frequently precipitate the condition by increasing protein losses and metabolic demand. Kwashiorkor occurs most often in regions affected by famine, drought, and poverty.

Treatment

Treatment follows staged protocols developed by the World Health Organization: cautious initial feeding with low-protein therapeutic milk (F-75) while correcting hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, dehydration, and infection, followed by rehabilitation feeding with energy- and protein-dense food (F-100 or ready-to-use therapeutic food) to support catch-up growth. Rapid refeeding must be carefully managed to avoid refeeding syndrome.
