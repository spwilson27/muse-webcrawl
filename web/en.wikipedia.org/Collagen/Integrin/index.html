Integrin

Integrins are transmembrane receptors that facilitate cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix adhesion. Upon ligand binding, integrins activate signal transduction pathways that mediate cellular signals such as regulation of the cell cycle, organization of the intracellular cytoskeleton, and movement of new receptors to the cell membrane. The presence of integrins allows rapid and flexible responses to events at the cell surface, for example to signal platelets to initiate an interaction with coagulation factors.

Structure

Integrins are obligate heterodimers composed of one alpha (α) and one beta (β) subunit. In mammals, 18 α and 8 β subunits combine to form 24 distinct integrins with different binding specificities. Many integrins recognize the RGD (arginine–glycine–aspartate) sequence in ligands such as fibronectin; others bind collagen, laminin, or cell-surface counter-receptors such as ICAMs.

Function

Integrins perform bidirectional signalling: "inside-out" signals regulate their affinity for ligands, while "outside-in" signalling follows adhesion. They anchor cells mechanically to the matrix via focal adhesions and hemidesmosomes, enable leukocyte trafficking, and are essential to embryogenesis, wound healing, haemostasis, and immune function. Defects cause diseases such as Glanzmann's thrombasthenia (defective platelet integrin αIIbβ3) and leukocyte adhesion deficiency. Integrins are also drug targets, e.g. in thrombosis and multiple sclerosis, and are exploited by some viruses and bacteria for cell entry.
