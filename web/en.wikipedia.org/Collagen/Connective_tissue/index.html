Connective tissue

Connective tissue is one of the four primary types of animal tissue, along with epithelial tissue, muscle tissue, and nervous tissue. It develops from the mesenchyme derived from the mesoderm germ layer. Connective tissue is found in between other tissues everywhere in the body, including the nervous system. The three meninges, membranes that envelop the brain and spinal cord, are composed of connective tissue.

Composition

Connective tissue consists of cells dispersed in an extracellular matrix of ground substance and protein fibres, principally collagen — the most abundant protein in the body — and elastic fibres of elastin. Fibroblasts produce and maintain the matrix in most connective tissues. The proportions of fibres, ground substance, and cells determine the tissue's properties, from the soft gel of areolar tissue to the densely packed collagen of tendons.

Classification

Connective tissue proper includes loose (areolar, adipose, reticular) and dense (regular, as in tendons and ligaments; irregular, as in the dermis) varieties. Specialized connective tissues include cartilage, bone, blood, and lymph. Embryonic forms include mesenchyme and Wharton's jelly.

Disorders

Disorders of connective tissue include inherited conditions such as Ehlers–Danlos syndrome and Marfan syndrome, both involving defective collagen or related proteins, and autoimmune "connective tissue diseases" such as rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and scleroderma.
