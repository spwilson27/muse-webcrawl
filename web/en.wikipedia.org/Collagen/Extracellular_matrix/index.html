Extracellular matrix

In biology, the extracellular matrix (ECM), also called intercellular matrix, is a three-dimensional network consisting of extracellular macromolecules and minerals, such as collagen, enzymes, glycoproteins, and hydroxyapatite, that provide structural and biochemical support to surrounding cells. Because multicellularity evolved independently in different multicellular lineages, the composition of ECM varies between tissues, but cell adhesion, cell-to-cell communication, and differentiation are common functions of the ECM.

Structure

The ECM has two main forms: the interstitial matrix, a gel of polysaccharides and fibrous proteins filling spaces between cells, and the basement membrane, a sheet-like deposit on which epithelial and other cell layers rest. Its chief components are proteoglycans — including heparan sulfate, chondroitin sulfate, and the water-binding hyaluronic acid — and fibrous proteins. Collagens, the most abundant proteins in the ECM and in the human body, form networks and fibrils that give tensile strength; elastin confers recoil; and adhesive glycoproteins such as fibronectin and laminin link cells to the matrix via integrin receptors.

Functions

Beyond scaffolding, the ECM regulates cell behaviour: its stiffness and composition influence proliferation, migration, and differentiation, partly through mechanotransduction. It sequesters growth factors, serves as a reservoir released during remodelling, and provides pathways for cell migration in development and wound healing. Cells continually remodel the matrix with matrix metalloproteinases and other enzymes.

Medical relevance

Defects in ECM genes cause disorders such as osteogenesis imperfecta and Ehlers–Danlos syndromes. Excessive deposition produces fibrosis of the liver, lung, and kidney. Cancer cells exploit matrix degradation to invade and metastasise. Decellularised ECM scaffolds are used in regenerative medicine and tissue engineering.
