Vitamin C

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)
Chemical nameL-ascorbic acid
FormulaC6H8O6
Good sourcesCitrus fruits, peppers, kiwifruit, broccoli, strawberries
Deficiency diseaseScurvy

Vitamin C (also known as ascorbic acid and ascorbate) is a water-soluble vitamin found in citrus and other fruits, berries and vegetables. Vitamin C is an essential nutrient involved in the repair of tissue, the formation of collagen, and the enzymatic production of certain neurotransmitters. It is required for the functioning of several enzymes and is important for immune system function. It also functions as an antioxidant.

Biological role

Vitamin C is a cofactor for enzymes including prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases, which hydroxylate proline and lysine residues during collagen synthesis, stabilizing the collagen triple helix. Humans, unlike most animals, cannot synthesize vitamin C because they lack the enzyme gulonolactone oxidase, so it must be obtained from the diet. It also aids iron absorption, regenerates vitamin E, and is required for synthesizing carnitine and catecholamine neurotransmitters.

Scurvy and history

Deficiency causes scurvy, characterized by fatigue, swollen bleeding gums, joint pain, poor wound healing, and the reopening of old wounds — consequences of defective collagen. In 1747 the Scottish surgeon James Lind showed that citrus fruit cured scurvy in one of the earliest clinical trials, though the Navy adopted juice rations only decades later. Vitamin C was isolated in 1928 and identified as the antiscorbutic factor in 1932 by Albert Szent-Györgyi and Charles Glen King; Norman Haworth determined its structure.

Intake and claims

Recommended intakes are roughly 75–90 mg/day for adults in the United States, with higher needs for smokers. Despite popular claims popularized by Linus Pauling, evidence that high-dose vitamin C prevents colds in the general population is lacking, though it may slightly shorten their duration. Excess intake is generally excreted, but very large doses can cause gastrointestinal upset.
