Basement membrane

The basement membrane, also known as the base membrane, is a thin, dense sheet of extracellular matrix that lines most tissues, separating epithelial, endothelial, and other parenchymal cells from the underlying or surrounding connective tissue. It provides cell and tissue support and acts as a platform for complex signalling. The basement membrane sits between epithelial cells and the underlying mesenchyme or connective tissue.

Structure and composition

Under the electron microscope the basement membrane consists of the basal lamina — itself divided into the electron-lucent lamina lucida and electron-dense lamina densa — and, in some tissues, a reticular lamina of anchoring fibrils. Its principal components are type IV collagen, which forms a sheet-like network, laminins, nidogen/entactin, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans such as perlecan. The glomerular basement membrane of the kidney acts as a filtration barrier whose integrity depends on this collagen network.

Function and disease

Basement membranes anchor cells via integrins and hemidesmosomes, regulate cell polarity, migration, and differentiation, and serve as barriers to cell invasion — their breaching is a hallmark of invasive cancer. Genetic defects in basement-membrane components cause diseases including Alport syndrome (type IV collagen mutations affecting kidneys, ears, and eyes) and junctional epidermolysis bullosa (laminin mutations causing skin blistering). Goodpasture syndrome is an autoimmune attack on type IV collagen.
