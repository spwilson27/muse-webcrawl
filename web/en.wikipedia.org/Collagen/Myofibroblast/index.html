Myofibroblast

A myofibroblast is a cell phenotype intermediate between a fibroblast and a smooth muscle cell. Myofibroblasts are the principal producers and organisers of extracellular matrix during wound healing, and they generate the contractile force that pulls wound edges together. When their activity persists abnormally, they drive the excess matrix deposition of fibrosis and contribute to the desmoplastic stroma of many cancers.

Characteristics

The defining feature of the mature myofibroblast is the incorporation of alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) into cytoplasmic stress fibres, giving the cell strong contractile capability. Myofibroblasts are anchored to the surrounding matrix through specialised adhesion complexes (fibronexi) linking intracellular actin, via integrins, to extracellular fibronectin fibrils, allowing force transmission across the tissue.

Formation

Myofibroblasts most commonly differentiate from resident fibroblasts under the combined influence of the cytokine TGF-β1, mechanical tension in a stiffening matrix, and specific matrix proteins such as the EDA splice variant of fibronectin. Other precursor sources reported in particular tissues include pericytes, smooth muscle cells, bone-marrow-derived fibrocytes, and epithelial or endothelial cells undergoing mesenchymal transition. A two-step model is often used: fibroblasts first acquire stress fibres as "proto-myofibroblasts", then incorporate α-SMA to become differentiated myofibroblasts.

Function in healing and disease

In normal repair, myofibroblasts appear transiently in granulation tissue, deposit and remodel collagen, contract the wound, and are then removed by apoptosis as healing completes. Failure of this resolution step leads to pathological scarring and organ fibrosis — in the liver (driven largely by activated hepatic stellate cells), lung, kidney, and heart. Cancer-associated fibroblasts with myofibroblastic features shape the tumour microenvironment, promote angiogenesis, and can influence therapy resistance.
