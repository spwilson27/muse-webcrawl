Forage cap

The soft forage cap was standard undress headgear for many 19th-century armies.

A forage cap is a soft military cap worn for undress, fatigue, and working duties, as opposed to the more formal headdress worn on parade. The term arose because soldiers originally wore such caps when sent out foraging. Patterns varied by country and period: the best-known examples are the floppy-crowned caps worn by both Union and Confederate soldiers in the American Civil War and the pillbox and peaked variants worn by British soldiers in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

History

Soft caps for working dress appeared in European armies in the late 18th and early 19th centuries as a practical complement to stiff shakos and helmets. The British Army adopted various forage caps from the 1830s onward, including the round "pork pie" and later pillbox styles, and the Kilmarnock bonnet in Scottish regiments. In the Crimean War and later colonial campaigns the forage cap, sometimes with a protective neck curtain, was widely worn in the field.

In the United States, the Model 1858 forage cap — a soft-crowned képi-like cap with a sloping visor, often called a "bummer's cap" — was issued in enormous numbers during the Civil War (1861–65) and became an enduring image of the common soldier, worn by Union troops in dark blue and by Confederates in grey or butternut variations. Soldiers frequently personalized them with corps badges and unit insignia.

Later use

In the 20th century, forage caps evolved into or were replaced by field caps, side caps (garrison caps), and peaked fatigue caps. The British Army retained styles of forage cap for barrack dress in some regiments, and similar caps survive in many armed forces and uniformed services as working or undress headgear.
