The human stomach: cardia, fundus, body and pylorus.

The stomach is a muscular, hollow organ in the gastrointestinal tract of humans and many other animals. Located between the oesophagus and the small intestine, it secretes digestive enzymes and hydrochloric acid, churns food by muscular contraction, and stores and partially digests a meal, turning it into a semi-liquid mixture called chyme.

Anatomy

The stomach is divided into the cardia (where the oesophagus enters), the dome-shaped fundus, the central body (corpus), and the pylorus, whose pyloric sphincter controls emptying into the duodenum. Its wall has an extra oblique muscle layer enabling churning, and its mucosa is folded into rugae that allow expansion; an adult stomach holds roughly 1–1.5 litres when full.

Function

Gastric glands secrete hydrochloric acid (from parietal cells), pepsinogen (chief cells, activated to pepsin in acid), mucus (protecting the wall from self-digestion), intrinsic factor (needed for vitamin B12 absorption) and the hormone gastrin (from G cells), which stimulates acid secretion. The hormone ghrelin, produced mainly in the stomach, stimulates hunger. Food typically leaves over 2–4 hours; only a few substances — water, alcohol, some drugs — are absorbed directly through the stomach wall.

Clinical significance

Common disorders include gastritis, peptic ulcers (strongly associated with infection by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori and with NSAID use), gastro-oesophageal reflux, gastroparesis and stomach cancer, which remains among the leading causes of cancer death worldwide. Surgical procedures include gastrectomy and bariatric (weight-loss) operations such as the gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy.
