Formalism (music)

The 1948 Zhdanov decree condemned leading Soviet composers as formalists.

Formalism in music refers both to an aesthetic position — that music's meaning lies in its formal structures rather than in any represented content or emotion — and, in Soviet cultural politics, to a term of abuse for music considered elitist, dissonant or inaccessible to the people. The aesthetic sense derives from Eduard Hanslick's influential 1854 treatise On the Musically Beautiful, which argued that music consists of "tonally moving forms" and does not represent feelings.

Soviet anti-formalism campaigns

In the Soviet Union, "formalism" became a charge levelled against composers whose work departed from the officially favoured doctrine of socialist realism. In 1936, Pravda's editorial "Muddle Instead of Music" attacked Dmitri Shostakovich's opera Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District, which had previously been a success; Shostakovich withdrew his Fourth Symphony and responded with the more accessible Fifth.

The campaign peaked with the Zhdanov Doctrine of 1948. Following a conference convened by Andrei Zhdanov, the Central Committee issued a resolution on Vano Muradeli's opera The Great Friendship that condemned Shostakovich, Sergei Prokofiev, Aram Khachaturian, Nikolai Myaskovsky and others for "formalistic distortions" and anti-democratic tendencies. The composers were forced to make public apologies, many works were banned from performance, and teachers lost their posts. The resolution was formally rescinded in 1958.

Use elsewhere

The word was also applied in Maoist China during campaigns against Western-influenced composition, and in the West some critics accused serialist and avant-garde composers of formalism in the pejorative sense of cerebral, audience-alienating technique. Composers such as Igor Stravinsky embraced the aesthetic sense of the term, insisting that music is "powerless to express anything at all" beyond itself.
