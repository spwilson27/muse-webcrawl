Style (form of address)

A style of office or form of address is an official or legally recognised form of address for a person or other entity (such as a government or company), and may often be used in conjunction with a personal title. A style, by tradition or law, precedes a reference to a person who holds a post or political office, and is sometimes used to refer to the office itself. An honorific can also be awarded to an individual in a personal capacity.

Examples

Common styles in English-speaking and Commonwealth usage include:

  • His/Her Majesty — for a reigning king or queen
  • His/Her Royal Highness — for princes and princesses of a royal house
  • His/Her Excellency — for ambassadors, governors, and some heads of state and government
  • The Right Honourable — for members of the UK Privy Council, peers below the rank of marquess in direct address, and certain Commonwealth officials
  • The Honourable — for judges, legislators, and ministers in many jurisdictions
  • His Holiness — for the Pope and certain Orthodox patriarchs and the Dalai Lama
  • His Eminence — for cardinals of the Roman Catholic Church
  • The Venerable, The Reverend, and related forms — for clergy of various ranks

Usage

Styles are governed by a mixture of statute, letters patent, official guides (such as Debrett's in the United Kingdom), and customary practice, and they differ from titles proper: a style is the manner of addressing or referring to the holder rather than the office or rank itself. Practices have modernised over time — for example, in the United Kingdom the use of styles for civil servants and military ranks in routine correspondence has been simplified, and some countries have abolished honorific styles for officials altogether.
