Zwitterion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemistry, a zwitterion (/ˈtsvɪtəˌraɪən/; from German zwitter "hermaphrodite"), also called an inner salt or dipolar ion, is a molecule that contains an equal number of positively and negatively charged functional groups. With amino acids, for example, in solution a chemical equilibrium will be established between the "parent" molecule and the zwitterion.

Amino acids

An amino acid contains both an amine group and a carboxylic acid group. In aqueous solution near neutral pH, the carboxyl group is deprotonated (–COO⁻) and the amino group is protonated (–NH₃⁺), so the molecule carries both charges while remaining electrically neutral overall. The pH at which the molecule has zero net charge is called its isoelectric point (pI). At lower pH the molecule becomes net positive; at higher pH, net negative. Solid amino acids such as glycine crystallise in zwitterionic form, which accounts for their high melting points and salt-like properties.

Other examples

Betaines are a class of permanently zwitterionic compounds, including trimethylglycine. Sulfobetaines are used as mild detergents, and many phospholipids in cell membranes are zwitterionic at physiological pH. Ampholytes and buffer molecules such as the Good's buffers are also commonly zwitterionic.
