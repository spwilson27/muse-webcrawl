Cascade Range

Cascade Range
Length: ~1,100 km (700 mi)
Highest peak: Mount Rainier, 4,392 m (14,411 ft)
Countries: Canada, United States
Provinces/states: British Columbia, Washington, Oregon, California

The Cascade Range or Cascades is a major mountain range of western North America, extending from southern British Columbia through Washington and Oregon to Northern California. It includes both the non-volcanic North Cascades and the notable volcanoes of the High Cascades, part of the Pacific Ring of Fire.

Geology

The Cascades are formed by subduction of the Juan de Fuca Plate beneath the North American Plate (the Cascadia subduction zone). The High Cascades are a chain of stratovolcanoes including Mount Rainier, Mount St. Helens, Mount Hood, Mount Shasta, and Lassen Peak. The 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens was the deadliest and most destructive volcanic event in U.S. history.

Geography and climate

The range divides the wet coastal lowlands of the Pacific Northwest from the drier interior plateau. Western slopes receive very heavy rainfall and support dense conifer forests; the rain shadow to the east produces semi-arid conditions. The Columbia River Gorge is the only major break in the range.

History

The range takes its name from the Cascades Rapids of the Columbia River. Indigenous peoples inhabited the region for thousands of years before the Lewis and Clark Expedition crossed near the Columbia in 1805–06.
