Fresnel diffraction

In optics, the Fresnel diffraction equation describes the pattern of waves in the near field, close to the diffracting object, where the wavefronts retain noticeable curvature. It is named after the physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel and applies when the Fresnel number is of order one or larger; at greater distances the simpler Fraunhofer diffraction approximation holds instead.

Theory

Fresnel diffraction results from the Huygens–Fresnel principle: every point of a wavefront acts as a source of secondary spherical wavelets, and the field at a nearby observation point is their superposition. The aperture can be divided into Fresnel zones—concentric half-period regions whose contributions alternate in sign—which explains phenomena such as the bright Poisson (Arago) spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow. Mathematically the pattern is expressed with Fresnel integrals, and the amplitude distribution can be analysed with the Cornu spiral.

Applications

Near-field diffraction governs the behaviour of beams just beyond apertures and edges, the design of Fresnel zone plates used as lenses for X-rays and other radiation, and diffraction effects in microscopy, holography, and radio propagation over obstacles.
