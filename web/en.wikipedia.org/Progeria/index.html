Progeria

Progeria is the name given to a group of extremely rare genetic disorders in which symptoms resembling aspects of ageing are manifested at a very early age. The term derives from the Greek pro ("before, premature") and geras ("old age"). Its best-known form is Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), first described by Jonathan Hutchinson in 1886 and Hastings Gilford in 1897.

Hutchinson–Gilford syndrome

HGPS is caused by a sporadic dominant mutation in the LMNA gene, which encodes the nuclear proteins lamin A and C. The mutation activates a cryptic splice site, producing an abnormal protein called progerin that lacks a cleavage site and remains permanently farnesylated, distorting the nuclear envelope and disrupting cell function. The condition is not usually inherited; it arises as a new mutation.

Children with HGPS appear normal at birth but develop characteristic features within the first two years: growth failure, loss of body fat and hair (alopecia), aged-looking skin, stiffness of joints, hip dislocation, and a distinctive facial appearance with prominent eyes and a small chin. Intelligence is unaffected. Average life expectancy is about 14–15 years, with death most often caused by cardiovascular disease — severe atherosclerosis leading to heart attack or stroke.

Treatment

In November 2020 the United States Food and Drug Administration approved lonafarnib, a farnesyltransferase inhibitor, for HGPS — the first approved treatment for the condition. Studies associated lonafarnib with reduced mortality. Supportive care addresses cardiovascular disease and joint stiffness. Research into progeria has also informed the understanding of normal ageing, since progerin is produced at low levels in healthy cells.

Other progeroid syndromes

Related disorders include Werner syndrome ("adult progeria"), caused by mutations in the WRN helicase gene and appearing after puberty; Cockayne syndrome; and mandibuloacral dysplasia. These are collectively called progeroid syndromes.
