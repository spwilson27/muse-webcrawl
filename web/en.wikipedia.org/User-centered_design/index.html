User-centered design

User-centered design (UCD) is an iterative design process in which designers focus on the users and their needs in each phase of the design process. It involves users throughout — through interviews, surveys, personas, usability testing, and evaluation of prototypes — so that products, especially interactive systems, are usable and accessible. The approach is codified in the international standard ISO 9241-210, "Human-centred design for interactive systems".

Principles

Core principles include an explicit understanding of users, tasks, and environments; involvement of users throughout design and development; design driven and refined by user-centred evaluation; iteration; attention to the whole user experience; and multidisciplinary design teams. The term was popularised by Donald Norman, whose book The Design of Everyday Things (1988) argued that failures attributed to "human error" are often failures of design.

Process

A typical UCD cycle comprises four phases: understand the context of use, specify user requirements, produce design solutions (from sketches to prototypes), and evaluate against the requirements — repeating until the design performs adequately. Methods include contextual inquiry, card sorting, think-aloud usability tests, and A/B testing.

Applications and criticism

UCD underlies modern human–computer interaction, web and app design, and service design, and overlaps with design thinking and participatory design (which gives users a stronger creative role). Critics note that an excessive focus on current users can entrench incremental thinking, and that users' stated preferences do not always predict behaviour; nonetheless, user involvement is associated with higher adoption and fewer costly late redesigns.
