Levantine architecture

Levantine architecture is the architecture of the Levant, the region of the eastern Mediterranean encompassing Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Palestine and Israel. Shaped by a hot, dry climate, local stone and successive Roman, Byzantine, Islamic, Crusader and Ottoman influences, it forms a recognisable regional tradition.

Characteristics

Traditional Levantine houses are built of local limestone or sandstone with thick walls, small street-facing openings and inward-looking courtyards that provide shade and privacy. Distinctive elements include the cross-vaulted riwaq gallery, the central triple-arched facade of the Lebanese central-hall house, wooden mashrabiya-like screens, coloured ablaq masonry of alternating black and white stone, and flat or tiled pitched roofs depending on rainfall.

Historical development

Roman and Byzantine temples, theatres and churches — as at Baalbek, Palmyra and Gerasa — underpin the region's classical heritage. Under Islam came Umayyad palaces and mosques, Mamluk madrasas and caravanserais in Damascus and Aleppo, and Ottoman domestic architecture. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, Beirut, Haifa, Jaffa and Jerusalem saw elegant merchant houses combining Levantine plans with European details; in the Mandate period this evolved into styles such as Beirut's Art Deco and Tel Aviv's Bauhaus-influenced International Style.

Preservation

War and rapid development have destroyed much historic fabric, notably in Beirut during the Lebanese Civil War and in Syria since 2011, prompting documentation and reconstruction efforts by local and international bodies.
