The Final Solution (German: die Endlösung) or the Final Solution to the Jewish Question (Endlösung der Judenfrage) was the Nazi plan for the genocide of the Jews of Europe during World War II — the culminating phase of the Holocaust, in which some six million Jews were murdered. The euphemistic term referred to the systematic, industrialised extermination of all Jews within reach of the German regime.

Origins

Nazi policy toward Jews escalated through the 1930s from discrimination and forced emigration to, after the invasion of Poland in 1939, ghettoisation, and after the invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, mass shootings by the Einsatzgruppen that killed over a million people. By late 1941 policy had shifted to total extermination: the first extermination camp, Chełmno, began killing operations in December 1941, and gassing facilities were built at Bełżec, Sobibór and Treblinka under Operation Reinhard.

Wannsee and implementation

On 20 January 1942, Reinhard Heydrich convened the Wannsee Conference of senior officials to coordinate the deportation of eleven million European Jews to the occupied East for extermination. Jews were deported by rail from across Europe to six extermination camps in occupied Poland, where most were killed in gas chambers on arrival; the largest, Auschwitz-Birkenau, murdered roughly one million Jews. In total some three million died in the camps, the rest by shooting, ghetto conditions, and death marches.

Aftermath

Evidence of the Final Solution — camp sites, documents such as the Wannsee Protocol, and the testimony of survivors and perpetrators — formed the core of the prosecution case at the Nuremberg trials. The term and the policy it names are central to Holocaust historiography and to the scholarly debate between "intentionalist" and "functionalist" accounts of how the decision for total murder emerged.
