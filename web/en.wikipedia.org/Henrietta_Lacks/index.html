Henrietta Lacks

Henrietta Lacks

Born: Loretta Pleasant, 1 August 1920, Roanoke, Virginia, US

Died: 4 October 1951 (aged 31), Baltimore, Maryland, US

Known for: Source of the HeLa cell line

Henrietta Lacks in the 1940s

Henrietta Lacks (born Loretta Pleasant; 1 August 1920 – 4 October 1951) was an African-American woman whose cervical cancer cells, taken without her knowledge in 1951 during treatment at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, became the source of the HeLa cell line — the first immortalised human cell line and one of the most important tools in biomedical research.

Life and illness

Lacks was born in Roanoke, Virginia, and raised largely in Clover, Virginia, where she worked as a tobacco farmer before moving with her husband to Turner Station near Baltimore. In 1951 she was diagnosed with an aggressive cervical carcinoma at Johns Hopkins, then one of the few hospitals treating poor African-American patients. During radium treatment, samples of her tumour were given to researcher George Otto Gey, whose laboratory discovered that her cells, unlike any cultured before, survived and multiplied indefinitely. Lacks died of the cancer in October 1951, aged 31.

HeLa and legacy

HeLa cells were mass-produced and distributed worldwide, contributing to the development of the polio vaccine, cancer and virology research, gene mapping, and thousands of patents. For decades Lacks's family did not know of the cell line; her identity became public in the 1970s. Her story, told in Rebecca Skloot's 2010 book The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks, raised lasting questions about informed consent and patients' rights. In 2013 the Lacks family reached an agreement with the National Institutes of Health over access to the HeLa genome sequence, and in 2023 the family settled a lawsuit with a biotechnology company over the commercial use of the cells.
