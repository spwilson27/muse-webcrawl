Carotene is a family of unsaturated hydrocarbons with the general formula C40Hx, synthesised by plants but not by animals. Carotenes are photosynthetic pigments that give the characteristic orange colour to carrots (from which the name derives, via Latin carota), sweet potatoes, cantaloupe melons and autumn leaves. They absorb ultraviolet, violet and blue light and scatter orange and red light, and they protect photosynthetic tissues by quenching excess energy and reactive oxygen species.

Forms

The best known carotenes are α-carotene, β-carotene, γ-carotene, δ-carotene, ε-carotene and ζ-carotene, which differ in the structure of their end rings. Lycopene, the red pigment of tomatoes, is also a carotene. Carotenes lack oxygen atoms, distinguishing them from the related xanthophylls (such as lutein), which are oxygenated carotenoids.

β-Carotene and vitamin A

β-Carotene is a precursor (provitamin) of vitamin A: the enzyme beta-carotene 15,15′-dioxygenase cleaves it in the intestine to retinal, which is converted to retinol. Because the conversion is regulated, excess dietary β-carotene does not cause vitamin A toxicity, though heavy intake can produce carotenemia, a harmless yellowing of the skin. β-Carotene is used commercially as a food colouring (E160a) and in dietary supplements.

Role in photosynthesis

In photosynthetic organisms, carotenes are accessory pigments embedded in the thylakoid membrane. They broaden the spectrum of light harvested by chlorophyll and dissipate excess excitation energy as heat (non-photochemical quenching), preventing photo-oxidative damage.

Dietary sources and health

Rich sources include carrots, sweet potatoes, dark leafy greens, butternut squash and cantaloupe. Large trials of high-dose β-carotene supplements in smokers found an increased risk of lung cancer, and supplements are not recommended for that group; carotene obtained from food is considered safe.
