Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict

The Hague Convention for the Protection of Cultural Property in the Event of Armed Conflict is an international treaty adopted at The Hague on 14 May 1954, the first international agreement devoted entirely to protecting cultural property—monuments, archaeological sites, works of art, manuscripts, archives, and museums—during armed conflict. It was drafted in response to the massive destruction and looting of cultural heritage during the Second World War.

Provisions

Parties undertake to safeguard cultural property in their own territory in peacetime and to respect cultural property in occupied and enemy territory during hostilities, refraining from its use for military purposes and from acts of hostility against it, except in cases of imperative military necessity. The Convention also prohibits theft, pillage, and vandalism. Its distinctive emblem—a blue-and-white shield—marks protected property.

Protocols

A First Protocol (1954) addresses cultural property exported from occupied territory; a Second Protocol (1999) strengthened the system with a regime of "enhanced protection" and provisions on criminal responsibility for serious violations.
