Iridescence

Iridescence is an optical phenomenon in which a surface appears to change colour as the angle of view or angle of illumination changes. Familiar examples include soap bubbles, oil films on water, butterfly wings, and the feathers of hummingbirds and peacocks.

Mechanism

Iridescence is a form of structural colouration. Microscopic structures — thin films, multilayer stacks, or diffraction gratings — interfere with light waves: reflections from successive layers reinforce some wavelengths and cancel others, and because the path differences depend on viewing angle, the reflected colour shifts with position. Unlike pigment colours, iridescent colours can be exceptionally vivid and metallic in appearance.

In nature

Many animals use iridescence for display and camouflage: the throats of hummingbirds, the eyespots of peacock feathers, morpho butterfly wings, and the shells of beetles and molluscs (nacre, or mother of pearl). In plants, iridescent leaves occur in some shade-dwelling species. Iridescent clouds and nacreous clouds show the effect in the atmosphere.

Uses

Engineers mimic natural iridescent structures in security printing on banknotes, cosmetics, automotive paints, and photonic sensors, where a colour change can signal strain, temperature, or chemical exposure.
