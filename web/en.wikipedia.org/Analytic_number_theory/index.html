Analytic number theory is a branch of number theory that uses methods from mathematical analysis to solve problems about the integers, especially the distribution of prime numbers. Where algebraic number theory studies number fields and their structure, analytic number theory typically seeks quantitative, asymptotic answers: roughly how many primes there are below a bound, or how often an equation can be solved.

The distribution of the primes is governed by the behaviour of the Riemann zeta function.

History

The subject's roots lie in Euler's work in the 1730s–40s, including his product formula connecting the zeta function to the primes and his proof that the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges. In 1837 Dirichlet introduced L-functions and characters to prove that there are infinitely many primes in any coprime arithmetic progression. Riemann's 1859 memoir "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" linked prime distribution to the zeros of the zeta function in the complex plane and posed the Riemann hypothesis. Building on this, Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin independently proved the prime number theorem in 1896: the count of primes up to x is asymptotic to x / log x.

Main topics

Central themes include the distribution of primes and prime gaps; arithmetic functions and their average orders, studied through Dirichlet series and summation methods; additive problems such as Goldbach's conjecture and Waring's problem, attacked with the Hardy–Littlewood circle method (which yielded Vinogradov's 1937 proof that every large odd number is a sum of three primes); sieve methods, from Eratosthenes and Brun to modern work on bounded prime gaps; the theory of L-functions and modular forms; and exponential sums. The field also encompasses probabilistic number theory and the study of multiplicative functions.

Methods

Analytic number theorists combine complex analysis (contour integration, the theory of entire functions), Fourier analysis, estimates for character and exponential sums, and mean-value theorems. Results are often conditional on, or measured against, the generalised Riemann hypothesis, which remains unproved and is one of the Clay Millennium Prize problems.

Notable figures

Major contributors include Euler, Dirichlet, Chebyshev, Riemann, Hadamard, Hardy and Littlewood, Ramanujan, Vinogradov, Selberg, Erdős, Deligne, and, in the modern era, Zhang Yitang, whose 2013 proof of bounded gaps between primes was a landmark of 21st-century analytic number theory.
