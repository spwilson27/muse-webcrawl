Whodunit

A whodunit (a contraction of "who [has] done it?") is a complex, plot-driven variety of detective fiction in which the puzzle of who committed a crime — usually a murder — is the main focus. The reader is given clues from which they can attempt to deduce the criminal's identity before the solution is revealed at the end.

Characteristics

The classic whodunit presents a closed circle of suspects, a detective (professional or amateur), and a fair-play puzzle: all clues are shown to the reader, following the "rules" codified by writers such as S. S. Van Dine and Ronald Knox, whose "Ten Commandments" banned devices such as secret passages and undiscovered poisons. The climax typically gathers the suspects together while the detective explains the solution.

History

The form developed from 19th-century detective fiction and flourished in the Golden Age of Detective Fiction between the world wars, above all in the works of Agatha Christie, whose The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926) and Murder on the Orient Express (1934) remain landmarks. Christie's detectives Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple became archetypes, and her play The Mousetrap is the world's longest-running play.

In other media

The whodunit structure thrives in film and television — from classic adaptations to modern films such as Knives Out (2019) — and in board games such as Cluedo (Clue). A related form, the "howcatchem" or inverted detective story, shows the crime first and follows the detective's pursuit.
