Nefertiti (c. 1370 – c. 1330 BC) was a queen of the 18th Dynasty of ancient Egypt, the Great Royal Wife of Pharaoh Akhenaten. Nefertiti and her husband were known for their radical overhaul of Egyptian state religion, in which they promoted a form of proto-monotheistic worship centred on the sun disc, the Aten.

Life

Nefertiti's origins are uncertain; she may have been the daughter of the courtier Ay, who later became pharaoh. She bore Akhenaten six daughters and appears prominently alongside him in the art of the Amarna period, shown in roles of unusually great power — smiting enemies, driving her own chariot and officiating in cult rituals. The royal family moved to a new capital, Akhetaten (modern Amarna), devoted to the Aten.

Nefertiti disappears from the historical record around year 12–16 of Akhenaten's reign. Theories include that she died of plague, fell from favour, or ruled briefly as co-regent or even as the pharaoh Neferneferuaten (or Smenkhkare) after her husband's death. Her tomb and mummy have never been conclusively identified, though a much-publicised (and disputed) claim suggested hidden chambers behind Tutankhamun's tomb might contain her burial.

The bust

Nefertiti is best known today for her painted limestone bust, discovered in 1912 at Amarna by a German archaeological team led by Ludwig Borchardt in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose. It was taken to Berlin and is now the centrepiece of the Neues Museum, part of the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin. Egypt has repeatedly requested its repatriation, arguing it was removed under a disputed partage agreement; Germany maintains its acquisition was lawful.

Legacy

The bust's striking beauty made Nefertiti a global icon of ancient Egypt and of feminine beauty, reproduced on everything from book covers to cosmetics branding.
