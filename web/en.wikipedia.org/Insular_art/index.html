Insular art

Insular art, also known as Hiberno-Saxon art, is the style of art produced in post-Roman Britain and Ireland from about 600 to 1100 AD, and exported to continental Europe by the Hiberno-Scottish and Anglo-Saxon missions. It is characterised by an extraordinarily dense abstract ornament combining Celtic, Germanic and Pictish motifs — interlace (plaitwork), spirals, trumpet patterns, key patterns and stylised interlaced animals — with Mediterranean-derived elements such as evangelist portraits that arrived with Christianity. The term "Insular" (from Latin insula, island) reflects that Ireland and Britain shared the style across ethnic lines, complicating older national labels.

Media and masterpieces

Illuminated manuscripts. The supreme achievements are gospel books: the Book of Durrow (c. 650–700), the Lindisfarne Gospels (c. 700–720), with their full-page "carpet pages" of pure ornament facing evangelist portraits, the Book of Kells (c. 800, probably begun on Iona), and the Lichfield Gospels. Miniatures use brilliant, durable pigments on vellum, with ornament overwhelming text — initial letters may expand to fill entire pages.

Metalwork. Surviving secular and church metalwork includes the brooches of the Tara Brooch type, the Ardagh and Derrynaflan chalices, house-shaped shrines and croziers, ornamented with filigree, enamel, amber and millefiori glass. Hoards such as Sutton Hoo (Anglo-Saxon, c. 620) show the Germanic animal style that fed into the Insular synthesis.

Sculpture. Stone high crosses — such as those at Monasterboice and Clonmacnoise in Ireland, and the Ruthwell and Bewcastle crosses in Britain — combine interlace panels with scriptural figure scenes, serving as preaching monuments.

Development and spread

The style crystallised in the monasteries founded in the wake of St Columba's mission from Ireland to Iona (563) and St Aidan's to Lindisfarne (635), fusing Irish, Pictish, British and Anglo-Saxon craftsmanship. Irish and English missionaries — Columbanus, Willibrord and others — carried Insular manuscripts and scribal practice to Francia and Italy, influencing Carolingian art. After the Viking raids began (Lindisfarne was sacked in 793), production shifted and gradually merged with Romanesque art by the 12th century, though the style left a permanent mark on medieval ornament.
