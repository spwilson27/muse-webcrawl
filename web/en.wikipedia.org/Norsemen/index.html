Norsemen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Norsemen (or Norse people) were the North Germanic people of early medieval Scandinavia — the inhabitants of what are now Norway, Sweden, and Denmark — who spoke Old Norse and are best known for the seaborne expansion of the Viking Age (c. 793–1066 CE). "Norseman" literally means "man of the north"; those who went abroad on expeditions of raiding, trading, and settlement were called víkingar (Vikings), though most Norse people were farmers, craftspeople, and traders who never left Scandinavia.

Terminology

In Old Norse sources the people are described by regional terms — Danes, Swedes (Svíar), Gautar (Geats), and Norwegians — while "Norsemen" (Norðmenn) in western European chronicles often specifically denotes Norwegians. Related diaspora groups include the Varangians and Rus' of Eastern Europe, the Normans of northern France (descendants of Norse settlers under Rollo from 911), and the Norse-Gaels of Ireland and Scotland.

Expansion

Beginning with the raid on Lindisfarne (793), Norse seafarers ranged across the known world: settling Iceland (from c. 870), Greenland (c. 985), and briefly North America (Vinland, c. 1000, confirmed at L'Anse aux Meadows); conquering parts of England (the Danelaw) and Ireland; serving as the Varangian Guard of the Byzantine emperors in Constantinople; and trading down the Volga and Dnieper rivers to the Caspian and Black Seas. Silver hoards, runestones, and Arabic dirhams in Scandinavian graves attest to the reach of these networks.

Society and culture

Norse society combined farming on marginal northern lands with fishing, iron production, and long-distance trade in furs, walrus ivory, slaves, and amber. Its culture — law assemblies, kinship and feud, pagan religion, runic literacy, and a heroic literary tradition later preserved in the Icelandic sagas and Eddas — is treated in detail in the article on Norse culture. By the eleventh and twelfth centuries the Scandinavian kingdoms of Denmark, Norway, and Sweden had consolidated and adopted Christianity, and the Norsemen merged into the general population of medieval Europe, though Norse language survived longest as Norn in Orkney and Shetland and as Icelandic and Faroese in the Atlantic settlements.

Legacy

Norse words entered English in large numbers during the Danelaw period (e.g. sky, egg, knife, they), and Norse ancestry is widespread in Iceland, coastal Scotland and Ireland, and Normandy. Since the nineteenth century the Norsemen — romanticised and sometimes mythologised — have occupied a prominent place in Scandinavian national identity and in global popular culture.
