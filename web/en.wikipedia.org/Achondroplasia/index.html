Achondroplasia

Cause: Gain-of-function mutation in FGFR3
Inheritance: Autosomal dominant
Frequency: ~1 in 15,000–40,000 births
Prognosis: Near-normal life expectancy

Achondroplasia is a genetic disorder of bone growth and the most common cause of dwarfism (short-limbed short stature). People with achondroplasia have disproportionately short arms and legs, an average-sized trunk, a large head with a prominent forehead, and a characteristic facial appearance. Intelligence and lifespan are typically normal or near-normal.

Cause

The condition results from a gain-of-function mutation in the gene for fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) on chromosome 4. FGFR3 normally restrains the growth of long bones at the growth plate; the mutation makes the receptor overactive, inhibiting cartilage-to-bone conversion (endochondral ossification). In about 80% of cases the mutation arises anew (de novo), with a demonstrated association with advanced paternal age; in the remainder it is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. A child of two parents with achondroplasia has a 25% chance of inheriting both copies, a form (homozygous achondroplasia) that is usually fatal in infancy.

Features and complications

Typical adult height is about 124–131 cm. Complications can include narrowing of the spinal canal (spinal stenosis), bowing of the legs, sleep apnoea, recurrent ear infections, obesity, and, in infants, compression of the brainstem at the foramen magnum, which requires monitoring.

Treatment

Management is supportive: monitoring of growth and the spine, treatment of complications, and sometimes surgery. Vosoritide, a C-type natriuretic peptide analogue that counteracts FGFR3 signalling, was approved in 2021 to increase growth velocity in children with achondroplasia. Limb-lengthening surgery remains controversial within the dwarfism community, many of whom regard short stature as an identity rather than a defect to be corrected.
