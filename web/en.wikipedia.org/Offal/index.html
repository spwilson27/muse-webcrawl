Offal

Offal, also known as variety meats, pluck, or organ meats, is the internal organs of a butchered animal. The word does not refer to a particular list of organs but includes most internal organs other than muscle and bone. Some cultures strongly consider offal consumption taboo, while others use it as delicacies or staple foods.

A selection of offal at a market butcher.

Types and dishes

Common forms of offal include liver, kidneys, heart, tripe (stomach lining), sweetbreads (thymus and pancreas), brain, tongue, and intestines used as sausage casings. Dishes built on offal range from Scottish haggis and French andouillette to steak and kidney pie, menudo, and chitterlings. Many are now celebrated in "nose-to-tail" cooking associated with chefs such as Fergus Henderson.

Nutrition and regulation

Organ meats are rich in iron, B vitamins (especially B12), and fat-soluble vitamins, though some carry high cholesterol. Food-safety rules governing offal production fall within broader workplace and food hygiene regimes, paralleling the exposure-control concerns of occupational hygiene in abattoirs.
