Sander

A sander is a power tool used to smooth surfaces by abrasion with sandpaper. Sanders are staples of woodworking, metalworking and automotive finishing: a motor drives an abrasive surface in a linear, orbital or rotary motion, removing material far faster and more evenly than hand sanding. Larger industrial machines, such as wide-belt sanders, perform the same function at production scale.

Types

  • Belt sander — drives a continuous loop of abrasive belt over two drums; aggressive, used for rapid material removal and floor sanding (in its large drum-sander form).
  • Orbital sander — vibrates a square or rectangular pad in small circular motions; suited to finish sanding, with sheets clamped or fixed to the pad.
  • Random orbital sander — spins a round disc while simultaneously moving it in an elliptical orbit, a combination that avoids swirl marks and produces fine finishes; dust is usually collected through holes in the disc.
  • Disc sander — a bench-mounted abrasive disc used for shaping and end-grain work, often combined with a belt station.
  • Detail (palm/mouse) sander — a small sander with a pointed pad for corners and tight spaces.
  • Spindle and oscillating sanders — use rotating or oscillating abrasive drums for curves and edges.

Use and safety

Work proceeds through progressively finer abrasive grits — coarse grits (40–80) for shaping and paint removal, medium (100–150) for general smoothing, and fine (180–320 and up) for final finishes. Modern sanders feature variable speed, dust extraction ports and sealed bearings. Because sanding generates fine respirable dust — hazardous with hardwoods, MDF and old paint — dust masks, extraction and eye protection are recommended.

History

Hand sanding with sandpaper, sharkskin and abrasive minerals is centuries old. Portable electric sanders appeared in the early twentieth century; the belt sander was commercialised in the 1920s and the random orbital design — which transformed finish quality — became widespread with manufacturers such as Festool and Mirka in the later twentieth century, alongside pneumatic sanders in vehicle body shops.
