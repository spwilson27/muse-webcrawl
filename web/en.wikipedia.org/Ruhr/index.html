Ruhr

The Ruhr (German: Ruhrgebiet), also known as the Ruhr area, Ruhr valley or Ruhr district, is a polycentric urban area in North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany. Named after the Ruhr River, it stretches along the Ruhr, Emscher and Lippe rivers and includes the major cities of Duisburg, Essen, Bochum, Dortmund, Gelsenkirchen, Oberhausen and Hagen. With over five million inhabitants it is Germany's largest urban agglomeration and one of the largest in Europe.

Industrial history

Rich seams of hard coal made the Ruhr the industrial heartland of Germany in the 19th and 20th centuries: coal mining, coking, and steel production (dominated by firms such as Krupp and Thyssen) powered German industrialisation and both world wars' armaments. Because of its strategic value, the region was occupied by French and Belgian troops in 1923 after reparations defaults (the Ruhr Occupation), provoking passive resistance, and was pulverised by Allied bombing in the Second World War, notably the Battle of the Ruhr (1943).

Structural change

From the coal crisis of 1958 and the steel crisis of the 1970s, the region underwent painful restructuring; the last hard-coal mine, Prosper-Haniel, closed in 2018. Former industrial sites became cultural landmarks — the Zollverein Coal Mine Industrial Complex in Essen is a UNESCO World Heritage Site — universities (Bochum's Ruhr University, founded 1962, was the region's first new university) and service industries replaced heavy industry, and the Ruhr was European Capital of Culture in 2010 ("RUHR.2010").
