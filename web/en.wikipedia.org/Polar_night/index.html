Polar night

The polar night is a phenomenon in the northernmost and southernmost regions of Earth where night lasts for more than 24 hours. This occurs only inside the polar circles. The opposite phenomenon, polar day or midnight sun, occurs when the Sun remains above the horizon for more than 24 hours.

"Night" is understood as at least the centre of the Sun being below the horizon. The phenomenon is caused by the tilt of Earth's axis relative to its orbit around the Sun: near the winter solstice, the pole of the winter hemisphere points away from the Sun, so the Sun never fully rises there.

Characteristics

The duration of the polar night depends on latitude, ranging from about 20 hours at the polar circles to roughly 179 days at the poles themselves. Because of atmospheric refraction, and because the Sun is a disc rather than a point source, the polar night is somewhat shorter than the polar day at corresponding latitudes.

Not all of the polar night is pitch black: for much of the period, the Sun is only just below the horizon, producing periods of twilight around midday. Depending on how far below the horizon the Sun lies, this twilight may be civil (brightest), nautical or astronomical, and only in the darkest weeks and highest latitudes does true night, with the Sun more than 18 degrees below the horizon, persist around the clock.

Effects on humans

The prolonged darkness can affect residents' sleep, mood and circadian rhythms, and has been associated with seasonal affective disorder. Settlements such as Utqiaġvik (Barrow), Alaska, and Tromsø and Longyearbyen in Norway experience polar night each winter; at Longyearbyen in Svalbard the Sun does not rise between late October and mid-February.
