Government shutdown in the United States

In United States politics, a government shutdown occurs when Congress fails to pass, or the President refuses to sign, appropriations legislation funding federal agencies, causing a lapse in appropriations. Under the Antideficiency Act, as interpreted by Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti in 1980–1981, agencies must suspend operations funded by annual appropriations until funding is restored, except for activities essential to protect life and property.

Effects

During a shutdown, "non-essential" federal employees are furloughed without pay (though since 2019 they are guaranteed back pay once funding resumes), national parks and museums close, and many regulatory and grant-making activities pause. Essential personnel—including military members, law enforcement, and air traffic controllers—continue working without immediate pay. Shutdowns increase government debt burdens indirectly through lost revenue and restart costs, and the Congressional Budget Office has estimated that the 2018–2019 shutdown reduced economic output by billions of dollars.

Notable shutdowns

Funding gaps have occurred repeatedly since 1976, but full shutdowns became a recurring feature of budget politics from the 1990s. The 1995–1996 shutdowns under President Bill Clinton lasted a combined 26 days. A 16-day shutdown followed in October 2013 over the Affordable Care Act. The longest in US history ran from 22 December 2018 to 25 January 2019—35 days—during a dispute over funding for a border wall, ending when President Donald Trump signed a temporary funding bill.

Causes

Shutdowns typically arise when the presidency and one or both chambers of Congress are controlled by different parties and budget negotiations become entangled with unrelated policy disputes. Because the US fiscal year begins on 1 October, failure to enact appropriations by that date—or to pass a continuing resolution—triggers a lapse.
