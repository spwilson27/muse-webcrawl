Utu

Utu (Sumerian: 𒀯𒌓, "sun") was the god of the sun in Sumerian mythology and, under his Akkadian name Shamash, in Babylonian and Assyrian religion. Beyond his solar character, Utu was revered as the god of justice, morality and truth, the divine judge who sees everything that happens in the daylight and who upholds law and right order. His principal places of worship were the E-babbar temples at Sippar and Larsa.

Family and character

In Sumerian tradition Utu was the son of the moon god Nanna and his wife Ningal, and the twin brother of Inanna (Ishtar), goddess of love and war; he was thus the grandson of Enlil. His wife was the dawn goddess Aya (in Sumerian, Sherida). Utu was believed to emerge each morning from the doors of heaven in the east, travel across the sky in his chariot — driven by his charioteer Bunene — and descend in the west, passing through the underworld at night, where he could judge the dead. Because nothing under the sun escaped his sight, he was invoked as the patron of divination, the protector of travellers and herdsmen, and the god before whom oaths were sworn and legal decisions rendered.

Myths

Utu appears in numerous Mesopotamian myths. In the Sumerian poem of Dumuzid's dream he protects his brother-in-law Dumuzid from demons. In the Epic of Gilgamesh he aids Gilgamesh and Enkidu against the monster Humbaba in the Cedar Forest, and the hero Shamhat's king Shamash also assists the Babylonian flood hero and is invoked at the opening of the Code of Hammurabi, whose prologue declares that Hammurabi received his laws so that "justice might shine in the land" under the authority of Shamash, depicted at the top of the stele handing the king the symbols of rule. Utu also helps the shepherd Etana and figures in the descent narratives, bringing life-giving water and plants.

Worship

Utu's main sanctuaries were the E-babbar ("Shining House") temples in Sippar (in the north, associated with Shamash) and Larsa (in the south). Worship of the sun god is attested from the third millennium BCE and continued until the late Babylonian period, a span of more than two thousand years. Hymns to Utu, including the long Akkadian Great Hymn to Shamash, praise him as the overseer of the world whose light exposes wrongdoing and who cares for the poor, the widow and the oppressed. In the astral theology of later Mesopotamia, Shamash was paired with Sin (moon) and Ishtar (Venus) as a triad of celestial deities.

Iconography

Utu was depicted with rays or flames issuing from his shoulders, holding the rod-and-ring symbols and a distinctive saw-like knife (šaššaru) with which, in early Sumerian art, he cuts his way through the mountains at dawn. He is commonly shown between two gates of the eastern mountains, and on cylinder seals he receives worshippers presented before his throne.
