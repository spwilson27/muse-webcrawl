Inner product space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, an inner product space (or, rarely, a pre-Hilbert space in its general form) is a vector space endowed with an additional structure called an inner product: an operation that associates to each ordered pair of vectors a scalar, in such a way that geometric notions such as length, angle, and orthogonality can be defined. Inner product spaces generalize Euclidean spaces, in which the inner product is the dot product, to vector spaces of any (possibly infinite) dimension.

Definition

Let V be a vector space over the field F, where F is either the real numbers or the complex numbers. An inner product on V is a map ⟨·, ·⟩ : V × V → F satisfying, for all vectors x, y, z in V and all scalars s in F: conjugate symmetry (⟨x, y⟩ equals the complex conjugate of ⟨y, x⟩), linearity in the first argument, and positive-definiteness (⟨x, x⟩ ≥ 0, with equality only for x = 0).

Norm and metric

Every inner product induces a norm, defined by ||x|| = √⟨x, x⟩, and hence a metric d(x, y) = ||x − y||. An inner product space that is complete with respect to this metric is called a Hilbert space. The norm satisfies the parallelogram law, which characterizes those norms that arise from an inner product.

Orthogonality

Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero. Sets of pairwise orthogonal unit vectors are called orthonormal, and in finite dimensions the Gram–Schmidt process converts any linearly independent set into an orthonormal basis. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, |⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ||x|| ||y||, is fundamental to the theory and underlies the definition of the angle between vectors.

Examples

The archetypal example is Euclidean space with the dot product. Function spaces provide infinite-dimensional examples: the space L2 of square-integrable functions carries the inner product given by the integral of the product of one function with the complex conjugate of another, and the sequence space ℓ2 carries the analogous sum.
