Scuola Grande di San Rocco

The Scuola Grande di San Rocco is a building in Venice, Italy, in the sestiere of San Polo, famous above all for the cycle of more than sixty paintings by Tintoretto that covers its walls and ceilings. It was the seat of a lay confraternity (a scuola grande) dedicated to Saint Roch (San Rocco), patron saint invoked against plague, founded in 1478 and still in existence today.

The building

Construction began in 1517 to designs associated with Pietro Bon and continued under Sante Lombardo and Antonio Abbondi (lo Scarpagnino), who completed the richly decorated Renaissance façade in the 1540s–1550s. The building has two great halls — the Sala Terrena on the ground floor and the Sala Superiore above — plus the smaller Sala dell'Albergo, where the confraternity's officers met. A grand staircase links the floors. Next door stands the church of San Rocco, housing the saint's relics.

Tintoretto's cycle

In 1564 the scuola held a competition for a ceiling painting for the Sala dell'Albergo. Tintoretto outmanoeuvred his rivals by installing a finished work, Saint Roch in Glory, rather than a sketch, and offering it as a gift. Over the following two decades he filled the entire building with a vast biblical cycle: Old Testament scenes on the ceiling of the Sala Superiore, the life of Christ on its walls, scenes from the life of the Virgin in the Sala Terrena, and, in the Sala dell'Albergo, the monumental Crucifixion (1565), often considered his masterpiece. The English critic John Ruskin wrote of the cycle in terms of awe, and Henry James called the Crucifixion one of the greatest paintings in the world. The scuola also contains works by Titian and Palma il Giovane and an imposing carved treasury staircase.

Today

The confraternity remains active in charitable work, and the building is open to the public as a museum; it also hosts concerts, its acoustics and setting making it a noted venue for Baroque music in Venice.
