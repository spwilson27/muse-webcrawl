Anticholinergic

An anticholinergic agent (abbreviated AChEI in some contexts, also called an anticholinergic drug or antimuscarinic) is a substance that blocks the action of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at synapses in the central and peripheral nervous systems. These agents inhibit the parasympathetic nervous system by selectively blocking the binding of acetylcholine to its receptors in nerve cells.

Medical uses

Anticholinergics are used to treat a variety of conditions, including overactive bladder (oxybutynin, tolterodine), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma (ipratropium, tiotropium), gastrointestinal disorders (dicyclomine), motion sickness (scopolamine), Parkinson's disease (benztropine), and as premedication to reduce secretions before surgery. Atropine is used in emergencies to treat symptomatic bradycardia and as an antidote for organophosphate poisoning.

Side effects

Common adverse effects follow the classic mnemonic: dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, urinary retention, tachycardia, and confusion — sometimes summarized as "blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter, hot as a hare". Long-term cumulative anticholinergic burden has been associated in observational studies with increased risk of dementia in older adults.

Classes

Anticholinergics are divided into antimuscarinic agents, which act at muscarinic receptors, and antinicotinic agents, which act at nicotinic receptors and include neuromuscular blockers and ganglion blockers.
