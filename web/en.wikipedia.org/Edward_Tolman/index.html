Edward Chace Tolman (14 April 1886 – 19 November 1959) was an American psychologist and a founder of the cognitive branch of behaviourism. He is best known for his studies of learning in rats and for introducing the concept of the cognitive map.

Work

A professor at the University of California, Berkeley, for most of his career, Tolman argued in Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men (1932) that behaviour is goal-directed and cannot be reduced to chains of stimulus–response associations. His maze experiments demonstrated latent learning — rats formed mental maps of mazes without reinforcement — and his 1948 paper "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" became a landmark of cognitive psychology.

Loyalty oath controversy

Tolman led the faculty resistance to the University of California's anti-communist loyalty oath in 1949–1951; dismissed for refusing to sign, he was reinstated after a court ruling. Berkeley's psychology building was named Tolman Hall in his honour (demolished in 2019).
