Rosa Parks

Rosa Louise McCauley Parks (February 4, 1913 – October 24, 2005) was an African-American activist in the civil rights movement best known for her pivotal role in the Montgomery bus boycott. The United States Congress has described her as "the first lady of civil rights" and "the mother of the freedom movement".

Montgomery bus boycott

On December 1, 1955, in Montgomery, Alabama, Parks rejected bus driver James F. Blake's order to vacate a row of four seats in the "colored" section in favor of a white passenger, after the "white" section was filled. She was arrested for violating the city's segregation ordinance. Parks was not the first person to resist bus segregation, but the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP), where she served as secretary of the Montgomery chapter, believed she would be the best candidate to challenge the law in court.

Her arrest sparked the Montgomery bus boycott, a 381-day mass protest led in part by a young Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. The boycott ended in December 1956 after the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Browder v. Gayle that bus segregation was unconstitutional.

Later life and legacy

Parks suffered reprisals and moved to Detroit in 1957, where she worked for Congressman John Conyers. She continued activism throughout her life, co-founding the Rosa and Raymond Parks Institute for Self Development. After retiring, she wrote an autobiography and received many honours, including the Presidential Medal of Freedom (1996) and the Congressional Gold Medal (1999). Upon her death in 2005, she became the first woman to lie in honor in the Capitol Rotunda.
