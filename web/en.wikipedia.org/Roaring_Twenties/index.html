The Roaring Twenties, sometimes styled as the Roaring '20s, refers to the decade of the 1920s in Western society and culture. It was a period of economic prosperity with a distinctive cultural edge in the United States and Europe, particularly in major cities such as New York, Chicago, Paris, Berlin and London. In France the decade was called the années folles ("crazy years"), emphasising the era's social, artistic and cultural dynamism.

Culture

The decade saw the spread of jazz — hence its other name, the Jazz Age — the rise of radio, cinema, record sales and spectator sports, and a new mass consumer culture of automobiles, electrical appliances and advertising. Young women known as flappers bobbed their hair, wore short skirts and flouted Victorian social conventions, while the Harlem Renaissance brought African American literature and music to wide audiences. Charles Lindbergh's solo transatlantic flight of 1927 made him an international hero.

Prohibition and crime

The nationwide prohibition of alcohol in the United States (1920–1933) gave rise to illegal speakeasies and bootlegging empires run by organised-crime figures such as Al Capone, and a glamourised culture of defiance in cities.

End of the era

American prosperity rested partly on a speculative stock-market bubble. When the bubble burst in the Wall Street Crash of 1929, the decade ended abruptly and the world entered the Great Depression.
