The Hominidae, whose members are known as the great apes or hominids, are a taxonomic family of primates that includes eight extant species in four genera: Pongo (orangutans), Gorilla, Pan (chimpanzees and bonobos), and Homo, of which only modern humans remain. Numerous extinct species and genera, including Australopithecus, Paranthropus and Gigantopithecus, are also placed in the family.

Evolution and classification

Hominids diverged from the gibbons (family Hylobatidae) roughly 15–20 million years ago; orangutans split from the other great apes about 12–16 million years ago, gorillas about 8–9 million years ago, and the human and chimpanzee lineages about 6–7 million years ago. Historically, "hominid" referred only to humans and their extinct relatives, with the other great apes placed in Pongidae; genetic evidence of close relationship led to the current broader usage, with humans and their fossil kin termed hominins (tribe Hominini).

Characteristics

Great apes are large, tailless primates with relatively large brains, forward-facing eyes, opposable thumbs and complex social behaviour. They are capable of tool use, problem solving and, in some cases, elements of learned communication. Most are endangered or critically endangered due to habitat destruction, hunting and disease; only Homo sapiens is listed as Least Concern.
