Calculus (dental)

In dentistry, calculus or tartar is a form of hardened dental plaque that has been mineralized by the precipitation of mineral salts, mainly calcium phosphates from saliva and gingival crevicular fluid. Calculus deposits form on the teeth and can extend below the gumline, and their rough, porous surface harbours further plaque, contributing to gingivitis and periodontitis.

Types

Supragingival calculus forms above the gumline, most heavily on the lingual surfaces of the lower front teeth and the buccal surfaces of the upper molars, near the openings of the major salivary gland ducts. It is usually whitish-yellow and clay-like. Subgingival calculus forms below the gumline within periodontal pockets; it is denser, darker (often brown or black from blood pigments), and firmly attached.

Formation

Plaque begins to mineralize within about 24–72 hours of forming, and mature calculus develops over roughly two weeks. The mineral content — mainly hydroxyapatite, whitlockite, octacalcium phosphate, and brushite — makes calculus about 70–90% inorganic by weight.

Removal and prevention

Unlike plaque, calculus cannot be removed by brushing; it requires professional cleaning (scaling) with hand instruments or ultrasonic scalers. Regular brushing and flossing slow its formation, and anti-tartar toothpastes containing pyrophosphates can reduce supragingival deposits.
