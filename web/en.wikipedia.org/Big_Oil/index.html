Big Oil

Big Oil is a name used to describe the world's largest publicly traded oil and gas companies, also known as supermajors. The term, analogous to Big Tech and Big Tobacco, is often used pejoratively to describe the industry's economic and political influence, particularly in debates about climate change.

The supermajors

The group is usually taken to include BP, Chevron, ExxonMobil, Shell, TotalEnergies, and sometimes ConocoPhillips and Eni. These companies emerged from waves of consolidation in the late 1990s and early 2000s, including the mergers that created ExxonMobil and ChevronTexaco, and BP's acquisitions of Amoco and ARCO.

History

The forerunners of Big Oil were the "Seven Sisters" — the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company (later BP), Gulf Oil, Royal Dutch Shell, Standard Oil of California, Standard Oil of New Jersey (Exxon), Standard Oil of New York (Mobil), and Texaco — which dominated the global petroleum market from the 1940s to the 1970s, before nationalisations and the rise of OPEC shifted control to state-owned producers.

Criticism

Big Oil companies have been criticised for lobbying against climate policy, funding climate change denial, and the environmental damage of oil spills such as the Deepwater Horizon disaster. Defenders point to the industry's role in energy supply, employment, and investment in lower-carbon technologies, and the companies have increasingly adopted net-zero pledges covering their operations.
