ARM (stylised in lowercase as arm, previously an acronym for Advanced RISC Machines and originally Acorn RISC Machine) is a family of reduced instruction set computer (RISC) instruction set architectures for computer processors, configured for various environments. Arm Ltd. develops the architectures and licenses them to other companies, which design their own products that implement one or more of those architectures.

History

The first ARM design was developed at Acorn Computers in Cambridge, England, in the early 1980s; the ARM1 was delivered in 1985 and the ARM2 powered the Acorn Archimedes in 1987. In 1990 the division was spun out as Advanced RISC Machines Ltd, a joint venture between Acorn, Apple and VLSI Technology, in connection with developing the processor for Apple's Newton handheld. The ARM7TDMI became hugely successful in mobile phones in the late 1990s, and ARM-based chips came to dominate smartphones, tablets and embedded systems because of their high performance per watt.

Architecture

ARM processors follow a load–store architecture with a large uniform register file. The original 32-bit architecture (AArch32) provides 16 visible registers and conditional execution of most instructions. The 64-bit AArch64 state, introduced with ARMv8-A in 2011, provides 31 general-purpose registers, a dedicated stack pointer and zero register, and the A64 instruction set. Profiles include the A-profile for applications processors, R-profile for real-time systems, and M-profile for microcontrollers (Cortex-M).

Licensing

Unlike most processor vendors, Arm does not manufacture chips. It licenses processor designs (such as the Cortex and Neoverse core families) as intellectual property, and also offers architectural licences allowing companies such as Apple, Qualcomm and NVIDIA to design their own compatible CPUs. Cumulative shipments of ARM-based chips have surpassed 250 billion units.
