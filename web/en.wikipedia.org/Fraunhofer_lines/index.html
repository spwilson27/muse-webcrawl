Fraunhofer lines

The visible solar spectrum with Fraunhofer lines

The Fraunhofer lines are a set of spectral absorption lines named after the German physicist Joseph von Fraunhofer. They are dark lines visible in the optical spectrum of the Sun, produced by the absorption of specific wavelengths of light by gases in the solar atmosphere.

History

Dark lines in the solar spectrum were first noticed by William Hyde Wollaston in 1802. In 1814 Fraunhofer independently rediscovered them and systematically measured their positions, cataloguing some 570 lines and labeling the most prominent with the letters A through K — a notation still in use. Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen later explained the lines (around 1859) as absorption by chemical elements in the Sun's cooler outer layers, founding the science of spectroscopy and astrophysics.

Principal lines

  • C line — 656.3 nm, hydrogen alpha (Hα)
  • D lines — a sodium doublet near 589.0 and 589.6 nm
  • F line — 486.1 nm, hydrogen beta
  • G line — a blend of iron and calcium lines near 430.8 nm
  • H and K lines — ionized calcium at 396.8 and 393.4 nm
  • A and B bands — molecular oxygen in Earth's atmosphere

Applications

Because their wavelengths are precisely known, Fraunhofer lines serve as wavelength calibration standards in spectrometers. They also provide reference points for measuring stellar radial velocities via the Doppler effect and for determining the chemical composition of stars.
