Zellweger syndrome

SpecialtyMedical genetics
InheritanceAutosomal recessive
CauseMutations in PEX genes impairing peroxisome biogenesis
PrognosisUsually fatal in the first year of life
FrequencyAbout 1 in 50,000 births

Zellweger syndrome is a rare congenital disorder characterised by the absence or reduced function of peroxisomes in the cells of the body. It is the most severe of the Zellweger spectrum disorders, a group of peroxisome biogenesis disorders caused by mutations in any of the PEX genes. The condition is named after Hans Zellweger, the Swiss-American paediatrician who described it in the 1960s.

Signs and symptoms

Infants with Zellweger syndrome typically present at birth with profound hypotonia ("floppy baby"), poor feeding, seizures and distinctive craniofacial features including a high forehead, large fontanelle and hypertelorism. Liver dysfunction, cystic kidney disease, eye abnormalities such as cataracts and retinopathy, and neuronal migration defects of the brain are common.

Genetics and mechanism

The disorder follows an autosomal recessive pattern. Because functional peroxisomes are absent or severely reduced, cells cannot break down very-long-chain fatty acids or synthesise plasmalogens, leading to their accumulation and widespread organ damage.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on elevated levels of very-long-chain fatty acids in blood, imaging of the brain and kidneys, and genetic testing of the PEX genes. There is no cure; treatment is supportive. Most affected infants die within the first year of life.
