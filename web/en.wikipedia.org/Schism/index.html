Schism

A schism (pronounced SIZ-əm or, less commonly, SKIZ-əm) is a division between people, usually members of an organization, movement, or religious denomination. The word is most frequently applied to a split in what had previously been a single religious body, such as the Great East–West Schism or the Western Schism. It may also apply to a separation within a non-religious organization, such as a political party.

Etymology

The word comes from the Greek skhisma, meaning "cleft" or "division", from the verb skhizein, "to split". It entered English through Latin and Old French in the context of divisions within the Church.

In Christianity

In Christian theology, schism is distinguished from heresy: a schism is a breach of communion or unity without necessarily involving doctrinal error, whereas heresy is a denial of an essential doctrine. The most consequential Christian schisms include the East–West Schism of 1054, which divided the Chalcedonian Church into the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches, and the Western Schism (1378–1417), in which rival claimants to the papacy resided in Rome and Avignon. The Protestant Reformation of the 16th century produced a further series of lasting divisions in Western Christianity.

In other religions

Schisms have occurred in many traditions. In Islam, the early split between Sunni and Shia Islam originated in a dispute over succession to Muhammad. Judaism has seen divisions between Rabbanite and Karaite communities, and among modern denominations. Buddhism divided early into Theravada and Mahayana traditions, among others.
