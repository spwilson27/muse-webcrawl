Estonian literature

Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald, compiler of the national epic Kalevipoeg

Estonian literature is literature written in the Estonian language. Because written Estonian developed late under foreign rule, the corpus begins with religious texts of the Reformation era; a mature national literature emerged only in the nineteenth century, during the Estonian national awakening.

Early literature

The earliest known Estonian text is the Kullamaa prayers of the 1520s–30s; a catechism by Simon Wanradt and Johann Koell (1535) is the first printed Estonian book. Heinrich Stahl produced grammars and sermons in the seventeenth century, and a full Bible translation appeared in 1739. Kristjan Jaak Peterson (1801–1822), the first Estonian-language poet of note, celebrated the beauty of his mother tongue in the early nineteenth century.

National awakening

Friedrich Robert Faehlmann's mythological tales inspired Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald to compile the national epic Kalevipoeg (1857–1861), assembled from folk songs in the regivärss metre. Lydia Koidula became the era's foremost poet and a founder of Estonian theatre. The Young Estonia (Noor-Eesti) movement after 1905, led by Gustav Suits and the novelist and playwright Friedebert Tuglas, sought to Europeanise Estonian culture.

Twentieth century and after

The classic novel of independent Estonia is Anton Hansen Tammsaare's five-volume Truth and Justice (1926–1933). Soviet occupation brought socialist realism and drove many writers — among them Marie Under — into exile, while others, like Jaan Kross, wrote historical novels that evaded censorship through allegory. After 1991, writers such as Tõnu Õnnepalu, Andrus Kivirähk — whose novel The Man Who Spoke Snakish became an international success — and Sofi Oksanen (Finnish-Estonian) gained wide readership. Estonia maintains high reading and library-use rates, and its literature is promoted abroad by the Estonian Literature Centre.
