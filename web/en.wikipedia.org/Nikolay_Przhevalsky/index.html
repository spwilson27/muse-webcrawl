Nikolay Mikhaylovich Przhevalsky (Russian: Никола́й Миха́йлович Пржева́льский; April 12, 1839 – November 1, 1888) was a Russian geographer and explorer of Polish descent, renowned for his expeditions into Central and East Asia. Sponsored by the Imperial Russian Geographical Society, he travelled through Mongolia, Xinjiang, the Tibetan Plateau, and the Ussuri region, collecting vast scientific specimens and mapping territories largely unknown to Europeans. The wild horse known as Przewalski's horse, along with numerous other species, is named for him.

Nikolay Przhevalsky, explorer of Central Asia

Early life

Przhevalsky was born in the village of Kimborovo in Smolensk Governorate to a family of Polish noble descent. He studied at the military academy in St. Petersburg, entered the army, and pursued geography with intense self-discipline, eventually securing posting to Siberia so as to position himself for exploration.

Expeditions

From 1867 to 1869 he explored the Ussuri River region. Between 1870 and 1885 he led four major expeditions into Central Asia, crossing the Gobi Desert, reaching the northern fringes of the Tibetan Plateau, exploring the Tarim Basin and the sources of the Yellow River, and repeatedly attempting — but failing, due to Tibetan opposition — to reach Lhasa. His journeys covered some 30,000 kilometres and produced extensive zoological collections, including the first scientific description of the wild Bactrian camel and the wild horse later named Equus przewalskii.

Death and legacy

While setting out on a fifth expedition in 1888, Przhevalsky contracted typhoid, reportedly from drinking river water, and died at Karakol on the shore of Lake Issyk-Kul in present-day Kyrgyzstan. The town was renamed Przhevalsk in his honor (later reverting to Karakol). Though his ethnographic views reflected the imperial attitudes of his time, his scientific contributions to Central Asian geography and natural history were immense; his travel accounts were translated across Europe.
