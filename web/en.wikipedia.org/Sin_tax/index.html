Sin tax

A sin tax (also sumptuary tax) is a state-sponsored tax levied on goods or services considered harmful or undesirable, such as alcohol, tobacco, drugs, soft drinks, fast foods, coffee, sugar, gambling, and pornography. In contrast to Pigovian taxes, which are intended to internalize the negative externalities of production or consumption, sin taxes are a source of government revenue and a means of discouraging the taxed behaviour.

Rationale

Supporters argue that the consumption of alcohol and tobacco imposes social costs — such as publicly funded health care and lost productivity — that are not reflected in prices, and that taxation reduces consumption. Revenues are sometimes earmarked for health programmes, as with the Philippine Sin Tax Reform Law of 2012, which raised alcohol and tobacco taxes to fund universal health care.

Criticism

Critics note that sin taxes can be regressive, falling disproportionately on lower-income consumers, and may encourage smuggling and illicit trade. There is also debate over whether governments should use the tax code to shape personal morality, and sin taxes have historically been associated with moral campaigns such as Prohibition in the United States.
