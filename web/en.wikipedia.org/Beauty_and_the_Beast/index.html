Beauty and the Beast

Beauty and the Beast (French: La Belle et la Bête) is a fairy tale written by French novelist Gabrielle-Suzanne Barbot de Villeneuve and published in 1740 in La Jeune Américaine et les contes marins. Villeneuve's lengthy story was abridged, rewritten, and published by Jeanne-Marie Leprince de Beaumont in 1756 in Magasin des enfants to produce the version most commonly retold. Later, Andrew Lang retold the story in Blue Fairy Book, a part of the Fairy Book series, published in 1889. The fairy tale was influenced by Ancient Greek stories such as "Cupid and Psyche" from The Golden Ass, written by Lucius Apuleius Madaurensis in the second century AD, and "The Pig King", an Italian fairy-tale published by Giovanni Francesco Straparola in The Nights of Straparola around 1550.

Plot

A widowed merchant lives in a mansion with his six children, three sons and three daughters. All daughters are very beautiful, but the youngest, Beauty, is the most lovely, as well as kind, well-read, and pure of heart; her sisters, in contrast, are cruel, selfish, vain, and spoiled. The merchant eventually loses all of his wealth in a tempest at sea, and he and his children are consequently forced to live in a small farmhouse in a forest and work for their living. After some years of this, the merchant hears that one of the trade ships he had sent off has arrived back in port, having escaped the destruction of its compatriots; therefore, he returns to the city to discover whether it contains anything of value. Before leaving, he asks his children if they wish for him to bring any presents back for them. The sons ask for weaponry and horses to hunt with, whereas the oldest daughters ask for clothing, jewels, and fine dresses, as they think his wealth has returned. Beauty asks only for a rose, since no flower grows in the part of the country where they currently live.

When the merchant arrives in the port, to his dismay, his ship's cargo has been seized to pay his debts, leaving him penniless and unable to buy his children's presents. During his return, he becomes lost in a forest. Seeking shelter and food, he discovers a magical castle. The merchant enters and sees tables laden with food and drink, which have apparently been left for him by the castle's owner. The next morning, as the merchant is about to leave, he sees a rose garden and remembers that Beauty wanted a rose. Upon picking the loveliest rose he can find, the merchant is confronted by a hideous "Beast", who tells him that for stealing a rose, a gift more valuable than any, the merchant must give his life. The merchant begs to be set free, arguing that he picked the rose only as a present for his youngest daughter. The Beast agrees to let him go on the condition that he give the Beast his daughter.

Beauty agrees to go to the Beast's castle, sacrificing herself for her father. She discovers the Beast is not the monster she expected: he is courteous, provides her with every luxury, and each night asks her to marry him, which she gently refuses. She dreams of a handsome prince who begs her to answer why she keeps refusing him. Over time she grows fond of the Beast, but when she asks to visit her sick father, the Beast allows her one week, warning that if she does not return she will lose him forever. Her jealous sisters persuade her to stay longer, hoping to anger the Beast. Ashamed, Beauty uses a magic ring to return and finds the Beast dying of heartbreak in the rose garden. She weeps over him, declaring her love, and her tears break the curse: the Beast transforms into the handsome prince of her dreams. The prince explains he had been cursed by a fairy to take the form of a hideous beast until a maiden agreed to marry him despite his appearance. Beauty's sisters are turned into statues, while Beauty and the prince marry and live happily ever after.

Analysis

The tale is classified as Aarne–Thompson type 425C, "Beauty and the Beast", within the wider cycle of the animal as bridegroom. Scholars have traced its ancestry to Apuleius's "Cupid and Psyche" and to Straparola's "The Pig King". Villeneuve's original novella-length version included a backstory for the Beast and a subplot about Beauty's royal fairy parentage, which Beaumont removed in her widely circulated abridgement, written as a moral lesson for young girls.

Adaptations

The story has been adapted many times, including Jean Cocteau's 1946 French film La Belle et la Bête, Walt Disney Animation Studios' 1991 animated film Beauty and the Beast — the first animated film nominated for the Academy Award for Best Picture — and Disney's 2017 live-action remake. It has also inspired stage musicals, ballets, operas, and television series.
