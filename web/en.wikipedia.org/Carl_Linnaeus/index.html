Carl Linnaeus (23 May 1707 – 10 January 1778), known after his ennoblement in 1761 as Carl von Linné, was a Swedish botanist, zoologist, taxonomist and physician who formalised binomial nomenclature, the modern system of naming organisms. He is known as the "father of modern taxonomy" and is also considered one of the fathers of modern ecology.

Much of his writing was in Latin, and his name is rendered in Latin as Carolus Linnæus and, after his ennoblement, Carolus a Linné.

Early life and education

Linnaeus was born in the village of Råshult in Småland, the son of a Lutheran minister and keen gardener, Nils Ingemarsson Linnaeus, who passed on his love of plants. Intended for the priesthood, he showed little interest in it and was sent to study medicine instead, first at Lund and then at Uppsala, where he came under the patronage of the theologian-botanist Olof Celsius and lectured in the botanical garden while still a student. In 1732 the Uppsala Academy of Sciences funded his expedition to Lapland, which resulted in the Flora Lapponica. In 1735 he travelled to the Netherlands, took his medical degree at Harderwijk, and published the first edition of Systema Naturae — initially a pamphlet of just eleven pages.

The Linnaean system

Linnaeus classified nature in a hierarchy of kingdom, class, order, genus and species, and — his most lasting innovation — gave each species a stable two-word Latin name: a genus name plus a single specific epithet, replacing the long descriptive phrases of earlier authors. For plants he devised a "sexual system" of classification based on the number and arrangement of stamens and pistils — artificial, as he acknowledged, but brilliantly usable. The publication of Species Plantarum (1753) for plants and the tenth edition of Systema Naturae (1758) for animals mark the starting points of botanical and zoological nomenclature; in the latter he classified Homo sapiens among the primates, a bold step for the period.

Later career

Returning to Sweden, Linnaeus became professor of medicine and botany at Uppsala in 1741, rebuilt the university botanical garden, and was appointed physician to the royal family. His students — the "apostles of Linnaeus" — travelled the world collecting specimens, several dying on expedition. Ennobled in 1761, he bought the estate of Hammarby outside Uppsala, now a museum. He suffered a series of strokes from 1774 and died in 1778; he is buried in Uppsala Cathedral.

Legacy

The Linnaean hierarchy survives, much modified, at the heart of biological classification, and the binomial names he coined are still in universal use. His collections are held by the Linnean Society of London, founded in 1788. Later scholarship has also scrutinised the racial subdivisions of humans listed in his works, which contributed to the development of scientific racism, alongside his immense scientific legacy.
