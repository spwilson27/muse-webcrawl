Meditations on First Philosophy

Meditations on First Philosophy (Latin: Meditationes de Prima Philosophia) is a philosophical treatise by René Descartes first published in Latin in 1641. The French translation, authorized by Descartes himself, was published in 1647 as Méditations métaphysiques. The book is composed of six meditations, in which Descartes first discards all belief in things that are not absolutely certain, and then tries to establish what can be known for sure. It is one of the most influential works in the history of modern philosophy and the classic statement of Cartesian dualism.

The six meditations

  1. On what can be called into doubt — Descartes applies methodical doubt, suspecting even the senses and entertaining the hypothesis of an "evil demon" who deceives him about everything, including mathematics.
  2. On the nature of the human mind — The one thing that cannot be doubted is that he is doubting, and therefore thinking, and therefore existing: cogito ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"). The wax argument then contends the mind is better known than the body.
  3. On God, that he exists — From the idea of an infinite, perfect God in his mind, Descartes argues God must exist as its cause (the trademark argument), alongside a version of the causal argument.
  4. On truth and falsity — Error arises when the will judges beyond what the intellect clearly and distinctly perceives; a non-deceiving God guarantees that clear and distinct perceptions are true.
  5. On the essence of material things — Presents the ontological argument: existence belongs to God's essence as necessarily as properties belong to a triangle's essence.
  6. On the existence of material things and the real distinction between mind and body — Argues that material things probably exist, that the mind is really distinct from the body (mind–body dualism), yet intimately united with it in the human being.

Objections and Replies

The first edition was published together with six sets of Objections, solicited from prominent thinkers including Thomas Hobbes, Antoine Arnauld, and Pierre Gassendi, with Descartes's Replies — a supplement almost as famous as the Meditations themselves. A seventh set, by Pierre Bourdin, was added in the second edition (1642).

Influence

The Meditations founded modern epistemology's "foundationalist" project and set the agenda of the mind–body problem for centuries. Critics have long charged Descartes with circularity (the "Cartesian circle"): using clear and distinct perception to prove God, and God to guarantee clear and distinct perception.
