The Vulgate is a late-4th-century Latin translation of the Bible. It was largely the work of Saint Jerome, who, in 382, was commissioned by Pope Damasus I to revise the Vetus Latina Gospels then in use in Rome, on the basis of accepted Greek texts, and later translated most of the Old Testament directly from Hebrew. The Vulgate became progressively the Catholic Church's definitively acknowledged Latin Bible throughout the Middle Ages and remains influential in the Western Church's theology, liturgy, and vocabulary.

Authorship and composition

Jerome began by revising the Gospels (completed about 384) and subsequently, while living in Bethlehem, translated the books of the Hebrew Bible directly from Hebrew between roughly 390 and 405, a decision that was controversial in his day because the Septuagint was widely regarded as authoritative. The remaining books of the Vulgate derive from revised Old Latin versions by Jerome's contemporaries and successors. The name Vulgata ("commonly used version") was applied to the text in the later Middle Ages.

Transmission and influence

Over a thousand years of hand copying introduced textual variants; Alcuin of York and Theodulf of Orléans produced corrected recensions under Charlemagne, and the 13th-century "Paris Bible" standardized the chapter divisions still used today. The Vulgate was the first major book printed by Gutenberg (c. 1455). At the Council of Trent in 1546, the Catholic Church declared the Vulgate the authentic Latin text for public use; the Sixtine (1590) and Clementine (1592) editions attempted to provide an official corrected text. In 1979, the Nova Vulgata, a revision made with reference to modern critical editions of the Hebrew and Greek texts, became the Church's official Latin Bible.

Criticism and the Reformation

Renaissance humanists such as Lorenzo Valla and Erasmus identified translation errors in the Vulgate—most famously Jerome's rendering of the Greek metanoeite as poenitentiam agite ("do penance") rather than "repent"—which became points of dispute in the Reformation, as Protestant translators produced vernacular Bibles from the original languages. The Douay–Rheims Bible translated the Vulgate into English for Catholic readers.
