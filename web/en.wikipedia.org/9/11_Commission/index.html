The National Commission on Terrorist Attacks Upon the United States, commonly known as the 9/11 Commission, was an independent, bipartisan commission created by congressional legislation and signed into law by President George W. Bush in late 2002. Its mandate was to prepare a full account of the circumstances surrounding the September 11, 2001 attacks, including the United States' preparedness for and immediate response to them, and to provide recommendations to guard against future attacks.

Membership and work

The commission was chaired by former New Jersey Governor Thomas Kean, with former Representative Lee Hamilton as vice chair. It interviewed more than 1,200 people in 10 countries, reviewed more than 2.5 million pages of documents, and held 19 days of public hearings with testimony from current and former officials.

The 9/11 Commission Report

The commission issued its final report on 22 July 2004. The 567-page report became a surprise bestseller and a National Book Award finalist. It found failures of imagination, policy, capabilities and management across the government, and recommended a sweeping reorganization of the intelligence community, which led to the creation of the Director of National Intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center. The commission formally closed on 21 August 2004; its members later formed the 9/11 Public Discourse Project to press for adoption of its recommendations.
