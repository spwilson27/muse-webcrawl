Secondary surveillance radar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An SSR antenna (the flat bar) mounted above a primary surveillance radar.

Secondary surveillance radar (SSR) is a radar system used in aviation and air traffic control that, unlike primary radar, relies on targets equipped with a radar transponder, which responds to interrogation signals by transmitting encoded data such as the aircraft's identity and altitude. The system is based on the military identification friend or foe (IFF) technology developed during the Second World War.

Operation

The ground interrogator transmits pulses on 1030 MHz. The aircraft transponder replies on 1090 MHz with a coded pulse train. Depending on the mode of interrogation, the reply encodes a four-digit identity (squawk) code in Mode A, barometric altitude in Mode C, or, in Mode S, a unique 24-bit address and a wealth of additional data, with selective interrogation of individual aircraft. Because the reply is transmitted rather than reflected, SSR requires far less power than primary radar and provides positive identification.

Advantages and limitations

SSR gives controllers the aircraft's identity and flight level, which primary radar cannot. However, it only detects aircraft with operating transponders, and it suffers from problems such as garbling (overlapping replies), false replies unsynchronized in time (FRUIT), and over-interrogation. Mode S and modern monopulse SSR largely address these issues. Automatic dependent surveillance–broadcast (ADS-B), which derives position from GPS and broadcasts it, builds on the same 1090 MHz datalink and is progressively supplementing SSR.
