2012 phenomenon

The 2012 phenomenon was a range of eschatological beliefs that cataclysmic or transformative events would occur on or around 21 December 2012. The date marked the end of a 5,126-year cycle in the Mesoamerican Long Count calendar used by the Maya civilisation, and was interpreted by New Age writers and internet culture as the end of the world, a galactic alignment, a pole shift, or the beginning of a spiritual transformation. Mainstream scholars of Maya culture and astronomers rejected the apocalyptic interpretations.

A Maya Long Count inscription; the calendar's cycle rolled over on 21 December 2012.

Origins

The Long Count date 13.0.0.0.0 corresponds to 21 December 2012 (in the most widely accepted correlation), completing the thirteenth b'ak'tun. Maya inscriptions treat the date as the completion of a cycle rather than an ending of time; the only ancient text that may reference it, Tortuguero Monument 6, is partly eroded and ambiguous. The apocalyptic reading grew from 1970s–1990s New Age literature — notably José Argüelles's The Mayan Factor (1987) and the "Harmonic Convergence" — and from speculative books and websites connecting the date with a galactic alignment, the hypothetical planet Nibiru, geomagnetic reversal, or a solar maximum.

Scientific responses

NASA and other scientific bodies published extensive rebuttals: no planet was on a collision course, the Sun's activity was unexceptional, the "galactic alignment" is an annual, harmless occurrence, and magnetic reversals take thousands of years. Maya scholars, and modern Maya communities in Guatemala and Mexico, protested that their culture predicted no apocalypse; many treated the date as the start of a new era and an occasion for celebration.

Cultural impact

The phenomenon became a major media event, inspiring the Roland Emmerich disaster film 2012 (2009), which grossed over $790 million, as well as survivalist preparations, tourism to Maya sites and to "refuge" locations such as Bugarach in France, and official reassurance campaigns in several countries. The date passed without incident, and 21 December 2012 is now commonly cited as a case study in doomsday psychology and viral misinformation.
