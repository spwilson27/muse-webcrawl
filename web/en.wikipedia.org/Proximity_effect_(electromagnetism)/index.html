Proximity effect (electromagnetism)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In electromagnetics, proximity effect is the tendency for current to flow in loops or concentrated distributions, due to the presence of magnetic fields generated by nearby conductors. In conductors carrying alternating current (AC), the resistance of a conductor will increase due to eddy currents induced in nearby conductors, causing the overall circuit's Q factor to decrease, a phenomenon that becomes more pronounced at higher frequencies. The proximity effect is closely related to, but distinct from, the skin effect, in which a conductor's own magnetic field confines its current to a surface layer.

Explanation

A changing magnetic field from one conductor induces eddy currents in a neighboring conductor. These eddy currents add to the main current on one side of the conductor and subtract from it on the other side, redistributing the current. When two conductors carry current in opposite directions (as in the two wires of a transmission pair, or adjacent turns of a coil), the magnetic fields reinforce in the region between them, and current crowds toward the facing surfaces. When the currents are in the same direction, current is pushed toward the outer edges. In either case, the effective cross-section available for conduction shrinks, and the effective AC resistance rises above the DC resistance.

Consequences and mitigation

Proximity effect is a major source of loss in high-frequency magnetics: the windings of transformers, inductors, and switched-mode power supplies can exhibit AC resistances many times their DC values, reducing efficiency and causing heating. It is also significant in closely spaced bus bars and in the windings of electric machines. Mitigation techniques include:

  • Using Litz wire, made of many individually insulated, woven strands whose positions are transposed so each strand samples all positions in the bundle.
  • Using thin foil or multiple interleaved winding layers to control the field distribution.
  • Increasing the spacing between conductors, at the cost of size and leakage inductance.
  • Choosing conductor dimensions comparable to or smaller than the skin depth at the operating frequency.

The classic analysis of the effect in transformer windings is due to P. L. Dowell (1966), whose curves relate AC-to-DC resistance ratios to layer count and conductor thickness relative to skin depth; Dowell's method remains a standard design tool.
