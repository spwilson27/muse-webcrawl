Indicative mood

The indicative mood is a grammatical mood used in a clause to indicate that something is, or can be taken to be, a statement of fact. It is the mood of ordinary declarative sentences and questions about facts, and contrasts with moods that express doubt, wishes, commands, or counterfactual situations, such as the subjunctive, conditional, optative, and imperative. The indicative is the most common mood in the world's languages and in many languages is the only mood with a full set of tenses.

The indicative is the default mood for statements of fact.

Usage

In English, the indicative is used in sentences such as "He goes to school" and "Did she arrive?" English has no distinct indicative inflection beyond ordinary tense forms. In languages such as Spanish, French, and German, the indicative is morphologically rich and is systematically opposed to the subjunctive in subordinate clauses: "Es cierto que viene" (indicative, certainty) versus "Dudo que venga" (subjunctive, doubt).

Realis and irrealis

Linguists group the indicative among the realis moods, which present situations as actual or certain, as opposed to irrealis moods, which present them as potential, hypothetical, or desired. Some languages mark the indicative explicitly, while in others it is the unmarked default from which other moods are distinguished.
