CAPE ratio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The CAPE ratio (cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio), also known as the Shiller P/E, is a valuation measure for stock markets, defined as the price of a share index divided by the average of ten years of earnings, adjusted for inflation. By smoothing earnings over a full business cycle, it is intended to give a better picture of long-run value than the ordinary price-to-earnings ratio.

History

The measure was developed from the work of Benjamin Graham and David Dodd, and was popularised by economists John Y. Campbell and Robert Shiller, who showed in work presented to the Federal Reserve in 1996 — and in Shiller's book Irrational Exuberance (2000) — that high CAPE values have historically been followed by poor long-term real returns.

Interpretation

The long-run average CAPE for the US market is around 16–17. The ratio peaked near 44 in December 1999 during the dot-com bubble, the highest ever recorded, and also reached very high levels before the 1929 crash. Critics note that changes in accounting rules (notably the treatment of goodwill write-downs) and in payout policy (share buybacks replacing dividends) may make historical comparisons misleading, and that CAPE is a poor market-timing tool even if it says something about expected long-run returns.

Use

CAPE is calculated for many national markets and is used by investors for long-horizon asset allocation and for comparing valuations across countries.
