Spanish flu


An emergency influenza hospital in 1918

The Spanish flu, also known as the 1918 influenza pandemic, was a deadly global influenza pandemic caused by the H1N1 influenza A virus. Lasting from February 1918 to April 1920, it infected about a third of the world's population — an estimated 500 million people — and killed between 17 million and 50 million, with some estimates as high as 100 million, making it one of the deadliest pandemics in history.

Name and origin

The pandemic received its popular name because Spain, neutral in the First World War, had an uncensored press that freely reported the illness, creating the false impression that Spain was hardest hit. The geographical origin of the virus is debated; hypotheses have pointed to Haskell County, Kansas, where cases were recorded in early 1918, to the British army camp at Étaples in France, and to northern China. The virus was of avian origin and proved unusually lethal to healthy young adults, in part through cytokine storms that overwhelmed their immune systems.

Course of the pandemic

The pandemic came in waves: a mild first wave in spring 1918, a catastrophic second wave in the autumn of 1918 that spread along troop movements and shipping routes to every continent, and further waves in 1919 and 1920. Mortality showed an unusual W-shaped age curve, peaking among adults aged 20 to 40 as well as the very young and old. Cities that imposed early social distancing, such as St. Louis, fared better than those, like Philadelphia, that held mass gatherings.

Legacy

The pandemic transformed public health worldwide, spurred the creation of national health ministries and epidemiological services, and faded into descendants of the H1N1 virus that continued to circulate as seasonal flu. In 2005, scientists reconstructed the 1918 virus from preserved tissue samples, confirming its avian ancestry and exceptional virulence.
