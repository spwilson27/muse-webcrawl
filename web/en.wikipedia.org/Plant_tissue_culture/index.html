Plant tissue culture

Plant tissue culture is a collection of techniques used to maintain or grow plant cells, tissues, or organs under sterile conditions on a nutrient culture medium of known composition. The methods exploit totipotency — the ability of many plant cells to regenerate a whole plant — and are widely used in research, horticulture, and agriculture.

Techniques

A small piece of tissue, the explant, is surface-sterilised and placed on a medium such as Murashige and Skoog medium, supplemented with plant growth regulators. Auxins and cytokinins in different ratios direct the explant to form an undifferentiated callus, shoots, or roots. Related methods include micropropagation, embryo culture, protoplast culture, and meristem culture.

Applications

Tissue culture is used to mass-produce uniform clones of ornamentals, fruit crops, and forestry species; to produce disease-free planting material, particularly virus-free stock from meristems; to conserve rare and endangered species; and as a platform for genetic transformation and genome editing. Commercial micropropagation produces hundreds of millions of plantlets annually worldwide.

History

The field rests on the cell theory of Schleiden and Schwann and on Gottlieb Haberlandt's 1902 proposal that plant cells are totipotent; practical culture was achieved in the 1930s and 1940s, and the influential MS medium was published by Murashige and Skoog in 1962.
