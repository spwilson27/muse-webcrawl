The International Workingmen's Association (IWA), often called the First International, was an international organization founded in London on 28 September 1864 that aimed at uniting a variety of different left-wing socialist, communist, and anarchist political groups and trade union organizations based on the working class and class struggle. It is regarded as the first mass international organization of the labor movement.

Founding

The IWA was founded at a meeting at St Martin's Hall in London, organized in response to the suppression of the Polish January Uprising and to workers' solidarity with Poland and with the Union cause in the American Civil War. Karl Marx, then living in exile in London, was elected to its Provisional Central Council (later the General Council) and drafted its Inaugural Address and Provisional Rules, which declared that "the emancipation of the working classes must be conquered by the working classes themselves."

Activities and congresses

The International supported strikes across Europe, campaigned for the eight-hour day, and held congresses at Geneva (1866), Lausanne (1867), Brussels (1868), Basel (1869), and The Hague (1872). It grew to a claimed membership in the millions, though its organizational strength varied greatly by country, with strong sections in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Spain, and Italy.

Paris Commune and the Marx–Bakunin split

The Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune of 1871, which many International members supported and Marx defended in The Civil War in France, brought government repression across Europe. At the same time, an ideological and organizational struggle between Marx's supporters and the anarchist followers of Mikhail Bakunin split the movement. At the Hague Congress of 1872, Bakunin and James Guillaume were expelled, and the General Council was transferred to New York. The Marxist IWA was formally dissolved at Philadelphia in 1876, while the anarchist St. Imier International continued separately until 1877.

Legacy

The First International established the template of a coordinated world labor movement and was succeeded by the Second International in 1889. Its internal conflict between Marxists and anarchists shaped the development of both ideologies for generations.
