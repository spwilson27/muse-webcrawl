Cinéma vérité (French: [sinema veʁite]; "truthful cinema") is a style of documentary filmmaking developed by Edgar Morin and Jean Rouch, inspired by Dziga Vertov's theory of Kino-Pravda ("film truth"). It combines improvisation with the use of the camera to unveil truth or highlight subjects hidden behind crude reality.

It is sometimes called observational cinema, if understood as pure direct cinema: mainly without narration or commentary, and relying on the camera's recording of everyday life. The movement was made possible by new lightweight synchronous-sound recording equipment and portable 16mm cameras, which freed filmmakers from the studio and allowed intimate filming of real people in real situations.

Origins

The term translates Vertov's Kino-Pravda newsreels of the 1920s into French. Rouch and Morin's Chronique d'un été (Chronicle of a Summer, 1961), in which Parisians were asked "Are you happy?", is regarded as the movement's foundational work. Unlike the unobtrusive "fly-on-the-wall" approach of North American Direct Cinema, cinéma vérité embraced the presence of the camera and the filmmaker's interaction with subjects — Rouch described a "fly in the soup" — believing the camera's provocation could reveal truths that observation alone could not.

Techniques and influence

Characteristic techniques include handheld shooting, natural light, direct sound, interviews conducted on camera, and the film-maker's willingness to be seen and heard. The vérité approach profoundly influenced documentary practice, television journalism, and fiction filmmaking, and its aesthetics live on in reality television and the found-footage style.

Criticism

Critics questioned whether provoked behaviour could be called truth, and whether the presence of the camera necessarily distorts the reality it records. The debate between vérité's participatory method and Direct Cinema's observational ideal defined documentary theory for decades.
