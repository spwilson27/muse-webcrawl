Causality (physics)

Causality is the relationship between causes and effects. In physics, the principle of causality requires that an effect cannot occur before its cause, and — in relativistic physics — that no signal or influence can travel faster than the speed of light in vacuum. Causal structure is a central feature of the theories of special and general relativity.

Relativity

In special relativity, events that are separated by a spacelike interval cannot influence one another, because doing so would require superluminal communication and would allow the temporal order of cause and effect to be reversed in some reference frames. The set of events reachable from a given event at or below light speed forms its future light cone; causal relations between timelike-separated events are the same for all observers.

Quantum physics

Quantum mechanics complicates the picture: entangled particles display correlations that Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen found paradoxical. However, the no-cloning and no-communication theorems show that entanglement cannot be used to send information faster than light, preserving relativistic causality. In quantum field theory, causality is expressed by requiring that observables at spacelike separation commute (microcausality).

Causality and time

General relativity permits exotic solutions containing closed timelike curves — paths that return to their own past — in rotating universes and around certain massive objects. Whether such solutions are physically realizable is constrained by Hawking's chronology protection conjecture, which suggests that the laws of physics prevent time travel to the past.
