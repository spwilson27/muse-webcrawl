The Diamond Sutra (Sanskrit: Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra, "The Perfection of Wisdom Text that Cuts Like a Thunderbolt/Diamond") is a Mahayana Buddhist sutra from the Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom") genre. Composed in Sanskrit, probably in the early centuries CE, it takes the form of a dialogue between the Buddha and his disciple Subhūti at the Jetavana monastery. It is one of the most influential texts of East Asian Buddhism, central to the Chan/Zen tradition, and a printed copy dated 868 CE — recovered from the Dunhuang caves — is the world's earliest dated printed book.

Content

The Buddha teaches how a bodhisattva should practise generosity and compassion without attachment: one should give without dwelling on any notion of a giver, a gift or a recipient. Again and again the sutra deploys paradoxical formulations — "What the Tathāgata calls X is not X; therefore it is called X" — to dissolve attachment to fixed concepts, including the concepts of a self, a being, and even the Dharma itself, which is compared to a raft to be abandoned once the shore is reached. The sutra insists that all conditioned phenomena are empty of inherent existence, closing with the celebrated verse:

"Thus shall you think of this fleeting world: / A star at dawn, a bubble in a stream, / A flash of lightning in a summer cloud, / A flickering lamp, a phantom, and a dream."

The title's metaphor suggests wisdom that, like a diamond or thunderbolt, cuts through illusion and delusion.

Translations and transmission

The sutra was translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva in 401 CE, and several further translations followed, including those of Bodhiruci, Paramārtha, Xuanzang and Yijing. It became immensely popular in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam; the Sixth Chan Patriarch Huineng is said to have awakened on hearing a line from it: "Let the mind arise without abiding anywhere." In Tibet it is recited as the "rdor gcod" and remains a staple of monastic study.

The Dunhuang printed copy

In 1907 the archaeologist Aurel Stein acquired, from the sealed Library Cave (Cave 17) at Mogao near Dunhuang, a complete woodblock-printed scroll of the sutra bearing a colophon dated to 11 May 868 CE (Xiántōng 9), stating that one Wang Jie had it made "for universal free distribution" to honour his parents. Printed with a finely carved frontispiece of the Buddha preaching to Subhūti, it is the earliest surviving printed book bearing a precise date. It is now held by the British Library.
