Orphan work

An orphan work is a copyright-protected work whose author or rights holder cannot be identified or located, making it impossible to obtain permission for reuse. The problem affects large proportions of library, archive and museum collections — photographs, films, letters and out-of-print books whose owners are unknown — and it blocks digitisation and public access because institutions fear infringement liability if an owner later appears.

Causes

Works become orphaned because copyright lasts for many decades (typically the author's life plus 70 years), formalities such as registration and renewal were abolished under the Berne Convention, works are often published anonymously or by defunct companies, and ownership records are incomplete.

Legislative responses

Canada has operated a licensing scheme through its Copyright Board since 1990. The European Union adopted the Orphan Works Directive (2012/28/EU), allowing cultural heritage institutions to digitise and display orphan works after a diligent search. In the United States, the Copyright Office recommended legislation in 2006 and the Shawn Bentley Orphan Works Act passed the Senate in 2008 but was never enacted; Google Books litigation partly turned on the treatment of orphan books. Policy proposals generally combine a diligent-search requirement, registries of searches, and limits on remedies if an owner reappears.
