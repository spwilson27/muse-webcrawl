Self-categorization theory

Self-categorization theory is a theory in social psychology that describes the circumstances under which a person will perceive collections of people (including themselves) as a group, as well as the consequences of perceiving people in group terms. Although the theory is often presented as an explanation of psychological group formation (which was one of its early goals), it is more accurately thought of as general analysis of the functioning of the categorization process in social perception and interaction that speaks to issues of individual identity as much as group phenomena.

It was developed by John Turner and colleagues, and along with social identity theory it is a constituent part of the social identity approach. The theory was originally developed in the 1980s and is used mainly to explain such phenomena as social influence, group polarization, crowd behaviour and stereotyping.

Overview

Self-categorization theory argues that the self is not a fixed entity but is defined at different levels of abstraction in different contexts. Turner proposed three levels of self-categorization: the superordinate level of the self as a human being (human identity), the intermediate level of the self as a member of a social group as distinct from other social groups (social identity), and the subordinate level of personal self-categorizations based on interpersonal comparisons (personal identity).

Depersonalization

A central concept is depersonalization, which refers not to a loss of identity but to a redefinition of the self at a more inclusive level of categorization. When a social identity becomes salient, people perceive themselves less as differing individuals and more as interchangeable exemplars of a group prototype. This process underlies group phenomena such as conformity to group norms and ethnocentrism.

Referent informational influence

The theory explains social influence through referent informational influence: people conform to the norms of a group with which they share a self-categorization because those norms provide information about appropriate behaviour in context. This contrasted with earlier accounts based on normative and informational influence under conditions of dependence.

Applications

Self-categorization theory has been applied to topics including leadership, group decision-making, crowd psychology, and the study of stereotypes, where stereotyping is understood as perceiving individuals through the lens of salient group categories.
