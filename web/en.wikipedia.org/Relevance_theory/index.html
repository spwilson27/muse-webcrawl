Relevance theory

Sperber and Wilson's Relevance (1986) founded the theory.

Relevance theory is a framework for the study of cognition and communication, developed by Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson, principally in their book Relevance: Communication and Cognition (1986; 2nd ed. 1995). It builds on Paul Grice's theory of implicature but replaces his maxims with a single cognitive principle grounded in the trade-off between processing effort and cognitive effects.

Core claims

An input is relevant to an individual to the extent that the positive cognitive effects it yields (new conclusions, strengthened or overturned assumptions) are large, and the processing effort required is small. The Cognitive Principle of Relevance states that human cognition tends to be geared to maximising relevance. The Communicative Principle of Relevance states that every utterance conveys a presumption of its own optimal relevance: it is worth the hearer's attention, and is the most relevant the speaker could produce compatibly with their abilities and preferences.

Comprehension procedure

Hearers are predicted to follow a path of least effort in computing interpretations — testing hypotheses in order of accessibility and stopping at the first interpretation that satisfies their expectations of relevance. The theory gives a unified account of decoding and inference, metaphor, irony, and explicit content ("explicature"), and underpins work on lexical pragmatics. It is deliberately a psychological theory: it claims communication exploits a general relevance-oriented comprehension system, arguably rooted in theory of mind.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether "relevance" is defined with enough precision to be falsifiable, and whether a single principle can replace Gricean social norms of conversation.
