High Gothic

High Gothic is a period of Gothic architecture and art, conventionally dated from about 1195 to 1250 in France, following Early Gothic and preceding the Rayonnant style. It is characterised by the harmonious proportions, great height, and large windows of the great cathedrals built in northern France in the first half of the thirteenth century.

Characteristics

High Gothic cathedrals such as Chartres (rebuilt from 1194), Reims (begun 1211) and Amiens (begun 1220) employed ribbed vaults, pointed arches and flying buttresses to open the walls to expansive stained glass, including enormous rose windows. The style favoured unified interior spaces, three-level elevations of arcade, triforium and clerestory, and bar tracery in the windows.

Spread and legacy

From France, High Gothic spread across Europe, influencing cathedral building in England, the Holy Roman Empire, Spain and Italy, where it was adapted to local materials and traditions. It is often regarded as the classical phase of Gothic architecture, in which the structural innovations of the twelfth century were brought into balance.
