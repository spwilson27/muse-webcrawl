Salvia divinorum

KingdomPlantae
FamilyLamiaceae
GenusSalvia
SpeciesS. divinorum
Binomial nameSalvia divinorum Epling & Játiva
Active compoundSalvinorin A

Salvia divinorum is a plant in the sage genus native to the Sierra Mazateca of Oaxaca, Mexico, where it has long been used by Mazatec shamans in traditional spiritual and healing ceremonies. It contains salvinorin A, one of the most potent naturally occurring psychoactive compounds known, which acts as a kappa-opioid receptor agonist — a mechanism unlike that of classical serotonergic psychedelics.

Botany and cultivation

The plant is a perennial herb of the mint family, growing over a metre tall with large, soft, serrated green leaves and, rarely, white-and-violet flowers. It seldom sets viable seed and is propagated almost entirely from cuttings, so cultivated plants worldwide descend from a small number of clones collected in the twentieth century. The species was formally described in 1962 by Carl Epling and Carlos Játiva from material gathered during fieldwork with the Mazatec.

Traditional use

Mazatec curanderos traditionally prepare the fresh leaves by chewing them as a quid or crushing them into a drink. The plant, known in Mazatec as ska María Pastora ("leaves of Mary the Shepherdess"), is used in divination, healing rituals and as a gentler introductory entheogen for apprentice shamans. Gordon Wasson and Albert Hofmann were among the first outsiders to document the practice in the early 1960s.

Pharmacology

Salvinorin A, isolated in the 1980s and structurally characterised in the 1990s, is a neoclerodane diterpene and the first non-nitrogenous compound known to produce powerful psychedelic effects. Smoked or vaporised extracts act within seconds and typically last only a few minutes, producing intense alterations in perception, body image and awareness; chewed leaf produces milder, longer effects. Researchers study salvinorin A and the kappa-opioid system for possible treatments of pain, addiction and mood disorders without the abuse profile of mu-opioid drugs.

Legal status

The plant's legal status varies widely: it is controlled in several countries and in a number of U.S. states, while remaining legal in others and at U.S. federal level. Concerns about recreational use of concentrated extracts in the 2000s prompted much of the legislation.
