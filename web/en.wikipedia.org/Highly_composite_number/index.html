Highly composite number

A highly composite number is a positive integer that has more divisors than any smaller positive integer. The concept was studied by Srinivasa Ramanujan in a celebrated 1915 paper, in which he investigated the maximal order of the divisor function d(n). The term itself was coined by Ramanujan, though related notions go back to Plato, who suggested 5040 as a good number of citizens for a city precisely because of its many divisors.

Properties

A highly composite number greater than 1 must have a prime factorisation in which the primes are the first k primes, each appearing at least once, and the exponents are non-increasing: writing n = 2a·3b·5c···, the exponents satisfy a ≥ b ≥ c ≥ ··· This follows because swapping a larger exponent onto a smaller prime, or using a smaller prime in place of a larger one, would produce a smaller number with the same number of divisors. Except for 4 and 36, every highly composite number ends in an even digit and has its largest exponent on the prime 2.

Highly composite numbers greater than 6 are also abundant numbers. Their practical convenience — many divisors make for easy subdivision — underlies traditional units such as 12 inches in a foot, 60 minutes in an hour, and 360 degrees in a circle.

Related concepts

Ramanujan also defined superior highly composite numbers, which maximise d(n)/nε for some ε>0; these include 2, 6, 12, 60, 120, 360, 2520, 5040 and 720720. A still stronger notion is the colossally abundant number. The asymptotic growth of the divisor function's maximum was bounded by Ramanujan and later refined using results related to the Riemann hypothesis.
