Aleut

The Aleuts (Aleut: Unangax̂) are an Indigenous people of the Aleutian Islands of Alaska and Russia's Kamchatka Krai and Commander Islands. The name "Aleut" was given by Russian fur traders in the 18th century. The Aleuts speak the Aleut language, which belongs to the Eskimo–Aleut language family.

Culture

Traditional Aleut society was organized in ranked villages, with hereditary chiefs (toyon), commoners, and slaves taken in warfare. Subsistence centred on the sea: sea mammals, fish, seabirds, and shellfish were hunted and gathered, supplemented by berries and plants. The baidarka (kayak), sewn gut-skin parkas, bentwood hats, and finely woven grass baskets are hallmarks of Aleut material culture.

History

Russian contact began in 1741 with the expedition of Vitus Bering. The fur trade in sea otters brought violent exploitation and disease, drastically reducing the Aleut population. Under Russian rule many Aleuts adopted Orthodox Christianity, which remains influential. During the Second World War, Aleut communities in the western Aleutians were evacuated by the United States government to camps in southeast Alaska, where many died; the Aleut Restitution Act of 1988 later provided compensation.

Modern era

Under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971, Aleuts are represented by the Aleut Corporation, one of thirteen Alaska Native regional corporations. Population estimates for people of Aleut descent range from roughly 7,000 to 18,000.
