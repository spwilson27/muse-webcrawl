Gamelan is the traditional ensemble music of the Javanese, Sundanese and Balinese peoples of Indonesia, made up predominantly of percussive instruments. The most common instruments are metallophones (such as saron, gender and slenthem) played by mallets, a set of hand-tuned drums called kendang that keep the beat, hanging gongs (kempul, gong ageng), and bowed and plucked strings and flutes. The ensemble is conducted, in effect, by the rhythmic patterns of the kendang player.

History

Gamelan originated in the courts of Java, with evidence of gong-chime ensembles dating to the Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms of the first millennium CE; reliefs on the 9th-century Borobudur temple depict early forms of the instruments. The music developed in the royal courts of Yogyakarta and Surakarta in Central Java and in the villages of Bali, each with distinct styles: Javanese gamelan tends to be softer and more contemplative, while Balinese gamelan is faster, brighter and more dynamic.

Tuning and structure

Gamelan uses two tuning systems: sléndro, a five-tone scale of roughly equidistant intervals, and pélog, a seven-tone scale from which subsets of five notes are drawn. Each ensemble is tuned uniquely, so instruments from different gamelans are not interchangeable. Music is built on cyclic structures: a core melody (balungan) is elaborated by higher instruments and punctuated by gongs at regular intervals, creating nested cycles (gongan) that can last from seconds to many minutes.

Cultural context

Gamelan accompanies dance, puppet theatre (wayang kulit), rituals and ceremonies. In 2021, gamelan was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The tradition has influenced Western composers including Claude Debussy, who heard a Javanese gamelan at the 1889 Paris Exposition, and later Steve Reich, Philip Glass and Lou Harrison; gamelan ensembles are taught at many universities outside Indonesia.
