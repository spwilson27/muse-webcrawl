Bletchley Park

Bletchley Park is an English country house and estate in Bletchley, Milton Keynes (Buckinghamshire), that became the principal centre of Allied code-breaking during the Second World War. The mansion was constructed following a series of additions during the Victorian era. During the war, the estate housed the Government Code and Cypher School (GC&CS), which regularly penetrated the secret communications of the Axis powers — most importantly the German Enigma and Lorenz ciphers.

Code-breaking

Working at Bletchley were mathematicians and cryptanalysts including Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman, Dilly Knox, and Joan Clarke. The team developed the Bombe, an electromechanical machine that sped the daily solution of Enigma settings, and Colossus, the world's first programmable electronic digital computer, built by Tommy Flowers to break the Lorenz cipher used by the German high command. Intelligence produced at Bletchley, codenamed Ultra, is credited with shortening the war by as much as two years.

The estate

The site was purchased in 1938 by Admiral Sir Hugh Sinclair, head of the Secret Intelligence Service. Staff grew from about 200 in 1939 to roughly 9,000 by 1945, three-quarters of them women, many serving in the Women's Royal Naval Service. Work was conducted in a series of wooden huts and later concrete blocks around the mansion.

Legacy

After decades of secrecy, Bletchley Park was opened as a museum in 1993 and is now managed by the Bletchley Park Trust. The estate also hosts The National Museum of Computing. Its story has been popularized by books and films such as The Imitation Game (2014).
