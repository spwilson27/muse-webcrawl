A language family is a group of languages related through descent from a common ancestral language or parental language, called the proto-language of that family. Members of a family are shown to be related by systematic correspondences in vocabulary, grammar, and sound patterns, established through the comparative method of historical linguistics. The term "family" reflects the model of descent with modification borrowed from evolutionary biology, conventionally visualised as a family tree (Stammbaum).

Structure

Families are subdivided hierarchically into branches, groups, and subgroups: for example, the Indo-European family contains the Germanic branch, which contains the West Germanic group including English and German. Relationships more distant than about 8,000–10,000 years of divergence become very difficult to demonstrate reliably, which is why proposals for "macro-families" such as Nostratic remain controversial. Languages with no demonstrated relatives, such as Basque, Korean (in some classifications), and Japanese (in some classifications), are called language isolates.

Major families

By number of native speakers the largest families are Indo-European (nearly half of humanity) and Sino-Tibetan, followed by Niger-Congo — the largest by number of distinct languages — Afro-Asiatic, Austronesian, and Trans–New Guinea. The world's roughly 7,000 living languages are grouped into well over a hundred families, concentrated especially in regions of high linguistic diversity such as New Guinea and the Caucasus.
