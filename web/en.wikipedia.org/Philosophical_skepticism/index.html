Core questionWhether knowledge is possible

Philosophical skepticism is a family of views that question the possibility of knowledge or justified belief, especially regarding areas such as the external world, other minds, the past, or morality. It differs from ordinary doubt and from scientific skepticism: the philosophical skeptic challenges our entitlement to claim knowledge at all, typically by presenting arguments that seem to undermine it.

Ancient skepticism

Greek skepticism took two main forms. Pyrrhonism, founded by Pyrrho of Elis and later systematized by Sextus Empiricus, prescribed suspension of judgment (epoché) about all non-evident matters, aimed at achieving tranquillity (ataraxia). The Academic skeptics of Plato's Academy, such as Arcesilaus and Carneades, argued that certainty is unattainable and that one should assent only to the plausible.

Skeptical arguments

Classic skeptical devices include Agrippa's trilemma (every justification ends in infinite regress, circularity, or an arbitrary stopping point), the problem of the criterion, dream and evil-demon scenarios, and brain-in-a-vat thought experiments. René Descartes famously used methodical doubt not to endorse skepticism but to find an indubitable foundation, arriving at cogito ergo sum. David Hume's skepticism about induction and causation reshaped epistemology and prompted Kant's critical philosophy.

Responses

Responses range from foundationalism and reliabilism to G. E. Moore's appeal to common sense and Wittgenstein's argument, in On Certainty, that doubt itself presupposes a background of undoubted commitments. Skepticism remains a standard benchmark: theories of knowledge are commonly evaluated by how well they answer it.
