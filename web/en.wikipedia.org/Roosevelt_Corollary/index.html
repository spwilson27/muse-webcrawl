Roosevelt Corollary

The Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine was articulated by United States President Theodore Roosevelt in his annual message to Congress on December 6, 1904. It stated that the United States would intervene in Latin American countries in cases of "flagrant and chronic wrongdoing" in order to pre-empt intervention by European powers, and it became a principal justification for United States intervention in the Caribbean and Central America in the early twentieth century.

Background

The Monroe Doctrine of 1823 had warned European powers against further colonisation or interference in the Americas, but it did not say what the United States would do if Latin American states defaulted on debts owed to Europeans. In 1902–1903, Britain, Germany, and Italy blockaded Venezuela to force repayment of debts, an episode that alarmed Washington: Roosevelt feared European powers might use debt collection as a pretext for territorial acquisition in the hemisphere, especially near the planned Panama Canal.

Content

Roosevelt argued that "chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society" in a Western Hemisphere nation might ultimately require intervention by "some civilized nation," and that in the Americas that role fell to the United States, "however reluctantly." The corollary thus transformed the Monroe Doctrine from a prohibition on European intervention into an assertion of a United States right — even a duty — to act as an "international police power" in the region.

Application

The corollary was invoked to justify numerous interventions. In 1905 the United States took over the customs houses of the Dominican Republic to guarantee debt repayment. Later administrations intervened in or occupied Nicaragua (1912–1933), Haiti (1915–1934), and the Dominican Republic (1916–1924). Roosevelt himself insisted the policy was defensive, intended to keep Europeans out rather than to acquire territory.

Repudiation

The corollary was formally softened by the Clark Memorandum of 1928, drafted under Secretary of State J. Reuben Clark, which concluded that the Monroe Doctrine did not justify unilateral United States intervention. President Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy of the 1930s further renounced armed intervention in Latin American affairs.
