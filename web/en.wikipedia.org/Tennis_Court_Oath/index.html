Tennis Court Oath

The Tennis Court Oath (French: Serment du Jeu de Paume) was a commitment to a national constitution taken on 20 June 1789 by members of the French Third Estate during the early stages of the French Revolution. Finding themselves locked out of their usual meeting hall at Versailles, the deputies gathered in a nearby indoor tennis court (a jeu de paume court) and swore not to separate until they had given France a constitution.

Background

The Estates-General of 1789 had deadlocked over whether voting would proceed by order or by head. On 17 June the Third Estate, joined by some clergy, declared itself the National Assembly, claiming to represent the nation. On the morning of 20 June the deputies found their hall, the Salle des Menus-Plaisirs, closed and guarded — officially for preparations for a royal session, but widely interpreted as an attempt to dissolve the Assembly.

The oath

Led by Jean Joseph Mounier's proposal and presided over by Jean-Sylvain Bailly, the deputies moved to the nearby royal tennis court and swore "never to separate, and to assemble wherever circumstances require, until the constitution of the kingdom is established." The oath was signed by 576 of the 577 deputies present; the single refusal was by Joseph Martin-Dauch, who wrote "opponent" beside his name. Honoré Gabriel Riqueti, comte de Mirabeau, was among the prominent participants.

Aftermath

At the royal session of 23 June, Louis XVI ordered the estates to deliberate separately, but the Assembly refused to disperse — Mirabeau reportedly declared they would yield only to bayonets. Within days, most of the clergy and many nobles joined the Assembly, and on 27 June the king conceded, ordering the remaining deputies to join. The Assembly became the National Constituent Assembly on 9 July. The event was later celebrated as a founding moment of the Revolution and immortalised in Jacques-Louis David's famous unfinished drawing The Tennis Court Oath.
