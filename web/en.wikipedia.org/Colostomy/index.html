Colostomy

A colostomy is a surgical procedure in which a stoma is formed by drawing the healthy end of the large intestine (colon) through an incision in the abdominal wall and suturing it into place. The opening diverts faeces into an external pouch (colostomy bag) worn over the stoma. A colostomy may be temporary, allowing the bowel downstream to rest and heal, or permanent, most often after removal of the rectum.

Indications

Colostomies are performed for colorectal cancer, diverticulitis with perforation, inflammatory bowel disease, bowel obstruction, traumatic injury, congenital abnormalities such as imperforate anus, and to protect a downstream surgical anastomosis. Depending on the segment of colon brought to the surface, colostomies are described as ascending, transverse, descending or sigmoid; an end colostomy has a single opening, while a loop colostomy brings a loop of bowel to the surface with two openings.

Care and complications

Patients are taught stoma care by specialist nurses: emptying and changing the appliance, skin protection and dietary adjustment. Modern pouches are odour-proof and discreet, and most patients return to normal activities. Complications include parastomal hernia, prolapse or retraction of the stoma, skin irritation and, rarely, ischaemia. Temporary colostomies can often be reversed ("taken down") in a later operation once the bowel has healed.
