Paul the Apostle (c. 5 – c. 64/67 AD), commonly known as Saint Paul and also by his Hebrew name Saul of Tarsus, was a Christian apostle who spread the teachings of Jesus in the first-century world. He is generally considered one of the most important figures of the Apostolic Age and is credited with founding several Christian communities in Asia Minor and Europe between the mid-40s and mid-50s AD.

Life

Paul was born in Tarsus in Cilicia, a Roman citizen from a devout Jewish family. A Pharisee, he initially participated in the persecution of early disciples of Jesus, present at the stoning of Stephen. According to the Acts of the Apostles, while travelling to Damascus to arrest Christians, he experienced a vision of the risen Jesus and was struck blind for three days, an event known as the Conversion of Paul or the Damascus Road experience. After his sight was restored, he was baptized and began preaching that Jesus was the Messiah and the Son of God.

Missionary journeys

Paul undertook several missionary journeys across the eastern Mediterranean, travelling through Cyprus, Anatolia, Greece and eventually Rome. He preached primarily to Gentiles, arguing that converts to Christianity need not follow the full Mosaic Law, a position that brought him into conflict with some Jewish Christian leaders, including a public disagreement with Peter recorded in the Epistle to the Galatians. The Council of Jerusalem (c. 48–49 AD) addressed these questions of Gentile admission to the church.

Writings

Fourteen of the twenty-seven books of the New Testament have traditionally been attributed to Paul; modern scholarship generally regards seven as undisputed (Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon), with the authorship of others debated. These epistles are among the earliest surviving Christian documents and have deeply shaped Christian theology, including doctrines of salvation, justification by faith, and the church.

Death

Paul was arrested in Jerusalem and, exercising his right as a Roman citizen, appealed to Caesar and was taken to Rome. Tradition holds that he was executed there during the reign of Nero, around 64–67 AD, by beheading — a method reserved for Roman citizens — while some traditions associate his martyrdom with the Great Fire of Rome persecutions.
