The Aventine Hill (Latin: Collis Aventinus) is one of the seven hills on which ancient Rome was built. It is the southernmost of the hills, overlooking the Tiber and the Circus Maximus, and today forms part of the rione of Ripa.

Ancient history

In Rome's early history the Aventine stood outside the sacred boundary (pomerium) and was associated with the plebeians, who were said to have been settled there by King Ancus Marcius. It was the destination of the first secessio plebis in 494 BC, when the plebeians withdrew from the city in protest, and it remained a plebeian stronghold associated with the tribunes and the cult of Diana, whose Aventine temple was a federal sanctuary of the Latins. Gaius Gracchus made his last stand on the hill in 121 BC.

Sights

The modern Aventine is a quiet, affluent residential district known for the Orange Garden (Parco Savello) with its celebrated view over Rome, the basilica of Santa Sabina (a fifth-century church and headquarters of the Dominican Order), the church of Sant'Anselmo, and the famous "keyhole of the Knights of Malta" on the Piazza dei Cavalieri di Malta, through which St Peter's Basilica can be seen framed by hedges.
