Caduceus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The caduceus (Latin: cādūceus, from Greek: κηρύκειον kērū́keion, "herald's wand or staff") is the staff carried by Hermes in Greek mythology and consequently by Hermes Trismegistus in Greco-Egyptian mythology. It is a short staff entwined by two serpents, sometimes surmounted by wings. In Roman iconography it was often depicted being carried in the left hand of Mercury, the Roman counterpart of Hermes.

Origins and mythology

The caduceus was the herald's staff (kerykeion), a symbol of heralds and messengers, who were protected in their duties. Hermes was said to have received a golden wand from Apollo; in one myth he separated two fighting serpents with it, and they thereafter remained entwined around the staff in peace — an origin story that made the caduceus a symbol of negotiation, commerce and peace. As a symbol of Hermes/Mercury it came to be associated with trade, merchants, thieves, travellers, eloquence, and alchemy.

Use as a symbol of medicine

The caduceus is frequently used — particularly in North America — as a symbol of medicine and healthcare, a usage often described as mistaken, since in classical tradition the medical symbol is the Rod of Asclepius, a single-serpent staff with no wings. The caduceus entered medical use partly through its adoption by US military medical units in the early 20th century (the US Army Medical Corps adopted it in 1902) and through confusion in commercial publishing. Many medical organisations outside the US, and a substantial number within it, use the Rod of Asclepius instead; the debate between supporters of the two emblems has continued for over a century.
