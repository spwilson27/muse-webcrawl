Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) is the use of various technologies to control the temperature, humidity and purity of the air in an enclosed space. Its goal is to provide thermal comfort and acceptable indoor air quality. HVAC system design is a subdiscipline of mechanical engineering, based on the principles of thermodynamics, fluid mechanics and heat transfer.

Components

A typical HVAC system includes a heat source such as a furnace, boiler or heat pump; a cooling source such as an air conditioner or chiller operating on the vapour-compression refrigeration cycle; air handlers and ductwork to distribute conditioned air; and ventilation equipment that exchanges stale indoor air with fresh outdoor air, often through energy recovery ventilators. Controls range from simple thermostats to building automation systems that coordinate entire commercial complexes.

History

Modern air conditioning is usually credited to Willis Carrier, who in 1902 designed a system to control humidity for a printing plant, and founded the Carrier Corporation. Refrigeration and heating technologies developed through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and HVAC became widespread in homes, vehicles and commercial buildings after the Second World War, profoundly shaping architecture — including the feasibility of skyscrapers and settlement in hot climates.

Efficiency and environment

HVAC accounts for a large share of building energy use. Efficiency is rated by measures such as SEER for cooling and AFUE for furnaces. Interest in heat pumps, passive design and low-global-warming-potential refrigerants has grown as buildings' carbon footprints have come under scrutiny.
