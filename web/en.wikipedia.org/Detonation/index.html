Detonation is a type of combustion involving a supersonic exothermic front accelerating through a medium that eventually drives a shock wave propagating directly in front of it. Detonations propagate through a material at a velocity significantly higher than the speed of sound in that material, and produce matter at very high pressure and temperature. Detonation is the process characteristic of high explosives such as TNT, RDX and dynamite, and contrasts with deflagration, the slower, subsonic burning of propellants and pyrotechnics.

Mechanism

In a detonation, a shock wave compresses and heats the explosive material ahead of the reaction zone, triggering chemical decomposition almost instantaneously. The energy released sustains the shock, so the combined shock–reaction complex travels at a steady velocity — the detonation velocity — which is a property of the explosive and its density and confinement. For TNT the velocity of detonation is about 6,900 m/s; for RDX about 8,750 m/s.

The classic theory of D. L. Chapman (1899) and Émile Jouguet (1905) treats the detonation as a discontinuity and predicts its velocity from thermodynamics: the stable detonation propagates at the minimum velocity such that the products' flow is sonic relative to the wave (the Chapman–Jouguet point). The later ZND model (Zel'dovich, von Neumann, Döring) resolves the structure into a leading shock followed by a finite reaction zone.

Initiation and transition

Detonations may be initiated directly by a strong shock, as from a detonator or booster charge, or may arise through deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT), in which a burning material's flame accelerates in confinement until it transitions into a detonation. The sensitivity of an explosive to initiation is a critical safety and design parameter; primary explosives such as lead azide detonate from weak stimuli, while secondary explosives require a substantial shock.

Engine knock and pulsed detonation

In spark-ignition engines, "knock" or "pinging" is an abnormal combustion phenomenon sometimes loosely called detonation, in which the unburned end-gas auto-ignites, producing damaging pressure oscillations; engine designers suppress it with high-octane fuel and knock sensors. True detonative combustion is exploited deliberately in research devices such as pulse detonation engines and rotating detonation engines, which promise higher thermodynamic efficiency.

Effects

The destructive power of a detonation derives from its extremely rapid energy release and the resulting blast wave, together with fragmentation and thermal effects. Structures exposed to the immediate vicinity of a detonation may experience pressures of tens of gigapascals inside the explosive, falling off with distance according to blast scaling laws.
