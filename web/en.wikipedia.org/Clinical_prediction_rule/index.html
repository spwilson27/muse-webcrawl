Clinical prediction rule

A clinical prediction rule, also known as a clinical decision rule, is a clinical tool that quantifies the contributions of patient history, physical examination, and simple tests to estimate the probability of a diagnosis, prognosis, or likely response to treatment for an individual patient. Such rules are typically derived from research using multivariable statistical methods and are intended to supplement, rather than replace, clinical judgement.

Development and validation

Prediction rules are usually developed in three stages: derivation, in which candidate predictors are identified in a study cohort; validation, in which the rule is tested in new populations to check that its accuracy is maintained; and impact analysis, in which its use in practice is shown to change clinician behaviour and improve outcomes. Many published rules never progress beyond derivation and perform less well in new settings.

Examples

Well-known examples include the Ottawa ankle rules, which indicate when radiography is needed after ankle injury; the Centor criteria for streptococcal pharyngitis; the Wells scores for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism; the CHA2DS2-VASc score for stroke risk in atrial fibrillation; the CURB-65 score for pneumonia severity; and the Apgar score for newborn infants. Cardiovascular risk scores such as Framingham and QRISK guide preventive treatment with statins and antihypertensives.

Criticisms

Critics note the proliferation of competing and poorly validated scores, difficulties in applying them during brief consultations, and the risk that scores derived in one population may encode biases when applied in others. Electronic health records increasingly embed validated rules to calculate them automatically.
