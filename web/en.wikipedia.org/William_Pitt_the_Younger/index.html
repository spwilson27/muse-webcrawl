William Pitt the Younger

William Pitt (28 May 1759 – 23 January 1806), known as Pitt the Younger to distinguish him from his father, Pitt the Elder, was a British statesman and the youngest prime minister of Great Britain, taking office at 24 in 1783. He dominated British politics through the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars until his death in 1806.

Rise to power

The son of Pitt the Elder, young Pitt was educated at Cambridge and called to the bar before entering Parliament in 1781. A brilliant debater, he became Chancellor of the Exchequer in 1782 and, after the fall of the Fox–North coalition, prime minister in December 1783 at just 24 — mocked by opponents as "a kingdom trusted to a schoolboy", yet he held power for over 17 years in his first ministry.

Domestic policy

Pitt restored public finances after the American war through new taxes, a sinking fund, and measures against smuggling; the India Act 1784 reformed the East India Company's government. He introduced Britain's first income tax in 1799 to fund the war against France. Although early sympathetic to parliamentary reform, the French Revolution and war drove his government to repressive measures against radicalism (the "Reign of Terror" of Pitt's critics, including suspension of habeas corpus and the Treason and Seditious Meetings Acts).

War and union

From 1793 Britain fought Revolutionary France; Pitt organised successive coalitions and financed allies with subsidies ("Pitt's gold"). After the Irish Rebellion of 1798 he carried the Acts of Union 1800, uniting Great Britain and Ireland as the United Kingdom from 1 January 1801, but resigned when George III blocked Catholic emancipation. Returning to office in 1804, he formed the Third Coalition; he died in January 1806, weeks after news of the disaster at Austerlitz, his last words reportedly invoking his country.
