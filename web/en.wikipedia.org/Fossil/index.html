Fossil

TypesBody fossils, trace fossils, chemical fossils
FormationPermineralisation, casts and moulds, amber, freezing, carbonisation

A fossil (from Classical Latin fossilis, "obtained by digging") is any preserved remains, impression, or trace of any once-living thing from a past geological age. Examples include bones, shells, exoskeletons, stone imprints of animals or microbes, objects preserved in amber, hair, petrified wood, and DNA remnants. The totality of fossils on Earth is known as the fossil record.

Formation

Fossilisation is an exceptionally rare process: most organisms decay too quickly to be preserved. Preservation typically requires rapid burial in sediment, followed by permineralisation, in which mineral-laden water fills the pores of bone or wood, or replacement, in which the original material dissolves and is substituted by silica, calcite or pyrite. Trace fossils (ichnofossils) — burrows, footprints and coprolites — record behaviour rather than bodies. Exceptional deposits called Lagerstätten, such as the Burgess Shale and Solnhofen limestone, preserve soft tissues.

History of study

Fossils were interpreted variously as sports of nature or remains of Noah's Flood until the 17th–19th centuries, when Nicolas Steno, Georges Cuvier and Mary Anning established extinction and deep time. Fossils became central evidence for evolution in Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), and index fossils remain fundamental to stratigraphy and dating rock layers.

Uses

Beyond science, fossils guide the search for fossil fuels, reconstruct past climates, and feed a large commercial and amateur collecting trade. Famous fossil organisms include trilobites, ammonites, dinosaurs, and transitional forms such as Archaeopteryx and Tiktaalik.
