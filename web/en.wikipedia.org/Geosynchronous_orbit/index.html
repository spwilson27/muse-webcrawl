Geosynchronous orbit

A geosynchronous orbit (GSO) is an Earth-centred orbit with an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation period — one sidereal day, about 23 hours, 56 minutes, and 4 seconds. A satellite in such an orbit returns to the same position in the sky after each day. The required altitude is approximately 35,786 kilometres (22,236 miles) above Earth's equator.

Geostationary orbit

A geostationary orbit is a special case: a circular geosynchronous orbit lying in the equatorial plane with zero inclination. A satellite there appears motionless in the sky, fixed over one longitude, which makes it ideal for communications and weather observation because ground antennas can be aimed permanently. The concept was popularised by science fiction author Arthur C. Clarke in 1945, and the geostationary belt is sometimes called the Clarke Belt. The first geosynchronous satellite was Syncom 2 (1963), and the first geostationary communications satellite was Syncom 3 (1964).

Other variants and disposal

Inclined geosynchronous orbits trace a figure-eight (analemma) ground track; tundra orbits are highly elliptical geosynchronous orbits used for high-latitude coverage. At the end of their service lives, satellites are typically boosted into a supersynchronous "graveyard orbit" a few hundred kilometres above the belt to keep the operational region clear of debris. Slots in the belt are allocated by the International Telecommunication Union.
