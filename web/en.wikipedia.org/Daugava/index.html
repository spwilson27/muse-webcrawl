The Daugava (Latvian), known as the Western Dvina in Russia, is a large river rising in the Valdai Hills of western Russia and flowing through Russia, Belarus, and Latvia to the Gulf of Riga of the Baltic Sea. At 1,020 km it is one of the principal rivers of the eastern Baltic region, and Latvia's capital, Riga, stands near its mouth.

Course

From its source near the source of the Volga, the river flows generally westward and southwest through the Vitebsk region of Belarus — where it is called the Dzvina — then turns northwest into Latvia, passing Daugavpils and Jēkabpils before reaching the lowland plain of Riga and the Baltic.

History

The Daugava was a major trade artery from the Viking Age onward, forming part of the route "from the Varangians to the Greeks" linking Scandinavia with Constantinople. Riga was founded at its mouth in 1201, and the river carried timber, flax, and grain exports for the Hanseatic League and later the Russian Empire. The river also served as a front line during parts of the First World War, remembered in Latvian culture as the battles of the Daugava riflemen.

Energy and environment

Three hydroelectric stations — Pļaviņas (the largest in the Baltic states), Ķegums, and Rīgas HES — dam the Latvian stretch, supplying much of the country's electricity but blocking migratory fish such as salmon and sturgeon. Long-standing proposals for further dams, notably at Daugavpils, have been controversial on environmental and cultural grounds.
