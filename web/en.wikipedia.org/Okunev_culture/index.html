Okunev culture

The Okunev culture (Russian: Окуневская культура) is an Early Bronze Age archaeological culture of southern Siberia, centred on the Minusinsk Basin in modern Khakassia and southern Krasnoyarsk Krai, dated approximately to 2500–1800 BCE. It succeeded the Afanasievo culture in the region and is noted for its distinctive stone stelae carved with stylised anthropomorphic faces.

Okunev stelae, with their distinctive solar-faced figures, are emblematic of the culture.

Characteristics

Okunev burials were placed in rectangular stone cists within square or round enclosures of upright slabs, often reusing older carved stelae as construction material. Grave goods include pottery with incised geometric ornament, bone tools, copper and bronze items, and elaborate bone plaques. The economy combined stockbreeding with hunting and fishing. The carved stelae — depicting faces with three eyes, horns, and solar symbols — represent one of the earliest monumental art traditions of the Siberian steppe and have attracted speculation about early shamanic religion.

Population

Ancient DNA studies indicate that the Okunev population was of mixed ancestry, combining local Siberian hunter-gatherer heritage with components related to the earlier Afanasievo and to Ancient North Eurasian populations. Cranial studies long noted a blend of Europoid and Mongoloid features.
