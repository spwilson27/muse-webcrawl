Florida Keys

The Florida Keys are a coral cay archipelago in the southeastern United States, forming the southernmost part of the continental United States. The island chain begins at the southeastern coast of the Florida peninsula and arcs southwest through about 180 kilometres (110 miles) of islands to Key West, with the uninhabited Dry Tortugas beyond. The Keys are connected to the mainland by the Overseas Highway (U.S. Route 1), which crosses 42 bridges, including the Seven Mile Bridge.

Geography and geology

The Keys are the exposed remnants of ancient coral reefs (Key Largo Limestone) and sand bars that formed when sea levels were higher. They divide the Atlantic Ocean to the east from Florida Bay and the Gulf of Mexico to the west. Offshore lies the Florida Reef, the only living coral barrier reef in the continental United States, protected within the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary. The islands have a tropical savanna climate, frost-free and warmed by the nearby Gulf Stream.

History

The Keys were inhabited by the Calusa and Tequesta peoples before Juan Ponce de León charted them in 1513, naming them Los Martires ("the martyrs"). Key West became a prosperous wrecking and cigar-making port in the nineteenth century. Henry Flagler's Florida East Coast Railway reached Key West in 1912; the devastating Labor Day Hurricane of 1935 destroyed much of the line, whose bridges were later reused for the Overseas Highway, completed in 1938.

Economy and environment

Tourism dominates the economy, drawn by sport fishing, diving on the reef and wrecks, and the laid-back culture celebrated in Key West. The islands are highly vulnerable to hurricanes — the whole chain is evacuated ahead of major storms — and to sea-level rise. The tiny Key deer, found only on Big Pine Key and neighbouring islands, is a protected endemic species.
