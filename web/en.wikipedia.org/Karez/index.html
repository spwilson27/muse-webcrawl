Karez

A karez (also spelled kariz) is a gently sloping underground channel used to transport water from an aquifer or water well to the surface for irrigation and drinking. It is essentially the same technology as the qanat of Persia and the foggara of North Africa. The karez system is notably associated with the Turpan region of Xinjiang, China, as well as with Afghanistan, Pakistan, and other arid parts of Central Asia.

Structure

A karez consists of a series of vertical wells linked by an underground sloping tunnel. The system taps groundwater from an alluvial fan at the foot of mountains and conveys it by gravity to fields and settlements, often many kilometres away, with minimal loss to evaporation. Vertical shafts, spaced roughly 20 to 50 metres apart, provide access for digging and maintenance and for removing excavated material.

History and distribution

The technology is ancient, believed to have originated in Persia and spread along the Silk Road. In Xinjiang, the Turpan karez systems are considered one of the great engineering achievements of ancient China, alongside the Great Wall and the Grand Canal. Karez irrigation remains important in parts of Afghanistan and Balochistan, though many systems have fallen into disrepair as pumped wells lower water tables.
