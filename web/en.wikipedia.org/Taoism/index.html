Taoism (also spelled Daoism) is a diverse tradition of philosophy and religious practice native to China, emphasizing living in harmony with the Tao (Chinese: 道, "the Way"), the ineffable source and pattern of the universe. Taoist thought draws on the Tao Te Ching, attributed to the sage Laozi, and the writings of Zhuangzi, and has profoundly influenced Chinese culture, including medicine, martial arts, art, and politics.

Core ideas

Central concepts include wu wei (effortless action — acting in accordance with the natural flow of things rather than forcing outcomes), spontaneity and simplicity, the interdependence of opposites expressed in yin and yang, and the cultivation of qi (vital energy) for health and longevity. The Tao Te Ching opens with the famous paradox that "the Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao," stressing the limits of language.

History

Philosophical Taoism emerged during the Warring States period (4th–3rd centuries BCE). Organized religious Taoism took shape in the 2nd century CE with movements such as the Way of the Celestial Masters founded by Zhang Daoling, developing priesthoods, rituals, alchemy, and pantheons of deities — notably the Three Pure Ones. Taoism coexisted and interacted with Confucianism, Buddhism, and Chinese folk religion, and spread to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam. Major schools surviving today include Quanzhen and Zhengyi Taoism.

Practices

Practices include meditation, feng shui, qigong and tai chi, dietary and alchemical regimens aimed at longevity, ritual offerings, and temple festivals. The Taoist canon, the Daozang, contains over a thousand texts.
