Buddhism, also known as Buddha Dharma, is an Indian religion and philosophical tradition based on the teachings of Siddhartha Gautama, called the Buddha ("the Awakened One"), who lived in the northeastern Indian subcontinent some time between the 6th and 4th centuries BCE. It is the world's fourth-largest religion, with over 300 million adherents, and has profoundly shaped the cultures of South, Southeast, Central, and East Asia.

Core teachings

The Buddha's teaching begins with the Four Noble Truths: existence is marked by dukkha (suffering, unsatisfactoriness); dukkha arises from craving (taṇhā) and ignorance; dukkha can cease; and the way to its cessation is the Noble Eightfold Path — right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration. Closely connected doctrines include anattā (no permanent self), anicca (impermanence), dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda — all phenomena arise in dependence on conditions), and karma and rebirth within the cycle of saṃsāra, from which liberation — nirvāṇa — is the goal. Ethical life is structured by the Five Precepts and the cultivation of compassion (karuṇā) and loving-kindness (mettā); practice centres on meditation, devotion, and the monastic community (saṅgha) alongside the laity, together forming the "three jewels" of Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha in which Buddhists take refuge.

History

After the Buddha's death (c. 5th–4th century BCE), his followers compiled his teachings and monastic code in communal recitations; disagreement over discipline and doctrine produced early schools, of which Theravāda survives today, dominant in Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia. The emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) patronised Buddhism and sent missions across Asia. From about the 1st century CE, the Mahāyāna movement — with its ideal of the bodhisattva who vows to liberate all beings, and scriptures such as the Lotus Sutra and the Perfection of Wisdom texts — spread along the Silk Road into China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, where it generated schools including Chan/Zen, Pure Land, Tiantai, and Nichiren. The esoteric Vajrayāna developed in India and flourished in Tibet, Mongolia, and the Himalayas; its most famous figure is the Dalai Lama. Buddhism declined in India by the 12th century but was revived in the modern era, notably through B. R. Ambedkar's Dalit Buddhist movement from 1956. Since the 19th century Buddhism has also spread globally through migration, scholarship, and the popularity of mindfulness meditation.

Practices and festivals

Devotional practice includes offerings at temples and stupas, circumambulation, chanting and pilgrimage to sites of the Buddha's life (Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath, Kushinagar). Meditation traditions range from Theravāda vipassanā (insight) and samatha (calm) to Zen zazen and Tibetan visualisation and mantra practices. Major festivals include Vesak (the Buddha's birth, enlightenment, and death), Magha Puja, Kathina (robe-offering), ullambana/ghost festivals in East Asia, and Losar (Tibetan New Year).
