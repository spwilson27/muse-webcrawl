Oil spill
Largest marine spillDeepwater Horizon, 2010 (~134 million US gallons)
Other major spillsExxon Valdez (1989); Torrey Canyon (1967); Amoco Cadiz (1978)
Key lawMARPOL 73/78; US Oil Pollution Act 1990

An oil spill is the release of liquid petroleum hydrocarbons into the environment, especially the marine environment, due to human activity. Spills occur from tanker accidents, offshore drilling blowouts, pipeline failures and operational discharges, and can devastate coastlines, wildlife and fisheries.

Effects

Spilled oil floats and spreads into slicks, coating birds and marine mammals, destroying insulation and buoyancy, and introducing toxic polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons into food webs. Shoreline oil can persist in sediments for decades, as surveys of the 1989 Exxon Valdez spill in Alaska have shown. Smaller chronic discharges collectively exceed the volume of headline accidents.

Response and prevention

Cleanup methods include containment booms and skimmers, in-situ burning, chemical dispersants, and bioremediation by oil-degrading microbes. The MARPOL convention regulates operational discharges and, after disasters such as Exxon Valdez, mandated double hulls for tankers; the US Oil Pollution Act of 1990 imposed strict liability and created a federal cleanup fund. The 2010 Deepwater Horizon blowout — the largest marine oil spill in history — reshaped offshore regulation in the Gulf of Mexico.
