The International Maritime Organization (IMO) is a specialised agency of the United Nations responsible for the safety and security of shipping and the prevention of marine and atmospheric pollution by ships. As the global standard-setting authority for international shipping, its conventions are implemented by member states through flag-state and port-state control.

History

The IMO Convention was adopted in Geneva in 1948, and the organisation — originally called the Inter-Governmental Maritime Consultative Organization (IMCO) — came into existence in 1958, holding its first meeting in 1959. It took its present name in 1982. Its headquarters stand on the Albert Embankment in London, the only UN agency headquartered in the United Kingdom.

Principal conventions

The IMO administers the core regulatory framework of shipping, including SOLAS (safety of life at sea, 1974), MARPOL 73/78 on prevention of pollution, the STCW convention on seafarer training, the COLREGs on collisions at sea, and conventions on tonnage measurement, load lines and liability. Amendments are typically adopted by tacit acceptance, allowing the rules to evolve quickly.

Structure

The Assembly of all member states meets every two years; a 40-member Council acts as executive organ between sessions. Technical work is done by committees — notably the Maritime Safety Committee and the Marine Environment Protection Committee — supported by a secretariat of about 300 staff.
