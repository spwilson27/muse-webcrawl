A grand jury is a jury — a group of citizens — empowered by law to conduct legal proceedings, investigate potential criminal conduct, and determine whether criminal charges should be brought. The grand jury originated in medieval England and survives today chiefly in the United States, where the Fifth Amendment to the US Constitution guarantees indictment by grand jury for capital or otherwise infamous federal crimes.

Function

Unlike a trial (petit) jury, a grand jury does not determine guilt. Prosecutors present evidence in closed proceedings, and the grand jurors decide whether there is probable cause to issue an indictment, traditionally called a "true bill"; otherwise they return a "no bill". Grand juries may also compel testimony and documents by subpoena and, in some jurisdictions, conduct investigations and issue reports on public affairs.

Criticism

Because proceedings are secret, one-sided and controlled by prosecutors — and because indictment requires only probable cause — critics argue that grand juries rarely refuse to indict, a sentiment captured in the saying that a prosecutor could persuade a grand jury to "indict a ham sandwich". Defenders stress the jury's role as a shield between the citizen and unfounded prosecution.
