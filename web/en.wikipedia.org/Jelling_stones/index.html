Jelling stones

Jelling stones
Location: Jelling, Vejle Municipality, Denmark
Type: Runestones
Period: 10th century AD
Erected by: Gorm the Old; Harald Bluetooth
UNESCO World Heritage Site: since 1994 (Jelling Mounds, Runic Stones and Church)

The Jelling stones (Danish: Jellingstenene) are massive carved runestones from the 10th century, found at the town of Jelling in Denmark. The older, smaller stone was raised by King Gorm the Old in memory of his wife Thyra. The larger stone was raised by Gorm's son, Harald Bluetooth, in memory of his parents, in celebration of his conquest of Denmark and Norway, and of his having made the Danes Christian. The runic inscriptions are considered the best known in Denmark.

The stones

Gorm's stone (c. 935) reads, in translation: "King Gorm made this monument in memory of Thyra, his wife, Denmark's adornment." It is the earliest known occurrence of the name "Denmark" within the country's present territory.

Harald's stone (c. 965) is a large granite block about 2.4 metres high bearing inscriptions on three sides and the earliest Scandinavian depiction of Christ, shown entwined in the interlace of carved ribbons. The main inscription reads: "King Harald ordered this monument made in memory of Gorm, his father, and in memory of Thyra, his mother; that Harald who won for himself all of Denmark and Norway and made the Danes Christian." Because it proclaims both the unity of the kingdom and the conversion, the stone is sometimes called "Denmark's birth certificate."

Setting

The stones stand in the churchyard of Jelling Church, between two enormous burial mounds, within the remains of a vast Viking Age royal monument complex that includes one of the largest known ship settings (stone outlines of a ship) and a timber palisade. The site, linked with the founding of the Danish monarchy, was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1994.
