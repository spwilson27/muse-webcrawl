Paul-Michel Foucault (15 October 1926 – 25 June 1984) was a French philosopher, historian of ideas, writer, political activist, and literary critic. Foucault's theories primarily address the relationships between power and knowledge and how these are used as a form of social control through societal institutions. Though often cited as a structuralist and postmodernist, Foucault rejected these labels. His work has influenced academics and activists working in many fields, from sociology and history to literary and gender studies.

Life and career

Born in Poitiers into a prosperous provincial family of surgeons, Foucault studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, where he took degrees in philosophy and psychology and came under the influence of Louis Althusser and the psychiatrist-philosopher Georges Canguilhem. After teaching in Sweden, Poland, and Germany, he published his doctoral work, Madness and Civilization (1961), a history of the treatment of insanity in Europe. His archaeological studies The Birth of the Clinic (1963), The Order of Things (1966) — which made him famous with its declaration of the coming "death of man" — and The Archaeology of Knowledge (1969) analysed the rules governing what can be said and counted as knowledge in successive eras (epistemes).

Genealogy, power, and sexuality

In the 1970s, at the Collège de France, Foucault turned to "genealogy" and the analysis of power. Discipline and Punish (1975) traced the shift from spectacular public punishment to the modern prison and the "disciplinary society", using Bentham's Panopticon as the emblem of a power that surveils and normalises. The History of Sexuality (vol. 1, 1976) attacked the "repressive hypothesis" and developed the concept of biopower — power exercised over bodies and populations. He was also a tireless activist: he co-founded the Groupe d'Information sur les Prisons (1971), supported Polish Solidarity, and reported on the Iranian Revolution. Foucault died in Paris in 1984 of complications from AIDS, one of the first prominent French deaths from the disease; the final volumes of the History of Sexuality appeared shortly before his death.

Legacy

Foucault is among the most cited scholars in the humanities and social sciences; his concepts of discourse, governmentality, and power/knowledge underpin entire research programmes.
