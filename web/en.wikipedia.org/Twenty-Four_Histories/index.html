The Twenty-Four Histories (Chinese: 二十四史; pinyin: Èrshísì Shǐ) are the canonical collection of Chinese dynastic histories covering the period from the legendary Yellow Emperor around 3000 BC to the end of the Ming dynasty in 1644. Together they comprise some 3,213 volumes and roughly 40 million characters, making them one of the largest bodies of historical writing in the pre-modern world and a primary source for the study of Chinese history.

Each history was conventionally compiled by the dynasty that succeeded the one it describes, following the model established by Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji). The standard format, known as the annals-biographies form (jizhuanti), combines imperial annals, chronological tables, treatises on subjects such as astronomy, ritual, law and economics, and biographies of notable individuals.

Contents

The collection begins with the Shiji and proceeds through the Book of Han, the Book of the Later Han, the Records of the Three Kingdoms, and the histories of the Jin, the Southern and Northern dynasties, Sui, Tang, the Five Dynasties, Song, Liao, Jin, Yuan and Ming. During the Qing dynasty the set was fixed at twenty-four works; the History of Ming, completed in 1735, was the last to be admitted.

A twenty-fifth work, the Draft History of Qing, was compiled in the early Republican period but never received the same canonical status, and discussion of a "Twenty-Five Histories" or "Twenty-Six Histories" has continued into the modern era.

Significance

Because each dynasty legitimated itself partly by writing the history of its predecessor, the histories reflect both rich archival preservation and official orthodoxy. Despite their biases, they preserve enormous quantities of otherwise lost material and remain indispensable for the study of traditional China, its institutions and its historiography.
