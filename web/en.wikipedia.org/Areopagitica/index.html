Areopagitica is a prose polemic by the English poet and civil servant John Milton, published on 23 November 1644, at the height of the English Civil War. It is a celebrated defence of freedom of speech and of the press, directed against the Licensing Order of 1643 by which Parliament had reimposed pre-publication censorship.

Argument

Addressed to Parliament in the form of a classical oration (its title alludes to the Areopagus of Athens and to Isocrates' Areopagiticus), the work argues that licensing books before publication is futile and harmful: truth emerges from open encounter with falsehood — "Let her and Falsehood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the worse, in a free and open encounter?" Milton defends reading heterodox books as necessary to virtue ("I cannot praise a fugitive and cloistered virtue"), and famously describes "a good book" as "the precious life-blood of a master spirit". He compares a nation under censorship to a stunted eagle and praises London as a workshop of reformation, "the mansion house of liberty". Milton's tolerance had limits — he expressly excluded Roman Catholicism ("popery") and open superstition from the liberty he claimed.

Legacy

Parliament ignored the tract, and licensing survived until 1695. Rediscovered by later liberals, Areopagitica became a foundational text of the free-press tradition and has been quoted in modern free-speech jurisprudence, including by the Supreme Court of the United States.
