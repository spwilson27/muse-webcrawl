A grape is a fruit, botanically a berry, of the deciduous woody vines of the flowering plant genus Vitis. Grapes are a non-climacteric type of fruit, generally occurring in clusters. The cultivation of grapes began perhaps 8,000 years ago, and the fruit has been used as human food over history. Eaten fresh or in dried form (as raisins, currants and sultanas), grapes are also the primary raw material for the production of wine and other alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks, including grape juice. Grapes also have a minor role as a food ingredient worldwide.

History

The domesticated grape, Vitis vinifera, originated in the region spanning the South Caucasus and the Zagros Mountains, where evidence of winemaking dates to about 6000 BC in Georgia. Cultivation spread through the Near East and Egypt and then around the Mediterranean with Phoenician, Greek, and Roman trade. Grapes and wine held religious significance — in the cult of Dionysus and Bacchus, in Jewish ritual, and in the Christian Eucharist — which aided their spread, later including to the Americas, Australia, and South Africa after European colonisation.

Description and cultivation

Grapevines are perennial woody lianas that climb by tendrils; they are trained on trellises or pergolas in vineyards and pruned annually. Fruits grow in clusters of 15 to 300 berries, each containing 1–4 seeds (seedless varieties are bred for table use). Skin colour ranges from green ("white" grapes) through red to blue-black, owing to anthocyanin pigments. The science and practice of grape cultivation is viticulture; the world's vineyard area is dominated by wine production (about half or more of production), followed by table grapes and dried grapes.

Uses

Grapes are made into wine, brandy and other spirits (such as grappa, pisco, and cognac distilled from wine), grape juice, jam and jelly, vinegar (including balsamic, traditionally from grape must), and grape seed oil and extract. Raisins are a major dried product; notable table varieties include Thompson Seedless, Concord (a V. labrusca cultivar used for juice and jelly), and muscadines (V. rotundifolia) in the southeastern United States. Famous wine cultivars of V. vinifera include Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot noir, Chardonnay, and Riesling.

Health

Grapes contain sugars, vitamin C and K, and polyphenols concentrated in the skins and seeds, including resveratrol, which has been much studied though clinical benefits in humans remain unproven. Grape products also pose a toxicity hazard to dogs, in which grapes and raisins can cause acute kidney injury.

Production

World grape production is roughly 75–80 million tonnes per year; China, Italy, Spain, the United States, and France are among the largest producers, with France, Italy, and Spain leading in wine. In the late 19th century the phylloxera louse devastated European vineyards, which were saved by grafting vinifera vines onto resistant American rootstocks — a practice still universal in most wine regions.
