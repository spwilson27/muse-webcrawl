Freethought

The printing press spread freethought literature

Freethought is an epistemological viewpoint that holds that beliefs should be formed on the basis of logic, reason, and empiricism rather than authority, tradition, revelation, or dogma. A freethinker is a person who thinks freely in this sense. The term arose in the late seventeenth century and became associated with deists, rationalists, and later secularists, atheists, and agnostics.

History

The word "freethinker" came into use after the anonymous publication of works critical of religious orthodoxy in England; Anthony Collins's Discourse of Free-thinking (1713) gave the movement its classic manifesto. Enlightenment figures such as Voltaire, Denis Diderot, and Thomas Paine — whose The Age of Reason (1794–1807) defended deism and attacked revealed religion — made freethought central to the political ferment of the age.

Nineteenth-century movement

In Victorian Britain, freethought overlapped with secularism, a term coined by George Holyoake in 1851; Charles Bradlaugh's campaign to take his seat in Parliament as an atheist (1880–1886) was a landmark. In the United States, Robert G. Ingersoll, "the Great Agnostic," was the era's most famous freethought lecturer, and freethought newspapers flourished during what is called the Golden Age of Freethought (c. 1875–1914).

Symbol and organizations

The pansy is traditionally a symbol of freethought (from the French pensée, "thought"). Organizations historically associated with freethought include the Rationalist Association, humanist associations, and the Freedom From Religion Foundation. The viewpoint is closely related to, but broader than, atheism: freethought describes a method of forming beliefs rather than a conclusion about gods.
