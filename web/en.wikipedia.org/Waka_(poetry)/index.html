Waka (和歌, "Japanese poem") is a type of poetry in classical Japanese literature. Although waka in modern Japanese is written as 和歌, in the past it was also written as 倭歌. Waka are composed in Japanese, and are contrasted with poetry composed by Japanese poets in Classical Chinese, which are known as kanshi.

The word waka originally encompassed a number of differing forms, principally tanka ("short poem") and chōka ("long poem"), together with the related sedōka, bussokusekika and katauta. By the Heian period, however, the long forms had fallen out of use, and waka came to mean tanka almost exclusively: a poem of thirty-one syllables (morae) in five lines of 5-7-5-7-7.

History

The earliest great anthology of waka is the Man'yōshū ("Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves", c. 759), containing some 4,500 poems, including many chōka by poets such as Kakinomoto no Hitomaro. In 905 the emperor Daigo commissioned the Kokin Wakashū ("Collection of Japanese Poems of Ancient and Modern Times"), whose preface by Ki no Tsurayuki is a landmark of Japanese literary criticism; its leading poets included Ariwara no Narihira, Ono no Komachi and Ki no Tomonori. The Shin Kokin Wakashū (c. 1205), edited by Fujiwara no Teika, represents the height of courtly waka aesthetics.

Twenty-one imperial anthologies (nijūichidaishū) were compiled between the 10th and 15th centuries. Waka was central to court culture, exchanged in courtship, diplomacy and poetic contests (uta-awase). From waka practice developed linked verse (renga) and, later, haiku, whose opening verse (hokku) derives from the first three lines of the tanka.

Modern waka

In the Meiji era, reformers such as Masaoka Shiki modernized the form; in the modern period waka usually refers specifically to tanka. The Imperial court still holds the annual Utakai Hajime poetry reading, and tanka remains a widely practised form in Japan.
