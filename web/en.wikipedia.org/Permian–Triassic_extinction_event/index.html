Permian–Triassic extinction event

Datec. 251.9 million years ago
Also known asThe Great Dying; end-Permian mass extinction
LossesUp to ~81–96% of marine species; ~70% of terrestrial vertebrate species
Principal suspect causeSiberian Traps flood-basalt volcanism
Recovery timec. 5–10 million years

The Permian–Triassic extinction event, colloquially known as the Great Dying, was an extinction event that occurred about 251.9 million years ago, at the boundary between the Permian and Triassic geologic periods. It is the Earth's most severe known extinction event, with the extinction of up to 96% of all marine species in older estimates (more recent work suggests around 81%) and about 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species. It is the only known mass extinction of insects.

Causes

The leading explanation is the eruption of the Siberian Traps, one of the largest known flood-basalt volcanic provinces, which released immense quantities of carbon dioxide and other gases over tens of thousands of years. Consequences probably included rapid global warming of roughly 8–10 °C, ocean anoxia and acidification, disruption of ocean circulation, and damage to the ozone layer from halogen gases. Evidence includes a sharp negative carbon-isotope excursion at the boundary and geochemical markers of widespread anoxia. Impact events and methane release from seafloor clathrates have also been proposed as contributors.

Effects on life

Entire groups vanished, including trilobites — survivors of two previous mass extinctions — tabulate and rugose corals, and eurypterids. Reef ecosystems collapsed and were replaced for millions of years by microbial structures. On land, the dominant synapsid fauna was devastated; the disaster taxon Lystrosaurus briefly dominated terrestrial ecosystems. Coal-forming forests disappeared for about 10 million years (the "coal gap").

Recovery

Recovery was unusually slow: complex ecosystems took roughly 5–10 million years to rebuild, far longer than after other mass extinctions, perhaps because environmental stresses persisted into the Early Triassic, which was marked by repeated extinction pulses and extreme heat.
