United States House of Representatives

The United States House of Representatives is the lower chamber of the United States Congress, the Senate being the upper chamber. Together they constitute the federal bicameral legislature. The House is composed of representatives who sit in single-member congressional districts allocated to each state on the basis of population, as measured by the US census. The number of voting representatives is fixed by law at 435, plus six non-voting members representing the District of Columbia and the U.S. territories.

Powers

Article I of the Constitution grants the House several exclusive powers: to initiate revenue bills, to impeach federal officials (the Senate tries impeachments), and — through the Twelfth Amendment's contingent election — to elect the President if no candidate wins a majority in the Electoral College, as happened in 1800 and 1824. Bills pass the House by majority vote; a quorum is 218 if no seats are vacant. Members must be at least 25 years old, a US citizen for at least seven years, and an inhabitant of the state they represent. The House's shorter terms and smaller districts were designed to make it the chamber closest to the people.

Organisation and procedure

The Speaker of the House, elected by the membership, is its presiding officer and second in the presidential line of succession after the Vice President. Party organisation is led by the majority and minority leaders and whips. Most legislative work is done in committees — around 20 standing committees (Appropriations, Ways and Means, Judiciary, Armed Services, among others) and the Rules Committee, through which the majority leadership tightly controls which bills reach the floor and under what terms. A famous mace, carried by the Sergeant at Arms, symbolises the House's authority.

History

The House first met in 1789. Its size grew with the country until the Apportionment Act of 1911 and the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 froze it at 435 (with a temporary increase to 437 when Alaska and Hawaii entered). Notable historical features include the gag rule controversies over antislavery petitions in the 1830s–40s, the strong speakerships of Henry Clay and Thomas Brackett Reed, the 1994 "Contract with America" election, and the first female Speaker, Nancy Pelosi (2007).
