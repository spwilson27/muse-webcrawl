Elimination theory

In commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, elimination theory is the theory of the algorithmic methods for eliminating variables from systems of polynomial equations. Given polynomials in several variables, elimination produces equations in fewer variables whose solutions correspond, by projection, to the solutions of the original system.

History

The problem of eliminating unknowns from polynomial equations dates to early work on resultants. Sylvester and Cayley developed determinant-based methods in the nineteenth century; Hilbert's basis theorem and Nullstellensatz later placed the subject on a modern footing. In the late twentieth century, Gröbner bases, introduced by Bruno Buchberger in 1965, provided a general and computable tool for elimination.

Resultants and elimination ideals

For two polynomials in one variable, the resultant vanishes exactly when they share a common root; it is given by the determinant of the Sylvester matrix. For polynomial rings, the k-th elimination ideal is the intersection of an ideal with the subring omitting the first k variables, and its variety describes the projection of the original variety. Lexicographic Gröbner bases compute elimination ideals directly.

Applications

Elimination methods are used in solving polynomial systems, robot kinematics, geometric theorem proving, implicitisation of parametric curves and surfaces, and computer-aided design.
