Ephorate

Greek
ἔφορος (ephoros), "overseer"
Number
Five
Term
One year, elected annually
State
Sparta

The ephors (Greek: ἔφοροι, ephoroi, singular ἔφορος, ephoros) were a board of five senior magistrates in ancient Sparta who shared power with the two Spartan kings. Elected annually by the citizen assembly, the ephors were among the most powerful officials in the Spartan state, supervising the kings, presiding over the courts, and overseeing the education of the young and the conduct of citizens.

Powers and functions

The ephors could prosecute any citizen, including kings, and accompanied a king on campaign as observers. Each year they declared ritual war on the helots, permitting Spartans to kill them without religious pollution. They received foreign ambassadors, controlled public finances, and could summon the assembly (apella) and the council of elders (gerousia). Every nine years the ephors watched the night sky for omens that might justify deposing a king.

Origins

Ancient authors disagreed on the origin of the office: Plutarch attributed it to the lawgiver Lycurgus, while others credited the king Theopompus in the eighth century BC. Aristotle criticised the ephorate in the Politics, noting that men of modest means could be elected and were open to bribery, yet he recognised the office as an important democratic element balancing Sparta's kings and aristocratic council.

Decline

The ephorate survived into the Hellenistic period. The reforming king Cleomenes III abolished it in 227 BC after having four of the five ephors murdered, but the office was restored after his defeat and continued, in diminished form, under Roman rule.
