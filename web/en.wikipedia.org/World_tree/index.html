World tree

The world tree connects heaven, earth, and the underworld.

The world tree is a motif found in the mythologies and folk religions of many cultures, particularly in Eurasia and the Americas. It is a colossal tree standing at the centre of the cosmos, connecting the heavens, the earth, and the underworld through its branches and roots, and often forming the axis mundi around which the universe is ordered.

Examples

The best-known example is Yggdrasil in Norse mythology, an immense ash tree beneath whose roots lie the wells of fate and wisdom, and in whose branches the nine realms are situated. In Hungarian folklore the életfa or "tree of life" rises through layered worlds climbed by the shaman-like táltos. Baltic traditions venerated a world oak; Slavic tales tell of an oak on the island of Buyan; and in Siberian shamanism the world tree serves as the ladder by which the shaman ascends to the sky.

Beyond Eurasia, the Maya conceived the Wacah Chan, a ceiba tree linking the underworld of Xibalba with the celestial realm, while the sacred iroko and the tree at the centre of numerous African and Oceanic cosmologies play similar roles. In Hindu iconography the inverted aśvattha tree of the Bhagavad Gita has its roots above and branches below.

Interpretation

Scholars of comparative mythology connect the world tree with the tree of life motif and with shamanic practice, in which the tree mediates travel between cosmic levels. Carl Jung and Mircea Eliade treated it as an archetype of wholeness and regeneration. Real ancient trees — such as the sacred oak of Dodona in Greece or Thor's Oak among the Germanic peoples — may have anchored the myth in ritual practice.
