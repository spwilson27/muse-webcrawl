Astronomical transit
DefinitionOne body passing in front of another
Famous casesTransit of Venus, Transit of Mercury
UseExoplanet detection

An astronomical transit (or astronomical passage) is the phenomenon in which a celestial body passes directly between a larger body and an observer. As viewed from a particular vantage point, the transiting body appears as a small black disc moving across the face of the larger body.

Transits in the Solar System

From Earth, only Mercury and Venus — the planets closer to the Sun — can be seen in transit across the Sun. Transits of Venus occur in pairs separated by eight years, with more than a century between pairs (most recently 2004 and 2012); transits of Mercury happen about 13 times per century. Historically, timing Venus transits from distant stations let astronomers measure the astronomical unit; expeditions to observe the 1761 and 1769 transits, including Captain Cook's voyage to Tahiti, were landmark scientific undertakings.

Exoplanet detection

The transit method detects planets around other stars by measuring the periodic dip in a star's brightness as a planet crosses it. The depth of the dip gives the planet's size relative to the star, and combined with radial-velocity measurements it yields density. Space missions such as Kepler and TESS have discovered thousands of exoplanets this way.

Related phenomena

When the smaller body is large enough to cover the larger one entirely, the event is an occultation or eclipse. Moons transiting their planets — such as the Galilean moons crossing Jupiter — are routine sights for amateur astronomers.
