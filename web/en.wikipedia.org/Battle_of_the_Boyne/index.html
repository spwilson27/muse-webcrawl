The Battle of the Boyne was fought on 1 July 1690 (Old Style) between King William III's multinational Williamite army and the Jacobite army of the deposed King James II, across the River Boyne near Drogheda in Ireland. Part of the Williamite War in Ireland and the wider Nine Years' War, the battle is commemorated annually by the Orange Order in Northern Ireland on "the Twelfth" of July.

Background

James II, removed from the thrones of England and Scotland in the Glorious Revolution of 1688, landed in Ireland with French support in 1689 and held most of the island through the Catholic Irish Parliament and army. William landed at Carrickfergus in June 1690 with a large, well-equipped polyglot army—English, Scots, Dutch, Danes, Huguenots and Ulster Protestants—and marched south toward Dublin.

The battle

James took position on the south bank of the Boyne. William sent a flanking force upstream to cross at Rosnaree, drawing off much of the Jacobite army under Lauzun, then forced the main crossings at Oldbridge in bitter fighting in which the elderly Duke of Schomberg was killed rallying his men. William himself crossed downstream with the cavalry. Outnumbered at the decisive point and threatened with envelopment, the Jacobites withdrew in generally good order toward Duleek; James fled to Dublin and then to France.

Aftermath

Dublin fell to William within days, but the war in Ireland continued until the Treaty of Limerick in 1691 after the far bloodier Battle of Aughrim. The Boyne's enduring importance is symbolic: in Ulster Protestant tradition it stands for the defence of Protestant liberties and the civil settlement of 1688–91, and William—"King Billy"—remains the central folk hero.
