Kupffer cell

Kupffer cells line the sinusoids of the liver, where they filter blood arriving from the gut.

Kupffer cells, also known as stellate macrophages or Kupffer–Browicz cells, are specialized resident macrophages located in the liver, lining the walls of the hepatic sinusoids. They form part of the mononuclear phagocyte system and constitute the largest population of tissue-resident macrophages in the body. First described by Karl Wilhelm von Kupffer in 1876 and correctly identified as macrophages by Tadeusz Browicz, they are essential sentinels of the liver.

SystemMononuclear phagocyte system
FunctionPhagocytosis, immune surveillance, iron recycling

Function

Kupffer cells are strategically positioned in the sinusoidal lumen, where they directly contact blood flowing from the portal vein. They phagocytose gut-derived bacteria, endotoxins, immune complexes, and cellular debris, preventing these from entering the systemic circulation. They also clear aged and damaged red blood cells, recycling iron and breaking down haemoglobin. Kupffer cells express pattern-recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptors, and can present antigens and secrete cytokines such as tumour necrosis factor alpha, interleukins, and reactive oxygen species.

Development

Unlike many macrophage populations that are replenished by circulating monocytes, Kupffer cells arise largely from embryonic precursors — yolk-sac and fetal-liver progenitors — and maintain themselves by local self-renewal in the healthy adult liver. Under conditions of depletion or injury, bone-marrow-derived monocytes can repopulate the niche and acquire the Kupffer cell phenotype, which is specified by transcription factors including ID3 and LXR-α under the influence of signals from neighbouring sinusoidal endothelial cells and hepatocytes.

Clinical significance

Kupffer cell activation is central to the pathogenesis of many liver diseases. Their inflammatory responses contribute to alcoholic and metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease, viral hepatitis, sepsis-induced liver injury, and drug-induced liver injury. Conversely, their tolerogenic functions help maintain immune tolerance to harmless food and microbial antigens arriving from the gut. Kupffer cells also play roles in liver regeneration, fibrosis (through interaction with hepatic stellate cells), and the response to liver transplantation and ischemia-reperfusion injury.
