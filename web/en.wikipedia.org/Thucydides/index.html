Thucydides

Thucydides
Born: c. 460 BC, Alimos, Athens
Died: c. 400 BC (aged about 60)
Occupation: Historian, general
Notable work: History of the Peloponnesian War

Thucydides (c. 460 – c. 400 BC) was an Athenian historian and general. His History of the Peloponnesian War recounts the conflict between Sparta and Athens in the 5th century BC up to 411 BC. Thucydides has been called the father of scientific history for his strict standards of evidence-gathering and analysis of cause and effect without reference to divine intervention, and the father of political realism in international relations for his examination of power politics, most famously in the Melian Dialogue.

Life

Little is known of Thucydides' life beyond what can be inferred from his work. He was an Athenian of aristocratic background with property in Thrace, including gold mines, and connections to the statesman Miltiades' family. He survived the Plague of Athens (430 BC), which he described in clinical detail. In 424 BC, as a strategos (general), he failed to prevent the Spartan general Brasidas from capturing the important city of Amphipolis in Thrace and was exiled for twenty years. His exile, he wrote, gave him the opportunity to study the war from both sides. Whether he returned to Athens before his death around 400 BC is uncertain.

The History

Thucydides wrote that he began his history at the outbreak of the war in 431 BC, believing it would be a great war "worthy of record". Unlike Herodotus, his near-contemporary, Thucydides excluded mythology and anecdote, restricting himself to events he witnessed or could verify. His history includes reconstructed speeches — such as Pericles' Funeral Oration — which he admitted represented what speakers "ought to have said" as much as what they actually said. Famous episodes include the Plague of Athens, the Mytilenean Debate, the Melian Dialogue (in which Athens declares that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must"), and the catastrophic Athenian expedition to Sicily (415–413 BC). The work breaks off unfinished in 411 BC, mid-sentence, seven years before the war's end.

Influence

Thucydides influenced later Greek and Roman historians including Xenophon, Polybius, and Sallust, and philosophers such as Thomas Hobbes, who translated his work. The concept of the "Thucydides Trap" — the danger of war when a rising power threatens a ruling one — has entered modern strategic discourse.
