Hydrogen economy

The hydrogen economy is an envisioned system in which hydrogen serves as a major energy carrier, produced from primary energy sources, transported and stored, and used in fuel cells or combustion to provide power, heat, and transport fuel with water as the principal emission at the point of use. The term was popularised in the 1970s, notably by John Bockris, and revived repeatedly — in the early 2000s and again in the 2020s as part of climate policy.

Rationale

Advocates argue hydrogen can decarbonise sectors that are hard to electrify directly — steelmaking, chemicals, shipping, aviation, and long-duration energy storage — and can store surplus renewable electricity at scale. Hydrogen can be produced by electrolysis of water using renewable electricity ("green hydrogen"), from natural gas with carbon capture ("blue hydrogen"), or conventionally by steam methane reforming ("grey hydrogen"), which accounts for the great majority of today's roughly 95-million-tonne annual hydrogen output, used mainly in refining and ammonia production.

Criticisms and challenges

Critics point to hydrogen's poor round-trip efficiency relative to batteries, the cost and energy demands of compression or liquefaction, leakage of the small hydrogen molecule, infrastructure expense, and the risk that "hydrogen-ready" policies prolong fossil fuel use. Debate continues over which applications merit priority, with many analysts favouring green hydrogen for industry and shipping over passenger cars and home heating, where heat pumps and batteries are generally more efficient.
