Found manuscript

The found-manuscript device pretends the text is a genuine discovered document.

A found manuscript is a literary device in which the author pretends that the work is not fiction but a real document that has been discovered, inherited, or received, and is merely being edited or translated for the reader. It is a variety of the frame story and was especially popular in the 18th and 19th centuries, before readers expected frank fiction, and it survives today in epistolary novels and "found footage" style narratives.

Purpose

The device serves several ends. It lends an air of authenticity and antiquity to fantastic or scandalous material; it distances the real author from the work's opinions, which can be disavowed as those of the fictitious manuscript's writer; and it allows playful forgeries, in which the "editor" comments on, doubts, or annotates the text. In Gothic fiction it heightens dread by insisting on the supposed truth of the horrors described.

Examples

Horace Walpole presented the first edition of The Castle of Otranto (1764) as a translation of an old Italian manuscript. James Macpherson's Ossian poems were offered as translations of ancient Gaelic manuscripts. Jan Potocki's The Manuscript Found in Saragossa (1805) takes its very title from the device. Later examples include the editorial frames of Bram Stoker's documents in Dracula, Umberto Eco's The Name of the Rose, which opens with a mock-scholarly account of a rediscovered codex, and Vladimir Nabokov's Pale Fire, where the "editor" hijacks the text. The device is closely related to literary hoaxes such as the Hitler Diaries, where forged "found" documents were presented as genuine.

Related devices

Found manuscripts shade into other metafictional frames: the epistolary novel, the "editor's preface" of works such as The Scarlet Letter, pseudotranslations like Don Quixote's supposed Arabic source by Cide Hamete Benengeli, and the modern found-footage film.
