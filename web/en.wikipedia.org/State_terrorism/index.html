State terrorism

State terrorism refers to acts of terrorism conducted by a state against its own citizens or against another state. The concept remains contested: there is no universally accepted legal definition, and governments accused of such acts typically reject the label.

Definition debate

Some scholars restrict "terrorism" to non-state actors, while others argue that state violence intended to instil fear in a population fits the same concept. Historically, the word terrorism originally described state violence: the Reign of Terror (1793–94) during the French Revolution was carried out by the revolutionary government itself.

Historical examples

Frequently cited cases include the Great Purge in the Soviet Union under Stalin, Nazi Germany's machinery of repression, the Dirty War in Argentina (1976–83), and the Khmer Rouge regime in Cambodia. States have also sponsored non-state groups, leading to designations such as the U.S. list of State Sponsors of Terrorism.

State-sponsored terrorism

State-sponsored terrorism is government support of violent non-state actors, including funding, training, or providing sanctuary. Documented controversies include support for armed groups by various states during the Cold War and allegations surrounding Operation Condor in South America.

Legal status

State violence against civilians is addressed by other bodies of law, including international humanitarian law, the Genocide Convention, and the concept of crimes against humanity, rather than by anti-terrorism conventions aimed at individuals.
