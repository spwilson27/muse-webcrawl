Multilevel selection

Multilevel selection (MLS) is a theory in evolutionary biology proposing that natural selection acts simultaneously at multiple levels of biological organisation — genes, cells, organisms and groups — so that a trait disadvantageous to an individual may evolve if it benefits the group to which the individual belongs. It modernises and formalises the older and once-discredited idea of group selection.

Theoretical framework

The Price equation allows selection to be partitioned into within-group and between-group components, providing a mathematical basis for MLS. Proponents such as David Sloan Wilson and Edward O. Wilson argue that traits like altruism, cooperation and the suppression of within-group conflict evolved because groups possessing them out-reproduced rival groups. The major evolutionary transitions — for example, from single cells to multicellular organisms, and from solitary insects to eusocial colonies — are often interpreted as cases where selection at a higher level overcame selection at lower levels, creating a new unit of individuality.

Debate

Multilevel selection remains debated: kin selection and inclusive-fitness theorists often regard group-level explanations as mathematically equivalent reformulations rather than distinct processes, while MLS advocates contend the levels framework is heuristically superior for studying cooperation, culture and major transitions.
