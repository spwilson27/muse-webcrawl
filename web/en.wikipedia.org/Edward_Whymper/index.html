Edward Whymper

Edward Whymper (27 April 1840 – 16 September 1911) was an English mountaineer, explorer, illustrator, and author best known for the first ascent of the Matterhorn in 1865. The triumph ended in tragedy when four members of his party fell to their deaths during the descent — an event that marked the close of the golden age of alpinism.

Early alpine career

Trained as a wood engraver, Whymper was sent to the Alps in 1860 to make illustrations. He turned to climbing and made a series of major first ascents, including Mont Pelvoux, the Barre des Écrins, the Aiguille Verte, and the Grandes Jorasses (by its second-highest point), often with the guide Michel Croz.

The Matterhorn

After repeated failed attempts from the Italian side, Whymper reached the summit from Zermatt on 14 July 1865, beating Jean-Antoine Carrel's Italian party by a day. On the descent, Douglas Hadow slipped and dragged Croz, Lord Francis Douglas, and Charles Hudson from the mountain; the rope broke, saving Whymper and the two Taugwalders. The disaster prompted public debate about the morality of mountaineering.

Later expeditions

Whymper explored Greenland in 1867 and 1872, collecting fossils, and in 1880 made the first ascent of Chimborazo in Ecuador — then thought possibly the highest mountain on Earth — studying altitude sickness with the guide Jean-Antoine Carrel. His classic account, Scrambles Amongst the Alps in the Years 1860–69, remains in print. He died in Chamonix in 1911.
