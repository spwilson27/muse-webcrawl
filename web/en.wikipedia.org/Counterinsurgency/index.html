Counterinsurgency

Counterinsurgency (abbreviated COIN) is the set of military, paramilitary, political, economic, psychological, and civic actions taken by a government to defeat an insurgency — an armed rebellion against constituted authority waged by irregular forces. Modern counterinsurgency doctrine treats the contested population, rather than enemy fighters, as the centre of gravity: whoever wins the population's allegiance or acquiescence wins the war.

Principles

Writers from T. E. Lawrence and Charles Gwynn to David Galula, Robert Thompson, and David Petraeus have distilled recurring principles: the primacy of political over military solutions, the need for a legitimate host government, unity of civil-military effort, intelligence as the driver of operations, isolation of insurgents from their support, and patience over long time horizons. The United States manual FM 3-24, published in 2006 amid the Iraq War, popularized the population-centric model and the paradoxes of COIN, such as "sometimes, the more force is used, the less effective it is."

History

Counterinsurgency has roots in colonial warfare — the Boer War, the Philippine–American War, and Britain's "small wars" — and was refined in the post-1945 wars of decolonization: Malaya, Kenya, Algeria, and Indochina. The Cold War brought American involvement in Vietnam and Latin America. After 2001 the concept returned to prominence in Afghanistan and Iraq, and its record there prompted renewed debate about whether foreign forces can succeed at COIN at all.

Approaches

Two broad approaches are distinguished: the enemy-centric approach, which seeks to destroy the insurgent organization kinetically, and the population-centric approach, which prioritizes security, governance, and development for civilians. In practice most campaigns mix the two, with varying balances and results.
