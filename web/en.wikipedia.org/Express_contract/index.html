Express contract

An express contract is a contract whose terms are stated directly and explicitly by the parties, either in writing or in spoken words, at the time the agreement is made. It is one of the principal classifications of contracts in common law, distinguished by the manner in which the parties manifest their assent.

Contrast with implied contracts

An express contract contrasts with an implied-in-fact contract, whose terms are inferred from the parties' conduct — for example, when a customer sits in a barber's chair and thereby impliedly agrees to pay the customary price. Both express and implied-in-fact contracts are enforceable as genuine agreements; they differ from implied-in-law contracts (quasi-contracts), which are not agreements at all but obligations imposed by courts to prevent unjust enrichment.

Requirements

Like other contracts, an express contract generally requires offer, acceptance, consideration, and an intention to create legal relations. Certain express contracts must be in writing to be enforceable under the Statute of Frauds, including contracts for the sale of land and agreements that cannot be performed within one year. Because the parties have stated their terms openly, disputes over express contracts usually concern interpretation rather than formation.
