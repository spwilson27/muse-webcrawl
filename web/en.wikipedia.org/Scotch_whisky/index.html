Scotch whisky (Scottish Gaelic: uisge-beatha, "water of life"), often simply called Scotch, is malt whisky or grain whisky (or a blend of the two) made in Scotland. By law it must be distilled and matured in Scotland in oak casks for at least three years, bottled at a minimum of 40% ABV, and made only from water, malted barley (or other whole grains), and yeast. The first written record of whisky-making in Scotland is in the Exchequer Rolls of 1495, noting malt given to Friar John Cor to make aqua vitae.

Categories and regions

The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 define five categories: single malt (from one distillery, malted barley only, pot stills), single grain, blended malt, blended grain, and blended Scotch whisky (the bulk of sales, e.g. Johnnie Walker, Chivas Regal). Five recognised regions impart broad styles: Speyside (fruity, e.g. Glenlivet, Macallan), Islay (peated and smoky, e.g. Laphroaig, Ardbeg), Highland (diverse), Lowland (light) and Campbeltown (briny).

Production

For malt whisky, barley is malted (sometimes dried over peat smoke), mashed, fermented to a beer-like wash, double-distilled in copper pot stills, and aged in oak — often ex-bourbon or sherry casks; the evaporative loss is the "angels' share". Blending combines malt and column-distilled grain whiskies. Scotch is one of Scotland's biggest exports, sold in around 180 markets.
