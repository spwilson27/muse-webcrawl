The Seljuk Empire (1037–1194), also known as the Great Seljuk Empire, was a Turko-Persian Sunni Muslim empire founded by the Oghuz Turkish leader Tughril Beg and his brother Chaghri. From their homeland near the Aral Sea, the Seljuks conquered Khorasan, Persia, Iraq, and most of Anatolia, ruling a vast territory from the Hindu Kush to the Mediterranean and becoming champions of Sunni orthodoxy against the Shia Fatimids and Buyids.

Rise

The dynasty took its name from Seljuk, a 10th-century Oghuz chief. His grandsons Tughril and Chaghri defeated the Ghaznavids at Dandanqan (1040). In 1055 Tughril entered Baghdad and was proclaimed sultan by the Abbasid caliph, establishing the pattern of Seljuk military rule legitimised by the caliphate. Alp Arslan (r. 1063–1072) crushed the Byzantines at Manzikert in 1071, opening Anatolia to Turkish settlement and laying the groundwork for the later Sultanate of Rum.

Golden age and decline

Under Malik-Shah I (r. 1072–1092) and his vizier Nizam al-Mulk, the empire reached its zenith: administration was systematised, the Nizamiyya network of madrasas spread Sunni learning, and Persian court culture flourished — the poet-astronomer Omar Khayyam worked under Malik-Shah's patronage. After 1092, succession wars fragmented the empire into rival sultanates and atabeg states, a disunity exploited by the Crusaders in Syria and Palestine. The eastern empire ended when Sultan Sanjar was defeated by the Khwarazmians' predecessors and, finally, Toghrul III was killed in 1194.

Legacy

The Seljuks permanently Turkified Anatolia and much of Iran, fused Turkish military rule with Persian administration, and set patterns of governance inherited by the Ottomans.
