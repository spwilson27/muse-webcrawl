Julian of Norwich (c. 1343 – after 1416) was an English anchoress and mystic whose book Revelations of Divine Love, describing sixteen visions ("showings") she received during a near-fatal illness in May 1373, is the earliest surviving book in the English language known to have been written by a woman.

Life

Little is known of Julian's life, including her birth name; she is named for the church of St Julian in Norwich, in whose cell she lived as an anchoress — a religious recluse enclosed for life, who gave spiritual counsel through a window. In 1373, aged thirty and believing she was dying, she received a series of visions of Christ's Passion. She recovered and spent decades meditating on their meaning, producing first a short account and then, some twenty years later, the much-expanded Long Text of the Revelations. The mystic Margery Kempe visited her around 1413 for advice.

Theology

Julian's writing is notable for its optimism about God's love, her visions of God as mother as well as father, and her famous assurance, spoken by Christ in a showing: "All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well." Her parable of the lord and the servant addresses the problem of sin and suffering. Her works, preserved in manuscript by English nuns in exile, were first printed in 1670 and gained wide readership in the 20th century, when T. S. Eliot quoted her in Four Quartets.
