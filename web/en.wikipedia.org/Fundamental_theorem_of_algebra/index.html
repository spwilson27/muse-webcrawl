Fundamental theorem of algebra

Fundamental theorem of algebra
StatementEvery non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root
First rigorous proofGauss (1799 dissertation); earlier attempts by d'Alembert, Euler, Lagrange
ConsequenceA degree-n polynomial has exactly n complex roots counted with multiplicity

The fundamental theorem of algebra states that every non-constant single-variable polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root. Equivalently, the field of complex numbers is algebraically closed: every polynomial of degree n factors into n linear factors over ℂ, counting multiplicity.

History

The result was asserted in the 17th century (Girard, 1629) but early proofs had gaps: d'Alembert's 1746 attempt and Euler's assumed existence of roots. Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the first substantially rigorous proof in his 1799 doctoral dissertation, criticising predecessors, and produced several proofs over his lifetime.

Proofs

No purely algebraic proof exists; all proofs use some analysis or topology, because the theorem ultimately rests on the completeness of the real numbers. Standard proofs use Liouville's theorem from complex analysis, the winding number in algebraic topology, or elementary analysis showing a polynomial's modulus attains a minimum that must be zero.

Corollaries

Since every real polynomial has complex roots in conjugate pairs, every real polynomial factors into linear and irreducible quadratic factors — and every odd-degree real polynomial has a real root.
