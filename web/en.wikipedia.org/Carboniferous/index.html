The Carboniferous is a geologic period and system spanning about 60 million years, from 358.9 to 298.9 million years ago. Its name, meaning "coal-bearing," reflects the vast coal deposits formed from its tropical swamp forests — deposits that later fuelled the Industrial Revolution. In North America the period is divided into the Mississippian (early) and Pennsylvanian (late) subperiods.

A Carboniferous coal-swamp forest of giant lycophytes and ferns.

Climate and environment

The early Carboniferous was warm with high sea levels and extensive shallow seas depositing limestones. As the supercontinent Gondwana drifted over the South Pole, a long ice age (the Late Paleozoic Ice Age) set in, producing glacial cycles and falling seas. Atmospheric oxygen reached levels far above today's — perhaps 30–35% — supporting giant arthropods such as the millipede-like Arthropleura and the griffinfly Meganeura with a wingspan near 70 cm.

Life

Forests of giant clubmosses (Lepidodendron), horsetails (Calamites), and tree ferns dominated wetlands; seed plants diversified on drier ground. Amphibians radiated on land, and the amniotic egg appeared, giving rise to the first reptiles, including Hylonomus. In the seas, crinoids, brachiopods, and fusulinid foraminifera flourished. The late Carboniferous rainforest collapse (~307 Ma) fragmented the coal forests and reshaped terrestrial ecosystems.

Tectonics

Continents converged through the period: Laurussia collided with Gondwana, raising the Appalachian, Ouachita, and Variscan mountain belts and assembling the supercontinent Pangaea, largely complete by the period's end.
