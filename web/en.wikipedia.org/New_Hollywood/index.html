New Hollywood

New Hollywood, also called the American New Wave, was a movement in American film history from the late 1960s to the early 1980s, when a new generation of filmmakers came to prominence, influenced by European art cinema and classical Hollywood.

Origins

The decline of the studio system, the success of low-budget films like Bonnie and Clyde (1967) and Easy Rider (1969), and the replacement of the Production Code with the MPAA ratings system opened the way for younger, film-school-trained directors.

Key filmmakers and films

Directors associated with the movement include Francis Ford Coppola (The Godfather, Apocalypse Now), Martin Scorsese (Taxi Driver), Steven Spielberg, George Lucas, Robert Altman, and William Friedkin (The French Connection, The Exorcist). Their films often featured antiheroes, ambiguous endings, and greater psychological and moral complexity.

Decline

The movement faded in the early 1980s as studios reasserted control following expensive failures such as Heaven's Gate (1980), even as blockbusters like Jaws and Star Wars — made by New Hollywood directors themselves — reshaped the industry toward high-concept hits.
