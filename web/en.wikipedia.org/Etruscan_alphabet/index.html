The Etruscan alphabet was used by the Etruscans of ancient Italy from about 700 BC to write the Etruscan language. Derived from the Euboean Greek alphabet brought by colonists to Pithecusae and Cumae, it became the ancestor, through early Italic scripts, of the Latin alphabet.

Development

The earliest Etruscan abecedaria, such as the Marsiliana tablet (c. 700 BC), list 26 letters matching the Greek model alphabet. In practice, Etruscan phonology needed fewer: letters for voiced stops (beta, delta, gamma) were dropped or repurposed because Etruscan lacked voiced stops, while the Greek digamma was retained for /f/ and a new sign, shaped like an 8, later represented /f/ as well. Writing ran right to left in early texts.

Corpus and decipherment

About 13,000 Etruscan inscriptions survive, mostly short funerary and votive texts. Because the alphabet is Greek-derived, Etruscan can be transliterated readily, even though the language itself — a non-Indo-European isolate — remains only partly understood. The bilingual Pyrgi Tablets (Etruscan and Phoenician) are a key source.

Legacy

Neighbouring Italic peoples adapted the script; the Romans took their alphabet from an Etruscan-mediated form of the Greek alphabet, giving the Latin alphabet letters whose shapes still reflect Etruscan usage.
