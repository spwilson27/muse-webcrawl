Safe withdrawal rate

The safe withdrawal rate estimates how much a retiree can spend annually without exhausting savings.

The safe withdrawal rate (SWR) is the maximum rate, as a percentage of the initial value of an investment portfolio, at which a retiree can withdraw funds — adjusting for inflation each year — with a high probability of not depleting the portfolio over a planned retirement horizon, typically 30 years. It is a central concept in retirement planning and in the financial independence (FIRE) movement.

The 4% rule

The best-known SWR is the "4% rule," derived from William Bengen's 1994 analysis of historical U.S. market returns and popularized by the 1998 Trinity study by Cooley, Hubbard, and Walz. Bengen found that in every historical period he examined, a portfolio of 50–75% equities survived 30 years of withdrawals beginning at 4% of the initial balance and adjusted annually for inflation; the worst-case historical scenario (retiring in 1966) supported roughly 4.15%.

Criticisms and refinements

Critics note that historical U.S. returns may not repeat, that bond yields and equity valuations vary, that international retirees face different data, and that a fixed real withdrawal ignores changing spending needs and the option of cutting expenses after poor returns. Proposed refinements include lower rates of 3–3.5% for early retirees with longer horizons, variable percentage withdrawal, guardrail strategies that adjust spending with portfolio performance, and dynamic rules based on valuation measures such as the Shiller CAPE ratio. Sequence-of-returns risk — poor returns early in retirement — is the principal danger the SWR framework addresses.
