American Indian Movement (AIM)

The American Indian Movement (AIM) is a Native American grassroots movement founded in Minneapolis, Minnesota, in July 1968. Initially formed to address issues of poverty, housing, treaty rights and police harassment affecting urban Native Americans — many of whom had been displaced by the federal Indian termination and relocation policies of the 1950s — AIM grew into the most prominent organisation of the Red Power movement and an international advocate for Indigenous sovereignty.

Origins

AIM was modelled in part on the civil rights and Black Power movements of the 1960s. Its first activities included an "Indian patrol" that monitored police treatment of Native people in Minneapolis bars and streets, legal-defence work, and cultural programmes such as survival schools teaching Indigenous languages and traditions to urban Indian children.

Major actions

In 1969 AIM members supported the nineteen-month occupation of Alcatraz Island by Indians of All Tribes. In 1972 the organisation helped lead the Trail of Broken Treaties, a cross-country caravan to Washington, D.C., that culminated in the week-long occupation of the Bureau of Indian Affairs headquarters and presented a Twenty-Point programme demanding restoration of treaty-making and tribal sovereignty. Most famously, in February 1973 AIM activists and Oglala Lakota residents occupied the village of Wounded Knee on the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota — the site of the 1890 massacre — for 71 days in an armed standoff with federal forces, drawing worldwide attention to conditions on reservations and broken treaties.

Aftermath and legacy

The Wounded Knee occupation ended with a negotiated disarmament, but the following years on Pine Ridge were marked by violence, including the 1975 shootout at Oglala in which two FBI agents and one AIM member were killed; AIM activist Leonard Peltier was convicted in the agents' deaths, a conviction long disputed by supporters. Despite internal divisions and intense federal surveillance under COINTELPRO, AIM's activism contributed to the end of termination policy and to legislation including the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975), the American Indian Religious Freedom Act (1978) and the Indian Child Welfare Act (1978). AIM chapters and affiliated organisations continue to campaign on land, water, treaty and cultural issues.
