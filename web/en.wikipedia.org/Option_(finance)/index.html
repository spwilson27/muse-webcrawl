Option (finance)

In finance, an option is a contract that gives its owner the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset or instrument at a specified strike price on or before a specified date, depending on the style of the option. Options are derivatives, traded on exchanges such as the CBOE and over the counter, and are used for hedging, speculation and constructing tailored payoff profiles.

Types

A call option gives the right to buy; a put option gives the right to sell. American options may be exercised at any time before expiry; European options only at expiry; Bermudan options on specified dates. The buyer pays a premium to the seller (writer), whose potential loss on a naked call is theoretically unlimited. Combinations such as spreads, straddles and strangles combine options to express views on direction and volatility.

Valuation

Option prices depend on the underlying price, strike, time to expiry, volatility, interest rates and dividends. The Black–Scholes model (1973) and the binomial options pricing model provide standard valuation frameworks, while sensitivity measures known as "the Greeks" — delta, gamma, theta, vega and rho — quantify exposure to each factor. Implied volatility, recovered from market prices, is a key market indicator (e.g., the VIX index).

History and uses

Option-like contracts date to antiquity — Aristotle recounts Thales' use of olive-press options — and were traded in seventeenth-century Amsterdam and during the tulip mania. Standardised exchange trading began in Chicago in 1973. Employee stock options, real options in capital budgeting, and options embedded in bonds and insurance products extend the concept widely through finance.
