Capitulations of the Ottoman Empire

The capitulations of the Ottoman Empire were a series of grants (ahidnames) made by successive sultans to Christian nations, conferring rights and privileges on their subjects residing or trading within Ottoman territory. The term derives from the Latin capitula, the chapters into which the grants were arranged, not from "capitulation" in the sense of surrender.

History

Early grants were made to Italian city-states such as Genoa and Venice in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. The most famous were those granted to France under Francis I and Suleiman the Magnificent in the 1530s, which made France the protector of Catholics in the Levant. Similar privileges were later extended to England (1580), the Dutch Republic (1612), and other powers. The capitulations fixed low customs duties, granted extraterritorial legal jurisdiction through consular courts, and exempted foreign residents from many local taxes and laws.

Abolition

By the nineteenth century the capitulations had become a resented symbol of Ottoman weakness. The Ottoman government unilaterally abrogated them in 1914, and their abolition was confirmed internationally by the Treaty of Lausanne in 1923.
