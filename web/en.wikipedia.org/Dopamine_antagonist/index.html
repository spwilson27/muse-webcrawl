Dopamine antagonist

A dopamine antagonist, also known as an anti-dopaminergic or a dopamine receptor antagonist (DRA), is a type of drug which blocks dopamine receptors by receptor antagonism. Most antipsychotics are dopamine antagonists, and as such they have found use in treating schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and stimulant psychosis.

Medical uses

Dopamine antagonists are used as antipsychotics (including haloperidol, chlorpromazine, risperidone, and olanzapine), as antiemetics such as metoclopramide and domperidone, and in some cases for the treatment of Tourette syndrome, hiccups, and nausea associated with migraine. Certain agents, such as amisulpride at low doses, paradoxically enhance dopaminergic transmission.

Side effects

Blockade of dopamine receptors can cause extrapyramidal symptoms such as parkinsonism, akathisia, dystonia, and tardive dyskinesia, as well as hyperprolactinaemia due to blockade of dopamine's tonic inhibition of prolactin release in the pituitary. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a rare but potentially fatal reaction.

Pharmacology

Most clinically used dopamine antagonists act primarily at D2 receptors. The therapeutic potency of typical antipsychotics correlates closely with their affinity for D2 receptors, a finding that gave rise to the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia.
