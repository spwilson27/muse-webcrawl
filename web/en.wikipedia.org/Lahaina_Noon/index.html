Lahaina Noon

Lāhainā Noon (Hawaiian: Ka lā hainā, "cruel sun") is the phenomenon in Hawaii when the Sun culminates at the zenith at solar noon, passing directly overhead. At this moment, upright objects such as flagpoles and bollards cast no shadow, and sunlight falls vertically into wells and shafts. It is also called a zero shadow day.

Occurrence

The event occurs only within the tropics, where the Sun can pass directly overhead. In Hawaii — the only U.S. state in the tropics — it happens twice a year at each location, once in May as the Sun's declination moves north past the local latitude, and once again in July as it moves back south. Dates vary by island, occurring earlier in the season for more southerly locations.

Name and tradition

The name was selected in a 1990 contest sponsored by the Bishop Museum in Honolulu; "Lāhainā" is also the name of a town on Maui, though the phenomenon occurs throughout the Hawaiian Islands. A popular viewing spot is the Sky Gate sculpture in Honolulu, designed so that at Lāhainā Noon a perfect ring of light forms around its circular opening.
