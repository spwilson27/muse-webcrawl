Ketamine

Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic used medically for induction and maintenance of anesthesia. It is also used as a treatment for depression and pain management. Ketamine was first synthesized in 1962 by Calvin Stevens at Parke-Davis as a safer alternative to phencyclidine (PCP), and it entered clinical practice in 1970.

Medical uses

Ketamine induces a trance-like state providing pain relief, sedation, and amnesia while preserving spontaneous breathing and airway reflexes better than most anesthetics, making it valuable in emergency medicine, battlefield and veterinary practice, and paediatric procedures. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

At subanesthetic doses, ketamine produces rapid antidepressant effects. Its enantiomer esketamine (Spravato) was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2019 for treatment-resistant depression, and racemic ketamine is used off-label in clinics for depression, chronic pain, and other conditions.

Mechanism and effects

Ketamine works primarily as an antagonist of the NMDA receptor, disrupting glutamatergic neurotransmission and producing dissociation between the thalamocortical and limbic systems. Users may experience hallucinations, out-of-body sensations ("the k-hole" at high recreational doses), increased heart rate and blood pressure, and nausea.

Recreational use and harms

Ketamine is used recreationally as a club drug ("Special K"). Chronic heavy use can cause ulcerative cystitis ("ketamine bladder"), biliary problems, and cognitive impairment. It is a controlled substance in most countries, though its essential medical role has kept international scheduling comparatively light.
