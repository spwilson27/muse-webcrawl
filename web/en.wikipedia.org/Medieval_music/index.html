Medieval music

Medieval music encompasses the sacred and secular music of Western Europe during the Middle Ages, roughly from the fall of the Roman Empire in the 5th century to the early 15th century. It comprises the chant traditions of the Church, the rise of polyphony, and a rich culture of troubadour and minstrelsy song.

Sacred music

The dominant tradition was monophonic chant — plainchant — sung in Latin in the liturgy; the repertory standardised under the Carolingians became known as Gregorian chant. From the 9th century, singers began adding parallel voices (organum); by the 12th and 13th centuries the composers of Notre Dame in Paris, Léonin and Pérotin, created elaborate polyphony. The 14th-century Ars Nova of Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut brought rhythmic complexity and the isorhythmic motet.

Secular music

Troubadours in Occitania, trouvères in northern France, and Minnesänger in Germany sang courtly love songs to instruments such as the vielle and lute. Dancing was accompanied by estampies and other instrumental forms, though little instrumental music was written down. Hildegard von Bingen stands out as a major composer of sacred monophony outside the chant mainstream.

Notation and theory

Medieval musicians developed staff notation — Guido d'Arezzo's four-line staff and solmisation syllables in the 11th century — enabling music to be composed, taught, and transmitted in writing for the first time in the West. Modal theory classified melodies into church modes.
