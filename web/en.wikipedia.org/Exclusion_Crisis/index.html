Exclusion Crisis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Exclusion Crisis was a political crisis in England between 1679 and 1681 arising from attempts in Parliament to exclude James, Duke of York — the brother and heir presumptive of Charles II — from the succession to the throne because he was a Roman Catholic. Three Exclusion Bills were introduced in successive parliaments and all failed, but the struggle gave rise to the first recognizably modern political parties in England, the Whigs and the Tories.

Background

Suspicion of the court's Catholic sympathies grew after the secret Treaty of Dover (1670) and James's public conversion to Catholicism, confirmed when he resigned as Lord High Admiral in 1673 rather than swear the oaths required by the Test Act. In 1678 Titus Oates fabricated the "Popish Plot," an alleged Catholic conspiracy to murder Charles II, provoking anti-Catholic hysteria and a series of trials and executions.

The bills

The first Exclusion Bill passed the House of Commons in May 1679, but Charles dissolved Parliament before it could complete its passage. Two further parliaments, in 1680 and 1681, pressed the measure; the Oxford Parliament of March 1681 was dissolved after only a week when Charles refused to accept exclusion. The king, secretly subsidised by Louis XIV of France, resolved to rule without Parliament, and governed personally until his death in 1685. James duly succeeded as James II, but his Catholicizing policies provoked the Glorious Revolution of 1688, which settled the succession on Protestant terms in the Bill of Rights 1689.

Legacy

The crisis crystallized party organization: the exclusionists became known as Whigs and their court opponents as Tories, labels that structured English and later British politics for two centuries. It also entrenched the principle, later made explicit, that a Catholic could not inherit the Crown.
