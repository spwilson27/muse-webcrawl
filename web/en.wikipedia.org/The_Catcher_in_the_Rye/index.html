The Catcher in the Rye is a novel by American author J. D. Salinger that was partially published in serial form in 1945–46 and as a novel in 1951. It was originally intended for adults, but is now commonly assigned in American high schools for its themes of teen angst and alienation. It has been translated into almost all of the world's major languages; around one million copies are sold each year, with more than 65 million copies sold in total. The novel's protagonist, Holden Caulfield, has become an icon of teenage rebellion.

Plot

Holden, a sixteen-year-old expelled from his Pennsylvania prep school, Pencey, delays returning home to Manhattan and wanders New York City for several days — staying in a seedy hotel, meeting former classmates and a young prostitute, visiting museums, and slipping home to see his beloved younger sister Phoebe. Obsessed with the "phoniness" of the adult world and grieving his dead brother Allie, Holden fantasises about being a "catcher in the rye", saving children from falling off a cliff at the edge of a rye field — a mishearing of Robert Burns's "Comin' Thro' the Rye". The frame narration reveals he is recounting events from a rest home or sanatorium.

Reception and controversies

The novel was an immediate bestseller and remains canonical, appearing on the Modern Library's 100 best English-language novels and Time's 100 best English-language novels (1923–2005). Its frank language and treatment of adolescent sexuality made it one of the most frequently challenged books in American schools and libraries. It has also been notoriously associated with several criminals, most infamously Mark David Chapman, who murdered John Lennon in 1980 while carrying a copy.
