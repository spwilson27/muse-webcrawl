Caenorhabditis elegans

Caenorhabditis elegans is a free-living, transparent nematode (roundworm), about 1 mm in length, that lives in temperate soil environments. It is the type species of its genus. In 1963, Sydney Brenner proposed it as a model organism for the investigation primarily of neural development; since 1974, when Brenner's landmark paper on its genetics appeared, it has been used extensively in developmental biology, neuroscience, ageing research and genomics. In 1998 it became the first multicellular organism to have its whole genome sequenced.

Biology

The species has two sexes: self-fertilising hermaphrodites (XX) and males (XO). The adult hermaphrodite has exactly 959 somatic cells, whose complete lineage — from fertilised egg to adult — was mapped by John Sulston and colleagues, the first such fate map for any animal. The hermaphrodite nervous system contains exactly 302 neurons, whose full wiring diagram (the connectome) was published by J. G. White and colleagues in 1986 — the first complete connectome. Under food scarcity or crowding, larvae enter the stress-resistant dauer stage, controlled by insulin/IGF-1 signalling, a pathway central to research on ageing; mutations in the daf-2 insulin receptor gene can double lifespan.

Research significance

Work in C. elegans led to the discovery of programmed cell death (apoptosis) genes — recognised by the 2002 Nobel Prize to Brenner, Sulston and H. Robert Horvitz — and of RNA interference, discovered by Andrew Fire and Craig Mello (Nobel Prize 2006), who showed that double-stranded RNA silences genes in the worm. Green fluorescent protein (GFP) was first expressed in the worm's touch neurons by Martin Chalfie (Nobel Prize 2008). The worm is easy to culture on agar plates feeding on E. coli, can be frozen indefinitely, and has even flown on space missions, including experiments aboard the International Space Station; wild worms were among the few survivors recovered from the wreckage of the Space Shuttle Columbia in 2003.
