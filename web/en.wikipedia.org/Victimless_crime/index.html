Victimless crime

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A victimless crime is an illegal act that typically is the result of a consensual transaction between two or more adults, and involves no direct harm to a third party. Because there is no identifiable victim who has been wronged against their will, such offences are sometimes described as crimes without a complainant.

Examples commonly cited include recreational drug possession and use, gambling, prostitution between consenting adults, public nudity, vagrancy, and historically offences such as sodomy and violations of Prohibition-era liquor laws. The term gained currency in the 1960s, notably through Edwin Schur's book Crimes Without Victims (1965).

Debate

Critics of criminalization argue, following John Stuart Mill's harm principle, that the state should prohibit only conduct that harms others, and that criminalizing consensual behaviour wastes enforcement resources, fosters black markets and organized crime, and infringes personal liberty. Advocates of prohibition respond that such activities impose indirect social costs — on families, public health, and communities — and that "victimless" is therefore a misnomer, since society or the participants themselves may still be harmed.

Legal reforms in many jurisdictions have decriminalized or legalized some formerly victimless offences, including cannabis possession, gambling, and (in some places) sex work, while others remain heavily penalized.
