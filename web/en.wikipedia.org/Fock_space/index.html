Fock space

In quantum mechanics, a Fock space is an algebraic construction used in quantum field theory to represent a state of a system with a variable or unknown number of particles. It is named after the Soviet physicist Vladimir Fock, who introduced it in 1932 for the quantisation of the electromagnetic field.

Definition

Formally, the Fock space over a one-particle Hilbert space H is the direct sum of tensor products of H with itself: the zero-particle sector (the vacuum, a one-dimensional space), the one-particle space H, the two-particle space H ⊗ H, and so on. For identical particles the tensor products are symmetrised for bosons or antisymmetrised for fermions, giving the bosonic and fermionic Fock spaces respectively.

Creation and annihilation operators

States are manipulated with creation and annihilation operators, which add or remove a particle in a given mode. Bosonic operators obey commutation relations and fermionic operators anticommutation relations — the algebra at the heart of second quantisation. A state with a definite number of particles in specified modes is called a Fock state or number state.

Applications

Fock spaces underpin quantum optics (where number states of the electromagnetic field describe photons), many-body theory, and perturbative quantum field theory, where Feynman diagrams track particles created and annihilated between Fock-space states. They are also used in quantum chemistry and condensed-matter physics for systems of interacting electrons and phonons.
