System of government

A system of government is the set of institutional arrangements by which a state organises political power — in particular the relationship between the executive, the legislature, and the judiciary, and between central and regional levels of authority. Political scientists classify systems along several partly independent dimensions.

Executive–legislative relations

In a parliamentary system (such as the United Kingdom, Germany, and India), the government is formed from and accountable to the legislature and can be removed by a vote of no confidence; the head of state and head of government are usually different people. In a presidential system (such as the United States, Brazil, and Nigeria), the president is independently elected, serves a fixed term, and is both head of state and head of government. Semi-presidential systems (France, Portugal) combine an elected president with a prime minister responsible to parliament.

Territorial organisation

States may be unitary, with power concentrated in the central government, or federal, with constitutionally protected regional governments, as in the United States, Canada, Germany, and Australia. A third family of distinctions concerns the head of state: republics elect or appoint theirs, while monarchies may be constitutional or absolute. These axes combine freely: Canada is a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy, while China is a unitary one-party republic.
