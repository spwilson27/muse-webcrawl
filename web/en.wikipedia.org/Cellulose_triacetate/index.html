Cellulose triacetate

Cellulose triacetate, also known simply as triacetate or CTA, is a chemical compound produced from cellulose and a source of acetate esters, typically acetic anhydride. In triacetate, essentially all of the hydroxyl groups of the cellulose glucose units are acetylated (a degree of substitution near 2.8–3.0), distinguishing it from cellulose diacetate (secondary acetate), in which roughly two-thirds are substituted.

Properties and production

Triacetate is produced by fully acetylating cellulose from wood pulp or cotton linters. It is heat-resistant, less moisture-absorbent than diacetate, and retains creases and pleats well, but it is soluble in fewer solvents (historically methylene chloride) and harder to dye, requiring special disperse dyes and carriers.

Applications

As a fibre, triacetate (sold under names such as Arnel) was used for dresses, linings, and permanently pleated fabrics. Its most important historical use was as a photographic film base: introduced as a "safety film" replacement for flammable cellulose nitrate, cellulose triacetate became the standard base for still and motion-picture film from the late 1940s onward. It is also used for LCD polarizer protective films and membranes. Triacetate film is subject to "vinegar syndrome", an autocatalytic deacetylation that releases acetic acid and shrinks the base as archives age.
