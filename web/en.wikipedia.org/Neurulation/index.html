Neurulation

Primary neurulation converts the flat neural plate into the hollow neural tube.

Neurulation refers to the folding process in vertebrate embryos, which includes the transformation of the neural plate into the neural tube. The embryo at this stage is termed the neurula. The process begins when the notochord induces the overlying ectoderm to thicken and form the neural plate, and it is complete by the end of the fourth week in human development.

Primary neurulation

In primary neurulation, cells surrounding the neural plate proliferate and invaginate: the plate's lateral edges elevate as neural folds, a midline neural groove deepens, and the folds converge and fuse at the dorsal midline to create the neural tube. Bending is driven by hinge points — a median hinge point above the notochord and paired dorsolateral hinge points — and by apical constriction of neuroepithelial cells. Fusion begins at multiple sites along the axis in mammals ("zippering"), and the resulting tube separates from the surface ectoderm as neural crest cells delaminate and migrate away.

Secondary neurulation

In the caudal region, the neural tube forms instead by secondary neurulation: mesenchymal cells of the tail bud condense into a solid medullary cord that cavitates and fuses with the primary tube, producing the distal spinal cord.

Defects

Failure of closure causes neural tube defects: failure at the cranial end produces anencephaly, and failure caudally produces spina bifida. Folate deficiency is a major environmental risk factor, and periconceptional folic acid supplementation or food fortification greatly reduces incidence. Planar cell polarity genes such as VANGL1 and VANGL2 and the sonic hedgehog and BMP signalling pathways are essential to the process.
