Vitrectomy is a surgery to remove some or all of the vitreous humour from the eye. Anterior vitrectomy entails removing small portions from the front structures of the eye, often because these structures are to be addressed surgically; pars plana vitrectomy, the commonest form, removes gel from the posterior segment through small incisions in the pars plana of the ciliary body.

Indications

Vitrectomy is performed for retinal detachment, vitreous haemorrhage (for example from diabetic retinopathy), macular hole, epiretinal membrane (macular pucker), severe floaters, retained lens fragments after cataract surgery, endophthalmitis, and intraocular foreign bodies.

Procedure

Through two or three ports the surgeon inserts an infusion cannula that maintains the eye's shape with balanced salt solution, a light pipe, and a vitreous cutter that cuts and aspirates the gel. Modern systems use high cut rates and small-gauge (23, 25 or 27 gauge) transconjunctival trocars that usually need no sutures. The removed vitreous may be replaced with saline, a gas bubble (such as SF6 or C3F8) or silicone oil to hold the retina in place while it heals. Adjuncts include endolaser photocoagulation, membrane peeling, and air–fluid exchange.

History

The modern operation was pioneered by Robert Machemer in the early 1970s, who built the first vitreous infusion suction cutter with Jean-Marie Parel and performed the first human pars plana vitrectomy in 1970. Instrumentation has shrunk steadily from the original 17-gauge instrument to today's microincisional systems, expanding the range of treatable conditions.
