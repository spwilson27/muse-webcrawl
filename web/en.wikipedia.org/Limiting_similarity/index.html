Limiting similarity

Limiting similarity is a concept in theoretical ecology concerning the maximum level of niche overlap — or similarity in resource use — that two competing species can sustain while still coexisting. It derives from the competitive exclusion principle associated with Gause: complete competitors cannot coexist indefinitely, so coexisting species must differ by at least some amount.

Theory

The concept was formalised by Robert MacArthur and Richard Levins in 1967 using Lotka–Volterra competition models along a one-dimensional resource spectrum. In their model, species with niche width w can coexist only if the distance d between their niche centres exceeds a limit related to w (d/w ≳ 1), suggesting a roughly constant ratio governing how similar species can be — an idea echoed in G. Evelyn Hutchinson's observations that coexisting species often differ in feeding-apparatus size by a factor near 1.3.

Debate

Limiting similarity became one of the most contested ideas in community ecology. Critics argued that the classical models depend on special assumptions (equilibrium, symmetric competition, logistic growth) and that environmental variability, predation and spatial heterogeneity allow much more similar species to persist; neutral theory further questioned whether niche differences are required at all. Modern coexistence theory reframes the idea in terms of the balance between stabilising niche differences and average fitness differences, under which limiting similarity emerges only when stabilising mechanisms are weak.
