Polder
DefinitionLow-lying tract of land enclosed by dikes, artificially drained
Most associated countryNetherlands
Key elementsDikes, drainage canals, pumping stations (historically windmills)
Famous examplesBeemster, Noordoostpolder, Flevoland, Zuiderzee Works

A polder is a low-lying tract of land that forms an artificial hydrological entity, enclosed by embankments known as dikes. Polders lie at or below the level of the surrounding water — sea, lake or river — and remain dry only because water is continuously drained or pumped out. The ground level inside a polder subsides over time as drained peat soils oxidise and compact, so most polders lie progressively lower than their surroundings.

The Netherlands

Polders are iconic of the Netherlands, where roughly a third of the country lies below sea level and some 17% of its land area has been reclaimed from the sea, marshes and lakes over roughly a thousand years. Water boards (waterschappen), among the oldest democratic institutions in the Netherlands, were created to maintain the dikes and drainage of individual polders. Early drainage used windmills driving scoop wheels and Archimedes' screws; steam, diesel and electric pumping stations later took over. Famous polders include the Beemster (1612, a UNESCO World Heritage Site), the Haarlemmermeer (1852), and the vast 20th-century Zuiderzee Works polders of Flevoland, drained from the IJsselmeer, whose land lies up to about 6 metres below sea level.

Elsewhere

Polders also exist in Belgium (along the coast and the Scheldt), northern Germany, Denmark, France (the marais poitevin), the English Fens, the Po delta in Italy, and around river deltas in Bangladesh, Japan, China and Guyana, where Dutch engineers exported the technique during the colonial era.

Use and risks

Polder land is typically fertile and used for intensive agriculture, horticulture and increasingly urban development. The permanent risks are flooding by dike breach or pump failure, saltwater intrusion, and continuing soil subsidence, which climate-driven sea-level rise aggravates.
