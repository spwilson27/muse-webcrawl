The Xia dynasty (Chinese: 夏朝) is the first dynasty in traditional Chinese historiography. According to tradition, it was established by Yu the Great around 2070 BCE after the legendary sage-king Shun abdicated in his favour, and it lasted until about 1600 BCE, when it was overthrown by Tang of Shang.

Traditional account

Ancient texts such as the Records of the Grand Historian and the Bamboo Annals list seventeen Xia kings spanning roughly four and a half centuries. Yu is credited with taming the great floods of the Yellow River through years of dredging and channel works. His son Qi succeeded him, an event traditionally seen as the beginning of hereditary kingship in China. The last king, Jie, is depicted as a tyrant whose corruption and cruelty justified his overthrow by the Shang.

Historicity

No contemporary written records of the Xia have been found, and its existence was long debated. Many archaeologists associate the Xia with the Bronze Age Erlitou culture (c. 1900–1500 BCE) of the middle Yellow River valley in Henan, where a large urban site with palatial foundations and bronze-casting workshops was excavated near Luoyang. Other scholars link the Xia to the earlier Longshan culture. The question remains a major topic of Chinese archaeology, partly investigated by the state-sponsored Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project.
