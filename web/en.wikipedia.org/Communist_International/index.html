The Communist International (Comintern), also known as the Third International, was an international organisation founded in Moscow in March 1919 that advocated world communism. It was controlled by the Communist Party of the Soviet Union and sought to coordinate revolutionary activity by communist parties around the world, with the stated goal of overthrowing the international bourgeoisie and creating an international Soviet republic.

The Comintern held seven World Congresses between 1919 and 1935 and was formally dissolved by Joseph Stalin in 1943, partly as a gesture to the Soviet Union's wartime allies.

History

Founding

The First International (International Workingmen's Association) had been founded by Karl Marx in 1864, and the Second International in 1889. The collapse of the Second International amid the First World War — when most socialist parties supported their own governments' war efforts — convinced Vladimir Lenin and the Bolsheviks that a new, revolutionary international was required. The founding congress met in Moscow from 2 to 6 March 1919, attended by delegates from some 30 countries, many of them small groups rather than mass parties.

To join, parties were required to accept the Twenty-one Conditions adopted at the Second Congress in 1920, which demanded centralised, disciplined parties modelled on the Bolsheviks, the expulsion of reformists, and subordination of national sections to the Comintern's Executive Committee (ECCI).

Early years and the united front

After the failure of revolutionary uprisings in Germany and elsewhere, the Comintern adopted the "united front" tactic in 1921, calling for temporary cooperation with reformist socialist parties and trade unions. Under its first chairman, Grigory Zinoviev (1919–1926), the organisation also pursued the "Bolshevisation" of its member parties, tightening Moscow's control.

The Third Period and social fascism

At the Sixth Congress in 1928, under Nikolai Bukharin and then increasingly under Stalin's direction, the Comintern entered the so-called Third Period, predicting an imminent final crisis of capitalism. Social democratic parties were denounced as "social fascists" and treated as the main enemy — a line widely blamed for dividing the German left in the face of the rise of Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party.

The Popular Front

After Hitler came to power in 1933, the Seventh Congress in 1935, led by Georgi Dimitrov, reversed course and adopted the Popular Front strategy, urging alliances of communists, socialists, liberals and other anti-fascist forces. Popular Front governments were elected in Spain and France in 1936.

Dissolution

During the Second World War, Stalin dissolved the Comintern on 15 May 1943, announcing that national communist parties were mature enough to act independently. The move also reassured Britain and the United States, then the USSR's allies. Its functions were partly continued after the war by the Cominform (1947–1956) and by the International Department of the Soviet party's Central Committee.
