Heliades

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, the Heliades (Ancient Greek: Ἡλιάδες, "daughters of Helios") were the daughters of the sun god Helios and the Oceanid Clymene, and sisters of Phaethon. Their names are usually given as Phaethusa ("radiance"), Lampetia ("shining"), and Aegiale or Aegle; some accounts list as many as seven, adding Merope, Helie, Phoebe, and Dioxippe.

Myth

When their brother Phaethon begged to drive Helios's sun chariot for a day, lost control of the horses, and was struck down by Zeus's thunderbolt to save the earth from burning, his body fell into the river Eridanus (identified with the Po in Italy). The Heliades gathered at the riverbank and mourned him ceaselessly for four months. In Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 2), the gods took pity on their grief: their feet rooted into the ground, bark grew over their bodies, and they were transformed into poplar trees. Their tears continued to fall into the river, hardening in the sunlight into drops of amber — the myth explained the origin of the amber found along the Po and traded from the Baltic.

Hyginus and other authors preserve variations in which the sisters were transformed for having helped Phaethon harness the chariot against their father's will.

Names and sources

The story appears in Aeschylus's lost tragedy Heliades, is alluded to by Euripides in Hippolytus, and receives its most famous telling in Ovid. The myth links the Heliades to the origins of amber (ēlektron in Greek), a substance the ancients connected with the sun because of its golden colour and electrostatic properties.
