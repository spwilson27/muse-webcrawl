Baccalauréat

The baccalauréat (French pronunciation: [bakaloʁea], often known in France colloquially as the bac), is a French national academic qualification that students can obtain at the completion of their secondary education (at the end of the lycée) by meeting certain requirements. Though it has only existed in its present form since the mid-20th century, it has its origins in the first universities of the Middle Ages.

History

The baccalauréat was introduced by Napoleon in 1808, modelled on the medieval university degree, and originally examined orally. Over time it became the standard school-leaving and university-entrance examination in France and in countries influenced by the French system.

Types and reform

The general baccalauréat is organized in three broad series historically known as L (literature), ES (economics and social sciences), and S (sciences); a reform implemented from 2019–2021 replaced these series with a common core plus specialty courses (spécialités), with a substantial share of continuous assessment. There are also technological (e.g. STMG, STI2D) and professional baccalauréats. The qualification is broadly comparable to British A levels or the German Abitur, and in some countries it underpins the separate International Baccalaureate namesake tradition.
