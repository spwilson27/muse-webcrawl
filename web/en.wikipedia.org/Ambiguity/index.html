Ambiguity

Ambiguity is the property of a word, phrase, sentence, image or situation that allows more than one interpretation. Where a specific and distinct meaning can be identified it is the opposite of vagueness: an ambiguous expression has two or more definite but incompatible meanings. Ambiguity occurs in language, logic, law, mathematics, computing and perception, and is exploited deliberately in literature, humour and rhetoric.

Types

Lexical ambiguity arises when a single word has multiple meanings ("bank" as a river bank or a financial institution). Syntactic (structural) ambiguity arises from sentence structure, as in "I saw the man with the telescope". Semantic ambiguity involves quantifier scope, and pragmatic ambiguity depends on context. Ambiguous figures such as the Necker cube and the duck–rabbit illusion demonstrate perceptual ambiguity.

Significance

In law and contracts, ambiguity is resolved by rules of interpretation, often against the drafter. In computing, ambiguous grammars complicate parsing. Writers from Shakespeare to James Joyce have used ambiguity as a source of richness, while philosophers and linguists study how hearers resolve it using context. William Empson's Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930) was a landmark of literary criticism.
