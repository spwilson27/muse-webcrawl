Sextus Empiricus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sextus Empiricus (Greek: Σέξτος ὁ Ἐμπειρικός; c. 160 – c. 210 AD) was a Greek physician and philosopher who lived in the Roman period, associated with Alexandria, Rome, and Athens. He is the most important surviving source for Pyrrhonian scepticism: his Outlines of Pyrrhonism and Against the Dogmatists (part of the larger work Against the Professors) preserve the arguments of the school founded by Pyrrho of Elis.

Life

Little is known of Sextus's life. His name suggests he belonged to the Empirical school of medicine, whose anti-theoretical attitude fitted naturally with philosophical scepticism. Diogenes Laërtius lists him in the succession of Pyrrhonist teachers. His works probably date to the late second century AD.

Works

The Outlines of Pyrrhonism (Πυρρωνεῖαι ὑποτυπώσεις) gives a systematic account of sceptical method: the sceptic opposes arguments and appearances to one another, arrives at suspension of judgement (epoché), and thereby attains tranquillity (ataraxia). It presents the Ten Modes of Aenesidemus, the Five Modes of Agrippa, and detailed attacks on logic, physics, and ethics as dogmatically conceived. Against the Professors (Adversus Mathematicos) extends the critique to grammar, rhetoric, geometry, arithmetic, astrology, and music.

Influence

Sextus's writings were lost to the Latin West for much of the Middle Ages, but Latin translations printed in the 1560s made them widely available, with a dramatic effect on early modern philosophy. His arguments shaped the "Pyrrhonian crisis" of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, influenced Michel de Montaigne — whose tower bore slogans drawn from Sextus — and supplied the problem of the criterion confronting Descartes and later Hume.
