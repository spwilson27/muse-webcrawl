Cooperativity

Cooperativity is a phenomenon displayed by enzymes and receptors that have multiple binding sites, in which the binding of a ligand at one site alters the affinity of the remaining sites for the same ligand. When binding increases subsequent affinity, the cooperativity is positive; when it decreases it, the cooperativity is negative; if the sites are independent, the system is non-cooperative.

Haemoglobin as the classic example

The textbook case is haemoglobin, whose four subunits bind oxygen sequentially. Binding of the first oxygen molecule shifts the protein from its tense (T) state toward the relaxed (R) state, increasing the affinity of the remaining subunits. The result is a sigmoidal (S-shaped) oxygen dissociation curve, which allows haemoglobin to load oxygen efficiently in the lungs and release it in respiring tissues. Myoglobin, with a single binding site, shows the hyperbolic curve expected of a non-cooperative protein.

Quantifying cooperativity

The Hill equation and its coefficient, the Hill coefficient (nH), provide an empirical measure: nH = 1 indicates no cooperativity, values above 1 indicate positive cooperativity, and the coefficient cannot exceed the number of binding sites. Two classical mechanistic models account for the effect — the concerted MWC model of Monod, Wyman and Changeux (1965), in which all subunits switch state simultaneously, and the sequential KNF model of Koshland, Némethy and Filmer (1966), in which binding induces conformational change site by site.

Other contexts

Cooperativity also describes interactions in supramolecular chemistry and self-assembly, cooperative binding of transcription factors to DNA, and — in a distinct but related usage — the collective binding behaviour in chelation, where multidentate ligands bind metal ions with enhanced stability.
