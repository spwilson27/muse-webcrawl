Self-determination refers to a people's right to form its own political entity. As a principle of international law, it holds that peoples, based on respect for the principle of equal rights and fair equality of opportunity, have the right to freely choose their sovereignty and international political status with no interference. The concept originated in the Enlightenment and the American and French revolutions, and was advanced after the First World War by both Woodrow Wilson — in his Fourteen Points — and by Vladimir Lenin, in rival liberal and socialist forms.

Legal status

The Charter of the United Nations (1945) lists developing friendly relations "based on respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples" among its purposes. The principle became a legal right in common Article 1 of the International Covenants on Civil and Political Rights and on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (1966): "All peoples have the right of self-determination. By virtue of that right they freely determine, without external interference, their political status and pursue their economic, social and cultural development." The UN General Assembly's 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples underpinned the decolonisation that created dozens of new states in Africa and Asia.

Internal and external self-determination

Scholars distinguish internal self-determination — a people's pursuit of its political, economic, social, and cultural development within an existing state, including representative government and minority rights — from external self-determination, the creation of an independent state through secession or independence. International law strongly protects existing states' territorial integrity, so an external right is generally accepted for peoples under colonial rule or foreign occupation, and is contested in other cases. Disputes over Kosovo, Catalonia, Western Sahara, and Indigenous peoples' rights all turn on the principle's scope.
