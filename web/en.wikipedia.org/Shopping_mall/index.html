Shopping mall

A shopping mall (or simply mall) is a large indoor or open-air shopping complex containing a variety of retail stores and services, typically anchored by department stores and managed as a single property. The modern enclosed, climate-controlled mall was pioneered in the United States in the 1950s — Southdale Center in Edina, Minnesota (1956), designed by the Austrian-born architect Victor Gruen, is generally considered the first — and malls became the dominant retail and social form of suburban North America before spreading worldwide.

History

Precursors include ancient marketplaces and bazaars (Istanbul's Grand Bazaar, 15th century), Parisian arcades and London's Burlington Arcade, and the great department stores. After the Second World War, mass car ownership and suburbanisation in the United States created demand for suburban shopping centres with free parking; early open-air centres gave way to Gruen's enclosed, inward-facing design with a central court, intended (idealistically) as a civic heart for suburbia. The format peaked in the US in the 1980s–90s — the Mall of America in Minnesota (1992) long ranked among the largest — and was exported globally, producing mega-malls in Canada (West Edmonton Mall), the Gulf states, China and Southeast Asia, where malls continue to grow as mixed retail-leisure destinations.

Design and economics

Malls are planned around anchor tenants (large department stores that draw footfall and pay lower rents), an in-line mix of chain and independent shops, food courts, and controlled interior environments with no weather, traffic or — traditionally — clocks and few exits, encouraging dwell time. Leases typically combine base rent with a percentage of sales. Classification ranges from neighbourhood centres to super-regional malls of more than 800,000 sq ft. The inward-looking design, private ownership and rules of conduct have made the mall a contested "pseudo-public" space in urban studies.

Decline and reinvention

From the 2000s, e-commerce, the bankruptcy of anchor chains, and oversupply produced thousands of failing American malls — the "dead mall" phenomenon — many demolished or converted to housing, offices, schools or logistics. Surviving malls have shifted toward luxury retail, dining and entertainment ("experiential" retail); meanwhile mall construction continues in Asia and the Middle East, where malls serve as air-conditioned public leisure space.
