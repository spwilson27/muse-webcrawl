Saint Patrick

Saint Patrick (Latin: Patricius; Irish: Pádraig) was a fifth-century Romano-British Christian missionary and bishop in Ireland. Known as the "Apostle of Ireland", he is the primary patron saint of Ireland, along with Brigid of Kildare and Columba. The dates of Patrick's life are uncertain, but he is generally thought to have been active as a missionary in Ireland during the fifth century.

Sources

Two Latin works survive that are generally accepted as having been written by Patrick himself: the Confessio, a spiritual autobiography, and the Letter to the Soldiers of Coroticus, denouncing the mistreatment of Irish Christians by British raiders. These are the most reliable sources for his life, though they give few dates or place names.

Early life and captivity

Patrick was born in Roman Britain into a wealthy family; his father Calpurnius was a deacon and a decurion, and his grandfather a priest. At about sixteen he was captured by Irish raiders and taken to Ireland as a slave, where he worked as a herdsman, traditionally on Slemish Mountain in County Antrim. After six years he escaped, following a dream that told him his ship was ready, and returned to his family in Britain. There he received religious training and was ordained.

Return to Ireland

Patrick recounts a vision — sometimes called the "Voice of the Irish" — in which the Irish people called him back to walk among them once more. He returned to Ireland as a bishop and missionary bishop, preaching Christianity in the north and west of the island, where it had never been preached before. Tradition credits him with baptizing thousands, ordaining priests, and founding churches and monasteries.

Writings

The Confessio defends Patrick's mission against critics in Britain and recounts his captivity, calling, and inner life. Patrick's Breastplate (Lorica), the hymn beginning "I bind unto myself today", is traditionally attributed to him but is likely of a later date.

Legends

Later tradition surrounds Patrick with famous legends: that he used the three-leaved shamrock to explain the doctrine of the Trinity; that he banished all snakes from Ireland by driving them into the sea (Ireland never had snakes in the post-glacial period); and that he lit the Paschal fire on the Hill of Slane in defiance of the High King at Tara. His legendary contest with druids at Tara is a centrepiece of medieval Irish hagiography.

Saint Patrick's Day

Patrick's feast day, 17 March — traditionally the date of his death — is celebrated as Saint Patrick's Day, a public holiday in Ireland and Northern Ireland and a widely observed cultural celebration of Ireland around the world, marked by parades, green clothing, and public festivities.
