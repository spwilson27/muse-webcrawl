Front velocity

Front velocity
DefinitionSpeed of the leading edge (first precursor) of a wave
Upper boundSpeed of light in vacuum, c
RelatedPhase velocity, group velocity, signal velocity

The front velocity is the speed at which the very first, leading part of a wave — its earliest precursor — propagates through a medium. It describes the advance of the absolute front of a disturbance that begins at a definite time, such as a suddenly switched-on signal.

Definition and significance

Wave propagation involves several distinct velocities: the phase velocity of individual crests, the group velocity of a wave packet, and the front velocity of the first onset. In media with anomalous dispersion, phase and group velocities can exceed the speed of light or even become negative, which at first sight seems to threaten causality. The front velocity resolves the paradox: analysis by Arnold Sommerfeld and Léon Brillouin showed that the true front of a signal — the point before which the field is exactly zero — always travels at the vacuum speed of light, c, regardless of the medium, so no information outruns light.

Precursors

The faint forerunners that make up the front are called Sommerfeld and Brillouin precursors. Experiments with fast light and slow light in atomic vapours confirm that while pulse peaks may be reshaped and shifted, the information-carrying front remains bounded by c.
