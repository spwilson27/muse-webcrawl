Apeiron (cosmology)

Apeiron (/əˈpaɪrɒn/; ἄπειρον) is a Greek word meaning "(that which is) unlimited," "boundless," "infinite," or "indefinite," from ἀ- a-, "without," and πεῖραρ peirar, "end, limit," the Ionic Greek form of πέρας peras, "end, limit, border." The apeiron is central to the cosmological theory created by Anaximander, a 6th-century BC pre-Socratic Greek philosopher whose work is mostly lost. From the few existing fragments, we learn that he believed the beginning or first principle (ἀρχή, arkhē) is an endless, unlimited mass (apeiron), subject to neither old age nor decay, which perpetually yields fresh materials from which everything we can perceive is derived.

Anaximander's cosmology

Anaximander proposed that the universe originates from the apeiron, a substance without fixed qualities or limits. According to his theory, the opposites — hot and cold, wet and dry — separated out from the apeiron, giving rise to the ordered world. The world itself was temporary and would eventually dissolve back into the apeiron, in keeping with the sole surviving fragment of his work:

Whence things have their origin, there their destruction happens as it is ordained. For they give justice and compensation to one another for their injustice according to the ordering of time.

Interpretations

Scholars debate whether the apeiron is a spatially infinite substance, an indefinite or indeterminate kind of matter, or simply something ageless and inexhaustible. Aristotle discussed the apeiron in his Physics, reporting Anaximander's view that the first principle is neither water nor any other determinate element, but a different, indefinite nature from which all the heavens and the worlds arise. Later philosophers, including Anaximenes and the Pythagoreans, also employed the concept of the unlimited in their systems.
