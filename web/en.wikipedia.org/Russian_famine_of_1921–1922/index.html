Russian famine of 1921–1922

The Russian famine of 1921–1922, also known as the Povolzhye famine, was a severe famine in the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic that began in the summer of 1921 and lasted into 1922. It centred on the Volga and Ural river regions and affected an estimated 30 million people; historians commonly estimate around five million deaths. The famine resulted from the combined effects of drought, the devastation of the Russian Civil War, and the Bolshevik policy of prodrazverstka — forced requisitioning of grain that stripped peasants of seed and food reserves.

Causes

A drought in 1921 devastated harvests across the Volga basin. Years of warfare had destroyed transport, livestock, and manpower, while requisitioning squads seized grain even from famine-stricken districts. In the spring of 1921 peasant uprisings, such as the Tambov Rebellion, protested the seizures, and the Kronstadt rebellion pushed Lenin to replace requisitioning with a fixed tax in kind as part of the New Economic Policy — but the change came too late to avert disaster in the Volga.

International relief

The writer Maxim Gorky issued a famous appeal "to all honest people" for help in July 1921. The American Relief Administration under Herbert Hoover became the largest relief operation, feeding over ten million people a day at its peak in 1922, alongside Fridtjof Nansen's missions for European relief organisations, Quaker aid, and others. American aid, combined with a better harvest and the end of requisitioning, brought the famine to an end by 1923. The episode left deep marks: epidemics of typhus and cholera accompanied starvation, and there were widespread reports of cannibalism in the worst-hit districts.
