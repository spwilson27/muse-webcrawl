Larvikite

Larvikite is a variety of monzonite or syenite, an igneous rock notable for the presence of ternary feldspars — mixtures of sodium, calcium, and potassium feldspar — which produce a striking blue iridescence known as schiller or labradorescence. It takes its name from the town of Larvik in Vestfold county, Norway, near which it is quarried in the Larvik plutonic complex of the Oslo Rift.

Geology

Larvikite crystallised from magma during the Permian rifting of the Oslo graben, around 292 million years ago. Its feldspar crystals are an intergrowth (perthite-like, more precisely a ternary feldspar) that scatters light in shimmering blue, silver, and green flashes as the viewing angle changes. The rock may contain olivine, augite, nepheline, and apatite as accessory minerals.

Use

Because it is durable, takes a high polish, and has a distinctive appearance, larvikite is one of Norway's most important natural stone exports, sold commercially under names such as "Blue Pearl" and "Emerald Pearl" granite (though it is not a true granite). It is used for building facades, countertops, monuments, and interior decoration worldwide; larvikite famously clads buildings such as the United Nations headquarters in New York and numerous banks and pubs in the United Kingdom.
