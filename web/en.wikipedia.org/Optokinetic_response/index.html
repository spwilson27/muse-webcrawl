Optokinetic response

The optokinetic response (OKR), also called optokinetic nystagmus (OKN), is a reflex eye movement that stabilises images on the retina when the whole visual scene moves — for example when watching the landscape from a train window. It combines a slow following movement in the direction of the stimulus with a fast resetting saccade in the opposite direction, and works together with the vestibulo–ocular reflex to keep gaze steady during self-motion.

Physiology

Motion of the retinal image is detected by direction-selective retinal ganglion cells projecting to the accessory optic system and the nucleus of the optic tract, with cortical contributions from motion areas such as MT and MST in primates. The reflex develops early in infancy and its presence, symmetry and quality are informative about the visual pathway.

Clinical and research use

Clinicians elicit OKN with a rotating striped drum or moving tape to test visual acuity in infants and non-verbal patients, to detect malingering in claimed blindness (a person who can see will show the reflex), and to localise lesions: asymmetries occur with parietal or occipital damage and in congenital strabismus. In research animals, especially zebrafish and mice, the optokinetic (and optomotor) response is a standard assay for visual function and for screening mutants affecting vision.
