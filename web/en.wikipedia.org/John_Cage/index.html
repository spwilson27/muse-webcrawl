John Milton Cage Jr. (5 September 1912 – 12 August 1992) was an American composer, music theorist, artist and writer. A pioneer of indeterminacy in music, electroacoustic music and the non-standard use of instruments, he was one of the leading figures of the post-war avant-garde. His best-known work is 4′33″ (1952), in which performers do not play, and the audience hears only the ambient sound of the hall.

Early work and the prepared piano

Cage studied briefly with Arnold Schoenberg in Los Angeles, who called him "not a composer, but an inventor — of genius." In 1938, needing percussive sounds in a small theatre, Cage invented the prepared piano — a piano with screws, bolts and rubber placed between its strings — and wrote landmark pieces for it, culminating in the Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48). His long collaboration and partnership with choreographer Merce Cunningham began in this period.

Chance and Zen

From the early 1950s, influenced by Zen Buddhism and the I Ching, Cage composed by chance operations, most radically in Music of Changes (1951). His essay collection Silence (1961) became a classic. Visiting an anechoic chamber and hearing his own nervous system and blood flow, Cage concluded that absolute silence does not exist — the insight behind 4′33″, premiered by David Tudor in Woodstock in 1952.

Later life and influence

Cage's ideas spread far beyond music into dance, happenings and the Fluxus movement; composers such as Stockhausen and Boulez engaged with them critically. He was also an avid mycologist, co-founding the New York Mycological Society, and won an Italian television quiz as a mushroom expert. He died of a stroke in New York in 1992.
