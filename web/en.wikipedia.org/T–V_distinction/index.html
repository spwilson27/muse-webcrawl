T–V distinction

The T–V distinction is the contextual use of different pronouns that are characteristic of varying levels of politeness, social distance, courtesy, familiarity, age or insult toward the addressee. The terms derive from the Latin pronouns tu ("you", singular) and vos ("you", plural), the starting points of second-person forms in many languages.

Examples

French contrasts tu with vous; German du with Sie; Spanish tú with usted (and vos in much of Latin America); Russian ty with vy; Hindi tū/tum with āp. Verbs, possessives and imperatives agree with the chosen form. English once had the same contrast between thou (T) and you (V), but standard English generalised the V form by the 18th century.

Power and solidarity

Roger Brown and Albert Gilman analysed pronoun choice as playing out two semantics: power — superiors give T and receive V — and solidarity — equals exchange mutual T or mutual V depending on closeness. Choosing the "wrong" form can signal intimacy, contempt, or social revolution, as when movements have abruptly mandated universal T.

Change over time

Many languages are drifting toward wider T use: Sweden underwent a du-reform in the 1960s, and informalisation in workplaces and advertising has pushed tu and du into previously formal domains, though the distinction remains fully alive in French, German, Spanish and many other languages.
