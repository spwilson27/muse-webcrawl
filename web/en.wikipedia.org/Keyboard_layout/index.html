Keyboard layout

Keyboard layout
Common layoutsQWERTY, AZERTY, QWERTZ, Dvorak, Colemak
OriginTypewriter keyboards, 1870s
StandardsISO/IEC 9995; national variants

A keyboard layout is any specific physical, visual, or functional arrangement of the keys, legends, or key-meaning associations of a computer or typewriter keyboard. Layouts differ between countries and languages, and alternative layouts have been designed to improve typing speed, comfort, or language coverage.

QWERTY

QWERTY, named for the first six letters of its top row, was designed by Christopher Latham Sholes and Carlos Glidden and sold to Remington in 1873. Its arrangement spread with the commercial success of Remington typewriters and became the dominant layout for English and many other languages. The popular claim that it was designed to slow typists down is a simplification; it separated common letter pairs to reduce typebar jams.

National variants

Languages with accented characters adopted variants: AZERTY in France and Belgium rearranges letters and moves digits to shifted positions; QWERTZ in Germany and Central Europe swaps Y and Z and adds umlaut keys. Many other national layouts adapt the base design for local scripts and punctuation.

Alternative layouts

The Dvorak layout, patented in 1936 by August Dvorak and William Dealey, places the most common letters on the home row to reduce finger travel; its claimed speed advantages remain debated. The Colemak layout (2006) keeps QWERTY's shortcut positions while reassigning 17 keys. Specialised layouts also exist for one-handed typing, chorded input, and ergonomic split keyboards.

Non-Latin scripts

Keyboards for Chinese, Japanese, and Korean typically print Latin letters alongside characters and rely on input method editors to convert phonetic keystrokes into characters, while Arabic, Hebrew, Cyrillic, and other scripts have their own standard layouts, often used in bilingual combinations.
