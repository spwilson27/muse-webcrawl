Nonconstructive proof

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nonconstructive proof is a mathematical proof that demonstrates the existence of a mathematical object without providing a specific example or a method for constructing it. Such proofs often proceed by contradiction: assume that no such object exists and derive an absurdity. They are contrasted with constructive proofs, which exhibit the object explicitly.

Example

A classic example shows that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational. Consider √2√2. If it is rational, the claim is proved; if it is irrational, then (√2√2)√2 = √2² = 2 is rational, and the claim is again proved — without determining which case actually holds. (In fact the Gelfond–Schneider theorem implies √2√2 is transcendental, but the elementary proof does not.)

Philosophy

Nonconstructive methods rely on classical logic, particularly the law of excluded middle and double-negation elimination. Constructivists and intuitionists, following L. E. J. Brouwer, reject existence claims not backed by a construction, and results such as the axiom of choice and some uses of Zorn's lemma are considered nonconstructive.
