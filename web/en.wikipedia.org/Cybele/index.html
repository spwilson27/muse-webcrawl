Cybele (/ˈsɪbəliː/; Phrygian: Matar Kubileya/Kubeleya, "Kubileya Mother") is an Anatolian mother goddess, the "Great Mother" (Magna Mater) of the gods and of wild nature. She may have a possible forerunner in the earliest neolithic at Çatalhöyük in Anatolia. She is the only known Phrygian goddess, and her cult spread through the Greek world into Rome, where in 204 BC, during the Second Punic War, her sacred black stone was brought from Pessinus to Rome and installed on the Palatine Hill following the instruction of the Sibylline Books.

Mythology

Cybele's mythology centres on her consort Attis, who in the most common version castrated himself beneath a pine tree in a frenzy and died; the goddess restored him or mourned him in annual rites of death and renewal. Her priesthood in the Roman cult included the galli, eunuch priests who imitated Attis's self-castration, and the taurobolium, a rite of baptism in bull's blood. Her festivals — notably the Megalensia in April and the March cycle of Attis's mourning and the Hilaria — were among the most colourful of the Roman calendar.

Iconography

Cybele is typically shown enthroned, wearing a mural (turreted) crown representing city walls, holding a tympanum (frame drum) and a patera, flanked by lions. In Phrygia she was carved into living rock facades, and her shrines were associated with mountains, wild animals, and ecstatic music played on pipes, cymbals, and drums by her attendants, the Corybantes.
