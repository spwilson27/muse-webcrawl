Social anthropology is the study of patterns of behaviour in human societies and cultures. It is the dominant constituent of anthropology in the United Kingdom and much of Europe, where it is distinguished from cultural anthropology in the United States; the two overlap heavily and are often combined as sociocultural anthropology.

History

The discipline took shape in the early twentieth century, when Bronisław Malinowski in Britain pioneered long-term ethnographic fieldwork among the Trobriand Islanders and argued for understanding societies from "the native's point of view". A. R. Radcliffe-Brown developed structural functionalism, analysing institutions by their contribution to social order. Later generations — influenced by Claude Lévi-Strauss's structuralism, symbolic and interpretive approaches, and postcolonial critique — broadened both topics and theory.

Methods

The signature method of social anthropology is ethnography based on participant observation: living within a community for an extended period, learning its language, and recording everyday life. Contemporary practitioners also study organisations, cities, migration, media, science, and their own societies ("anthropology at home").

Major themes

Classic topics include kinship and marriage, exchange and gift-giving (after Marcel Mauss), ritual and religion, political organisation, economic life, and systems of classification. Modern work engages globalisation, identity, the state, and environmental change.
