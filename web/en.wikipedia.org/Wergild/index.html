Wergild
Meaning"Man price" (Old English wer + gield)
Legal systemEarly Germanic law
PurposeCompensation for homicide or injury, averting feud
Scaled bySocial rank of the victim

Early medieval law codes fixed the wergild payable for each rank of person.

Wergild (also spelled weregild, wergeld, or weregeld), literally "man price", was a system in early Germanic law under which a person who committed homicide or another serious offence was required to pay a set sum to the injured party or, in the case of killing, to the victim's kin. Payment was intended to settle the matter and prevent a blood feud.

Amounts and social rank

The amount payable depended on the social rank of the victim. In Anglo-Saxon England, the wergild of a king could reach 30,000 thrymsas, while a thegn was valued at 1,200 shillings and a ceorl (free peasant) at 200 shillings in the laws of Wessex. Separate schedules, such as those in the laws of Æthelberht of Kent (c. 602), fixed payments for particular injuries — so much for an eye, a foot, or a thumb — in graded detail.

Decline

As royal justice strengthened in the later Middle Ages, homicide increasingly became a plea of the Crown punishable by death rather than a private wrong compensable by payment, and wergild fell out of use. Similar compensation institutions are found in other cultures, such as the Irish éraic and the Islamic diyya (blood money).
