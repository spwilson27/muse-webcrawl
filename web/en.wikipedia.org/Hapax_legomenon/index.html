Hapax legomenon

A hapax legomenon (plural hapax legomena; from Greek ἅπαξ λεγόμενον, "being said once") is a word or form that occurs only once within a given context — typically a text, an author's body of work, or an entire language's recorded corpus. The concept is central to philology and lexicography, especially in the study of ancient texts such as the Hebrew Bible, Homer, and Shakespeare.

Significance

Because a hapax legomenon offers only a single context for interpretation, its meaning may be difficult or impossible to determine with certainty. Scholars rely on etymology, cognates in related languages, later commentary, and context to gloss such words. A famous example from the biblical book of Isaiah is the word pim, long obscure until archaeology revealed it denoted a weight. In large corpora, roughly half of all distinct words may be hapax legomena, a pattern described statistically by Zipf's law.

Related terms

Words occurring twice are called dis legomena; three times, tris legomena. A related notion is the nonce word — a word coined for a single occasion, such as James Joyce's "quark", later adopted by physics. Hapax legomena must be distinguished from rare words generally: the term is always relative to a specified corpus.
