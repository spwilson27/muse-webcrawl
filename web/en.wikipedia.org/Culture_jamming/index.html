Culture jamming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Culture jamming (sometimes also guerrilla communication) is a form of protest used by many anti-consumerist social movements to disrupt or subvert media culture and mainstream cultural institutions, including corporate advertising. It purports to expose the methods of domination of mass society.

Culture jamming employs techniques originally drawn from the Situationist International and Dada, using pranks, détournement, and the reworking of familiar images and slogans to expose underlying political assumptions. Practitioners modify logos, billboards, and advertisements so that their messages criticise the very products or corporations they originally promoted.

Origins

The term was coined in 1984 by Don Joyce of the sound collage band Negativland, and was popularised in the 1990s by cultural critic Mark Dery and by Kalle Lasn, founder of the magazine Adbusters. Lasn's 1999 book Culture Jam: How to Reverse America's Suicidal Consumer Binge — and Why We Must became a manifesto of the movement.

Techniques

Common culture jamming techniques include:

  • Subvertising — the parody of advertisements and advertising slogans.
  • Billboard liberation — the alteration of billboards, practised by groups such as the Billboard Liberation Front.
  • Media pranks and hoaxes designed to be reported as news.
  • Memes — short, easily copied images or phrases intended to spread an idea virally.
