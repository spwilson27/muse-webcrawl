The Compact Muon Solenoid (CMS) is a general-purpose particle detector at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN. Together with its sibling experiment ATLAS, it was designed to investigate a wide range of physics, including the Higgs boson, extra dimensions, supersymmetry, and particles that could make up dark matter. In July 2012, CMS and ATLAS jointly announced the discovery of a new boson consistent with the Higgs boson.

Design

CMS is built around a huge superconducting solenoid magnet — 6 metres in internal diameter, producing a field of 3.8 tesla — inside which sit the tracking and calorimetry systems. From the interaction point outward, particles traverse a silicon pixel and strip tracker, a lead tungstate crystal electromagnetic calorimeter, a brass-and-scintillator hadron calorimeter, and finally muon chambers embedded in the steel return yoke. Despite its name, the detector weighs about 14,000 tonnes and is anything but compact in absolute terms; the name refers to its dense, compact layout relative to its magnetic volume.

Construction

Unusually, CMS was assembled on the surface in large slices of up to about 2,000 tonnes, which were then lowered 100 metres into the underground cavern at Cessy, France, and bolted together — a strategy that allowed construction to proceed in parallel with cavern excavation.

Physics program

Beyond the Higgs discovery, CMS has measured Higgs properties, searched extensively (so far without confirmed signals) for supersymmetric particles and other phenomena beyond the Standard Model, and studied heavy-ion collisions that recreate quark–gluon plasma. High-luminosity upgrades of the LHC require major upgrades to the detector's tracker and endcap calorimetry.
