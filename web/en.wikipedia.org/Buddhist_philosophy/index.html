Buddhist philosophy

Buddhist philosophy is the system of thought developed within Buddhism from the teachings of the Buddha through more than two millennia of commentary and debate across Asia. It addresses metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, logic and the philosophy of mind, and is oriented toward liberation from suffering (dukkha).

Core doctrines

Central ideas shared by nearly all schools include the Four Noble Truths, dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) — the doctrine that phenomena arise in dependence on conditions — impermanence (anicca), and no-self (anattā), the denial of a permanent, independent self or soul. Karma and rebirth operate within this impersonal causal framework.

Schools and developments

Early Buddhist schools produced the Abhidharma literature, which analysed experience into elementary dharmas. The Mahayana tradition added the Madhyamaka philosophy of Nagarjuna, which argues that all things are empty (śūnya) of inherent existence, and the Yogācāra school, often interpreted as a form of idealism centred on consciousness. Thinkers such as Dignāga and Dharmakīrti developed sophisticated theories of logic and epistemology (pramāṇa). Later traditions — including Zen, Tibetan Buddhism and modern Buddhist philosophy — continued these debates.

Ethics

Buddhist ethics rests on the cultivation of compassion (karuṇā) and wisdom, the Five Precepts for laypeople, and the bodhisattva ideal in Mahayana, in which practitioners vow to work for the liberation of all beings.
