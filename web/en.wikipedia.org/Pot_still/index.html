Pot still

Type: Distillation apparatus

Material: Typically copper

Used for: Whisky, cognac, traditional spirits

Operation: Batch (discontinuous) distillation

A pot still is a type of distillation apparatus used to distil flavoured liquors such as whisky, cognac, rum and traditional gin. It consists of a single heated chamber — the pot — in which a fermented liquid (wash) is boiled, connected via a swan neck and lyne arm to a condenser where the alcohol vapour is cooled back into liquid.

Pot distillation is a batch process: after each run the still must be emptied and cleaned before the next charge. Because separation is less efficient than in a column still, pot-distilled spirits are usually run two or three times and retain more congeners — the flavour compounds that give traditional spirits their character.

Design

The shape of the still strongly influences the spirit. Tall stills with long lyne arms produce lighter spirit, because heavier compounds condense and fall back; short, squat stills give a richer, heavier distillate. Copper is favoured because it reacts with and removes sulphur compounds from the vapour.

Operation

The distiller divides the run into foreshots, the heart (the desirable middle fraction) and feints, discarding or recycling the first and last fractions. In Scotch whisky production the wash is distilled in a wash still, then the resulting low wines are distilled again in a spirit still.
