Antonie Philips van Leeuwenhoek (24 October 1632 – 26 August 1723) was a Dutch microbiologist and microscopist in the Golden Age of Dutch science and technology. A largely self-taught man in science, he is commonly known as "the Father of Microbiology," and one of the first microscopists and microbiologists. Van Leeuwenhoek is best known for his pioneering work in microscopy and for his contributions toward the establishment of microbiology as a scientific discipline.

Life and work

Born in Delft, van Leeuwenhoek worked as a draper and haberdasher, held municipal posts including chamberlain and surveyor, and ground his own lenses as a hobby that became an obsession. Unlike the compound microscopes of contemporaries such as Robert Hooke, his instruments used a single tiny spherical lens mounted between brass plates; the best of them magnified over 250 times with remarkable clarity. He guarded his lens-making secrets jealously and left no account of his methods.

Discoveries

Beginning in the 1670s, he reported his observations in some 300 letters, mostly to the Royal Society of London, which elected him a fellow in 1680. He was the first to observe and describe single-celled organisms — bacteria, protozoa and yeast — which he called "animalcules" (notably in his letter of 9 October 1676). He also described spermatozoa (1677), red blood cells, capillary circulation in the tails of fish and ears of rabbits, striations in muscle fibres, rotifers, and the structure of the crystalline lens. Initially sceptical, the Royal Society verified several of his claims by sending observers to Delft in 1677.

Legacy

Van Leeuwenhoek's work opened the microbial world to science two centuries before germ theory. The journal Antonie van Leeuwenhoek (founded 1934) bears his name, as does the Leeuwenhoek Medal of the Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences.
