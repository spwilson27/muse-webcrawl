Maratha Empire

The Maratha Empire at its peak around 1760, dominating much of the Indian subcontinent.

The Maratha Empire (also rendered as the Maratha Confederacy) was an early modern polity in the Indian subcontinent, existing from 1674 to 1818. It was founded by Shivaji, who carved an independent kingdom out of the declining Adilshahi sultanate of Bijapur and defied the Mughal Empire. At its peak in the mid-eighteenth century the Marathas dominated much of India, from the Punjab in the north to the Tamil country in the south, and were the principal power opposing first Mughal and then British expansion.

Origins under Shivaji

Shivaji (1630–1680), a Maratha chieftain in service to Bijapur, began seizing hill forts in the Western Ghats in the 1640s, using mobile cavalry and guerrilla tactics. He killed the Bijapuri general Afzal Khan in 1659, sacked Surat, and in 1674 was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad. His son Sambhaji resisted the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb's invasion of the Deccan but was captured and executed in 1689; the struggle continued under Rajaram and his widow Tarabai.

The Peshwa era and expansion

After Shahu I was released from Mughal captivity, his chief minister (Peshwa) Balaji Vishwanath and especially Bajirao I (r. 1720–1740) transformed the kingdom into an expanding empire, famous for rapid cavalry campaigns that reached Delhi in 1737. Under Balaji Bajirao the Marathas recovered from the devastating defeat by Ahmad Shah Durrani at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761), which cost them a generation of leaders and halted expansion in the northwest. The empire functioned as a confederacy of great houses — the Peshwa at Pune, Sindhia at Gwalior, Holkar at Indore, Gaekwad at Baroda and Bhonsle at Nagpur — united nominally under the Chhatrapati at Satara.

Decline and fall

Internal rivalries and succession disputes weakened the confederacy, and the British East India Company defeated its members piecemeal in the three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1782, 1803–1805, 1817–1818). The last Peshwa, Bajirao II, surrendered in 1818 and was pensioned off to Bithoor, ending the empire; the princely states of the Maratha houses survived under British paramountcy until 1947.

Administration and legacy

Maratha rule relied on revenue assessments, the chauth (one-quarter levy) and sardeshmukhi (additional tenth) collected from territories, and a navy pioneered by Shivaji under admirals such as Kanhoji Angre. The Marathas are remembered in Maharashtra as symbols of indigenous self-rule (swarajya), and their era shaped the political map of modern India.
