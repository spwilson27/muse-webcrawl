Cover version

Type: Musical recording
Definition: New performance of a previously released song
Related: Remake, tribute album, sampling

A cover version, cover song, or simply cover, is a new performance or recording by someone other than the original performer or composer of a song. The term derives from the early record industry practice of releasing a competing version of a hit song in order to "cover" the original in the marketplace.

History

In the first half of the 20th century, popular songs were commodities of sheet music publishers, and multiple artists routinely recorded the same tune. In the 1950s, white performers' sanitized covers of rhythm-and-blues records — Pat Boone's versions of Little Richard songs, for example — often outsold the originals on mainstream charts, a practice now widely criticized for denying Black artists commercial success. Later, rock and pop culture came to prize authenticity, yet covers remained staples of live sets and albums.

Notable covers

Some covers have eclipsed their originals: Jeff Buckley's rendition of Leonard Cohen's "Hallelujah", Johnny Cash's version of Nine Inch Nails' "Hurt", Jimi Hendrix's take on Bob Dylan's "All Along the Watchtower", and Whitney Houston's recording of Dolly Parton's "I Will Always Love You" are classic examples. Conversely, faithful tribute recordings and genre-crossing reinterpretations remain distinct artistic strategies.

Legal aspects

In the United States, once a song has been commercially released, anyone may record a cover by obtaining a compulsory mechanical licence and paying the statutory royalty rate, administered for many artists by the Harry Fox Agency or similar services. Altering the fundamental character or lyrics of a work goes beyond a cover and requires direct permission from the copyright holder.
