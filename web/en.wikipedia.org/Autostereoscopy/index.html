Autostereoscopy

TypeDisplay technology
MethodsParallax barrier, lenticular lens, integral imaging
RequirementNo glasses needed

Autostereoscopy is any method of displaying stereoscopic images (adding binocular perception of 3D depth) without the use of special headgear, polarized glasses, or other accessories worn by the viewer. Because no headgear is required, it is also called "glasses-free 3D" or "glasses-less 3D".

Methods

Two broad approaches are used. In spatial multiplexing, a parallax barrier—a layer with a series of precision slits—is placed in front of the display so that each eye sees a different set of pixels. In lenticular designs, an array of cylindrical lenses directs different image strips to each eye. More advanced light-field and integral imaging systems present many views simultaneously, allowing multiple viewers and horizontal motion parallax. Head-tracking systems steer a single pair of views to follow the viewer.

Examples

Early examples include the lenticular prints and stereoscopic postcards of the early twentieth century. In electronics, autostereoscopic displays have been used in the Nintendo 3DS handheld console (parallax barrier), various 3D televisions, advertising displays, and experimental automotive and medical imaging systems.

Limitations

Spatially multiplexed displays sacrifice resolution per eye, and fixed viewing zones can restrict the viewer position. Viewing-zone transitions may produce pseudoscopic (reversed-depth) images if the viewer moves too far.
