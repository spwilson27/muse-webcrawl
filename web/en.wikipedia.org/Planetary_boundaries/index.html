Planetary boundaries are a framework describing limits to the impacts of human activity on the Earth system. Beyond these limits, the environment may no longer be able to self-regulate, and the Earth system could leave the stable Holocene state in which human civilization developed. The framework was proposed in 2009 by a group of Earth system and environmental scientists led by Johan Rockström of the Stockholm Resilience Centre and Will Steffen of the Australian National University.

The nine boundaries

The framework identifies nine processes, each with a proposed boundary intended to mark a "safe operating space for humanity": climate change; biosphere integrity (biodiversity loss and extinctions); land-system change; freshwater change; biogeochemical flows (nitrogen and phosphorus cycles); ocean acidification; atmospheric aerosol loading; stratospheric ozone depletion; and novel entities (chemical pollution, plastics, and other human-made substances).

Assessments

The original 2009 paper estimated that three boundaries—climate change, biodiversity loss, and the nitrogen cycle—had already been crossed. A major update in 2015 concluded that four boundaries (adding land-system change) had been transgressed. A comprehensive 2023 assessment quantified all nine boundaries for the first time and found six transgressed: climate change, biosphere integrity, land-system change, freshwater change, biogeochemical flows, and novel entities. Stratospheric ozone depletion remains within the safe zone and is often cited as evidence that collective action can reverse a transgression; ocean acidification was assessed as approaching its boundary.

Relation to other frameworks

The planetary boundaries framework informed the "doughnut economics" model of Kate Raworth, which combines the environmental ceiling with a social foundation, and it has influenced science-based target setting for companies and policy discussions around the Anthropocene. Critics have questioned the scientific basis for some boundary values, interactions between boundaries, and the framework's neglect of regional variation and social dimensions.

See also

The concept is distinct from neighbouring topics in planetary science and environmental governance, and is frequently discussed alongside the Sustainable Development Goals and the Paris Agreement.
