New York School

This article is about the group of poets. For the painters, see New York School (art).

The New York School was an informal group of American poets, painters, dancers, and musicians active in the 1950s and 1960s in New York City, centred above all on the poets John Ashbery, Frank O'Hara, Kenneth Koch, and James Schuyler. Their work was influenced by surrealism, modernism, and the action painting of their artist friends, and it reacted against the confessional and academic poetry then dominant.

[ Image: Frank O'Hara in New York in the 1950s ]

Characteristics

The poets worked in close collaboration with visual artists of the New York School of painting — writing catalogue texts, collaborating on books, and sharing a downtown social world. Their poetry is often witty, urbane, improvisatory, and conversational, full of references to films, friends, popular culture, and the daily life of the city; O'Hara called his approach "Personism". Ashbery's Self-Portrait in a Convex Mirror (1975) won the Pulitzer Prize, the National Book Award, and the National Book Critics Circle Award.

Generations

A second generation, including Ted Berrigan, Ron Padgett, Joe Brainard, and Anne Waldman, developed around the Poetry Project at St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery. The term "New York School" was borrowed from the painters, whose milieu the first-generation poets shared.
