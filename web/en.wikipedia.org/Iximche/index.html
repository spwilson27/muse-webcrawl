Iximche (or Iximché, from Kaqchikel for "ramón tree") is a Postclassic Maya archaeological site in the western highlands of Guatemala, near the modern town of Tecpán. Founded around 1470 as the capital of the Late Postclassic Kaqchikel kingdom, it was built on a defensible plateau surrounded by deep ravines at an altitude of about 2,260 m (7,410 ft).

History

The Kaqchikel broke away from the K'iche' kingdom and established Iximche under kings including Wuqu-Batz'. The city grew to hold palaces, twin-pyramid temples, ballcourts and more than 160 structures organised in plaza groups. When Pedro de Alvarado arrived in 1524, the Kaqchikel allied with the Spaniards against the K'iche' and welcomed him; Iximche became the first capital of colonial Guatemala, proclaimed in July 1524. Spanish extortion of gold soon turned the alliance sour; the Kaqchikel abandoned the city and waged guerrilla war from the hills, and the deserted capital was burned and deserted within a few years.

The site today

Excavated and partially restored from the 1940s onward (notably under Jorge Guillemín), the ruins preserve temple pyramids with traces of painted murals, palace platforms and two ballcourts in a pine-covered setting maintained as a national monument. Iximche remains an important ceremonial site for contemporary Maya ritual specialists, with altars in use at the site.
