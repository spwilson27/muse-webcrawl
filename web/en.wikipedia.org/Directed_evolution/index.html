Directed evolution

Directed evolution is a method used in protein engineering and synthetic biology that mimics the process of natural selection to evolve proteins or nucleic acids toward a user-defined goal. It consists of subjecting a gene to iterative rounds of mutagenesis, selection or screening, and amplification, in a cycle intended to progressively improve a desired property such as catalytic activity, stability, or binding affinity.

Principles

The approach begins with a library of gene variants, typically created by error-prone PCR or DNA shuffling. The variants are expressed, and individuals with improved traits are isolated by screening or survival-based selection. The genes encoding the winners form the starting material for the next round, so beneficial mutations accumulate over successive generations.

Applications

Directed evolution has produced enzymes for detergents, biofuel production, pharmaceutical synthesis, and bioremediation, as well as antibodies and industrial catalysts with properties not found in nature. Frances Arnold shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for her pioneering work on the directed evolution of enzymes; the other half went to George P. Smith and Gregory Winter for phage display.

Comparison with rational design

Unlike rational protein design, directed evolution does not require detailed knowledge of structure–function relationships; it exploits the fact that many mutations are neutral or beneficial and that screening can find them empirically. Modern workflows increasingly combine the two, using structural insight to focus libraries and machine learning to guide selection.
