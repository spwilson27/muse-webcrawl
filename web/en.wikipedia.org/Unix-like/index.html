Unix-like

A Unix-like (sometimes referred to as UN*X or *nix) operating system is one that behaves in a manner similar to a Unix system, although not necessarily conforming to or being certified to any version of the Single UNIX Specification. A Unix-like application is one that behaves like the corresponding Unix command or shell.

Definition

There is no standard for defining the term, and some difference of opinion is possible regarding the degree to which a given operating system or application is "Unix-like". The term can include free and open-source operating systems inspired by Bell Labs' Unix, or designed to emulate its features, commercial and proprietary work-alikes, and even versions based on the licensed UNIX source code.

History

"Unix-like" systems started to appear in the late 1970s and early 1980s. Many proprietary versions, such as Idris (1978), UNOS (1982), Coherent (1983) and UniFlex (1985), aimed to provide businesses with Unix-style functionality without AT&T licensing costs. The spread of inexpensive systems was accelerated by the AT&T divestiture and the growth of the workstation market.

In the 1980s and 1990s, free software projects recreated the Unix environment without using AT&T code. MINIX (1987) was an influential teaching system; the GNU Project, begun in 1983 by Richard Stallman, developed a complete set of free Unix-compatible tools; and the Linux kernel, first released by Linus Torvalds in 1991, combined with GNU software to form widely used systems. The BSD family — FreeBSD, NetBSD and OpenBSD — descends more directly from the Berkeley Software Distribution of Unix.

Categories

Unix-like systems are commonly divided into those that share actual source-code ancestry with the original AT&T Unix (the BSD line, and certified Unix systems such as macOS), and functional clones written from scratch to follow Unix design principles and POSIX standards (such as Linux, MINIX and the GNU system). The Open Group, holder of the UNIX trademark, restricts use of the term "Unix" to systems certified against the Single UNIX Specification.

Design characteristics

Typical features of Unix-like systems include a hierarchical file system, the treatment of devices and inter-process communication as files, a large set of small command-line utilities connected by pipes, a multi-user multitasking kernel, and the shell as the primary user interface.
