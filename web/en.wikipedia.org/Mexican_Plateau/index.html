Mexican Plateau

LocationNorth-central Mexico
Average elevation1,825 m (5,988 ft)
Highest pointCerro el Potosí (3,713 m)

The Mexican Plateau (Spanish: Altiplanicie Mexicana, also called the Mexican Altiplano) is a large arid-to-semiarid plateau occupying much of north-central Mexico. It is one of the country's major physiographic regions and contains many of Mexico's largest cities, including Guadalajara and León, as well as extensive mining and agricultural districts.

Geography

The plateau averages about 1,825 metres (5,988 feet) in elevation and is wedged between the Sierra Madre Occidental to the west and the Sierra Madre Oriental to the east, closing off to the south at the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt. It is generally divided into two sections: the northern Mesa del Norte (Central Mesa of the North), a lower, flatter expanse of desert basins and ranges underlain by bolsones (internal drainage basins), and the southern Mesa Central, a higher volcanic upland of rolling plains, lava fields and lake basins.

Climate and drainage

The rain shadow of the surrounding cordilleras gives most of the plateau a dry climate, with rainfall concentrated in summer. Rivers generally fail to reach the sea, draining instead into interior basins that form shallow lakes and salt flats (playas), many of which have dried up over the centuries. The region around the former lakes of the Valley of Mexico and Lake Chapala remains among Mexico's most fertile and populous areas.

Human geography

The plateau was the heartland of pre-Hispanic civilizations including the Mexica (Aztec), whose capital Tenochtitlan stood on an island in Lake Texcoco, and it later became the colonial mining heartland of New Spain, source of silver from Zacatecas, Guanajuato and San Luis Potosí. Today the Bajío region in the southern plateau is one of Mexico's fastest-growing industrial and agricultural zones.
