The Port of Hamburg (German: Hamburger Hafen) is a seaport on the River Elbe in Hamburg, Germany, about 110 kilometres from the river's mouth on the North Sea. Known as Germany's "Gateway to the World" (Tor zur Welt), it is the country's largest port by volume and, after Rotterdam and Antwerp, one of Europe's largest container ports. The port occupies some 7,400 hectares in the heart of the city, and the tidal Elbe allows it to receive large ocean-going vessels far inland.

History

Hamburg's port tradition is conventionally dated to 7 May 1189, when Emperor Frederick Barbarossa allegedly granted the city duty-free navigation on the lower Elbe — the date is still celebrated as the port's birthday (Hafengeburtstag), though the charter is now regarded as a later forgery. As a leading member of the Hanseatic League, Hamburg grew rich on Baltic and North Sea trade. In 1888 it joined the German customs union while retaining a free-port district, around which rose the Speicherstadt, the world's largest warehouse complex of its kind, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The port was devastated in the Second World War, rebuilt rapidly, and transformed by containerisation from the 1960s onward.

Terminals and operations

Major container terminals include Burchardkai, Altenwerder (one of Europe's most automated terminals) and Tollerort, operated chiefly by HHLA (Hamburger Hafen und Logistik AG) and Eurogate. The port also handles bulk cargoes, cruise ships at its Hamburg Cruise Center terminals, and shipbuilding and repair at yards such as Blohm+Voss. Deepening of the Elbe fairway, completed in the early 2020s, improved access for the largest container ships. The historic polder-like interplay of port and city continues in projects such as HafenCity, built on former port land.
