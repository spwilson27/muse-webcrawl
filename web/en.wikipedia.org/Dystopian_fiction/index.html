Dystopian fiction

Dystopian fiction is a genre of speculative fiction that explores social and political structures in a dark, nightmare world — a dystopia, an imagined community or society that is undesirable or frightening, typically characterized by oppression, totalitarian government, environmental disaster, or the loss of individual freedom. The word, coined as the opposite of Thomas More's Utopia (1516), was used by John Stuart Mill in 1868.

Classic works

The foundations of the genre include Yevgeny Zamyatin's We (1924), set in a regimented glass city; Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932), a society pacified by pleasure and consumption; George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949), the defining vision of total surveillance and thought control; and Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451 (1953), about state censorship and book burning. Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale (1985) depicts a theocratic patriarchy, and late-20th- and 21st-century works such as The Hunger Games (2008) brought the genre to mass young-adult audiences.

Themes

Dystopian works commonly address totalitarianism, surveillance, propaganda, dehumanization through technology, environmental collapse, pandemic, and the suppression of dissent. Critics note the genre functions as political warning: it extrapolates tendencies of the author's own society — often fascism, communism, consumer capitalism, or religious extremism — to a horrifying conclusion.

Related genres

Dystopian fiction overlaps with post-apocalyptic fiction (society after catastrophe), cyberpunk (high technology, low life), and anti-utopian satire, but is distinguished by its focus on an oppressive order that still functions, rather than on ruins or collapse.
