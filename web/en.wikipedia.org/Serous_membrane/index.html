Serous membrane

A serous membrane has parietal and visceral layers separated by serous fluid

The serous membrane (or serosa) is a smooth epithelial membrane of mesothelium supported by connective tissue which lines certain internal cavities of the body, known as serous cavities, and covers the organs within them. Serous membranes secrete a lubricating fluid, serous fluid, which reduces friction between organs and the cavity walls.

Structure

Each serous membrane consists of two layers: a parietal layer lining the cavity wall and a visceral layer covering the organs. The two layers are continuous with one another and enclose a potential space containing a small amount of serous fluid composed of serum-like transudate rich in proteins and mesothelial cells.

Principal serous membranes

The three main serous cavities of the human body are lined by the pleura around the lungs, the pericardium around the heart, and the peritoneum in the abdominal and pelvic cavities. Each is named according to the organs it envelops.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of a serous membrane is called serositis. Excess accumulation of serous fluid in a cavity (an effusion) can occur in heart failure, infection, malignancy and inflammatory disease — for example pleural effusion around the lungs or ascites within the peritoneal cavity.
