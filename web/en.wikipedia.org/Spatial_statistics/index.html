Spatial statistics

Spatial statistics
Field: Statistics, geography, GIS
Core concept: Spatial autocorrelation
Key methods: Kriging, variograms, point process models

Spatial statistics is the branch of statistics concerned with data that are associated with locations in space — spatial data — and with the spatial relationships among those data. Unlike classical statistics, which typically assumes independent observations, spatial statistics explicitly models spatial autocorrelation: the tendency, summarised in Tobler's first law of geography, for nearby things to be more related than distant things.

Types of spatial data

Three broad types are distinguished. Geostatistical data arise from a continuous spatial process observed at fixed sample points (e.g. soil contaminant concentrations); methods include variogram modelling and kriging interpolation. Lattice (areal) data are aggregated over regions such as census tracts, analysed with spatial regression and measures such as Moran's I. Spatial point patterns concern the locations of events themselves — trees, disease cases, crimes — analysed with point process theory and tools such as Ripley's K function.

Applications

Spatial statistics is used across the environmental sciences (mining, hydrology, climate), epidemiology (disease mapping and cluster detection), ecology, real estate economics, criminology and image analysis. Its techniques underpin much of geographic information science.

History

The field grew from mining geostatistics developed by Georges Matheron and Danie Krige in the 1950s–60s, from spatial econometrics, and from the quantitative revolution in geography. Standard references include Cressie's Statistics for Spatial Data (1993).
