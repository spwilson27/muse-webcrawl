Homonym

In linguistics, homonyms are words that share the same spelling or the same pronunciation but have different meanings. In the strictest sense, a homonym is simultaneously a homograph (same spelling) and a homophone (same sound), like the two meanings of bank (a financial institution; to tilt or turn) or bark (of a tree; of a dog). In looser usage the term covers either relation. Homonymy is distinguished from polysemy, in which a single word has several related senses; whether two meanings count as one polysemous word or two homonymous words is a classic problem of lexicography.

Types

Homophones sound alike but may be spelled differently (right / write / rite) and are the source of spelling difficulties and much punning. Homographs are spelled alike; if they also sound alike they are full homonyms, and if pronounced differently they are heteronyms — tear (rip) versus tear (from the eye), or wind (air) versus wind (to turn). Some pairs are homographic in one capitalisation only, such as Polish / polish or August / august, sometimes called capitonyms. Interlingual homographs — "false friends" such as English gift and German Gift ("poison") — are related only by accident of form.

Origin of homonyms

Homonyms arise by several routes: phonological change that makes formerly distinct words sound the same (as the Great Vowel Shift and other mergers did for meet / meat); borrowing, which imports forms that collide with native words (Old English bank-related forms versus Italian banca); semantic drift, in which originally connected senses diverge until speakers no longer perceive a link; and shortening, as when fan (enthusiast, from fanatic) met fan (the air-moving device). Dictionaries typically give homonyms separate entries with superscript numbers (bank¹, bank²) while listing polysemous senses under one headword — a decision that must be made word by word and often rests on etymology.

Homonymy versus polysemy

The boundary is theoretically slippery. Mouth of a person and mouth of a river are usually treated as polysemy (one word extended by metaphor); bat the animal (Old English-derived, of Norse origin) and bat the club (a different Old English word) are homonyms. Tests based on speaker intuition, etymology, and the possibility of a "core" meaning give conflicting answers, and linguists such as John Lyons treat the distinction as gradient rather than absolute. Homonymy matters practically in lexicography, language teaching, natural-language processing (word-sense disambiguation), and psycholinguistics, where ambiguous words are used to study how the brain resolves meaning.

Wordplay and ambiguity

Homophones and homonyms fuel puns, riddles, and jokes ("Why is it so windy inside a sports arena? Because of all the fans"), as well as genuine ambiguity exploited in poetry, advertising, legal drafting disputes, and philosophy of language. Many languages' poetic traditions — Chinese regulated verse and Japanese wordplay among them — rely systematically on homophony, which is exceptionally rich in languages with small syllable inventories.
