Gregorian mission

The Gregorian mission, sometimes called the Augustinian mission, was a mission sent by Pope Gregory the Great in 596 to convert the pagan Anglo-Saxons of England to Christianity. Led by Augustine, prior of Gregory's monastery in Rome, the party of some forty monks landed in Kent in 597 and was received by King Æthelberht, whose Frankish wife Bertha was already a Christian. Æthelberht converted and was baptised, and Augustine was consecrated as the first Archbishop of Canterbury.

Course and legacy

Gregory's letters to the missionaries, preserved by Bede, famously advised adapting pagan temples for Christian worship and accommodating local customs. A second group of missionaries arrived in 601 bringing the pallium for Augustine and sacred vessels and books. The mission established sees at Canterbury, Rochester and London, though conversion of other kingdoms was uneven and some relapsed after their royal patrons died; Northumbria's enduring conversion came largely through the rival Irish mission from Iona. Nevertheless the Gregorian mission founded the institutional English Church and its enduring link with Rome. Gregory's responses to Augustine's questions, the Libellus responsionum, became an important early medieval text.
