Meninges

The meninges (singular: meninx) are the three membranes that envelop the brain and spinal cord — the central nervous system. From outermost to innermost they are the dura mater, arachnoid mater and pia mater. They protect the central nervous system, enclose the cerebrospinal fluid, and support blood vessels supplying the brain.

Layers

  • Dura mater — a thick, tough membrane. In the skull it has periosteal and meningeal layers that separate to form the dural venous sinuses and folds such as the falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli.
  • Arachnoid mater — a delicate web-like membrane beneath the dura. Beneath it lies the subarachnoid space, filled with cerebrospinal fluid and traversed by arachnoid trabeculae and cerebral veins.
  • Pia mater — a thin membrane adhering closely to the surface of the brain and spinal cord, following every sulcus.

Spaces

The epidural space (outside the dura, prominent in the spine) and the potential subdural space (between dura and arachnoid) are clinically important sites of haemorrhage: epidural and subdural haematomas respectively. The subarachnoid space is sampled by lumbar puncture and is the site of subarachnoid haemorrhage from ruptured aneurysms.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the meninges is meningitis, usually infectious and potentially fatal. Tumours arising from meningothelial cells are meningiomas, among the commonest primary intracranial tumours and usually benign. Meningeal involvement also occurs in carcinomatous meningitis and neurosarcoidosis.
