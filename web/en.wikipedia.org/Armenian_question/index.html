Armenian question

The Armenian question was the debate and diplomatic problem, prominent in European politics from the 1870s until the First World War, concerning the treatment, security and governance of the Armenian population of the Ottoman Empire. Modelled on the broader "Eastern Question", the term described the great powers' involvement in pressing the Ottoman government for reforms and protection for its Armenian subjects.

Origins

Following the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78, the Treaty of San Stefano (March 1878) required reforms in the Armenian-inhabited provinces and Russian guarantees for them. The Treaty of Berlin (July 1878) replaced this with Article 61, placing reform obligations under the collective supervision of the European powers. Armenian delegations, led by figures such as Mkrtich Khrimian, lobbied the Berlin Congress for autonomy or international protection.

Escalation

Reforms were never implemented to the Armenians' satisfaction. Armenian revolutionary parties — the Armenakan, Hunchak and Dashnaktsutyun (Armenian Revolutionary Federation) — formed in the 1880s and 1890s to press for change, sometimes by armed action. Under Sultan Abdul Hamid II, the Hamidian massacres of 1894–1896 killed tens of thousands of Armenians. The Adana massacre followed in 1909. An Armenian reform agreement, negotiated with European involvement and providing for European inspectors-general in the eastern provinces, was signed in February 1914, but the outbreak of the First World War voided it; the following year the Ottoman government carried out the Armenian genocide.

Aftermath

The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) envisaged an independent Armenian state with borders to be arbitrated by US President Woodrow Wilson, but the treaty was never implemented and was superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923), which made no provision for Armenia. The "question" in its diplomatic form thus ended, leaving the issue of genocide recognition to later generations.
