Guanine

Guanine (symbol G or Gua) is one of the four main nucleobases found in the nucleic acids DNA and RNA, together with adenine, cytosine and thymine (uracil in RNA). It is a purine derivative: a fused pyrimidine–imidazole double ring with conjugated double bonds. In double-stranded DNA, guanine base-pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds. The nucleoside of guanine is guanosine.

History

Guanine was first isolated in 1844 by the German chemist Julius Bodo Unger from guano, the accumulated excrement of seabirds, from which it takes its name. Its structure was worked out later in the 19th century by Emil Fischer as part of his pioneering studies of purines.

Functions

Beyond its role in nucleic acids, guanine derivatives are central to cell signalling and energy transfer: the nucleotide GTP powers molecular processes and signal-transduction proteins, and cyclic GMP acts as a second messenger. In DNA, runs of guanines can fold into four-stranded structures called G-quadruplexes, which influence gene regulation and telomere biology.

Occurrence and uses

Guanine crystals give the silvery sheen to fish scales and are used as a pearlescent pigment (natural pearl essence) in cosmetics and paints. Spiders and other organisms store nitrogenous waste as guanine, and its reflective crystals are used for camouflage and light manipulation in the eyes and skin of various animals.
