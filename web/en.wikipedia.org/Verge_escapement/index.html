Verge escapement

The verge escapement (or crown wheel-and-verge escapement) is the earliest known type of escapement, the mechanism in a mechanical clock that controls its rate by giving timed impulses to the timekeeper and allowing the gear train to advance in equal steps. It was used in the first mechanical clocks from the late 13th century until the 19th century, and in early watches.

How it works

The escapement consists of a crown wheel, shaped like a crown with teeth pointing axially, and a vertical shaft called the verge, carrying two small paddles or "pallets" set at an angle to one another. As the crown wheel's teeth push against one pallet, the verge rotates until the tooth escapes, whereupon a tooth on the opposite side engages the other pallet and pushes the verge back the other way. The verge's oscillation is regulated by a foliot — a horizontal bar with adjustable sliding weights — or, after the 1650s, by a pendulum. Each oscillation lets the wheel advance by one tooth.

History

The verge escapement appeared with the first mechanical clocks in Europe around the late 13th century, driving the great turret clocks of cathedrals and monasteries. It remained the only escapement for nearly 400 years. Its main defects are that it is not isochronous — its rate varies with driving force — and that it requires the gear train to recoil backwards slightly during each cycle, causing wear. When Christiaan Huygens applied the pendulum to clocks in 1656, the verge was retained at first, but the more accurate anchor escapement (c. 1670) displaced it in clocks. In watches, the verge persisted until the mid-19th century, long after more accurate escapements existed, because it was thin and cheap to make.
