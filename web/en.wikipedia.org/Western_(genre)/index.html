Western (genre)

Western
Setting: American frontier, chiefly second half of the 19th century
Media: Film, literature, television, radio, comics
Key figures: John Ford, John Wayne, Sergio Leone, Clint Eastwood

The Western is a genre of fiction typically set in the American frontier — the "Old West" — during the second half of the nineteenth century. Rooted in the dime novels and Wild West shows of the 1800s, it became one of the defining genres of American cinema, built around archetypes such as the cowboy, the gunfighter, the sheriff, the outlaw and the settler.

Themes

Western stories commonly explore the conflict between wilderness and civilisation, law and lawlessness, and the individual and the community. Recurring settings include frontier towns, ranches, deserts and mountain ranges, and recurring devices the cattle drive, the gunfight at high noon, the stagecoach and the cavalry rescue.

Film history

Early successes such as The Great Train Robbery (1903) were followed by the classics of John Ford, including Stagecoach (1939) and The Searchers (1956), which made John Wayne the genre's defining star. In the 1960s Italian "spaghetti Westerns" by Sergio Leone, such as The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (1966), revitalised the form, and revisionist Westerns from The Wild Bunch (1969) to Unforgiven (1992) subjected its myths to darker scrutiny.

Literature

Major Western novelists include Owen Wister (The Virginian), Zane Grey, Louis L'Amour and Larry McMurtry (Lonesome Dove).
