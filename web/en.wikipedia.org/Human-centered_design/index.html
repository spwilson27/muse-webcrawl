Human-centered design

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Human-centered design (HCD), also called user-centered design, is an approach to designing products, services and interactive systems that grounds the process in the needs, capabilities and behaviour of the people who will use them. It is standardised in ISO 9241-210, which defines it as an approach that “aims to make systems usable and useful by focusing on the users, their needs and requirements”.

Principles and process

ISO 9241-210 sets out key principles: design based on an explicit understanding of users, tasks and environments; involvement of users throughout development; iteration driven by user feedback and evaluation; attention to the whole user experience; and multidisciplinary teams. A typical process cycles through observing and researching users in context, generating ideas, prototyping, and testing with real users, repeating the loop until the design meets defined usability goals.

History

The approach grew out of human factors and ergonomics and the participatory design traditions of Scandinavia. Donald Norman popularised the term “user-centered design” in his 1988 book The Design of Everyday Things, and design consultancies such as IDEO carried HCD methods into product, service and social-sector design. Related frameworks include design thinking, usability engineering and, in software, the user-centred methods embedded in agile development.

Criticism

Critics argue that excessive focus on existing users can stifle radical innovation, that self-reported preferences may mislead, and that HCD sometimes neglects stakeholders other than end users, including communities affected by a system’s wider consequences.
