A hedge fund is a pooled investment fund that holds liquid assets and employs complex investment, portfolio-construction, and risk-management strategies to improve performance, such as short selling, leverage, and derivatives. Hedge funds are generally available only to sophisticated investors, such as institutions and high-net-worth individuals, and are subject to lighter regulation than mutual funds offered to the public.

History

The first hedge fund is generally credited to Alfred Winslow Jones, who in 1949 raised money to buy stocks while short-selling others, thereby "hedging" market risk; his partnership also charged an incentive fee of 20% of profits and invested the managers' own money—features that remain characteristic. The industry grew through the 1960s, contracted in the bear markets of the 1970s, and expanded dramatically from the 1990s as pension funds and endowments sought uncorrelated returns. Notable episodes include George Soros's Quantum Fund profiting from sterling's 1992 exit from the ERM, and the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management, whose highly leveraged bets required a Federal Reserve-brokered bailout.

Structure and fees

Hedge funds are commonly organised as limited partnerships or limited liability companies, domiciled offshore (for example in the Cayman Islands) with management companies in financial centres such as New York, London, or Hong Kong. The classic fee schedule is "two and twenty": a management fee of 2% of assets per year plus a performance fee of 20% of profits, usually subject to a high-water mark so that losses must be recovered before performance fees resume. Investors typically face lock-up periods and limited redemption windows.

Strategies

Major strategy families include long/short equity (buying undervalued and shorting overvalued shares), global macro (directional bets on currencies, rates, and commodities), event-driven investing (mergers, bankruptcies, restructurings), relative value arbitrage (exploiting pricing differences between related securities), and quantitative or systematic strategies that trade on mathematical models. Because hedge funds can use leverage and derivatives, their risk profiles vary enormously.

Regulation and criticism

Historically, hedge funds operated with minimal regulatory oversight in reliance on exemptions for private offerings to accredited investors. After the 2008 financial crisis, the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States required larger advisers to register with the SEC and report systemic-risk data, and the EU adopted the Alternative Investment Fund Managers Directive. Critics cite high fees, opacity, and the industry's mediocre aggregate returns relative to index funds, while defenders point to diversification benefits and absolute-return objectives.
