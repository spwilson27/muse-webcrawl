Book of Mormon

The Book of Mormon is a religious text of the Latter Day Saint movement, first published in March 1830 by Joseph Smith. Adherents believe it is a translation of ancient records engraved on golden plates, recording the history of peoples who migrated from the ancient Near East to the Americas and of a visit by the resurrected Jesus Christ to the Americas after his crucifixion. It is one of the standard works of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints and other Latter Day Saint denominations.

Contents

The book is divided into smaller books, attributed to prophets including Nephi, Jacob, Alma, Mormon and Moroni. It narrates the journey of Lehi's family from Jerusalem around 600 BC, the division of their descendants into Nephites and Lamanites, centuries of wars, preaching and prophecies of Christ, Christ's ministry among the Nephites (recorded in 3 Nephi), and the eventual destruction of the Nephites. Mormon is presented as the abridger of the records, and his son Moroni as their final custodian, who buried the plates in a hill near present-day Palmyra, New York.

Origin

According to Smith, the angel Moroni directed him to the plates in 1823, and he translated them "by the gift and power of God", dictating the text to scribes, principally Oliver Cowdery, in 1829. Eleven witnesses testified to having seen the plates. Mainstream historians and scholars regard the book as a 19th-century work composed by Smith, pointing to its King James Bible-influenced style, anachronisms and dependence on the Bible; believers cite internal complexity and claimed ancient parallels in its support.

Significance

The Book of Mormon is central to Latter-day Saint theology, standing alongside the Bible as scripture. It has been translated, in whole or in part, into more than one hundred languages, with well over 150 million copies printed. It also became the namesake of the hit Broadway musical The Book of Mormon (2011).
