The Yamnaya culture (from Russian ямная, "pit [grave] culture") was a Late Copper Age to Early Bronze Age archaeological culture of the Pontic–Caspian steppe, dating to roughly 3300–2600 BCE. Its people were semi-nomadic pastoralists who herded cattle and sheep, used wheeled wagons, and buried their dead in pit graves beneath mounds known as kurgans, often with red ochre.

Origins and society

The Yamnaya emerged from earlier steppe cultures such as Sredny Stog and Repin, with ancestry combining Eastern hunter-gatherers and Caucasus hunter-gatherers. Their economy centred on mobile stock-breeding supported by wagons, dairying and some exchange with neighbouring farming societies. Burials suggest a ranked, patriarchal society; grave goods include copper tools, weapons and, rarely, wagons and sceptres.

Migrations and genetics

Ancient DNA studies since 2015 have shown that Yamnaya-related ("steppe") ancestry spread widely in the third millennium BCE. To the west it formed a major component of the Corded Ware culture and thereby of later European populations; to the east, related groups such as the Afanasievo culture carried steppe ancestry toward the Altai. These findings strongly support the steppe hypothesis, which identifies the Yamnaya horizon with speakers of late Proto-Indo-European.

Language hypothesis

Under the Kurgan/steppe hypothesis, championed by Marija Gimbutas and refined by David Anthony and others, the Yamnaya and their predecessors spoke late Proto-Indo-European, and their expansions dispersed Indo-European languages across Europe and parts of Asia. Wheeled-vehicle vocabulary shared across Indo-European branches fits the Yamnaya timeframe, when wagons first became common on the steppe.
