Toleration Act 1689

The Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to Protestant nonconformists in England and Wales.

Citation1 Will. & Mar. c. 18
Royal assent24 May 1689
Full titleAn Act for Exempting their Majesties Protestant Subjects dissenting from the Church of England from the Penalties of certain Laws

The Toleration Act 1689 (1 Will. & Mar. c. 18), also referred to as the Act of Toleration, was an Act of the Parliament of England. Passed in the aftermath of the Glorious Revolution, it granted freedom of worship to nonconformists who had pledged to the oaths of Allegiance and Supremacy and rejected transubstantiation—that is, to Protestants who dissented from the Church of England such as Baptists, Congregationalists and Presbyterians, but not to Roman Catholics or Unitarians.

Provisions

The Act exempted qualifying dissenters from the penalties of laws that had compelled attendance at Anglican worship and punished separate meetings, including provisions of the Conventicle Act and the Five Mile Act. Meeting houses had to be registered and could not be locked during worship; ministers had to subscribe to most of the Thirty-nine Articles. Dissenters remained liable to pay tithes and were still excluded from public office by the Test and Corporation Acts, which were repealed only in 1828.

Significance

The Act was a pragmatic settlement rather than a declaration of universal religious freedom, but it marked a decisive break with the Restoration policy of enforced uniformity. John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration, published in the same year, supplied its philosophical counterpart. The Act remained a foundation of dissenters' civil position until the broader emancipation measures of the 19th century.
