Haussmann's renovation of Paris was a vast public works programme commissioned by French Emperor Napoleon III and directed by his prefect of the Seine, Baron Georges-Eugène Haussmann, between 1853 and 1870. It transformed Paris from a largely medieval city of narrow, unhealthy streets into a modern capital of broad tree-lined boulevards, parks, sewers and uniform stone facades.

The programme

Some 12,000 buildings were demolished and roughly 60 percent of the city's fabric rebuilt over the seventeen years of works. Haussmann cut grand avenues — the boulevards Saint-Germain, Sébastopol, Haussmann and others — through the old quarters, extended the rue de Rivoli, and linked the railway stations. Engineers built hundreds of kilometres of new water mains and sewers under Eugène Belgrand, while Jean-Charles Alphand created the Bois de Boulogne, Bois de Vincennes and many squares and gardens. New landmarks included the Palais Garnier opera house, Les Halles markets and the Hôtel-Dieu hospital; strict rules governed facade heights, materials and alignment, producing the characteristic cream-stone apartment blocks of Paris.

Motives and criticism

The motives were sanitary (cholera epidemics and overcrowding), aesthetic, economic and strategic: wide avenues were harder to barricade and allowed troops and artillery to move quickly, a consideration sharpened by the revolutions of 1830 and 1848. The works displaced hundreds of thousands of poorer Parisians to the periphery, and the programme's vast borrowing — much of it through off-budget mechanisms — was attacked by critics such as Jules Ferry ("Les Comptes fantastiques d'Haussmann"). Haussmann was dismissed in January 1870, months before Napoleon III fell, but the works continued and set the face of Paris to this day.
