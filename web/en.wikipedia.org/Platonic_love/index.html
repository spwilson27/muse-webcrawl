Platonic love
Named afterPlato
Key textsSymposium, Phaedrus
Key speechDiotima's ladder of love

Platonic love is a type of love, or close relationship, that is non-romantic and non-sexual. In modern usage it denotes an affectionate bond between friends without sexual desire. The term derives from the philosophy of Plato, although Plato himself never used it: it was coined in the Renaissance by Marsilio Ficino as amor platonicus, from Plato's treatment of eros in the Symposium and Phaedrus.

In Plato

In the Symposium, Plato dramatises a drinking-party discussion of love. In the speech attributed to Socrates — reporting the teaching of the priestess Diotima — love begins in desire for a particular beautiful body, but can ascend a "ladder": from love of one beautiful body, to all beautiful bodies, to beautiful souls, laws and practices, knowledge, and finally to the Form of Beauty itself. Erotic desire is thus redirected upward toward philosophy and contemplation of the eternal. In the Phaedrus, Plato similarly describes the lover and beloved rising together, restraining physical appetite and growing "wings" for the soul.

Later usage

Ficino and the Renaissance Neoplatonists adapted the idea into a doctrine of chaste, spiritually uplifting love between men, and the term passed into European languages. William Davenant's The Platonick Lovers (1636) made "Platonic love" fashionable at the English court. Over time the philosophical and mystical connotations faded, leaving the current everyday sense of a deep, affectionate friendship without sexual involvement.

Interpretation

Scholars dispute how closely modern usage matches Plato: in the dialogues, eros remains a powerful desire, but one whose fulfilment is intellectual and spiritual rather than bodily. Platonic love is sometimes contrasted with romantic love, sexual love, familial love (storge) and brotherly love (philia).
