Polynesian navigation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Polynesian voyaging canoe (wa'a kaulua)

Polynesian navigation or Polynesian wayfinding is the traditional system by which the peoples of Polynesia navigated vast distances of the open Pacific Ocean and settled its islands. Using double-hulled voyaging canoes and knowledge transmitted orally over generations, navigators found their way without compasses, charts or instruments, centuries before European exploration of the Pacific.

Techniques

Wayfinding rests on close observation of natural signs. Navigators steer by the rising and setting points of stars — a "star compass" dividing the horizon into houses where familiar stars rise and set. They also read ocean swells and wave refraction patterns, winds, clouds (including stationary clouds that form over islands), the flight paths of land-based seabirds, floating debris, and changes in water colour and marine life to detect land beyond the horizon. In the Marshall Islands, stick charts recorded swell patterns for teaching.

Settlement of Polynesia

Beginning from the Lapita culture in Near Oceania, voyagers expanded eastward across Remote Oceania, reaching Samoa and Tonga by about 900 BC, and eventually the far corners of the Polynesian triangle: Hawai'i in the north, Rapa Nui (Easter Island) in the east, and Aotearoa (New Zealand) in the south-west, settled by roughly 1250–1300 AD. Voyages spanned thousands of kilometres of open ocean and likely included deliberate return voyages and continuing exchange.

Decline and revival

Long-distance voyaging declined after European contact, and traditional navigation nearly disappeared outside a few Micronesian atolls, where masters such as Mau Piailug of Satawal preserved it. In the 1970s the Polynesian Voyaging Society built the replica canoe Hōkūle'a; in 1976 Piailug navigated her from Hawai'i to Tahiti without instruments, sparking a Pacific-wide cultural renaissance. Hōkūle'a completed a worldwide voyage in 2014–2017.
