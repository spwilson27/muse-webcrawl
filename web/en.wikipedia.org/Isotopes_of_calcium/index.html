Calcium (Ca) has 26 known isotopes, from ³⁵Ca to ⁶⁰Ca. Five are stable: ⁴⁰Ca, ⁴²Ca, ⁴³Ca, ⁴⁴Ca and ⁴⁶Ca. A sixth, ⁴⁸Ca, has a half-life so long (about 6 × 10¹⁹ years, decaying by double beta decay) that it is treated as stable for most purposes.

Major isotopes

Calcium-40 makes up about 97% of natural calcium. It is the endpoint of alpha-process burning in stars and the decay product of ⁴⁰K, which is the basis of potassium–argon dating of rocks. Calcium-44 (about 2%) is the stable daughter of ⁴⁴Ti decay in supernovae and is used as a tracer in bone and nutrition studies; calcium-41, a cosmogenic radioisotope with a half-life of about 100,000 years, is used in dating and in biomedical research on bone resorption.

Calcium-48 is of special interest: it is doubly magic (20 protons, 28 neutrons), exceptionally neutron-rich for its mass, and undergoes two-neutrino and possibly neutrinoless double beta decay, making it a key nucleus for fundamental physics experiments. Because of its neutron excess, ⁴⁸Ca ion beams are used in laboratories to synthesise superheavy elements — it was the projectile used to create elements 114 through 118.

Applications

Calcium isotope ratios (δ⁴⁴Ca) measured in marine carbonates provide records of past ocean chemistry and climate. Radioactive ⁴⁵Ca (half-life 163 days) is used as a tracer in studies of calcium metabolism and in materials research.
