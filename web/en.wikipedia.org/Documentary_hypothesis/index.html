Documentary hypothesis

The documentary hypothesis (DH) is one of the models used by biblical scholars to explain the origins and composition of the Torah (or Pentateuch, the first five books of the Bible: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy). It holds that the Pentateuch was composed from several originally independent documents or sources that were later combined by a series of editors (redactors).

The sources

In the classic formulation developed by Julius Wellhausen in the 1870s (Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels, 1878), the four sources are:

  • J — the Jahwist: uses the divine name Yahweh (Jehovah); anthropomorphic portrayal of God; dated to c. 950 BCE in the southern kingdom of Judah.
  • E — the Elohist: uses Elohim for God until the revelation to Moses; dated to c. 850 BCE in the northern kingdom of Israel.
  • D — the Deuteronomist: the source of Deuteronomy and associated with the reforms of King Josiah, c. 622 BCE.
  • P — the Priestly source: formal, ritual-focused, concerned with genealogies, censuses and cultic law; dated to the exilic or post-exilic period, c. 550–400 BCE.

Scholars identified the sources by differences in terminology, divine names, style, narrative doublets (such as the two creation accounts in Genesis 1 and 2), and theological outlook.

Status of the hypothesis

Although the documentary hypothesis was the dominant model through much of the 20th century, its consensus has fragmented since the 1970s. Many scholars now favour supplementary or fragmentary models, or revised versions of the hypothesis (the "neo-documentary" approach). Nevertheless, the broad conclusion that the Torah is a composite work assembled from sources of different dates commands wide support in academic biblical studies, distinct from traditional attribution to Moses.
