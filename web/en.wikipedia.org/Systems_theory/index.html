Systems theory

[Image placeholder: system diagram]

Systems theory is the transdisciplinary study of systems—cohesive collections of interrelated and interdependent parts that interact with each other and with their environment. It seeks principles common to systems in nature, society, engineering, and technology, including feedback, emergence, self-organization, homeostasis, and hierarchy. Rather than reducing a phenomenon to its components, systems theory emphasizes the organization of the whole.

History

The field is principally associated with biologist Ludwig von Bertalanffy, who developed general system theory from the 1930s onward, arguing that open systems in biology exchange matter and energy with their surroundings and that similar organizational laws appear across disciplines. Parallel developments included cybernetics, founded by Norbert Wiener in the 1940s, which studied control and communication in animals and machines; information theory of Claude Shannon; and the work of the Macy conferences. The Society for General Systems Research (now the International Society for the Systems Sciences) was founded in 1954.

Key concepts

  • Open and closed systems: open systems exchange energy, matter, or information with their environment; closed systems do not.
  • Feedback: negative feedback stabilizes a system around a set point, while positive feedback amplifies change.
  • Emergence: properties of the whole that are not predictable from the properties of the parts alone.
  • Homeostasis: self-regulation maintaining internal stability.
  • Equifinality: the principle that an open system can reach the same final state from different initial conditions.
  • Hierarchy and boundaries: systems are nested within larger systems and demarcated by boundaries of varying permeability.

Applications

Systems theory underlies systems engineering, systems dynamics (developed by Jay Forrester), family therapy, ecological modeling, organizational theory, and systems biology. Later developments include second-order cybernetics, which includes the observer within the system studied, and Niklas Luhmann's theory of social systems. Complexity science and chaos theory share systems theory's emphasis on nonlinear interactions.
