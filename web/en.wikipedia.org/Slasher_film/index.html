A slasher film is a subgenre of horror film involving a killer—often masked and wielding a bladed weapon—who stalks and murders a group of people, usually teenagers, one by one. Slashers typically feature point-of-view shots from the killer's perspective, inventive kill scenes, and a resourceful survivor, often dubbed the "final girl".

History

The genre drew on Alfred Hitchcock's Psycho (1960), Italian giallo films of the 1960s–70s, and Bob Clark's Black Christmas (1974). John Carpenter's Halloween (1978), made on a tiny budget, became one of the most profitable independent films ever and launched the golden age of the slasher, followed by Friday the 13th (1980) and hundreds of imitators.

Conventions and criticism

Common tropes include the killer with a traumatic backstory, victims punished for transgressive behaviour, and the virginal "final girl" who survives through resourcefulness—an archetype analysed by theorist Carol Clover in Men, Women, and Chain Saws (1992). The genre was criticised in the 1980s for graphic violence, and many titles were caught up in Britain's "video nasties" panic.

Revival

After a decline in the late 1980s, Wes Craven's self-aware Scream (1996) revived the slasher, spawning a wave of teen horror. The genre has continued through remakes, legacy sequels such as Halloween (2018), and streaming-era originals.
