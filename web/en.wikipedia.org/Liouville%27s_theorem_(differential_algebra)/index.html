Liouville's theorem (differential algebra)

Joseph Liouville (1809–1882), French mathematician.

In mathematics, Liouville's theorem, originally formulated by the French mathematician Joseph Liouville in 1833 to 1841, places an important restriction on antiderivatives that can be expressed as elementary functions.

The antiderivatives of certain elementary functions cannot themselves be expressed as elementary functions. A standard example of such a function is e−x², whose antiderivative is a commonly encountered special function, the error function. Other standard examples include the functions sin(x)/x and xx. More generally, however, the argument of Liouville's theorem is applicable to all indefinite integrals of elementary functions — the only functions whose indefinite integrals might be elementary are these functions themselves and polynomials in them.

Statement

For any differential field F and any algebraic extension g of F, there exists an element h in some extension of F such that h′ = g if and only if there is a solution of the form shown in the theorem, where the elements involved are built from F using algebraic operations, exponentiation, and logarithm.

Differential algebra

Differential algebra is the field of mathematics consisting of the study of differential equations in terms of algebraic notations. The theorem is a foundational result of the subject and underlies algorithms such as the Risch algorithm, which decides whether an elementary function has an elementary antiderivative.
