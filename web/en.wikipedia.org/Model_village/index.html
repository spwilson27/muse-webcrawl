Model village

A model village is a planned settlement built by an industrial employer or landowner to house workers and their families, typically to higher standards than ordinary working-class housing of the period and often embodying the founder's social or moral ideals. The term also denotes miniature villages built as tourist attractions, a distinct usage described below.

Industrial model villages

During the Industrial Revolution, some employers built entire communities for their workforce. Notable British examples include:

  • Saltaire (from 1851), built by Titus Salt beside his mill near Bradford, with stone houses, public baths, a school, and an institute — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Port Sunlight (from 1888), built by Lever Brothers for soap-factory workers on the Wirral.
  • Bournville (from the 1890s), developed by the Cadbury family near Birmingham, notable for gardens, low-density housing, and (in line with the family's Quaker temperance) no pubs.
  • New Earswick and the garden-city movement, influenced by Joseph Rowntree and Ebenezer Howard.

Comparable settlements appeared internationally, such as Pullman in Chicago, built by George Pullman's railroad car company, whose paternalistic controls contributed to the Pullman Strike of 1894. Model villages influenced town planning and garden-city and council-housing design, though critics noted the social control employers could exert over tenants.

Miniature model villages

Outdoor miniature villages — scaled replicas of buildings and landscapes, such as Bekonscot in Buckinghamshire (opened 1929, the oldest in the UK) and Madurodam in The Hague — are popular tourist attractions and are generally built at scales around 1:12 to 1:25.
