Women in medicine

Women in medicine have practised as healers, midwives, nurses, and physicians throughout history, though formal medical education was closed to them in most societies until the nineteenth century. Today women form a majority of medical students in many countries, while remaining underrepresented in some surgical specialties and senior academic posts.

History

Ancient sources record female physicians in Egypt and Greece. In medieval Europe, women such as Trotula of Salerno were associated with texts on women's health, while midwifery remained a predominantly female occupation. The professionalisation of medicine in the nineteenth century initially excluded women; Elizabeth Blackwell became the first woman to earn a medical degree in the United States in 1849, and Elizabeth Garrett Anderson qualified in Britain in 1865.

Twentieth century

Women's medical colleges and wartime demand gradually opened hospital and university posts. Florence Nightingale professionalised nursing, and figures such as Marie Skłodowska-Curie, Gerty Cori, and Virginia Apgar made foundational research contributions.

Contemporary issues

Research has documented gender pay gaps, differential referral and citation patterns, and the underrepresentation of women in clinical-trial leadership, prompting reforms in many health systems.
