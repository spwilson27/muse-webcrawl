Radial tire

Construction of a radial tire: carcass plies run from bead to bead at 90° to the tread, stabilised by steel belts.

A radial tire (more properly, a radial-ply tire) is a particular design of vehicle tire. In this design, the cord plies are arranged at 90 degrees to the direction of travel, or radially (from the center of the tire), in contrast to the diagonal arrangement of a bias-ply (cross-ply) tire. A belt of steel or fabric cords, running at a low angle under the tread, stabilises the tread area.

Because the sidewalls and tread work more independently, radial tires offer a larger, more stable contact patch, lower rolling resistance, improved grip and handling, and markedly longer tread life. They have been the dominant design for passenger-car tires worldwide since the 1980s.

History

The radial principle was patented as early as 1915 by Arthur W. Savage, but practical radial tires arrived with Michelin, which developed the design in 1946 and launched the Michelin X in 1948. Michelin had acquired the bankrupt Citroën in 1934, and the radial tire suited Citroën's front-wheel-drive cars; the Lancia Aurelia became the first car fitted with radials as standard. Adoption in the United States was slow until the 1970s fuel crisis, when the fuel savings and longevity of radials overcame industry resistance; by the 1980s nearly all new American cars used them.

Construction

The carcass cords — typically polyester — run straight across from bead to bead. Over them lie belt plies, usually two steel belts plus a nylon cap ply, angled a few degrees from the circumferential direction. This structure lets the flexible sidewall absorb bumps while the belted tread stays flat against the road, reducing the scrubbing and heat build-up that wear out bias tires. Radial sidewalls are more vulnerable to impact damage and are generally not interchangeable with bias tires on the same axle.
