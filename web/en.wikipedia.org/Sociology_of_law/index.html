Sociology of law

The sociology of law (or legal sociology) is the sociological study of law as a social institution: how legal rules, professions, courts, and enforcement interact with the wider society, and how law both reflects and shapes social norms, power, and inequality. It treats law not merely as a system of doctrines but as a field of social practice — often summarized by the contrast between "law in books" and "law in action," a phrase associated with Roscoe Pound.

Classical foundations

Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) is an early precursor. Émile Durkheim argued that legal forms index social solidarity — repressive law predominating in mechanically solidary societies, restitutive law in organically solidary ones — and used law as a visible measure of the division of labor. Max Weber analyzed the rationalization of law, distinguishing formal and substantive rationality and the types of legitimate domination behind legal orders. Karl Marx and later conflict theorists treated law as an instrument and arena of class power, while Eugen Ehrlich's concept of "living law" pointed to the norms actually governing social life beyond state codes.

Modern field

The modern subdiscipline consolidated after World War II with the law-and-society movement and journals and associations devoted to empirical legal studies. Major themes include the legal profession and its stratification; courts and dispute processing, including why most grievances never become cases (the dispute pyramid); policing, punishment, and mass incarceration; legal consciousness — how ordinary people experience and invoke law; law and social movements, including litigation strategies; legal pluralism in colonial and postcolonial settings; and globalization and transnational law. Niklas Luhmann's systems theory and Pierre Bourdieu's account of the juridical field are influential theoretical frameworks.
