A neutrino is an elementary subatomic particle with a very small (but non-zero) mass, no electric charge, and spin of 1/2. Neutrinos interact only via the weak interaction and gravity, making them extraordinarily difficult to detect: most pass through ordinary matter unimpeded, and billions pass through every square centimetre of Earth each second. Neutrinos come in three flavours—electron, muon, and tau—corresponding to the three charged leptons.

History

The neutrino was postulated by Wolfgang Pauli in 1930 to explain the continuous energy spectrum of beta decay, which appeared to violate conservation of energy. Enrico Fermi incorporated the particle into his theory of beta decay and gave it its name, Italian for "little neutral one". The electron antineutrino was first detected in 1956 by Clyde Cowan and Frederick Reines in experiments at a nuclear reactor. The muon neutrino was discovered in 1962 and the tau neutrino in 2000.

Oscillation and mass

Experiments beginning with the solar neutrino problem—the persistent shortfall of electron neutrinos detected from the Sun—showed that neutrinos change flavour as they travel, a phenomenon called neutrino oscillation. Oscillation requires neutrinos to have non-zero mass, contradicting the original Standard Model, in which they were assumed massless. The 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Takaaki Kajita and Arthur McDonald for demonstrating neutrino oscillation. Individual neutrino masses remain unmeasured, though cosmological observations bound their sum to well under one electronvolt.

Sources and detection

Neutrinos are produced in nuclear fusion in the Sun, in supernovae, in cosmic ray interactions in the atmosphere, in nuclear reactors, and in particle accelerators. Detectors such as Super-Kamiokande in Japan, the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in Canada, and IceCube at the South Pole use huge volumes of water, heavy water, or ice to catch the rare interactions, whose flashes of Cherenkov radiation reveal the neutrino's energy and direction. The burst of neutrinos detected from supernova SN 1987A inaugurated neutrino astronomy.
