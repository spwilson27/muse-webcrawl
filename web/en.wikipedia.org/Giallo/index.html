Giallo

Giallo (Italian: [ˈdʒallo], pl. gialli) is a genre of Italian murder-mystery horror and thriller fiction and film, characterised by stylish cinematography, convoluted whodunit plots, graphic violence and a black-gloved killer. The name, meaning "yellow", derives from the yellow covers of the cheap paperback mystery novels published in Italy from 1929 onwards, notably by Mondadori.

As a film genre, giallo flourished from the mid-1960s through the 1970s. Mario Bava established its visual grammar with The Girl Who Knew Too Much (1963) and Blood and Black Lace (1964), and Dario Argento popularised it internationally with The Bird with the Crystal Plumage (1970), Deep Red (1975) and Suspiria (1977, adjacent to supernatural horror).

Characteristics

Giallo films typically feature an amateur detective — often a foreigner in Italy, or a witness who has seen a crucial detail they cannot quite recall — pursuing a mysterious killer whose identity is revealed only at the climax. Stylised violence, fetishistic attention to gloves, blades and fashion, pulsating scores (frequently by Ennio Morricone or Goblin) and dream-like logic are genre trademarks.

Influence

Giallo strongly influenced the North American slasher film of the late 1970s and 1980s, including Halloween and Friday the 13th, and later neo-giallo homages. In literature, the term still denotes the Italian crime and mystery genre generally.
