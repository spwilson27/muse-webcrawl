Edward FitzGerald

Edward FitzGerald (31 March 1809 – 14 June 1883) was an English poet and writer best known as the translator and populariser of the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam, the collection of quatrains attributed to the Persian astronomer-poet Omar Khayyam.

Life

Born Edward Purcell at Bredfield, Suffolk, into one of the wealthiest families in England, he took the surname FitzGerald, his mother's family name, in 1852. Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, he formed lasting friendships with figures such as William Makepeace Thackeray and Alfred Tennyson. Living mostly quietly in Suffolk — much of it aboard his yacht and in a cottage at Woodbridge — he published little and sought no fame.

The Rubaiyat

FitzGerald learned Persian with the orientalist Edward Cowell, who introduced him to a manuscript of Khayyam's quatrains in the Bodleian Library. His free, heavily reshaped rendering appeared anonymously in 1859 and at first sold so poorly that copies were remaindered at a penny — until the pamphlet was discovered and championed by Dante Gabriel Rossetti, Algernon Charles Swinburne, and others. FitzGerald revised the work through five editions (1859–1889).

The Rubaiyat became one of the most beloved poems of the Victorian and Edwardian eras, famous for lines such as "A Book of Verses underneath the Bough, / A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou." Its hedonistic, fatalistic philosophy, filtered through FitzGerald's own melancholy temperament, made Khayyam a household name in English — more famous, arguably, than in the Persian tradition itself.

Other works

FitzGerald also translated Spanish dramas of Calderón, the Persian romance Salaman and Absal by Jami, and (late in life) works of Aeschylus and Sophocles, though none approached the Rubaiyat's fame. His letters, published posthumously, are admired in their own right.
