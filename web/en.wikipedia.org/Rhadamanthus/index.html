Rhadamanthus

In Greek mythology, Rhadamanthus (also transliterated Rhadamanthys; Greek: Ῥαδάμανθυς) was a wise king of Crete. As the son of Zeus and Europa he was considered a demigod. He was famous for his wisdom, his sense of justice, and his role as a lawgiver; after his death he became one of the three judges of the dead in the underworld, alongside his brother Minos and Aeacus.

Family and life

Rhadamanthus was one of the three sons of Zeus and Europa, brother of Minos and Sarpedon. According to some traditions he ruled Crete before Minos, or governed part of the island justly from Phaistos; other accounts say he fled Minos's jealousy (or was driven out after a quarrel) and settled in Boeotia, where he married Alcmene, the widowed mother of Heracles, and lived at Ocalea. Still other traditions place him in the Cyclades, ruling islands of the Aegean. He was the father of Erythrus and Gortys in some genealogies.

Lawgiver

Rhadamanthus was said to have given the Cretans their laws, which in Plato's Laws are discussed as models that Minos received and passed on; the Spartans were said to have imitated Cretan law. A law attributed to Rhadamanthus, cited by Aristotle in the Nicomachean Ethics, prescribes pure retaliation: "Should a man suffer what he did, right justice would be done" — a strict lex talionis. His reputation for uncompromising righteousness made "judgment of Rhadamanthus" a byword for severe, incorruptible justice.

Judge of the dead

Because of his integrity, Rhadamanthus was appointed after death a judge of the souls of the dead. In Homer's Odyssey he dwells in the Elysian Fields. Plato's Gorgias describes Rhadamanthus judging the dead of Asia, while Aeacus judges those of Europe, with Minos as final arbiter in disputed cases. In later accounts of the underworld, including Virgil's Aeneid (Book 6), Rhadamanthus rules in Tartarus, where he "hears and reveals the frauds of men" and compels confession of sins, presiding over the punishment of the wicked. Dante placed him among the judges of the classical underworld tradition he inherited.

In later literature

The adjective "Rhadamanthine" entered English usage to describe rigorously just or inflexibly severe judgment. Rhadamanthus appears in various modern retellings of Greek myth and in popular culture, usually in his role as a stern judge of the dead.
