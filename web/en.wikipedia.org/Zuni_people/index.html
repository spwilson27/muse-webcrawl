The Zuni (Zuni: A:shiwi; formerly spelled Zuñi) are Native American Pueblo peoples native to the Zuni River valley in present-day western New Mexico and eastern Arizona. The Zuni are a federally recognized tribe (the Zuni Tribe of the Zuni Reservation) and most live in the Pueblo of Zuni on the Zuni River, a tributary of the Little Colorado River, in western New Mexico. Their language, Zuni, is a language isolate with no demonstrated relatives, and their elaborate ceremonial system, matrilineal clan organisation, and renowned arts — including silver and turquoise inlay jewellery, fetish carving and pottery — are distinctive among the Pueblos.

The Zuni descend from the Mogollon and Ancestral Pueblo peoples of the region and by the 1500s occupied six or seven towns, the "Seven Cities" sought by Spanish explorers. In 1540 Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, guided by reports of the fabled Seven Cities of Cíbola, attacked the Zuni town of Hawikuh — the first battle between Europeans and Native peoples in what is now the United States. Spanish missions followed, and the Zuni joined the Pueblo Revolt of 1680 before resettling and eventually concentrating in the single main pueblo of Halona.

Culture

Zuni religion centres on a pantheon of beings including the Sun Father and the kachinas (koko), with religious societies and priesthoods organising a year-round cycle of ceremonies, among them the Shalako festival in late autumn. The Zuni are celebrated for their "waffle gardens", a water-conserving dry-farming technique, and for artworks sold worldwide. The tribe's successful repatriation claims under NAGPRA, recovering sacred war gods (Ahayu:da) from museums, became a landmark in Native American cultural property law.
