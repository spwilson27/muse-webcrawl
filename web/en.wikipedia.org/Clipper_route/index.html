Clipper route

The clipper route was the traditional sailing route between Europe and the Far East, Australia and New Zealand used by clipper ships in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The route ran from the English Channel down the Atlantic, around the Cape of Good Hope, and then east across the Southern Ocean — riding the powerful westerly winds of the Roaring Forties — before turning north to the destination. The return voyage continued east across the Pacific, around Cape Horn, and back up the Atlantic, completing a circumnavigation driven almost entirely by prevailing winds.

The Brouwer route

The eastern Indian Ocean portion of the route derived from the Brouwer Route pioneered by the Dutch navigator Hendrik Brouwer in 1611 for the Dutch East India Company. By running east with the westerlies from the Cape of Good Hope before turning north to Java, Dutch ships cut the voyage to the East Indies from over a year to about six months, using timed latitude sailing to judge when to turn.

The clipper era

Fast clipper ships made the route famous: tea clippers raced from China to London, wool clippers from Australia, and grain ships continued the traffic into the twentieth century. Passages in the Roaring Forties were notoriously dangerous, with mountainous seas, icebergs, and frequent damage, but the wind-driven speed outweighed the risks. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, useless to sailing ships, and the spread of steamships gradually ended the route's commercial importance, though the last grain races were held in the 1930s.

Modern use

The clipper route is still followed by round-the-world yacht races such as the Volvo Ocean Race and the Clipper Round the World Yacht Race, and remains the fastest path under sail between the ports it connected.
