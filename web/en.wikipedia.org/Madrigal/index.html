Madrigal

A madrigal is a form of secular vocal music most typical of the Renaissance and early Baroque periods. Traditionally, madrigals are unaccompanied polyphonic settings of poetry for several voices — usually three to eight — with expressive musical treatment closely matching the text.

Italian madrigal

The madrigal emerged in Italy in the 1520s and 1530s, partly from the lighter frottola and partly from renewed interest in setting serious Italian poetry, especially Petrarch. Early madrigalists included Philippe Verdelot and Jacques Arcadelt. By mid-century, composers such as Adrian Willaert and Cipriano de Rore made the form more contrapuntal and chromatic. In the late Renaissance the madrigal became highly expressive and experimental: Luca Marenzio, Carlo Gesualdo, and Claudio Monteverdi pushed word-painting and chromaticism to extremes. Monteverdi's nine books of madrigals (1587–1638) trace the transition from Renaissance polyphony to the Baroque seconda pratica, eventually adding basso continuo and concertato instruments.

English madrigal

The form crossed to England after 1588, when Nicholas Yonge published Musica Transalpina, a collection of Italian madrigals in English translation. A native school flourished for about three decades, led by Thomas Morley, John Wilbye, and Thomas Weelkes, whose lighter balletts with "fa la la" refrains became especially popular.

Legacy

Although the madrigal declined in the 17th century, its expressive techniques shaped early opera and Baroque vocal music, and madrigal singing was revived by amateur societies in the 18th and 19th centuries.
