Francium is a chemical element with the symbol Fr and atomic number 87. It is an extremely radioactive alkali metal, the second-rarest naturally occurring element after astatine, and the last element of the periodic table to be discovered in nature rather than synthesised.

Discovery

Francium was discovered in 1939 by Marguerite Perey at the Curie Institute in Paris, who identified it as a decay product of actinium-227 while purifying actinium samples. She named the new element after France. Its existence in the group below caesium had been predicted by Mendeleev, who called it eka-caesium.

Properties and occurrence

All isotopes of francium are radioactive; the longest-lived, francium-223, has a half-life of only about 22 minutes. It occurs in uranium ores as an intermediate decay product, and at any time there is estimated to be only a few tens of grams of francium in the entire Earth's crust. Because it cannot be accumulated in visible quantities, its chemistry is studied with only thousands of atoms at a time using laser trapping techniques developed from the 1990s onward. Chemically it behaves much like caesium, as expected for the heaviest alkali metal.

Applications

Francium has no commercial uses. Research on trapped francium atoms serves precision tests of atomic theory and the weak interaction.
