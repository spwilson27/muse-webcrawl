Stocks

Stocks used for public punishment in England

Stocks are feet-anchoring public punishment devices used historically as a form of corporal punishment and public humiliation. The stocks are similar to the pillory and the pranger, though unlike the pillory, the stocks restrain only the ankles, with the victim typically seated on a low bench. Offenders were exposed in a public place such as a town square or market, where passers-by could jeer at, spit on, or throw rotten food at them.

History

Stocks were used across Europe from the medieval period into the 19th century, and were also common in colonial America, where statutes prescribed them for offences ranging from drunkenness and profanity to petty theft. In England, the devices were a standard fixture of parish and market towns; their use for punishment effectively ended in the 19th century, though they were never universally abolished by a single statute.

Use and design

The device consists of two hinged boards with semicircular cutouts that close around the ankles, locked with a padlock or latch. A seated version, sometimes called "sitting stocks", allowed longer confinement. Public exposure, rather than physical pain, was the principal element of the punishment.

Modern survivals

Stocks survive today as museum exhibits and as props in historical re-enactment, charity events, and fairs, where volunteers are placed in them to be sponged or photographed.
