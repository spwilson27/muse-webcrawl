Seed dormancy

Seed dormancy is an evolutionary adaptation that prevents seeds from germinating during unsuitable ecological conditions that would typically lead to a low probability of seedling survival. A dormant seed is one that fails to germinate in a specified period of time under a combination of environmental factors that are normally favorable for germination.

Types

Physiological dormancy is the most common class and is regulated by plant hormones, principally abscisic acid (which maintains dormancy) and gibberellins (which promote germination). Physical dormancy results from water-impermeable seed coats, broken by scarification, fire, or passage through an animal gut. Morphological dormancy occurs when the embryo is underdeveloped at dispersal and must grow before germination.

Ecological role

Dormancy spreads germination over time, forming persistent soil seed banks that buffer populations against catastrophic years. Cues that break dormancy include cold stratification, light, smoke compounds (karrikins), and temperature fluctuation.

Agriculture

Domestication has reduced dormancy in most crops to achieve uniform germination, though pre-harvest sprouting and weed seed dormancy remain significant management issues.
