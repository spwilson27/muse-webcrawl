European Union law

European Union law is a system of rules operating within the 27 member states of the European Union (EU). Since the founding of the European Coal and Steel Community following World War II, the EU has developed the aim to "promote peace, its values and the well-being of its peoples". The EU has political institutions, policies and a legal order beyond the nation state and parallel to national law.

EU law is based on two treaties: the Treaty on European Union and the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. Together with regulations, directives and decisions, these form the primary law of the Union. The Court of Justice of the European Union interprets the law and ensures its equal application across member states.

History

The legal order of the European Union traces its origins to the Treaty of Paris (1951), which created the European Coal and Steel Community, and the Treaties of Rome (1957), which established the European Economic Community. The Maastricht Treaty of 1992 formally created the European Union, and the Treaty of Lisbon (2007) gave the Union full legal personality.

Sources of law

The two main sources of EU law are primary law (the treaties and the Charter of Fundamental Rights) and secondary law, which includes regulations (directly applicable in all member states), directives (binding as to the result to be achieved, but implemented through national law), decisions, recommendations and opinions.

Principles

Key principles developed by the Court of Justice include direct effect, established in Van Gend en Loos (1963), and supremacy (primacy) of EU law over conflicting national law, established in Costa v ENEL (1964). Other important principles include subsidiarity, proportionality and the protection of fundamental rights.

Institutions

Law-making involves the European Commission, which proposes legislation, and the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union, which adopt it. The Court of Justice in Luxembourg is the highest judicial authority on EU law.
