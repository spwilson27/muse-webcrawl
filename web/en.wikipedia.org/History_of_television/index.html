History of television

The history of television records the work of many inventors who, from the 19th century onward, sought to transmit moving images by wire and radio. Mechanical television systems based on Paul Nipkow's scanning disk (patented 1884) gave way in the 1930s to all-electronic systems developed by Philo Farnsworth, Vladimir Zworykin, and EMI's team in Britain. Regular broadcasting began in the late 1930s, television became the dominant mass medium after the Second World War, and it has since evolved through colour, cable, satellite, digital, and internet streaming eras.

Mechanical television

The concept of scanning an image line by line dates to the 1880s: Paul Nipkow's 1884 patent described a rotating disk with spiral apertures for dissecting a picture into sequential signals. Practical demonstrations came in the 1920s. In 1925–26 John Logie Baird in London transmitted recognisable human faces and gave the first public demonstration of a mechanical television system; Charles Francis Jenkins worked in parallel in the United States, and AT&T's Herbert Ives demonstrated television over wire and radio in 1927. Mechanical systems reached 240 lines of resolution but were a technological dead end: they could not match the brightness and definition of electronic cameras.

Electronic television

Vladimir Zworykin, at Westinghouse and then RCA, developed the iconoscope camera tube (patented 1923, practical by the 1930s) and the kinescope receiver. Philo Farnsworth, conceiving his image dissector as a schoolboy, transmitted the first all-electronic television image — a straight line — on 7 September 1927 in San Francisco, and won key patent battles against RCA. In Britain, the EMI team under Isaac Shoenberg developed the Emitron camera and a 405-line system adopted by the BBC, which began the world's first regular high-definition service in November 1936 from Alexandra Palace, quickly dropping Baird's mechanical system. In Germany, regular broadcasts began in 1935.

Expansion after the war

The Second World War halted most services, but postwar America saw explosive growth: US households with television rose from a few thousand in 1946 to a majority by 1955. The FCC's 1948–52 licence "freeze" ended with a channel allocation plan, and networks (NBC, CBS, ABC, DuMont) built national programming of news, drama, comedy, and sport. Coronations, presidential debates (Kennedy–Nixon, 1960), the assassination of John F. Kennedy (1963), and the Apollo 11 Moon landing (1969) demonstrated television's power as a shared public medium. Europe, Japan, and the rest of the world built largely public-service or mixed systems.

Colour, cable and satellite

Compatible colour arrived with the US NTSC standard of 1953 (broadcasts in earnest from the mid-1960s); Europe adopted PAL and SECAM in the 1960s. Cable television, begun as community antenna service in 1948, expanded channel capacity and spawned dedicated networks such as HBO (1972), CNN (1980), and MTV (1981). Communications satellites (Telstar, 1962) enabled live transoceanic broadcasts, and direct-to-home satellite and digital compression multiplied channels in the 1990s.

Digital and streaming era

Digital broadcasting standards (DVB, ATSC, ISDB) replaced analogue signals in most countries between 2006 and the early 2020s, bringing high-definition and flat-panel displays. From the late 2000s, internet streaming services — YouTube (2005), Netflix streaming (2007), and many successors — shifted viewing to on-demand models, fragmenting the broadcast audience while making television production global. Television remains one of the most influential media ever developed, with billions of viewers worldwide.
