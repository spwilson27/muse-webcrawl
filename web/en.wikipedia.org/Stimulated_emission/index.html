Stimulated emission

Stimulated emission is the process by which an incoming photon of a specific frequency can interact with an excited atomic electron (or other excited molecular state), causing it to drop to a lower energy level. The liberated energy transfers to the electromagnetic field, creating a new photon with a frequency, polarization, and direction of travel that are all identical to the photons of the incident wave. This is in contrast to spontaneous emission, which occurs at random without regard to the ambient electromagnetic field.

History

Albert Einstein proposed the process in 1917 as part of his quantum theory of radiation, introducing what are now called the Einstein coefficients. His derivation showed that, in thermal equilibrium, the rate of stimulated emission must balance absorption and spontaneous emission in a way consistent with Planck's law of black-body radiation. The phenomenon was later confirmed experimentally and became the physical basis of the maser (1953) and the laser (1960).

Overview

When an electron in an excited state E2 interacts with a photon whose energy equals the gap E2 − E1, it may decay to the lower state E1, emitting a second photon identical to the first. The two photons are coherent: they share the same phase, frequency, direction, and polarization. A population inversion — more atoms in the excited state than the lower state — allows an avalanche of such events, producing amplification of light.

Einstein coefficients

Einstein described three processes: absorption (coefficient B12), spontaneous emission (A21), and stimulated emission (B21). For a two-level system in equilibrium with a radiation field, detailed balance yields the relations g1B12 = g2B21 and A21/B21 = 8πhν³/c³, where g are the degeneracies of the levels.

Applications

Stimulated emission is the operating principle of lasers and masers, which are used in communications, medicine, manufacturing, and scientific research. See also spontaneous emission, population inversion, and photon.
