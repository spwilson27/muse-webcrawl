Edible mushroom

Edible mushrooms are the fleshy fruiting bodies of several species of fungi that can be eaten safely and are valued for their flavour, texture, and nutritional content. They are consumed worldwide, both gathered wild and cultivated commercially on substrates such as compost, straw, and logs.

A selection of cultivated and wild edible mushrooms

Cultivated species

The most widely cultivated mushroom in the world is the button mushroom, Agaricus bisporus, sold in immature white form and as the mature portobello. Other major commercial species include the oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus), shiitake (Lentinula edodes), enoki (Flammulina velutipes), and the paddy straw mushroom (Volvariella volvacea). China accounts for the great majority of world production.

Wild species

Highly prized wild mushrooms include the porcini (Boletus edulis), chanterelles (Cantharellus), morels (Morchella), and truffles (Tuber), the last hunted with trained dogs or pigs because they grow underground in association with tree roots. Wild gathering carries risks: several deadly species, such as the death cap (Amanita phalloides), can be mistaken for edible look-alikes, so identification must be certain before consumption.

Nutrition

Mushrooms are low in calories and fat while providing B vitamins, selenium, potassium, and — when exposed to ultraviolet light — vitamin D. Their savoury taste comes largely from glutamates, making them a natural source of umami flavour in cooking.

History

Mushroom consumption dates back thousands of years; the ancient Egyptians, Greeks, and Romans all valued them, and systematic cultivation began in France in the 17th century, where Agaricus was grown in caves and quarries around Paris.
