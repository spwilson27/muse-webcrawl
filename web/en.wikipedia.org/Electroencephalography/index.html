Electroencephalography

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a method to record an electrogram of the spontaneous electrical activity of the brain. The biosignals detected by EEG have been shown to represent the postsynaptic potentials of pyramidal neurons in the neocortex and allocortex.

History

The German physiologist Hans Berger recorded the first human EEG in 1924 and described the alpha rhythm. Modern systems place electrodes on the scalp according to the international 10–20 system.

Brain waves

EEG signals are classified by frequency band: delta (below 4 Hz, deep sleep), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz, relaxed wakefulness), beta (13–30 Hz, active thinking), and gamma (above 30 Hz). Patterns change with sleep stage, attention, and disease.

Clinical use

EEG is a principal diagnostic tool for epilepsy, sleep disorders, encephalopathies, and the assessment of brain death. It is also used in anaesthesia monitoring and in brain–computer interfaces.

Limitations

Scalp EEG has excellent temporal resolution but poor spatial resolution, since signals are smeared by the skull and scalp. Related techniques include magnetoencephalography and event-related potentials.
