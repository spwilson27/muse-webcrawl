War in the Vendée

The War in the Vendée (French: Guerre de Vendée) was a counter-revolutionary insurrection that took place in the Vendée region of western France from 1793 to 1796, during the French Revolution. Peasants, royalists and Catholic clergy rose against the revolutionary government, provoked above all by the levy of 300,000 men decreed in February 1793 and by the persecution of refractory priests.

Course of the war

The insurgents formed the Catholic and Royal Army, led by figures such as Jacques Cathelineau, Maurice d'Elbée, the Marquis de Bonchamps, and the young Henri de La Rochejaquelein. After early victories at Thouars, Saumur and Fontenay, the Vendéens failed to take Nantes and were decisively defeated at Cholet in October 1793. Their desperate march north — the Virée de Galerne — ended with catastrophic defeats at Le Mans and Savenay in December 1793.

Repression

The republican repression that followed was exceptionally brutal: General Turreau's "infernal columns" devastated the region in early 1794, and mass drownings (noyades) were carried out at Nantes under Carrier. Historians estimate the war and repression cost well over 170,000 lives, and the scale of the killing has fueled a continuing debate over whether it should be described as genocide. Fighting flared again in 1795 and 1799 before the region was finally pacified under Napoleon.
