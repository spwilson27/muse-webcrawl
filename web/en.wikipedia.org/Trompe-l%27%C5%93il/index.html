Trompe-l'œil

Trompe-l'œil (French for "deceives the eye") is an artistic technique that uses realistic imagery to create the optical illusion that the depicted objects exist in three dimensions. It has been used in painting, murals, architecture and street art for two thousand years.

History

Pliny the Elder tells of the ancient Greek painters Zeuxis, who painted grapes so convincingly that birds tried to eat them, and Parrhasius, who fooled Zeuxis himself with a painted curtain. The technique flourished in Renaissance perspective painting, in Baroque ceiling frescoes that seem to open buildings to the sky (quadratura), and in 19th-century American still life by artists such as William Harnett.

Modern use

Trompe-l'œil murals adorn building facades in many cities, and the technique underlies forced-perspective set design in theatre and theme parks. Painters such as Salvador Dalí and M. C. Escher played with related illusions, though their work often departs from strict realism.
