The Anti-Corn Law League was a successful political movement in Great Britain aimed at the abolition of the unpopular Corn Laws, which protected landowners against competition from cheap foreign grain. Founded in Manchester in 1838 and led by Richard Cobden and John Bright, the League mobilised the industrial middle classes in a pioneering modern campaign of meetings, pamphlets, lectures, and electoral registration.

The Corn Laws

The Corn Laws, notably that of 1815, imposed tariffs and restrictions on imported grain, keeping bread prices high. Manufacturers argued that dear bread forced up wages and harmed exports, while reformers framed repeal as a moral cause against aristocratic privilege and hunger.

Campaign and victory

The League raised large funds, flooded the country with literature, and built a mass membership. Faced with the Irish potato famine and League pressure, the Conservative prime minister Sir Robert Peel repealed the Corn Laws in 1846 with Whig support — a decision that split his party. The League is remembered as a landmark in organised pressure-group politics and in the advance of free trade.
