Brownian noise

In science, Brownian noise, also known as Brown noise or red noise, is the type of signal noise produced by Brownian motion. It is named after Robert Brown, who observed the irregular movement of pollen grains in water, rather than after the colour brown. Its power spectral density decreases with frequency at a rate proportional to 1/f², so it carries much more energy at low frequencies than pink noise or white noise.

Properties

Brownian noise can be generated by integrating white noise: each sample is the previous sample plus a random increment. Because the integration process causes the signal to wander, a generated Brownian signal tends to drift away from zero and usually needs its mean removed before use. In decibels its spectrum falls at about 6 dB per octave.

Applications

Brown noise is used in audio as a deep, rumbling sound resembling distant thunder, ocean surf or strong wind, and is popular in sound masking and sleep aids because of its concentration of low-frequency energy. In physics and engineering, Brownian noise models describe systems whose state is the cumulative integral of random fluctuations, such as certain random-walk and drift processes.
