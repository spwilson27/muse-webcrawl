Wedding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A wedding is a ceremony where two people are united in marriage. Wedding traditions and customs vary greatly between cultures, ethnic groups, religions, countries and social classes. Most wedding ceremonies involve an exchange of marriage vows by a couple, presentation of a gift (offering, rings, symbolic items, flowers, money or a dress), and a public proclamation of marriage by an authority figure or celebrant.

Customs and traditions

Special wedding garments are often worn, and the ceremony is sometimes followed by a wedding reception. Music, poetry, prayers or readings from religious texts or literature are also commonly incorporated. Many traditions include the exchange of wedding rings as a symbol of the union, the wearing of a white wedding dress — popularised in the West after Queen Victoria's marriage in 1840 — the throwing of a bouquet, and a wedding cake.

Religious and cultural variations

In Christian traditions the wedding is a sacrament or covenant celebrated in church; Jewish weddings take place under a chuppah and end with the breaking of a glass; Hindu weddings centre on rituals around a sacred fire; and Islamic marriages are solemnised by a contract and mahr. Civil ceremonies, required in many countries, are conducted by state officials independently of religion.

Legal significance

In most jurisdictions a valid wedding must satisfy legal requirements — a marriage license, minimum ages, witnesses and registration — and it changes the couple's legal status, affecting property, taxation, inheritance and next-of-kin rights. Same-sex weddings have become legally recognised in a growing number of countries since the Netherlands first legalised them in 2001.
