Democratic backsliding

Democratic backsliding is a process of regime change toward autocracy in which the quality of democracy in a state gradually deteriorates. It is the opposite of democratization. Democratic backsliding typically takes place incrementally and legally: elected incumbents weaken checks on executive power, tilt electoral rules in their favor, capture courts and media regulators, and harass opposition and civil society, often while retaining elections and democratic rhetoric.

Causes and mechanisms

Scholars identify several contributing factors, including political polarization, economic inequality or insecurity, distrust in institutions, populist appeals, and the weakening of informal democratic norms such as mutual toleration and institutional forbearance. Common mechanisms include court packing, changes to electoral laws, pressure on independent media, manipulation of state resources for partisan advantage, and delegitimization of election results.

Measurement

Democratic backsliding is tracked by indices such as those of the Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) Institute, Freedom House, and the Economist Intelligence Unit, which have reported a global trend of autocratization in the twenty-first century, sometimes described as a "third wave of autocratization".

Examples discussed in scholarship

Countries frequently analyzed in the literature on backsliding include Venezuela under Hugo Chávez and Nicolás Maduro, Hungary under Viktor Orbán, Turkey under Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, and Russia under Vladimir Putin; the degree and trajectory of backsliding in various other states remains a subject of scholarly debate.
