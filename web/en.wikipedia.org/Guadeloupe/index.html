Guadeloupe (French: [ɡwad(ə)lup]; Guadeloupean Creole: Gwadloup) is an archipelago and overseas department and region of France in the Caribbean. It consists of six inhabited islands — Basse-Terre, Grande-Terre, Marie-Galante, La Désirade and the two Îles des Saintes — as well as many uninhabited islands and outcrops. It lies south of Antigua and Barbuda and Montserrat and north of Dominica in the Leeward Islands of the Lesser Antilles.

Geography

The two main islands are separated by a narrow sea channel, the Rivière Salée, and together resemble a butterfly: mountainous, volcanic Basse-Terre in the west, dominated by the active La Soufrière volcano (1,467 m), and the lower limestone plateau of Grande-Terre in the east, where most of the population and the economic centre of Pointe-à-Pitre are located. Guadeloupe National Park covers much of Basse-Terre's rainforest interior.

History

The islands were settled by Arawak and later Carib peoples; Christopher Columbus landed in 1493 and named the island Santa María de Guadalupe after the Virgin of Guadalupe in Extremadura, Spain. France colonised Guadeloupe from 1635, developing sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans. Britain occupied the islands several times in the eighteenth century. Slavery was abolished in 1794 during the French Revolution, restored by Napoleon in 1802 against fierce resistance led by Louis Delgrès, and finally abolished in 1848. In 1946 Guadeloupe became an overseas department of France, and its residents are French and European Union citizens.

Economy and culture

The economy depends on agriculture (bananas and sugar cane, with rum distilling), tourism and public-sector employment, and unemployment has historically been high. Guadeloupean culture blends French, African and Caribbean influences; the Creole language, gwoka drumming and dance (inscribed by UNESCO), and the carnival are central traditions.
