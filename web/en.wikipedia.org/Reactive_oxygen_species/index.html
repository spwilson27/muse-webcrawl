Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive chemicals formed from diatomic oxygen (O2). They include peroxides, superoxide, the hydroxyl radical, singlet oxygen, and alpha-oxygen. ROS are natural byproducts of aerobic metabolism — especially electron leakage from the mitochondrial electron transport chain — and they also are deliberately produced by enzymes such as the NADPH oxidases of immune cells. In biology, ROS play a dual role: at controlled levels they function in cell signalling and host defence, while excess production causes oxidative stress, damaging DNA, proteins, and lipids.

Chemistry and sources

Sequential one-electron reduction of oxygen generates ROS: O2 → superoxide (O2•−) → hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) → hydroxyl radical (•OH) → water. The hydroxyl radical, often generated from peroxide by Fe2+ in the Fenton reaction, is among the most reactive species in biology, reacting at nearly diffusion-limited rates with almost any biomolecule. Exogenous sources include ionising and ultraviolet radiation, cigarette smoke, and certain drugs and pollutants.

Biological roles

Phagocytes such as neutrophils and macrophages exploit ROS in the "respiratory burst," generating superoxide via NADPH oxidase to kill engulfed microbes; patients with chronic granulomatous disease, who lack this enzyme, suffer recurrent infections. Hydrogen peroxide, relatively stable and membrane-permeable, acts as a second messenger in redox signalling, modulating pathways involving NF-κB, MAP kinases, and growth factors, and contributes to thyroid hormone synthesis and wound responses.

Oxidative stress and disease

When ROS production exceeds antioxidant capacity, oxidative damage accumulates: DNA base oxidation (e.g., 8-oxoguanine) and strand breaks, lipid peroxidation of membranes, and protein carbonylation. Oxidative stress has been implicated in ageing and in many diseases, including cancer, atherosclerosis, diabetes, neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's and Parkinson's), and ischemia–reperfusion injury. The free-radical theory of ageing, proposed by Denham Harman in the 1950s, links cumulative ROS damage to senescence, though later research has nuanced the picture.

Antioxidant defences

Cells maintain layered defences: the enzymes superoxide dismutase (converting superoxide to H2O2), catalase and glutathione peroxidase (reducing peroxide to water), the thioredoxin and glutathione systems, and repair enzymes for oxidised DNA and proteins. Dietary antioxidants such as vitamins C and E contribute modestly; large clinical trials of antioxidant supplements have generally not shown the benefits once expected, partly because ROS also serve essential signalling roles.
