Nehemiah

Nehemiah (Hebrew: נְחֶמְיָה, Nəḥemyā, "Yah has comforted") is the central figure of the Book of Nehemiah in the Hebrew Bible. A Jewish official at the Persian court of Artaxerxes I in Susa, he was appointed governor of the Persian province of Yehud (Judah) in the mid-5th century BCE and led the rebuilding of the walls of Jerusalem and a programme of social and religious reform.

Life and work

According to his memoir, Nehemiah served as cupbearer to the king. Hearing from his brother Hanani that Jerusalem's walls lay in ruins, he obtained the king's permission and royal letters of safe passage, and travelled to Jerusalem in the twentieth year of Artaxerxes (445/444 BCE). After a secret night inspection, he organised the returned exiles to rebuild the city wall, an effort completed — despite opposition from Sanballat the Horonite, Tobiah the Ammonite and Geshem the Arab — in a traditionally reported 52 days.

As governor, Nehemiah cancelled debts and returned mortgaged fields to the poor, refused the governor's food allowance, and, in a second term, enforced Sabbath observance, opposed intermarriage arrangements he considered a threat to the community's identity, and supported Ezra's public reading of the Torah.

The Book of Nehemiah

The Book of Nehemiah forms the second half of the combined work Ezra–Nehemiah. Its core is a first-person account often called the "Nehemiah Memoir", one of the earliest examples of autobiographical writing in the ancient world. Scholarship debates the exact dates of his governorships (commonly c. 445–433 BCE, with a second term after 433).
