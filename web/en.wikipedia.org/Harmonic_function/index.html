Harmonic function

In mathematics, mathematical physics, and the theory of stochastic processes, a harmonic function is a twice continuously differentiable function f defined on an open subset of Euclidean space that satisfies Laplace's equation, Δf = 0 — that is, the sum of its second partial derivatives is zero everywhere in its domain.

Properties

Harmonic functions enjoy remarkable regularity properties. They satisfy the mean value property: the value of the function at any point equals its average over any ball or sphere centred at that point. Consequently they obey the maximum principle — a non-constant harmonic function cannot attain a maximum or minimum inside its domain — and they are automatically infinitely differentiable and analytic, even if the definition requires only two derivatives.

Examples and applications

Examples include the real and imaginary parts of any holomorphic function (the connection is given by the Cauchy–Riemann equations), Newtonian gravitational and electrostatic potentials in source-free regions, steady-state temperature distributions, and velocity potentials of incompressible irrotational fluid flow. The study of boundary-value problems for harmonic functions — the Dirichlet problem — is a classical branch of partial differential equations, with solutions related to Brownian motion in probability theory.

Generalizations

Related notions include subharmonic and superharmonic functions, harmonic maps between Riemannian manifolds, and harmonic functions on graphs and metric spaces.
