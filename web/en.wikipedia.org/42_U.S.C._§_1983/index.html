42 U.S.C. § 1983

42 U.S.C. § 1983, commonly known as Section 1983, is a federal statute of the United States that provides an individual the right to sue state government employees and others acting "under color of" state law for violations of rights secured by the United States Constitution or federal law. It originated as Section 1 of the Civil Rights Act of 1871, also known as the Ku Klux Klan Act.

Text

The statute provides that every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage of a state, subjects any citizen of the United States or other person within its jurisdiction to the deprivation of rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws, shall be liable to the party injured in an action at law or suit in equity.

History and jurisprudence

Section 1983 lay largely dormant until the Supreme Court's 1961 decision in Monroe v. Pape, which held that Chicago police officers could be sued even though their conduct also violated state law. In Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978), the Court held that municipalities are "persons" subject to suit, though not on a theory of respondeat superior.

Qualified immunity

Defendants in Section 1983 actions frequently invoke qualified immunity, a judicially created doctrine shielding officials unless they violated "clearly established" law. The doctrine has been the subject of sustained debate in cases involving alleged police misconduct.
