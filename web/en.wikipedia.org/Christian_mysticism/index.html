Christian mysticism

Christian mysticism refers to mystical practices and theory within Christianity, concerning direct, experiential awareness of the presence of God and the pursuit of union with the divine. It has roots in the New Testament and the early Church and has been expressed in both Eastern and Western traditions, often — though not always — within monastic life.

Development

Early influences include the writings attributed to Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (c. 500), whose apophatic (negative) theology shaped medieval mysticism, and the desert fathers and mothers of Egypt. In the medieval West, figures such as Bernard of Clairvaux, Hildegard of Bingen, Francis of Assisi, Meister Eckhart, Julian of Norwich, Catherine of Siena, and the anonymous author of The Cloud of Unknowing developed rich accounts of contemplative prayer. The Spanish mystics Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross described the soul's journey through stages of purgation, illumination, and union, including the "dark night of the soul".

Eastern traditions

In Eastern Orthodoxy, the theology of Gregory Palamas defended the hesychast practice of contemplative prayer and the doctrine of theosis — the divinisation of the human person by grace — distinguishing between God's essence and energies.

Protestant and later mysticism

Although the Reformation was often suspicious of mysticism, mystical currents continued in figures such as Jakob Böhme, in Pietism and Quaker spirituality (with its doctrine of the Inner Light), and in later writers such as William Law and Evelyn Underhill, whose 1911 study Mysticism shaped modern understanding of the subject.
