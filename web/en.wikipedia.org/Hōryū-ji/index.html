Hōryū-ji

The pagoda (gojū-no-tō) and Golden Hall (kondō) of Hōryū-ji.

Hōryū-ji (法隆寺, "Temple of the Flourishing Dharma") is a Buddhist temple in Ikaruga, Nara Prefecture, Japan. Its full name is Hōryū Gakumonji, the "Temple of Learning and the Flourishing Law". The temple was founded by Prince Shōtoku and Empress Suiko and was completed in 607 AD. It is famous for housing some of the oldest surviving wooden buildings in the world, and in 1993 it was inscribed as Japan's first UNESCO World Heritage Site, together with neighbouring Hokki-ji, under the name "Buddhist Monuments in the Hōryū-ji Area".

History

The original temple, Wakakusa-dera, burned down in 670, according to the Nihon Shoki; the complex was rebuilt by the early 8th century, though a long scholarly controversy (the "reconstruction debate") once questioned whether the fire occurred. The present Western Precinct (Sai-in) contains the kondō (Golden Hall) and the five-storey pagoda, arranged in a layout reflecting continental Asuka-period styles. The Eastern Precinct (Tō-in), added in 739, is centred on the octagonal Yumedono, the Hall of Dreams, associated with Prince Shōtoku.

Architecture and treasures

The pagoda's central pillar was felled, dendrochronology shows, in 594, making it among the oldest timber used in any surviving building. The kondō preserves celebrated 7th-century wall paintings of Buddhist paradises, badly damaged by fire in 1949—an incident that spurred Japan's Law for the Protection of Cultural Properties—and houses treasures including the Shaka Triad cast by the sculptor Tori Busshi in 623, the Tamamushi Shrine (named for its jewel-beetle-wing decoration), and the Kudara Kannon. The temple's treasury of early Buddhist art is unmatched in Japan.
