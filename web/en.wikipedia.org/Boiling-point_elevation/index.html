Boiling-point elevation

Boiling-point elevation is the phenomenon whereby the boiling point of a liquid (a solvent) increases when another compound is dissolved in it, meaning that a solution has a higher boiling point than the pure solvent. This happens whenever a non-volatile solute, such as a salt, is added to a pure solvent, such as water.

Boiling-point elevation is a colligative property: it depends on the number of dissolved particles rather than their identity. The increase in boiling point, ΔTb, is proportional to the molal concentration of the solution:

ΔTb = Kb · b · i

where Kb is the ebullioscopic constant of the solvent, b is the molality, and i is the van 't Hoff factor accounting for dissociation of the solute into ions.

Explanation

The elevation occurs because the dissolved solute lowers the vapour pressure of the solvent; the solution must be heated to a higher temperature before its vapour pressure equals atmospheric pressure. The same vapour-pressure lowering underlies the related colligative properties of freezing-point depression and osmotic pressure.

Uses

Measuring the boiling-point elevation of a solution (ebullioscopy) historically allowed chemists to determine the molar mass of an unknown solute. The effect is also familiar in cooking, though the quantity of salt added to water raises the boiling point only slightly.
