Standard (motorcycle)

A typical standard, or "naked", motorcycle.

Standards, also called standard motorcycles, naked bikes, or historically roadsters, are motorcycles distinguished by an upright riding position, exposed engine and frame, and general-purpose design. The rider sits with feet roughly below the hips and hands on a moderately raised handlebar, giving neutral ergonomics suited to commuting, touring, and general riding rather than the specialised postures of sport bikes or cruisers. Standards are among the oldest configurations of motorcycle, descending from the plain machines built before the market fragmented into specialised categories.

Characteristics

Typical features include a steel or aluminium frame largely uncovered by fairings, a flat or slightly stepped seat usable by rider and passenger, mid-mounted footpegs, and engines ranging from small single-cylinder commuters to large four-cylinder machines. Because they lack expensive bodywork and extreme components, standards are usually affordable, easy to maintain, and highly customisable — a reason they are frequently recommended for learners and form the base for cafe racer, scrambler, and streetfighter custom builds.

History

From the early 20th century until the 1970s, nearly all motorcycles were standards, including classic British twins and the Japanese "Universal Japanese Motorcycle" (UJM) of the 1970s — exemplified by the Honda CB750, which combined an air-cooled inline-four engine, disc brakes, and electric starting in an upright all-rounder. As cruisers, sport bikes, and tourers emerged as separate segments, the standard was eclipsed in some markets, but it revived strongly from the 1990s as "naked bikes": unfaired derivatives of sport models such as the Ducati Monster, Triumph Speed Triple, and Yamaha MT series, which combine sportbike engines and chassis with upright, minimalist styling.
