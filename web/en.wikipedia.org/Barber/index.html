Barber


A barber at work with a straight razor.

A barber is a person whose occupation is mainly to cut, dress, groom, style and shave hair or beards, traditionally men's. A barber's place of work is known as a barbershop or a barber's. Barbershops are also places of social interaction and public discourse, and in some cases public forums. The trade is one of the oldest in the world: barbering was practised in ancient Egypt and Greece, and in the Middle Ages barbers also performed surgery and dentistry, giving rise to the figure of the barber-surgeon.

History

Razors have been found among relics of the Bronze Age, and barbering was a respected occupation in ancient Egypt, where barbers held positions in temples and palaces. In ancient Greece, men visited barbershops in the agora to have their beards trimmed and to exchange news and gossip — a social function barbershops retained ever after. In Rome the first shave (depositio barbae) marked a young man's coming of age.

In medieval Europe, barbers joined surgeons in guilds; in England the barbers and surgeons were united by statute in 1540, though the professions were formally separated again in 1745. From this era dates the barber's pole: its spiral stripes are said to represent the bandages used in bloodletting, once a routine part of the barber's work, with the pole itself the staff gripped by patients to encourage blood flow.

The nineteenth and early twentieth centuries were a golden age of the barbershop in Britain and America, where shops in hotels and town centres offered hot-towel shaves and tonics. The safety razor, marketed by Gillette from 1903, and later the electric razor, shifted much shaving to the home, and the rise of unisex hair salons in the 1960s–1980s eroded the traditional trade. From the 2010s, however, traditional barbershops enjoyed a strong revival alongside renewed interest in classic men's grooming, beard care and hot-towel shaves.

Work and training

Modern barbers cut and style hair with scissors and electric clippers, shave with straight or safety razors, trim beards and moustaches, and may offer scalp treatments and facial massage. In many jurisdictions — including most U.S. states — barbers must complete a course at a licensed barber school and pass an examination in both practical skills and sanitation before licensure, distinct from the licence held by cosmetologists or hairdressers. Tools of the trade include clippers, shears, combs, straight razors, shaving brushes and creams, aftershaves and tonics, and the hydraulic barber chair, a nineteenth-century innovation associated with manufacturers such as Koken and Takara Belmont.

Barbershop culture

The barbershop occupies a distinctive place in social life, particularly in African-American communities, where it has been studied and depicted — for example in the Barbershop films — as a hub of debate, humour and community news. The barbershop quartet singing style, with its close four-part harmony, takes its name from the impromptu harmonising once heard in American barbershops. Barbershop queues, regulars and the ritual of the haircut give the trade a cultural footprint far larger than its economic size.
