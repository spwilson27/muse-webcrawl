Barber-surgeon


Barbers performed surgery for centuries; the barber's pole is often said to derive from this era.

A barber-surgeon, common among Europe's medical practitioners during the Middle Ages and into the early modern period, was a person who could perform surgical procedures including bloodletting, cupping therapy, pulling teeth, and amputation. Barbers could also cut hair, shave beards, and perform other grooming tasks. Because barbers owned sharp instruments and were accustomed to working with blades, they were often called upon to perform surgery in addition to their regular duties.

History

In medieval Europe, surgery was not considered a respectable part of university medicine, and physicians often considered manual procedures beneath them. Monks, who were forbidden from shedding blood by a series of church edicts in the 12th and 13th centuries, had previously performed many medical procedures, and when they withdrew, barbers filled the gap. Barber-surgeons were organized in guilds, and in England the barbers and surgeons were formally united by Henry VIII in 1540 into the United Barber-Surgeon's Company, before being separated again in 1745 when the surgeons formed their own company, which eventually became the Royal College of Surgeons.

Practice

Barber-surgeons learned their trade through apprenticeship rather than university study. Their shops commonly displayed bowls of blood and bandages. The traditional barber's pole, with its red and white stripes, is frequently interpreted as a reference to blood and bandages, with the pole itself representing the stick patients gripped during bloodletting.

Notable barber-surgeons

The French surgeon Ambroise Paré, considered one of the fathers of modern surgery, began his career as a barber-surgeon before rising to serve several kings of France.
