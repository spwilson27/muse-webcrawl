Barbershop music


A barbershop quartet in characteristic dress.

Barbershop vocal harmony is a style of a cappella close harmony singing characterized by consonant four-part chords for every melody note in a primarily homorhythmic texture. Each of the four parts has its own role: generally the lead sings the melody, the tenor harmonizes above the melody, the bass sings the lowest harmonizing notes, and the baritone completes the chord, usually below the lead. The melody is not usually sung by the tenor or bass.

Characteristics

Barbershop music is typified by four-part harmony built on dominant seventh chords (the "barbershop seventh"), strong melodies in the middle of the range, and extensive use of "ringing" chords that produce audible overtones. Songs are frequently drawn from popular music of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, including Tin Pan Alley standards.

History

The style has roots in African American musical traditions of the late 19th century, including close-harmony singing in barbershops, minstrel shows, and church and social gatherings. Quartets became widely popular in the early 1900s, and the organized preservation movement began with the founding of the Society for the Preservation and Encouragement of Barber Shop Quartet Singing in America (SPEBSQSA, now the Barbershop Harmony Society) in Tulsa, Oklahoma, in 1938. Sweet Adelines International, founded in 1945, organizes women's barbershop singing, and Harmony, Incorporated was founded in 1959.

Performance

Ensembles range from quartets to large choruses. Performances are typically unaccompanied and often feature choreography, matching costumes, and in quartets a playful performance style. Competitions judge singing, music, and presentation categories.
