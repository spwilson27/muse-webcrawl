In organic chemistry, a hydrocarbon is an organic compound consisting entirely of hydrogen and carbon. Hydrocarbons are the principal constituents of petroleum and natural gas and serve as fuels and lubricants, as well as raw materials for the production of plastics, fibres, rubbers, solvents, and industrial chemicals. Because carbon can form chains, rings, and multiple bonds, hydrocarbons exist in an enormous variety of structures; systematic study of them laid the foundations of organic chemistry.

Classification

Hydrocarbons are divided into saturated and unsaturated types. The alkanes (general formula CnH2n+2) contain only single bonds; alkenes contain at least one carbon–carbon double bond and alkynes at least one triple bond. Ring-shaped saturated hydrocarbons are cycloalkanes. Aromatic hydrocarbons (arenes), such as benzene, toluene, and naphthalene, contain delocalised pi-electron rings with characteristic stability and reactivity. Hydrocarbons may also be grouped as aliphatic or cyclic according to their carbon skeletons.

Occurrence and use

Hydrocarbons occur naturally in crude oil, natural gas, coal, and in smaller quantities in plants and animals (for example, the waxes and terpenes). Fractional distillation of petroleum yields fractions from methane and liquefied petroleum gas through petrol, kerosene, diesel, and lubricating oils to bitumen. Combustion of hydrocarbons in oxygen produces carbon dioxide and water and releases energy, the basis of the internal combustion engine and of much electricity generation.

Environmental aspects

The burning of fossil hydrocarbons is the largest source of anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions and a principal driver of climate change. Spills of liquid hydrocarbons cause serious environmental damage, and some polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are toxic or carcinogenic. Methane is itself a potent greenhouse gas when released unburned.
