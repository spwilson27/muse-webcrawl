Conservatoire

A conservatoire (also conservatory, especially in American English) is an educational institution devoted to training in music — and sometimes in drama and dance — at a professional level. The name derives from the Italian conservatorio, originally charitable institutions in Naples and Venice that housed and educated orphaned or poor children (conservati, "conserved"), giving them intensive musical training.

History

The Neapolitan conservatories such as Santa Maria di Loreto (founded 1537) became famous schools of composition whose pupils and teachers shaped European opera and sacred music. The modern model crystallized with the Paris Conservatoire, founded in 1795 during the French Revolution, which combined free or state-supported tuition, admission by competitive examination, and a structured curriculum. Institutions modelled on it spread across Europe in the 19th century: the Leipzig Conservatory (1843, associated with Mendelssohn), the Royal Academy of Music in London (1822), the Vienna Conservatory, and the Saint Petersburg (1862) and Moscow (1866) conservatories founded by the Rubinstein brothers. In the United States, schools such as the Juilliard School, the Curtis Institute of Music, and the New England Conservatory adopted the model.

Training

Conservatoires offer undergraduate and postgraduate degrees in performance, composition, conducting, and musicology, typically centred on one-to-one instrumental or vocal lessons, ensemble work (orchestras, choirs, opera productions), and classes in theory, aural skills, and music history. Many also run junior departments for children and pre-college students. Admission is highly competitive and based primarily on audition.
