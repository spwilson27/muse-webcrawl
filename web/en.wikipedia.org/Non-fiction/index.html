Non-fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Non-fiction (or nonfiction) is any document, narrative, or other content that purports in good faith to represent truth and accuracy regarding information, events, or people. Its claims are made by an author who asserts their truthfulness, generally on the basis of evidence, research, or personal experience, in contrast to fiction, which presents imagined narratives. Non-fiction works may nonetheless employ literary techniques — scene construction, dialogue, and narrative voice — as in the "non-fiction novel" and narrative journalism.

Forms and genres

  • Biography and memoir: accounts of a life, written by another person or by the subject (autobiography).
  • Essays: short reflective or argumentative prose, from Montaigne and Bacon to the personal essay.
  • History and journalism: narratives and reports of past and current events.
  • Scientific, technical, and reference writing: textbooks, manuals, encyclopedias, dictionaries, and scholarly monographs.
  • Self-help, travel writing, cookbooks, and true crime: major commercial categories of modern publishing.
  • Documentary film and photography: non-fiction in visual media.

Distinguishing features

Unlike fiction, non-fiction traditionally invokes a truth claim that can be checked: its authors cite sources, submit to fact-checking, and can be held to account for fabrication, as in literary hoaxes and fabricated memoirs (for example, James Frey's A Million Little Pieces, revealed in 2006 to contain extensive inventions). The distinction is not absolute — works such as Truman Capote's In Cold Blood (1966) blend documentary reporting with novelistic method, and New Journalism of the 1960s and 1970s deliberately applied fiction's tools to factual reporting. In libraries, non-fiction is typically shelved by subject under classification schemes such as the Dewey Decimal system, while fiction is arranged by author.
