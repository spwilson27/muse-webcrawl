Fourth Crusade

The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) was a Latin Christian armed expedition called by Pope Innocent III to recover Jerusalem from Muslim control by way of Egypt. Instead, diverted by Venetian interests and Byzantine dynastic politics, the crusaders twice attacked Christian cities, culminating in the sack of Constantinople in April 1204 and the partition of the Byzantine Empire among the victors.

Background

Unable to pay Venice the full contracted price for transport, the crusaders agreed to attack the Catholic city of Zara in 1202 — earning excommunication from the outraged Pope. The exiled Byzantine prince Alexios IV then offered money, troops and church union if the crusade restored him to the throne.

Sack of Constantinople

After Alexios IV was overthrown and murdered in 1204, the crusaders stormed Constantinople on 13 April and subjected it to three days of looting, destroying or carrying off countless works of art — including the bronze Horses of Saint Mark — and relics. Baldwin of Flanders was crowned Latin Emperor.

Consequences

The empire was partitioned between Venice, the crusader barons and Greek successor states at Nicaea, Epirus and Trebizond. Though the Nicaeans retook Constantinople in 1261, the sack permanently embittered Greek–Latin relations and fatally weakened Byzantium; in 2001 Pope John Paul II twice apologized for the events.
