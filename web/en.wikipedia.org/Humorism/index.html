Humorism

Humorism, the humoral theory, or humoralism, is a system of medicine detailing a supposed makeup and workings of the human body, adopted by Ancient Greek and Roman physicians and philosophers. Humorism began to fall out of favour in the 17th century and was definitively disproved in the 1850s with the advent of germ theory of disease. According to the theory, the human body is filled with four basic substances, called humors, which are in balance when a person is healthy. All diseases and disabilities supposedly resulted from an excess or deficit of one of these four humors.

The four humors

The four humors were blood, phlegm, yellow bile (choler), and black bile (melancholy). Each was associated with one of the four elements (air, water, fire, earth), a season, an organ, and a temperament: sanguine, phlegmatic, choleric, and melancholic respectively. The theory is usually credited to Hippocrates and was systematised by Galen, whose writings dominated Western medicine for well over a millennium.

Treatments

Because disease was understood as imbalance, treatment aimed at restoring equilibrium: bloodletting and emetics to remove excess humor, dietary regulation, and the administration of substances thought to possess opposite qualities of hot, cold, wet, and dry. Methods such as cupping, purging, and the use of leeches persisted in European practice into the nineteenth century.

Decline and legacy

The theory was progressively undermined by anatomical and physiological discoveries, including Harvey's demonstration of the circulation of the blood (1628), the development of cellular pathology by Rudolf Virchow, and the germ theory established by Pasteur and others in the mid-nineteenth century. Humoral concepts survive in language (words such as "melancholy", "sanguine", and "bad humour") and in the Ayurvedic tradition of the three doshas, which developed in parallel in India and remains in practice.
