Physics engine

A physics engine is computer software that provides an approximate simulation of certain physical systems, such as rigid body dynamics (including collision detection), soft body dynamics, and fluid dynamics. It is of use in the fields of computer graphics, video games and film (CGI). Their main uses are in video games (typically as middleware), in which case the simulations are in real-time. The term is sometimes used more generally to describe any software system for simulating physical phenomena, such as high-performance scientific simulation.

Description

There are generally two classes of physics engines: real-time and high-precision. High-precision physics engines require more processing power to calculate very precise physics and are usually used by scientists and computer-animated films. Real-time physics engines, like those used in video games and other forms of interactive computing, use simplified calculations and lower precision to calculate physics in a timely manner so that the game can respond at an appropriate speed.

Most physics engines are based on three primary components: a collision detection system, rigid body dynamics, and a solver that applies constraints and forces to the bodies in the scene.

History

One of the first general purpose physics engines was the Havok engine, released in 2000. Other widely used engines include PhysX by NVIDIA, Bullet, and the open-source Open Dynamics Engine. Physics simulation in games became increasingly common during the 2000s, with titles such as Half-Life 2 (2004) demonstrating advanced rigid-body interaction.

Applications

Physics engines are used in video games, film visual effects, robotics simulation, medical simulation, and scientific research. Scientific physics engines are used to simulate phenomena such as weather systems, structural stress, and aerodynamics, where accuracy is valued over speed.
