Reason

Reason is the capacity of consciously applying logic by drawing conclusions from new or existing information, with the aim of seeking the truth. It is closely associated with characteristics of human beings such as intentionality, language, rationality and problem solving. Reason is sometimes referred to as rationality and is contrasted with emotion, instinct and faith.

Forms of reasoning

Philosophers and logicians distinguish several forms: deductive reasoning, which derives conclusions that must be true if the premises are true; inductive reasoning, which generalises from observed cases; abductive reasoning, which infers the most likely explanation; and analogical reasoning, which argues from similar cases. A further distinction contrasts theoretical reason (concerned with what is true) with practical reason (concerned with what to do).

History of the concept

In ancient Greek philosophy, logos and nous named the rational order of the cosmos and the human faculty that grasps it; Aristotle's syllogistic defined formal logic for two millennia. Medieval scholasticism treated reason as subordinate to, though harmonious with, revelation. The Enlightenment elevated reason as the primary source of authority and legitimacy, exemplified by Descartes' rationalism and Kant's critiques of pure and practical reason. Hume argued that reason is, and ought only to be, the "slave of the passions" in practical matters.

Reason and science

Reasoning underlies the scientific method: forming hypotheses, deriving predictions and evaluating evidence. Modern cognitive science studies how actual human reasoning often deviates from logical norms through heuristics and biases, while dual-process theories distinguish fast, intuitive thinking from slow, deliberate reasoning.
