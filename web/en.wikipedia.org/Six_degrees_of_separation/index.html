Six degrees of separation

Six degrees of separation Origin: Frigyes Karinthy, 1929
Key experiment: Milgram's small-world experiment, 1967
Related concept: Erdős number; Bacon number

Six degrees of separation is the idea that all people are six or fewer social connections away from each other, so that a chain of "friend of a friend" statements can be made to connect any two people in a maximum of six steps. It is one instance of the broader small-world phenomenon.

Origins

The idea was first proposed in 1929 by the Hungarian writer Frigyes Karinthy in a short story called "Chains" (Láncszemek), in which characters wager that any person on Earth can be reached through at most five intermediaries. The concept was later popularised by John Guare's 1990 play Six Degrees of Separation, which was adapted into a 1993 film starring Will Smith.

Milgram's experiment

In 1967 the psychologist Stanley Milgram conducted his small-world experiment, in which packets were sent from randomly selected people in the American Midwest to a target person in Boston via acquaintances. Among the chains that completed, the average path length was about six, giving rise to the popular phrase. The methodology and conclusions have been debated, since most packets never arrived.

Modern measurements

Large-scale studies of online social networks have generally supported short average path lengths: a 2011 study of Facebook users found an average of about 4.7 degrees, and a 2016 update reduced the estimate to about 3.6 within Facebook's user base. The parlour game "Six Degrees of Kevin Bacon" applies the idea to film actors.
