Naser al-Din Shah Qajar
Shah of Persia: 1848–1896
Born: 16 July 1831, Tabriz
Died: 1 May 1896, Tehran (assassinated)
Predecessor: Mohammad Shah Qajar
Successor: Mozaffar ad-Din Shah Qajar

Naser al-Din Shah Qajar (16 July 1831 – 1 May 1896) was the Shah of Persia (Iran) from 1848 until his assassination in 1896. He was the third-longest reigning monarch in Iranian history, ruling for nearly half a century. His reign saw Persia caught between British and Russian imperial pressure yet also began a cautious programme of modernisation.

Reign

Naser al-Din came to the throne aged 17 with the support of his reforming prime minister, Amir Kabir, who founded the Dar ul-Funun polytechnic, reformed the army and finances, and suppressed the Bábí movement — but was dismissed and executed in 1852 on the Shah's orders. Persia lost Herat in the Anglo-Persian War of 1856–57. The Shah made three celebrated journeys to Europe (1873, 1878, 1889), introducing such novelties as photography (of which he was an enthusiastic practitioner), the telegraph and new court fashions. To fund the state he granted concessions to foreigners, most notoriously the 1890 tobacco concession to a British company, whose mass boycott in the Tobacco Protest of 1891–92 forced its cancellation and foreshadowed the Persian Constitutional Revolution.

Assassination and legacy

On 1 May 1896, on the eve of the fiftieth anniversary of his reign, Naser al-Din was shot at the shrine of Shah Abdol-Azim in Rayy by Mirza Reza Kermani, a follower of the pan-Islamic activist Jamal al-Din al-Afghani. He was succeeded by his son Mozaffar ad-Din Shah. His diaries, photographs and the Golestan Palace complex in Tehran remain important records of Qajar Iran.
