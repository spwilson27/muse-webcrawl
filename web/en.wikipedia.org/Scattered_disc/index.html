The scattered disc (or scattered disk) is a distant circumstellar disc region of the Solar System sparsely populated by icy small Solar System bodies known as scattered-disc objects (SDOs). It lies beyond the Kuiper belt and is thought to be the principal source of short-period comets. Scattered-disc objects have highly eccentric and inclined orbits: their perihelia lie near Neptune (around 30–38 AU), while their aphelia can reach 100 AU or more.

Formation

SDOs are believed to be former Kuiper belt objects that were "scattered" by gravitational interactions with Neptune as the giant planets migrated early in Solar System history (as described by the Nice model). Unlike classical Kuiper belt objects on stable, near-circular orbits, scattered-disc objects continue to interact with Neptune and are dynamically unstable over the age of the Solar System; their orbits evolve slowly, and some are injected inward to become centaurs and eventually Jupiter-family comets.

Notable objects

The largest known scattered-disc object is the dwarf planet Eris, discovered in 2005—slightly more massive than Pluto and accompanied by the moon Dysnomia. Sedna, with a perihelion of 76 AU, belongs to an even more distant "extended" or detached population and is grouped by some astronomers with the inner Oort cloud. Other notable members include Gonggong and the first recognized SDO, 1996 TL66.

Composition

Like other trans-Neptunian objects, scattered-disc objects are composed largely of water, methane, and ammonia ices with rocky cores. Their surfaces show a range of colours from neutral grey to very red, reflecting differing compositions and irradiation histories.
