Historicism

Historicism
German termHistorismus
Key figuresHerder, Ranke, Dilthey
Famous criticKarl Popper

Historicism is the idea that human societies, cultures and ideas must be understood in their own historical context — as products of particular times and places — rather than judged by timeless universal standards. Emerging in German thought around 1800 with figures such as Johann Gottfried Herder, it reacted against Enlightenment rationalism and its universal "man".

Meaning in historiography

For Leopold von Ranke, every epoch is "immediate to God": each age has its own values and must be grasped on its own terms. Wilhelm Dilthey extended this into a method for the human sciences, distinct from natural science: understanding (Verstehen) rather than explanation. The approach underlies modern academic history, anthropology and much literary study.

Popper's critique

Karl Popper, in The Poverty of Historicism (1957), used the word differently and influentially: for him "historicism" meant the claim that history follows discoverable laws allowing prediction of the future — a doctrine he attacked in Marxism and other grand theories. The two senses are often confused; Popper's target was historical determinism, not contextual interpretation.

Other uses

In architecture, historicism denotes the 19th-century revival of past styles (Gothic Revival, Neoclassicism). In theology it describes reading prophecy as fulfilled in past events, and in economics it names the German Historical School, which held that economic laws vary by era. See historiography.
