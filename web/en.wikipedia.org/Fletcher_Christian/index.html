Fletcher Christian (25 September 1764 – 20 September 1793) was an English seaman who led the mutiny on HMS Bounty against its captain, Lieutenant William Bligh, on 28 April 1789. After seizing the ship in the Pacific, Christian attempted to settle at Tubuai, then returned to Tahiti, and finally sailed with a small party of mutineers and Polynesian men and women to the remote, incorrectly charted Pitcairn Island, where they burned the Bounty and founded a settlement whose descendants live there today.

Early life

Christian was born at Moorland Close, near Cockermouth in Cumberland, into a well-connected family of Manx descent. He attended Cockermouth Free School — where the poet William Wordsworth was a younger contemporary — and joined the Royal Navy as a teenager, serving under Bligh on earlier voyages and rising to master's mate.

The Bounty voyage and mutiny

The Bounty sailed to Tahiti in 1787–88 to collect breadfruit plants for transport to the West Indies. During a five-month stay, discipline relaxed and many crew formed attachments ashore. After departure in April 1789, relations between Bligh and Christian — who had been promoted to acting lieutenant — deteriorated sharply amid accusations over stolen coconuts. Near Tofua in the Tonga islands, Christian and his followers seized Bligh in his cabin and set him adrift with eighteen loyalists in the ship's open launch; Bligh famously navigated some 3,600 nautical miles to Timor without charts.

Pitcairn Island

Finding Tubuai hostile, Christian's party returned briefly to Tahiti, kidnapped or recruited Polynesians, and sailed in search of a hiding place beyond naval reach. In January 1790 they reached Pitcairn, mischarted on Admiralty maps, landed everything of use, and burned the ship. The settlement soon collapsed into violence: disputes over land and women, and the enslavement of the Polynesian men, led to a series of killings in which Christian himself died — by most accounts murdered in September 1793, though rumours that he escaped back to England persisted for decades.

Legacy

Christian's descendants, bearing his surname, remain prominent on Pitcairn and Norfolk Island. The mutiny has inspired numerous books and films, in which Christian has been variously portrayed as a romantic rebel against tyranny or as a troubled and violent man; the historical record supports elements of both readings.
