A presidential system, or single executive system, is a form of government in which a head of government (president) leads an executive branch that is separate from the legislative branch in systems that use separation of powers. This head of government is in most cases also the head of state. The president is elected directly by the people (or by an electoral college) for a fixed term and cannot be dismissed by the legislature except through extraordinary procedures such as impeachment.

Characteristics

  • Separate election: the president and the legislature are each elected by the voters, giving both a claim to democratic legitimacy.
  • Fixed terms: the president serves for a constitutionally fixed period; elections occur on schedule, not at the discretion of either branch.
  • Fused roles: the president is simultaneously head of state and head of government, appointing and directing the cabinet.
  • Separation of personnel: members of the legislature generally cannot sit in the executive branch, and vice versa.
  • Checks and balances: the legislature controls budgets and may override presidential vetoes; the president may veto legislation and command the armed forces.

History

The archetype is the United States, whose 1787 Constitution combined popular sovereignty with ideas of mixed government and Montesquieu's separation of powers. As Latin American nations won independence in the 19th century, most adopted presidential constitutions modelled on the United States. In the 20th century presidentialism spread to Africa and Asia, often combined with strong executives; France's Fifth Republic (1958) created a semi-presidential hybrid in which executive power is shared between a president and a prime minister responsible to parliament.

Criticism and debate

Political scientist Juan Linz famously argued that presidentialism is prone to democratic breakdown: dual legitimacy invites deadlock when president and legislature are controlled by opposing parties; fixed terms make crises rigid, since an unpopular or incompetent president cannot easily be removed; and winner-take-all elections encourage zero-sum politics. Other scholars, such as José Antonio Cheibub, counter that presidential democracies have not proved systematically less durable once wealth and regional factors are considered. Defenders point to the system's clear accountability, stability of the executive, and the direct mandate voters give the president.

Comparison with parliamentarism

In a parliamentary system, the executive (prime minister and cabinet) emerges from and remains accountable to the legislature, and the head of state is typically a separate, largely ceremonial office. Presidential systems keep the two roles united and the two branches independent, trading flexibility for stability and a clear separation of powers.
