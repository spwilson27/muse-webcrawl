Vajont Dam

LocationVajont valley, Friuli-Venezia Giulia, Italy
TypeDouble-curvature arch dam
Height262 m (860 ft)
Construction1956–1960
StatusDecommissioned after the 1963 disaster

The Vajont Dam is a double-curvature arch dam across the Vajont valley in northern Italy, completed in 1960. At 262 metres high it was among the tallest dams in the world. On 9 October 1963, a massive landslide from Monte Toc plunged into the reservoir behind the dam, displacing the water and sending a megatsunami wave over the dam crest that destroyed the town of Longarone and several villages, killing around 2,000 people. The dam itself survived almost intact, but it was never returned to service.

Construction

The dam was built by the Società Adriatica di Elettricità (SADE) as part of a hydroelectric scheme in the Piave river basin, and was nationalised into ENEL shortly before the disaster. The narrow, steep gorge of the Vajont was considered ideal for a high arch dam, but the geology of the valley slopes — fractured limestone over clay layers on Monte Toc — was unstable. Small landslides and measurable creep of the slope occurred as the reservoir was filled, yet filling continued.

The 1963 disaster

On the evening of 9 October 1963, some 260 million cubic metres of rock slid from Monte Toc into the reservoir at high speed. The displaced water rose roughly 250 metres up the opposite slope and overtopped the dam, sending a flood wave down the Piave valley. Longarone, Pirago, Rivalta, Villanova and Faè were destroyed, as were parts of Erto e Casso and Codissago; the death toll is commonly given as about 1,917 to 2,000. Remarkably, the dam structure withstood the impact with only minor damage.

Aftermath

Investigations and a long trial found that the instability of Monte Toc had been recognised and negligently disregarded. The disaster became a landmark case study in geology, engineering ethics and the management of natural hazards. The reservoir was drained, the area depopulated near the dam, and the site is now a memorial, with the landslide scar on Monte Toc still clearly visible.
