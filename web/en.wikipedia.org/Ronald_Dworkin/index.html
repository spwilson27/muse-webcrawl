Ronald Dworkin

Ronald Dworkin
Born: December 11, 1931, Providence, Rhode Island
Died: February 14, 2013, London
Occupation: Legal philosopher
Known for: Law as integrity; rights as trumps; interpretivism

Ronald Myles Dworkin (1931–2013) was an American philosopher of law and constitutional law scholar, widely regarded as one of the most influential legal theorists of the twentieth century. He taught at Yale Law School, University College London, and New York University School of Law.

Early life and education

Dworkin studied at Harvard University, where he earned his undergraduate degree and later his law degree, graduating magna cum laude. He also studied at Magdalen College, Oxford, as a Rhodes Scholar. After law school he clerked for Judge Learned Hand and worked briefly in private practice before joining the Yale faculty in 1962.

Legal philosophy

Dworkin is best known for his critique of legal positivism, particularly H. L. A. Hart's theory. In works such as Taking Rights Seriously (1977) he argued that rights should be treated as "trumps" over collective goals, and that judges in hard cases do not exercise strong discretion but instead reason from principles embedded in the legal practice. His later theory of "law as integrity," developed in Law's Empire (1986), holds that adjudication is a form of constructive interpretation: judges should decide cases in the way that best fits and justifies the legal record as a whole, like authors contributing to a chain novel.

Later work

In Justice for Hedgehogs (2011) Dworkin defended a unified theory of value, arguing that moral, ethical, and legal values form a single interconnected web. He was also a prominent public intellectual, writing frequently for The New York Review of Books on American constitutional controversies.
