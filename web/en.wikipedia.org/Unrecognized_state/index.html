Unrecognized state

An unrecognized state, or a state with limited recognition, is a sovereign state whose sovereignty is not recognised internationally, or is recognised by only a minority of countries. Such entities exercise effective control over territory and population and maintain governing institutions, but lack the full legal standing that comes with recognition by the international community, often because another state claims the same territory.

Theories of statehood

Under the declarative theory of statehood, expressed in the Montevideo Convention of 1933, a state exists if it has a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states — recognition by others is not required. Under the constitutive theory, by contrast, recognition by existing states is itself a necessary condition of statehood. In practice, unrecognized states fall between the two positions: they may function as states internally while being excluded from international organisations and diplomatic relations.

Consequences

Lack of recognition limits an entity's ability to join the United Nations and other international bodies, conclude treaties, participate in international trade and finance on normal terms, issue internationally accepted passports, and receive foreign aid and investment. Many unrecognized states depend heavily on a patron state for economic and military support.

Examples

Contemporary examples commonly described as states with limited recognition include Kosovo, recognised by roughly half of UN member states; the Republic of China (Taiwan), recognised by a small number of states but maintaining extensive unofficial relations; Northern Cyprus, recognised only by Turkey; and Abkhazia and South Ossetia, recognised by Russia and a few others. Somaliland, which has governed itself since 1991, historically received no formal recognition from any UN member state. Entities such as Transnistria are recognised only by other unrecognized states.

Categories

Unrecognized states are sometimes divided into those recognised by at least one UN member state, those recognised only by other non-UN states, and those recognised by no state at all. The status of such entities can change over time, as when South Sudan achieved broad recognition and UN membership in 2011.
