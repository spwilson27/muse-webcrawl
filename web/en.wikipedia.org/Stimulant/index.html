Stimulant

Stimulants (also called psychostimulants or, informally, uppers) are a class of psychoactive drugs that increase activity in the brain and body, producing heightened alertness, attention, energy, elevated mood, increased heart rate and blood pressure, and decreased appetite and need for sleep. Most stimulants act by increasing the activity of the neurotransmitters dopamine and norepinephrine.

Types

The most widely used stimulant in the world is caffeine, found in coffee, tea and many soft drinks. Nicotine, from tobacco, is another globally common stimulant. Stronger stimulants include amphetamine and methamphetamine, cocaine, and methylphenidate. Prescription stimulants such as mixed amphetamine salts (Adderall) and methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta) are first-line treatments for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and are also used for narcolepsy.

Effects

Short-term effects include euphoria, confidence, talkativeness, reduced fatigue and suppression of appetite. Adverse effects may include anxiety, insomnia, tremor, palpitations, and, at high doses, paranoia, psychosis, dangerous overheating and cardiovascular events. Many stimulants have significant potential for dependence and are controlled substances in most countries.

History

Traditional stimulants include coca leaves in the Andes, khat in East Africa and Arabia, ephedra in Chinese medicine, and betel nut in Asia. Cocaine was isolated in the 19th century, amphetamine synthesised in 1887, and methamphetamine in 1893. Stimulants were widely issued to soldiers in the Second World War, marketed as diet pills in the postwar decades, and have figured in recurring public-health crises, including the crack cocaine epidemic of the 1980s and the methamphetamine epidemic.
