Bayonet

A bayonet (from French baïonnette, named after the town of Bayonne) is a knife, dagger, sword, or spike-shaped weapon designed to fit on the end of the muzzle of a rifle, carbine, or similar firearm, allowing it to be used as a spear-like weapon. From the early 17th century to the present, the bayonet has been a standard infantry weapon. The early "plug" bayonet fitted directly into the barrel, preventing firing; this was replaced by the socket bayonet, which left the bore clear, and later by knife bayonets that double as tools. A soldier with a bayonet fixed to a rifled musket combined the roles of musketeer and pikeman, allowing armies to dispense with separate pike units altogether.

History

The plug bayonet appeared in France around the 1640s and was used by the French army at Killiecrankie (1689), where its drawbacks were exposed: once fixed, the musket could not be fired. The socket bayonet, offset on a ring around the barrel, solved this and was general by the early 1700s. During the 19th century long sword bayonets were issued for rifled weapons; in the First World War the bayonet remained standard, though actual bayonet casualties were few. Notable charges continued in the Second World War, Korea (e.g., the US Army at Osan), the Falklands War (1982), and Iraq (2004, British forces at Danny Boy).

Modern use

Modern bayonets are typically short knife bayonets that also serve as fighting knives, wire cutters, and general tools (the US M9, the British L3A1 socket bayonet for the SA80, the AK-pattern bayonets). Military debate continues over their psychological value versus weight; several armies retain bayonet training largely for aggression conditioning and ceremonial use.
