Sweetener

Sweeteners include sugars and high-intensity sugar substitutes.

A sweetener is a substance added to food or drink to impart a sweet taste. Sweeteners range from nutritive carbohydrates such as sucrose (table sugar), fructose, and honey to high-intensity sweeteners that are hundreds of times sweeter than sugar and contribute few or no calories.

Types

Sugars are the most common sweeteners and are metabolized for energy. Sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, xylitol, and erythritol provide sweetness with fewer calories and less effect on blood glucose. High-intensity sweeteners include saccharin (discovered in 1879), aspartame, sucralose, acesulfame potassium, and the plant-derived stevia glycosides and monk fruit extracts.

Use and safety

Sweeteners are used to reduce sugar intake in people with diabetes or obesity and to formulate low-calorie foods and beverages. Approved sweeteners are regulated by food-safety authorities such as the U.S. FDA and the European Food Safety Authority, which set acceptable daily intake levels. Excess sugar consumption is associated with dental caries, obesity, and type 2 diabetes.
