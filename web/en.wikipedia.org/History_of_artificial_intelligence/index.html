History of artificial intelligence

The history of artificial intelligence (AI) began in antiquity with myths of artificial beings, but as an academic field AI was founded at a workshop at Dartmouth College in 1956. Its history is marked by cycles of optimism and disappointment known as "AI winters", followed since the 2010s by rapid advances in machine learning and deep learning.

Foundations

Precursors include formal reasoning in philosophy and logic, Ada Lovelace's speculations about the Analytical Engine, and Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", which proposed the Turing test. The 1956 Dartmouth workshop, organized by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, and others, coined the term "artificial intelligence". Early successes included the Logic Theorist (1956) and checkers-playing programs.

Boom, bust, and expert systems

Generous funding in the 1960s supported work on symbolic AI, natural language, and robotics (Shakey the robot; ELIZA). The 1973 Lighthill report and unmet promises triggered the first AI winter. Expert systems such as DENDRAL, MYCIN, and XCON revived commercial interest in the 1980s, but the collapse of the Lisp machine market led to a second winter around 1987–1993. Milestones of the 1990s included IBM's Deep Blue defeating Garry Kasparov at chess in 1997.

Machine learning and deep learning

From the 1990s, statistical methods and Bayesian networks became dominant. In 2012 the AlexNet neural network's victory in the ImageNet competition launched the deep learning era, powered by large datasets and GPUs. AlphaGo defeated Lee Sedol at Go in 2016. The transformer architecture (2017) enabled large language models such as GPT-3 (2020) and ChatGPT (2022), bringing AI to mass public attention and sparking debate about safety, alignment, and regulation.
