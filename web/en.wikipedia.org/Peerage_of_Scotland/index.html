Peerage of Scotland

CountryScotland (Kingdom of Scotland, later United Kingdom)
RanksDuke, marquess, earl, viscount, lord of Parliament
CreationsBefore 1707 (Union with England)
Governing bodyLord Lyon King of Arms (arms); House of Lords (privileges)

The Peerage of Scotland comprises all peerages created by the King or Queen of Scots before the Acts of Union 1707, which united the Kingdom of Scotland with the Kingdom of England to form the Kingdom of Great Britain. Scottish peers rank below peers of England of the same rank created before 1707 and above peers of Great Britain and the United Kingdom.

Ranks

The ranks of the Scottish peerage, in descending order, are duke, marquess, earl, viscount and lord of Parliament. The lowest rank, "lord of Parliament", is distinctive to Scotland; the equivalent English rank is baron, and Scottish lords of Parliament are often loosely called barons, though in Scotland "baron" historically denoted a lesser feudal dignity. Unusually, a number of Scottish peerages can descend through the female line, and the rules of succession are governed by the terms of each creation.

After the Union

After 1707, Scottish peers did not automatically sit in the House of Lords. Instead, the peerage elected sixteen representative peers to sit for each Parliament. This arrangement lasted until the Peerage Act 1963, which admitted all Scottish peers to the Lords. The House of Lords Act 1999 removed the automatic right of most hereditary peers, Scottish included, to sit.

Characteristics

Scottish peerage law differs from English in several respects, including the possibility of special remainders, the treatment of dormant and abeyant titles, and the role of the Lord Lyon King of Arms, Scotland's heraldic authority, in matters of arms and chiefship of clans. The oldest Scottish peerages include the earldoms of the medieval kingdom, such as Angus, Dunbar and Sutherland; the Earldom of Sutherland, created around 1230, is among the oldest surviving peerages in the British Isles.
