Lee de Forest

Lee de Forest (August 26, 1873 – June 30, 1961) was an American inventor, electrical engineer and early pioneer in electronics of fundamental importance. He invented the first practical electronic amplifier, the three-element "Audion" triode vacuum tube, in 1906. This helped start the Electronic Age and enabled the development of electronics, radio broadcasting, and long-distance telephony. De Forest held more than 300 patents and styled himself, sometimes controversially, the "Father of Radio".

Born: August 26, 1873, Council Bluffs, Iowa
Died: June 30, 1961, Hollywood, California
Known for: Triode (Audion) vacuum tube
Education: Yale University (PhD)

The Audion

Working from John Ambrose Fleming's two-element diode valve of 1904, de Forest in 1906 added a third element — a grid of fine wire between the cathode and the plate — creating the Audion. The device could amplify weak electrical signals, a breakthrough that made practical long-distance telephone repeaters, radio broadcasting, public-address systems, and sound-on-film motion pictures possible. His understanding of the device's operation lagged behind its practical value, and priority disputes, notably with Edwin Howard Armstrong over the regenerative circuit, occupied courts for decades.

Later work

De Forest also pioneered sound-on-film recording with his Phonofilm system in the early 1920s, demonstrated some of the first experimental radio broadcasts, and in 1916 transmitted the first radio news report, of the Wilson–Hughes presidential election. He was a founder of the Institute of Radio Engineers. In 1960 he received an honorary Academy Award for his pioneering inventions in motion pictures.
