Urinary catheterization

In urinary catheterization, a catheter — a flexible tube, usually of latex, polyurethane or silicone — is inserted through the urethra into the urinary bladder to drain urine. Catheterisation may be intermittent (performed as needed and removed immediately) or indwelling, in which case a balloon-inflated Foley catheter remains in place and drains into a collection bag. It is among the most common procedures in hospital care.

Indications

Catheters are used for urinary retention, during and after many surgical procedures, to measure urine output accurately in critically ill patients, to manage incontinence when other methods fail, and to deliver medication or irrigate the bladder. Intermittent self-catheterisation is a long-term strategy for people with neurogenic bladder, for example after spinal cord injury.

Technique

The procedure is performed with sterile technique: the urethral opening is cleaned, lubricant (often with local anaesthetic) is applied, and the catheter is advanced until urine flows. For an indwelling catheter, the retention balloon is then inflated with sterile water. Catheter sizes are measured on the French scale. Suprapubic catheterisation, through the abdominal wall, is an alternative when the urethral route is impossible.

Complications

The most common complication is catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI), a leading source of healthcare-associated infection; risk rises with each day the catheter remains in place, so guidelines emphasise removing catheters as soon as they are no longer needed. Other complications include urethral trauma, false passage, encrustation and blockage, and bladder spasms.

History

Catheters were used in ancient Egypt, Greece and India, fashioned from reeds and metal. The modern self-retaining balloon catheter was developed by the American urologist Frederic Foley in the 1930s.
