Binding of Isaac

The Binding of Isaac (Hebrew: עֲקֵידַת יִצְחָק, Akedat Yitzhak), or simply the Akedah, is a story from the Book of Genesis in which God tests the patriarch Abraham by commanding him to sacrifice his son Isaac as a burnt offering on Mount Moriah. As Abraham prepares to carry out the command, an angel stops him, and a ram caught in a thicket is sacrificed instead.

The narrative

According to Genesis 22, God commands Abraham: "Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering." Abraham journeys for three days, builds an altar, binds Isaac upon it, and raises the knife — when the angel of the Lord calls out for him to stop: "Do not lay your hand on the boy... now I know that you fear God." Abraham then sacrifices a ram caught by its horns in a nearby thicket, and God renews his promises of blessing and numerous descendants.

Interpretations

In Judaism, the Akedah is recited during Rosh Hashanah, and the sounding of the shofar recalls the ram substituted for Isaac. In Christianity, the story has been read as a type prefiguring the sacrifice of Christ. In Islam, a parallel story is commemorated at Eid al-Adha, though many Muslim traditions hold that the son was Ishmael rather than Isaac. Philosophers have debated the story intensely; Søren Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling (1843) treats Abraham's willingness to obey as a paradoxical "leap of faith" beyond ethical norms, while other commentators stress the story's rejection of child sacrifice.

In art

The Binding of Isaac is one of the most frequently depicted episodes of the Hebrew Bible, with famous treatments by Caravaggio, Rembrandt, and Donatello, and it appears on the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery.
