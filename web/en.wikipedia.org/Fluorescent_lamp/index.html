Fluorescent lamp

A fluorescent lamp, or fluorescent tube, is a low-pressure mercury-vapor gas-discharge lamp that uses fluorescence to produce visible light. An electric current excites mercury vapor, which emits short-wave ultraviolet light; a phosphor coating inside the tube then fluoresces, converting the ultraviolet energy into visible light.

Principle of operation

Electrodes at each end of the sealed tube emit electrons that ionize the argon fill gas and vaporize mercury. The excited mercury atoms emit ultraviolet radiation, principally at 254 nm. Phosphors on the inner wall absorb this and re-emit visible light; the blend of phosphors determines the color temperature and color rendering of the lamp.

History

Fluorescence had been observed in the 19th century in Geissler tubes, and commercial development followed work by Peter Cooper Hewitt, Edmund Germer, and others. General Electric introduced practical fluorescent lamps in 1938. Compact fluorescent lamps (CFLs) with integrated ballasts spread in the 1990s and 2000s as energy-saving replacements for incandescent bulbs.

Advantages and disadvantages

Fluorescent lamps convert a much larger share of input power to light than incandescent lamps and last far longer. Drawbacks include the small amount of toxic mercury in each tube (requiring careful disposal), flicker with magnetic ballasts, reduced output at low temperatures, and light quality issues. Since the 2010s, LED lamps have increasingly displaced them on efficiency and longevity grounds.
