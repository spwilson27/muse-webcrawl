Germanic umlaut

Umlaut fronted vowels under the influence of a following /i/ or /j/.

Germanic umlaut, sometimes called i-mutation or i-umlaut, is a type of sound change in the Germanic languages in which a vowel was raised or fronted in anticipation of a following /i/ or /j/ in the next syllable. Though the conditioning sounds often later disappeared, the altered vowels remained and came to serve grammatical functions, such as marking plurals.

Examples

Familiar English remnants include the irregular plurals foot/feet, goose/geese, man/men and mouse/mice, where a back vowel was fronted under the influence of an old plural ending containing *i. In German the process is far more visible and remains productive: Gast/Gäste ("guest/guests"), Buch/Bücher ("book/books").

Development

The change operated at different times in the daughter languages — in Old English and Old Norse in the early medieval period, and in Old High German somewhat later, initially affecting only /a/. Because the triggering suffixes were frequently reduced or lost, umlaut created pairs of vowels whose alternation is now morphologically conditioned rather than phonetic.

Related changes

A parallel process, a-mutation (u-umlaut in Old Norse, caused by following back vowels), lowered or rounded vowels in other environments. The term umlaut was coined by Jacob Grimm, and the phenomenon should be distinguished from the German diacritic of the same name, though the two are historically connected.
