Definition"Live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host" (FAO/WHO 2001)

Probiotics are live microorganisms promoted as providing health benefits when consumed, generally by improving or restoring the gut microbiota. The joint FAO/WHO definition (2001), adopted by the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP), describes probiotics as "live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host". Common probiotics include certain strains of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium bacteria, and the yeast Saccharomyces boulardii.

History

The concept originates with Élie Metchnikoff, who in The Prolongation of Life (1907) proposed that lactic acid bacteria in Bulgarian yogurt explained the longevity of Bulgarian peasants, by suppressing putrefactive gut bacteria. The term "probiotic" (from Greek pro bios, "for life") was introduced by Werner Kollath in 1953 as the opposite of "antibiotic", and was given its modern microbiological sense by Roy Fuller in 1989.

Health claims and evidence

Evidence is strongest for a few specific indications and strains: certain probiotics reduce the risk of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and of Clostridioides difficile infection, shorten acute infectious diarrhoea in children, and help prevent necrotising enterocolitis in preterm infants. Probiotics are widely marketed for irritable bowel syndrome, digestive comfort, immune support, and vaginal and skin health, but for most such claims the scientific evidence is weak, strain-specific, or inconsistent between trials. Regulators reflect this: the European Food Safety Authority has rejected nearly all submitted probiotic health claims, and in the EU the word "probiotic" itself is treated as an unauthorised health claim on labels.

Mechanisms

Proposed mechanisms include competitive exclusion of pathogens, production of antimicrobials such as bacteriocins and lactic acid, strengthening of the intestinal barrier, and modulation of the host immune system. Most ingested probiotic strains do not permanently colonise the gut; their effects are thought to occur during transit or through interaction with resident microbes and gut-associated lymphoid tissue.

Safety

Probiotics are generally regarded as safe for healthy people. However, case reports link them to bacteraemia, fungaemia (with S. boulardii), and D-lactic acidosis in seriously ill, immunocompromised, or critically ill patients and in infants, so clinical guidelines advise caution in those groups. Product quality varies widely: independent analyses have found supplements whose contents do not match their labels.

Related concepts

Prebiotics are substrates (typically fibres such as inulin) selectively used by host microorganisms to confer a benefit; synbiotics combine pre- and probiotics; postbiotics are preparations of inanimate microorganisms or their components that confer a benefit.
