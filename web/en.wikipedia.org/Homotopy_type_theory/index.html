Homotopy type theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homotopy type theory (HoTT) is a branch of mathematics that combines aspects of homotopy theory and type theory, and is also used as a foundation for mathematics that differs from the usual set-theoretic foundations such as ZFC. In homotopy type theory, types are interpreted not as sets but as spaces (homotopy types): terms of a type are points, and identity proofs between terms are paths, whose iterated structure yields the full apparatus of higher groupoids.

The central ideas emerged in the mid-2000s from work by Vladimir Voevodsky, and independently Steve Awodey and Michael Warren, who recognised that the identity types of Martin-Löf type theory carry a groupoid structure and admit models in homotopy theory. Voevodsky's univalence axiom — stating that equivalent types are identical, (A ≃ B) ≃ (A = B) — is the field's most distinctive principle, turning isomorphism-invariance of mathematical practice into a theorem of the foundations. The subject was consolidated by a year-long programme at the Institute for Advanced Study in 2012–13, which produced the community textbook Homotopy Type Theory: Univalent Foundations of Mathematics (2013), written collaboratively by more than thirty authors.

Key concepts

Because proofs of equality are paths, mathematics in HoTT is inherently higher-categorical: propositions are types with at most one term up to identity, sets are types whose identity types are propositions, and general types form a hierarchy by homotopy level. Higher inductive types define objects such as circles and spheres by their points and paths, allowing synthetic proofs in homotopy theory inside type theory — for example, a short formalisable proof that π1(S¹) ≅ ℤ. HoTT is implemented in proof assistants including Coq and Agda, and its cubical variants restore computational content to the univalence axiom, so that proofs using it can compute.
