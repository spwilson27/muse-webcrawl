The Siege of Dubrovnik (Croatian: Opsada Dubrovnika) was a military engagement fought between the Yugoslav People's Army (JNA) — supported by Montenegrin and Bosnian Serb forces — and Croatian defenders during the Croatian War of Independence. The siege began on 1 October 1991 and lasted into 1992, with the heaviest bombardments in late 1991. Dubrovnik, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, was besieged from land and sea, drawing international condemnation, particularly for the shelling of the historic Old Town on 6 December 1991.

Background

Croatia declared independence from Yugoslavia in June 1991. The JNA advanced through the Dubrovnik littoral from Bosnia and Herzegovina and Montenegro in the autumn of 1991, occupying the surrounding countryside, the islands and approaches to the city, and cutting Dubrovnik's water and electricity supplies. Yugoslav and Montenegrin leaders claimed — without substantiation accepted by historians — that Croatian forces were preparing to invade Montenegro.

The siege

The encircled city, defended by a small force of Croatian soldiers, police, and volunteers, depended on supplies brought by boat through the naval blockade. Artillery positioned on the heights around the city, including Mount Srđ and Žarkovica, shelled Dubrovnik repeatedly; the worst attack, on 6 December 1991, targeted the Old Town, damaging or destroying many historic buildings, including damage to the Stradun, churches, palaces, and the library of the Inter-University Centre, whose books burned. Hundreds of civilians and defenders were killed during the siege, and much of the region around the city — including the resort of Kupari and the Konavle area — was looted and burned.

International journalists inside Dubrovnik, among them reporters broadcasting live during the shelling, brought worldwide attention to the siege. In May 1992 the JNA formally withdrew from Croatia, though Yugoslav and Serb forces retained positions near the city until the Croatian Army's Operation Tiger in July 1992 liberated the Dubrovnik area.

Aftermath and prosecutions

The International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia prosecuted commanders responsible for the attack on the Old Town. Admiral Miodrag Jokić pleaded guilty to crimes including unlawful attack on civilians and destruction of cultural property, and General Pavle Strugar was convicted of responsibility for the 6 December shelling. Dubrovnik's damaged buildings were subsequently restored, and the siege remains a central memory of the war in Croatia; the Museum of the Croatian War of Independence on Mount Srđ commemorates it.
