Classical Hollywood cinema is a term used in film theory to describe a narrative and visual style of filmmaking that became characteristic of American cinema between about 1917 and 1960. Defined most influentially by David Bordwell, Janet Staiger and Kristin Thompson in The Classical Hollywood Cinema (1985), the style is marked by an emphasis on clear storytelling, psychologically defined characters pursuing goals, and editing techniques that render the narration seemingly invisible.

Style

The classical style subordinates technique to narrative clarity. Continuity editing — including the 180-degree rule, eyeline matches, and match-on-action cutting — maintains a coherent sense of space and time. Stories typically follow a cause-and-effect chain driven by a protagonist's desire, proceed through a rising structure of complications, and conclude with a decisive resolution, often pairing narrative closure with a romantic couple. Lighting, set design and music all serve the story rather than calling attention to themselves.

The studio system

Classical Hollywood was also an industrial mode of production. A handful of major studios — MGM, Paramount, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox and RKO — controlled production, distribution and exhibition, with actors and craftspeople under long-term contract. Production was organized on factory lines of specialized departments, enabling an output of hundreds of films a year in recognizable genres such as the western, the musical and the melodrama.

Decline

The style's institutional base eroded after the 1948 Paramount Decrees forced the studios to divest their theatre chains, and as television drew audiences away in the 1950s. By the 1960s the studio system had collapsed, giving rise to the more director-driven New Hollywood, though classical narrative conventions remain the default grammar of mainstream filmmaking worldwide.
