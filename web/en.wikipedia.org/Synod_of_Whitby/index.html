Synod of Whitby

Synod of Whitby
Date: 664 AD
Location: Whitby Abbey, Northumbria
Convened by: King Oswiu
Issue: Calculation of Easter; tonsure

The Synod of Whitby was a church council held in 664 at Whitby Abbey in the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Northumbria, at which King Oswiu decided that Northumbria would follow the Roman method of calculating the date of Easter rather than the Ionan method used by Irish missionaries.

Background

Northumbria had been Christianized largely by Irish monks from Iona, led by Aidan of Lindisfarne, whose Easter computus sometimes placed Easter a week or more apart from Roman practice — an embarrassment when the king and queen, who followed different reckonings, celebrated on different days. Differences in the shape of the clerical tonsure were also at issue.

Decision and consequences

At the synod, Wilfrid argued the Roman case and Colmán the Ionan one; Oswiu ruled for Rome, declaring he would follow the practice of St Peter, keeper of the keys of heaven. Colmán and his followers withdrew to Ireland, while the decision aligned the Northumbrian church with Rome and Canterbury. The synod, described by Bede in his Ecclesiastical History, is seen as a milestone in the unification of the English church.
