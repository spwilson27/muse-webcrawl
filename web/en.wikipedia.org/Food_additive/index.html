Food additive

TypeSubstances added to food
PurposesPreservation, flavour, appearance, texture
ExamplesPreservatives, colourings, emulsifiers, sweeteners
RegulationEFSA (EU), FDA (US), JECFA (international)

Food additives are substances added to food to preserve flavour or enhance taste, appearance, or other qualities. Some additives have been used for centuries as part of an effort to preserve food, for example vinegar (pickling), salt (salting), smoke (smoking), sugar (crystallisation), etc. With the advent of processed foods in the second half of the twentieth century, many additives have been introduced, of both natural and artificial origin.

Categories

Food additives can be divided into several groups, although there is some overlap between them:

  • Acids and acidity regulators — maintain a desired pH; examples include citric acid and acetic acid.
  • Antioxidants — prevent oxidation and rancidity, e.g. ascorbic acid (vitamin C).
  • Colourings — restore or add colour lost in processing; see food coloring.
  • Emulsifiers — allow water and oils to remain mixed, e.g. lecithin.
  • Flavour enhancers — such as monosodium glutamate (MSG).
  • Preservatives — inhibit the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds, e.g. sodium benzoate and sulphur dioxide.
  • Sweeteners — natural sugars and artificial sweeteners such as aspartame and sucralose.
  • Thickeners and stabilisers — such as guar gum and xanthan gum.

Numbering systems

In the European Union, approved additives are assigned E numbers, a system echoed in Australia, New Zealand and other jurisdictions. In the United States, additives are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration, and substances generally recognised as safe are known by the acronym GRAS.

Safety and regulation

To regulate these additives and inform consumers, each additive is assigned a unique number. Safety evaluations are performed by bodies such as the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA), which establish an acceptable daily intake (ADI) for each substance. Public debate about additives has at times driven demand for "clean label" products with fewer synthetic ingredients, even where approved additives have passed safety review.
