Hydrofluorocarbon

HFCs are widely used in refrigeration and air conditioning

Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) are synthetic organofluorine compounds composed of hydrogen, fluorine, and carbon. They were introduced commercially in the 1990s as replacements for chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs), which deplete the stratospheric ozone layer. Because HFCs contain no chlorine or bromine, their ozone depletion potential is zero; however, many HFCs are potent greenhouse gases, with global warming potentials hundreds to thousands of times that of carbon dioxide.

Uses

  • Refrigerants in domestic, commercial, and automotive air conditioning (e.g., HFC-134a, and blends such as R-410A and R-404A)
  • Blowing agents for insulating foams
  • Propellants in aerosols, including metered-dose inhalers
  • Solvents and fire-suppression agents

Environmental impact and regulation

HFC emissions grew rapidly in the early 21st century as demand for cooling increased. In 2016 the parties to the Montreal Protocol adopted the Kigali Amendment, which mandates a global phase-down of HFC production and consumption of more than 80 percent by mid-century, expected to avoid up to about 0.5 °C of additional warming by 2100. Regional measures, such as the European Union's F-gas regulation and the United States AIM Act of 2020, implement similar reductions. Industry is transitioning to low-GWP alternatives, including hydrofluoroolefins (HFOs), carbon dioxide, ammonia, and hydrocarbons such as propane.

The most common HFC, HFC-134a (1,1,1,2-tetrafluoroethane), has an atmospheric lifetime of about 14 years and a 100-year global warming potential of roughly 1,430. Some HFCs, such as HFC-23, a byproduct of HCFC-22 manufacture, have far higher potentials (around 14,800) and lifetimes of centuries.
