Microglia

Microglia are a type of glial cell found throughout the brain and spinal cord. They are the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, acting as its first and main form of active immune defense — the brain's macrophages. Microglia account for roughly 10–15 percent of all cells in the brain.

Origin and development

Unlike other glial cells, which derive from the neuroectoderm, microglia originate from primitive macrophages in the embryonic yolk sac that migrate into the developing brain and persist for life, renewing themselves locally. They were characterized by Pío del Río Hortega in the early twentieth century.

Functions

In the healthy brain, microglia constantly extend and retract their fine processes to survey the tissue. They clear dead cells, debris and pathogens by phagocytosis, prune excess synapses during development (in part via the complement system), support neurons with growth factors, and shape learning-related plasticity. Upon injury or infection they activate, change shape, proliferate, release cytokines, and present antigens.

Role in disease

Microglial activation is a hallmark of neuroinflammation and is implicated in Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke and chronic pain. Genetic risk factors for Alzheimer's such as TREM2 act largely through microglia, and "disease-associated microglia" states identified by single-cell sequencing are an intense focus of research and drug development.
