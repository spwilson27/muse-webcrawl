The Song dynasty (960–1279) was an imperial dynasty of China that began in 960 and lasted until 1279. Founded by Emperor Taizu after the fragmented Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period, it reunified most of China proper. The dynasty is conventionally divided into the Northern Song (960–1127), ruling from Kaifeng, and the Southern Song (1127–1279), which governed the south from Hangzhou after the Jurchen Jin dynasty seized the north.

Government and society

Song government was a sophisticated bureaucracy staffed increasingly through civil-service examinations, elevating scholar-officials over hereditary aristocrats and keeping the military subordinate to civil authority. Society saw explosive urban growth — Kaifeng and Hangzhou each exceeded a million inhabitants — vibrant markets, restaurants, and entertainment quarters, and a commercial revolution featuring paper money (jiaozi), maritime trade, and early industrial production of iron and porcelain.

Culture and technology

The Song is famed for landscape painting, ceramics, and poetry, and for the philosophy of Neo-Confucianism systematized by Zhu Xi, which later became state orthodoxy. Technologically, the era saw movable-type printing (Bi Sheng, c. 1040), the mariner's compass in navigation, gunpowder weapons, Su Song's astronomical clock tower, and Shen Kuo's wide-ranging scientific writings. Historians often describe the Song as a period of remarkable economic and cultural flourishing despite chronic military pressure from northern neighbours — Liao, Western Xia, Jin, and finally the Mongols.

Fall

Allied briefly with the Mongols against Jin, the Southern Song then faced Mongol invasion alone. After decades of resistance, Hangzhou fell in 1276 and the last Song loyalists were defeated at the naval battle of Yamen in 1279, completing Kublai Khan's conquest of China.
