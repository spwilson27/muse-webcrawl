Bread and circuses

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Games in a Roman circus

"Bread and circuses" (from Latin: panem et circenses) is a metonymic phrase referring to a superficial means of appeasement. The Roman poet Juvenal coined the expression in his Satires to lament that the Roman populace, once politically engaged, had come to care only for free grain and entertainments provided by the state, surrendering its civic duties and political power in exchange.

"...the people who once bestowed commands, consulships, legions and all else, now meddle no more, but long eagerly for just two things — bread and circuses." — Juvenal, Satires, X.77–81

Context in ancient Rome

From the late Republic onward, Rome distributed subsidised or free grain to its citizens (the cura annonae), a practice expanded under the emperors. Public games — chariot races at the Circus Maximus, gladiatorial combats, theatrical performances and public feasts — occupied a large number of days in the Roman calendar. Juvenal's satire criticised a populace that traded the responsibilities of citizenship for these provisions.

Modern usage

The phrase is now used widely in political commentary to describe policies or media spectacle that distract the public from more important issues, or a government that secures public approval through immediate gratification rather than substantive governance. It appears frequently in discussions of mass entertainment, consumerism and populist politics.
