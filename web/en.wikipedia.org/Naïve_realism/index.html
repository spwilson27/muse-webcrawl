Naïve realism

In philosophy of perception and philosophy of mind, naïve realism (also known as direct realism or perceptual realism) is the idea that the senses provide us with direct awareness of objects as they really are. When a perceiver looks at a tree, on this view, the mind does not construct an inner representation of the tree; rather the tree itself, with its mind-independent qualities, is a constituent of the perceptual experience.

Motivation

Naïve realism is intended to capture the manifest, pre-theoretical view of perception and to avoid the "veil of perception" problems of representative realism, in which perceivers are directly aware only of sense data and must infer the external world. Contemporary defenders often develop the view as disjunctivism: veridical perceptions and hallucinations, though subjectively alike in some cases, differ fundamentally in kind.

Objections

The standard challenges come from illusions, hallucinations, perceptual variation (such as colour shifts under different lighting), and the time-lag argument: light from distant stars left them long ago, so what we see is not the object "as it now is". Naïve realists respond by distinguishing the objects and properties presented in experience from the conditions under which they are presented.
