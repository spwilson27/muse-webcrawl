Equation of exchange

The equation of exchange is an identity in monetary economics stating that the money supply (M) multiplied by the velocity of money (V) equals the price level (P) multiplied by real output or transactions (Q or T):

M × V = P × Q

It was made famous by Irving Fisher in The Purchasing Power of Money (1911). As a pure identity it holds by definition, since velocity is defined as the ratio of nominal spending to the money stock; economic content enters when assumptions are made about which variables are stable or causal.

Quantity theory of money

If V is treated as institutionally stable and Q as determined by real factors at full employment, the equation yields the quantity theory of money: changes in the money supply produce proportional changes in the price level. This reasoning was revived by Milton Friedman and the monetarist school, who argued that a steady rule for money growth would stabilize prices — a position that heavily influenced central banking in the late twentieth century, though velocity proved less stable in practice than monetarists assumed.

Transactions and income versions

Fisher's original version used T, the volume of all transactions; modern macroeconomics usually uses the income version, with Q (or Y) denoting real GDP, so that P × Q equals nominal GDP.
