Theseus

Theseus (Greek: Θησεύς) is a hero and legendary king of Athens in Greek mythology. Son of Aegeus (or, in some versions, of the god Poseidon) and Aethra, he is best known for slaying the Minotaur in the Labyrinth of Crete. To the Athenians he was a culture hero and founding figure: credited with the unification (synoecism) of Attica, with reforms that made him an emblem of Athenian democracy, and with a string of adventures along the road from Troezen to Athens that paralleled the labours of Heracles.

Early life and the road to Athens

Aegeus left a sword and sandals under a heavy rock for his unborn son; when Theseus grew strong enough to lift the rock, he set out for Athens. Choosing the dangerous land route rather than the safer sea voyage, he cleared the Isthmus road of a succession of villains, killing each by his own method: Periphetes the club-bearer, Sinis the pine-bender, the Crommyonian Sow, Sciron of the cliff, Cercyon the wrestler, and Procrustes, who fitted travellers to his iron bed by stretching or cutting them.

The Minotaur

In Athens, Theseus volunteered to join the tribute of youths and maidens sent to Crete to be devoured by the Minotaur in the Labyrinth built by Daedalus. With the help of Ariadne, daughter of King Minos, who gave him a ball of thread to retrace his path, Theseus killed the Minotaur and led the Athenians out. He abandoned Ariadne on the island of Naxos — accounts differ as to why — and on the return voyage forgot to change the black sails for white, the agreed signal of success; seeing black sails, Aegeus threw himself into the sea that afterwards bore his name, the Aegean.

King of Athens

As king, Theseus was said to have unified the villages of Attica into one city, established festivals and minted coinage, and in later Athenian tradition to have laid the foundations of popular government. Other adventures include the war against the Amazons (from which he took Hippolyta as wife), the abduction of Helen with his friend Pirithous, and their disastrous descent to the Underworld to seize Persephone, from which Heracles rescued Theseus. His marriage to Phaedra ended in tragedy when she falsely accused her stepson Hippolytus, leading to the young man's death. Exiled in old age, Theseus died on the island of Skyros, pushed from a cliff by King Lycomedes. The Athenian general Cimon later claimed to have recovered his bones and brought them to Athens.

Legacy

Theseus was the subject of tragedies by Euripides and Sophocles, of Plutarch's Life of Theseus (paired with Romulus), and of much later literature including Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream. The philosophical puzzle known as the "Ship of Theseus" takes its name from the preserved ship attributed to him, discussed by Plutarch.
