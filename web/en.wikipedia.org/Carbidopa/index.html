Carbidopa is a medication used in the treatment of Parkinson's disease, almost always in combination with levodopa. It inhibits the enzyme aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (DOPA decarboxylase) outside the brain, preventing the premature breakdown of levodopa in the bloodstream and thereby allowing more levodopa to reach the brain, where it is converted to dopamine.

Chemical structure of carbidopa.

Mechanism

Levodopa taken alone is extensively decarboxylated in peripheral tissues, so only a small fraction reaches the brain and peripheral dopamine causes side effects such as nausea, vomiting, and cardiac arrhythmias. Carbidopa does not cross the blood–brain barrier, so it blocks decarboxylation in the periphery only. The combination allows the levodopa dose to be reduced by roughly 75–80 percent while decreasing peripheral adverse effects.

Formulations

The fixed combination carbidopa/levodopa (originally marketed as Sinemet) is available in immediate-release and extended-release tablets, an orally disintegrating form, an enteral suspension gel delivered by pump into the jejunum (Duopa/Duodopa) for advanced disease, and an inhaled levodopa powder uses carbidopa pretreatment. Typical ratios are 1:4 or 1:10 carbidopa to levodopa; at least about 70–100 mg of carbidopa per day is generally needed for full peripheral enzyme saturation.

Other uses

Carbidopa is also used to block serotonin synthesis from 5-HTP in certain diagnostic tests and, historically, in the dexamethasone–CRH and other endocrine testing protocols. It has no antiparkinsonian effect on its own.
