Lee Earle "James" Ellroy (born March 4, 1948) is an American crime fiction writer and essayist, known for a distinctive telegraphic prose style in his later works — most famously the L.A. Quartet novels The Black Dahlia (1987), The Big Nowhere (1988), L.A. Confidential (1990) and White Jazz (1992) — and the Underworld USA trilogy, beginning with American Tabloid (1995).

Early life

Ellroy was born in Los Angeles. When he was ten, his mother Jean Ellroy was murdered in El Monte in 1958; the crime was never solved, and the experience shaped his lifelong obsession with crime and with the unsolved 1947 "Black Dahlia" murder of Elizabeth Short. After his father's death in 1965, Ellroy drifted into petty crime, alcoholism and drug addiction, and was repeatedly jailed. Following a near-fatal illness in 1977 he gave up alcohol and drugs and worked as a golf caddie while writing his first novels.

Career

Ellroy's breakthrough came with The Black Dahlia (1987), a fictional treatment of the Short case. The L.A. Quartet charted corruption in Los Angeles from the late 1940s to the late 1950s; L.A. Confidential was filmed in 1997 by Curtis Hanson and won two Academy Awards. The Underworld USA trilogy (American Tabloid, The Cold Six Thousand, Blood's a Rover) reimagines American political history from 1958 to 1973. In 1996 he published My Dark Places, a memoir of his investigation into his mother's murder.

Style

Ellroy's mature writing uses a terse, staccato, telegram-like prose, omitting conjunctions and compressing sentences, along with period slang, alliteration and a bleak, obsessive tone. He frequently calls himself "the Demon Dog of American crime fiction". Later works include the Second L.A. Quartet — Perfidia (2014) and This Storm (2019) — set during the Second World War.
