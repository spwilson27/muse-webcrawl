Chemiosmosis

Chemiosmosis is the movement of ions across a semipermeable membrane bound structure, down their electrochemical gradient. An important example is the formation of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) by the movement of hydrogen ions (H+) across a membrane during cellular respiration or photosynthesis.

Mechanism

In cellular respiration, the electron transport chain uses energy from electrons (derived from food molecules) to pump protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, creating an electrochemical gradient — the proton-motive force. Protons then flow back through the enzyme ATP synthase, whose rotation drives the synthesis of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate. The same principle operates in chloroplasts during photosynthesis and in bacteria across their cell membranes.

History

The chemiosmotic theory was proposed by British biochemist Peter Mitchell in 1961. Initially controversial, it was vindicated by experiments such as Jagendorf's acid-bath demonstration of ATP synthesis in chloroplasts and the structural elucidation of ATP synthase as a rotary motor by Paul Boyer and John Walker. Mitchell received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978.

Significance

Chemiosmosis is a near-universal mechanism of energy transduction in living cells, linking respiration and photosynthesis to ATP production across bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes.
