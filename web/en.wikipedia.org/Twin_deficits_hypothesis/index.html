Twin deficits hypothesis

The twin deficits hypothesis in economics holds that a country's government budget deficit and its current account deficit are linked: an increase in the fiscal deficit tends to be accompanied by an increase in the current account deficit. The idea gained prominence in the United States during the 1980s, when the large federal budget deficits of the Reagan era coincided with large and growing trade and current account deficits.

Mechanism

By the national income accounting identity, the current account balance equals the difference between national saving and investment. Government dissaving (a budget deficit) lowers national saving; unless private saving rises to compensate or investment falls, the gap must be financed by borrowing from abroad, which corresponds to a current account deficit. Higher fiscal deficits may also push up interest rates and the exchange rate, making exports less competitive and worsening the trade balance.

Evidence and debate

The empirical relationship is debated. In some periods and countries the two deficits have moved together, while in others — for example the United States in the late 1990s, when the budget moved into surplus while the current account deficit widened — they have diverged, suggesting that private saving and investment behaviour, capital flows and exchange-rate regimes also matter. Critics invoking Ricardian equivalence argue that households may fully offset government deficits with higher private saving, breaking the link.
