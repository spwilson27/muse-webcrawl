Vampire

A vampire is a creature from folklore that subsists by feeding on the vital essence (generally in the form of blood) of the living. In European folklore, vampires are undead humanoid creatures that often visited loved ones and caused mischief or deaths in the neighbourhoods they inhabited while they were alive. They wore shrouds and were often described as bloated and of ruddy or dark countenance, markedly different from today's gaunt, pale vampire which dates from the early 19th century.

Origins and folklore

Vampire legends may have originated from the belief in revenants — corpses that return to harm the living — found in many cultures. Tales of the dead drinking blood and spreading plague were particularly strong in Eastern Europe, where the word "vampire" (from Serbian vampir) entered Western languages in the 18th century amid mass hysteria and public vampire hunts in Serbia and East Prussia. Common folk defences included garlic, crucifixes, sunlight, staking through the heart, and decapitation.

In literature and film

The vampire entered English literature with John Polidori's The Vampyre (1819), influenced by Lord Byron. Sheridan Le Fanu's Carmilla (1872) added the female vampire, and Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897) defined the modern archetype. In the 20th and 21st centuries, vampires became staples of horror and romance fiction, from Nosferatu (1922) to Anne Rice's Interview with the Vampire (1976) and Stephenie Meyer's Twilight series.

Explanations

Scholars have proposed natural explanations for vampire beliefs, including the appearance of decomposing corpses, premature burial, and diseases such as rabies and porphyria, though the porphyria link is now largely discounted. Vampire bats, named after the folklore creature, feed on blood but were unknown in Europe when the legends formed.
