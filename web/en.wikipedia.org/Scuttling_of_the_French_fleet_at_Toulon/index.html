Scuttling of the French fleet at Toulon

The scuttling of the French fleet at Toulon took place on 27 November 1942, when the Vichy French navy deliberately sank its own warships in the harbour of Toulon to prevent them from falling into German hands during Case Anton, the German occupation of Vichy France.

Background

Under the armistice of June 1940, France retained a substantial fleet based at Toulon, demilitarised under Axis supervision. French naval commanders had standing orders to scuttle rather than surrender their ships, and Admiral Darlan had assured the British that the fleet would never fall intact to Germany. After the Allied landings in North Africa (Operation Torch) in November 1942, Germany occupied the Vichy zone and moved to seize the fleet under Operation Lila.

The scuttling

As German troops entered the arsenal in the early hours of 27 November, Admiral Jean de Laborde, commanding the High Seas Fleet, gave the order to scuttle. Crews opened seacocks, set demolition charges and fired torpedoes into magazines. Seventy-seven vessels were sunk, including three battleships (Strasbourg, Dunkerque and Provence), eight cruisers, numerous destroyers and submarines; a handful of submarines escaped to Algiers. German attempts to board were largely frustrated, and Hitler condemned the operation as a failure.

Aftermath

The destruction removed the French fleet as a factor in the war. The Italians later raised and scrapped some of the smaller vessels. The scuttling is regarded in France as an act preserving the navy's honour, though it also deprived the Allies of ships that might have joined them.
