El Tajín

The Pyramid of the Niches, El Tajín's most famous structure

El Tajín is a pre-Hispanic archaeological site in the present-day Mexican state of Veracruz, near the modern city of Papantla. It was a major city of the Classic era of Mesoamerica, flourishing between about 600 and 1200 CE after the fall of Teotihuacan, and was one of the most important cities of the Gulf Coast.

Architecture

The site is celebrated for its unique architecture, extensive use of carved reliefs, and an unusually large number of ball courts — seventeen have been identified — indicating the ritual importance of the Mesoamerican ballgame there. Its most famous building is the Pyramid of the Niches, a six-tiered pyramid whose facade contains 365 niches, one for each day of the solar year. Other notable structures include the Tajín Chico group and the Great Ball Court, whose carved panels depict ballgame rituals and sacrifice.

History

El Tajín's name derives from the Totonac word for thunder or lightning, and the site is associated with the Totonac people, though its Classic-period builders are sometimes called the Classic Veracruz culture. The city was abandoned and partly burned around 1200 CE, and the ruins were hidden in jungle until officially reported in 1785. El Tajín was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992.
