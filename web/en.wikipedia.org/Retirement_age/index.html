Retirement age

This article lists the statutory retirement age in different countries. In some contexts, the retirement age is the age at which a person is expected or required to cease work and is usually the age at which they may be entitled to receive superannuation or other government benefits, such as a state pension.

Policy makers usually consider the demography and fiscal cost of aging populations when setting the retirement age, and many countries have raised, or are in the process of raising, their retirement ages to reflect increasing life expectancy and to offset the growing burden on pension systems that support the elderly, given that people are living longer and there is generally a decreasing ratio of workers to retirees.

History

The concept of a fixed retirement age is largely a product of the late 19th and 20th centuries. Germany under Otto von Bismarck introduced the first old-age pension system in 1889, with eligibility initially set at 70 and later reduced to 65 — an age that became a conventional benchmark in many countries, even though life expectancy at the time meant relatively few workers survived long past it. The United States Social Security Act of 1935 set 65 as the age for full benefits. Through much of the 20th century, mandatory retirement at a fixed age was common in employment contracts and public service rules, though many jurisdictions have since restricted or abolished mandatory retirement as age discrimination.

Variations by country

Statutory pension ages vary widely. Many European countries set the state pension age between 65 and 67; France's 2023 reform raising the age from 62 to 64 provoked prolonged protests. The United States full retirement age for Social Security is 66–67 depending on birth year, with reduced benefits available from 62. Several countries have linked, or plan to link, the pension age automatically to life expectancy, including Denmark and the Netherlands. Some countries historically maintained different ages for men and women, but most have been equalizing them. Occupations regarded as arduous or dangerous — miners, police officers, military personnel, firefighters — often have lower retirement ages.

Reform debates

Raising the retirement age is one of the most politically contentious of economic reforms. Supporters argue it is necessary for the fiscal sustainability of pension systems and reflects longer, healthier lives. Critics respond that life-expectancy gains are unevenly distributed by income and occupation, that workers in physically demanding jobs cannot simply work longer, and that older workers may face unemployment rather than extended careers. Alternatives debated include raising payroll taxes, reducing benefits, increasing immigration, and raising labor-force participation.
