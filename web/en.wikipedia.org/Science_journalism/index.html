Science journalism

Science journalism conveys reporting about science to the public. The field typically involves interactions between scientists, journalists, and the public. Science journalists report on new research findings, scientific controversies, and the societal implications of science for newspapers, magazines, broadcast media, and online outlets, translating specialized research into material understandable to lay audiences.

Scope and practice

Science journalists cover topics from astronomy and physics to medicine, climate change, and technology. Much science reporting is based on peer-reviewed studies and press releases from journals and universities; journalists must evaluate claims, seek independent expert comment, and convey uncertainty appropriately. The practice differs from science communication by scientists themselves in that journalists are expected to apply independent scrutiny rather than promotion.

Challenges

The field faces recurring problems: "churnalism" based on uncritical rewriting of press releases, hype of preliminary or unreplicated findings, false balance on settled topics such as vaccination and climate change, and the replication crisis, which has exposed weaknesses in widely reported studies. The decline of dedicated science desks in news media and the speed of online publishing have added further pressure.

History

Science reporting grew with the popular science press of the 19th century and professionalized in the 20th century as newspapers hired specialist correspondents. Specialist organizations include the National Association of Science Writers in the United States and the Association of British Science Writers. Major outlets with strong science sections include The New York Times, The Guardian, the BBC, Nature, and New Scientist.
