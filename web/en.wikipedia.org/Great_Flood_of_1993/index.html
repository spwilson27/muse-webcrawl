The Great Flood of 1993 (or Great Mississippi and Missouri Rivers Flood of 1993) was a flood that occurred in the Midwestern United States, along the Mississippi and Missouri rivers and their tributaries, from April to October 1993. It was among the most costly and devastating in United States history, measured by the number of levees broken, the area inundated, and the duration of flooding.

Causes and development

Persistent heavy rainfall over the upper Midwest during the summer of 1993, driven by a stationary weather pattern that repeatedly funneled moist air from the Gulf of Mexico over saturated soils, caused rivers to rise far above flood stage. Record crests were set at many gauges, including at St. Louis, Missouri, where the Mississippi crested at 49.58 feet on 1 August 1993.

Impact

Approximately 12,000 to 15,000 square miles of land were flooded. At least 50 people died, and damages were estimated at $12 to 16 billion, making it one of the most expensive natural disasters in U.S. history at the time. More than 1,000 levees were overtopped or failed, bridges were closed for months, and barge traffic on the Mississippi and Missouri rivers halted for nearly two months. Towns such as Valmeyer, Illinois, were relocated to higher ground after the flood.

Aftermath

The flood led to major reviews of federal flood policy, including the 1994 "Galloway Report," which recommended more sustainable floodplain management, expanded wetlands, and voluntary buyouts of repeatedly flooded properties.
