The Highland Potato Famine (Scottish Gaelic: Gaiseadh a' Bhuntàta) was a period of severe hardship in the Scottish Highlands and Islands from 1846 into the 1850s, caused by the same potato blight, Phytophthora infestans, that devastated Ireland in the Great Famine. The crofting population, heavily dependent on potatoes grown on tiny plots, lost its main food source in successive seasons.

Background

By the 1840s the kelp and herring booms that had sustained Highland townships had collapsed, and many estates were subdivided into small crofts whose rent was paid by migrant labour. Potatoes, productive on poor soils, supplied the bulk of calories; it has been estimated that in some districts they provided up to 80 percent of the diet. When blight struck in 1846, roughly three-quarters of the Highland potato crop failed, and the disease recurred for a decade.

Relief and response

Unlike Ireland, mass mortality was largely averted by an extensive relief effort. The Central Board for Highland Destitution, formed in 1847 by merging Edinburgh and Glasgow relief committees, distributed meal in return for labour on "destitution roads" and other works, reaching tens of thousands of people. The Free Church of Scotland organized charitable appeals, and the government, through officials such as Sir Edward Pine Coffin, supplied meal depots. Some landlords supported their tenants; others exploited the crisis to clear estates for sheep farming, evicting crofters in Sutherland, Skye, Mull, Harris and elsewhere.

Emigration and legacy

Between 1846 and 1856 an estimated 70,000 Highlanders emigrated, many assisted by landlords or by the Highland and Island Emigration Society (founded 1852), which sent over 5,000 people to Australia. The famine hastened the decline of the crofting population, deepened grievances that later fuelled the crofters' agitation of the 1880s and the Crofters' Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886, and remains a defining episode — alongside the Clearances — in Highland historical memory.
