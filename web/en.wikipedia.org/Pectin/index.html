Pectin

Pectin is a structural acidic heteropolysaccharide contained in the primary and middle lamella and cell walls of terrestrial plants. Its main component is galacturonic acid, a sugar acid derived from galactose. It was first isolated and described in 1825 by Henri Braconnot. In plant biology, pectin consists of a complex set of polysaccharides that are present in most primary cell walls and are particularly abundant in the non-woody parts of terrestrial plants.

Sources and production

Commercial pectin is extracted mainly from citrus peel (lemon and lime) and apple pomace, by-products of juice production. Under acidic conditions at high temperature, protopectin in the raw material is hydrolysed and extracted.

Uses

The main use of pectin is as a gelling agent, thickening agent and stabilizer in food. It is essential for giving jams, jellies and marmalades their set, and is used in confectionery, yoghurts and acidic milk drinks. As dietary fibre, pectin is fermented in the colon. It is also used in medicine, for example in wound dressings and as an anti-diarrhoeal agent in kaolin preparations.

Chemistry

Pectins are classified by degree of methyl esterification: high-methoxyl pectins gel with sugar and acid, while low-methoxyl pectins gel in the presence of calcium ions.
