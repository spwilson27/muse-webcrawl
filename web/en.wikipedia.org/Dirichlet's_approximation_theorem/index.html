Dirichlet's approximation theorem

Every irrational number admits infinitely many good rational approximations.

In number theory, Dirichlet's approximation theorem, named after Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, states that for any real number α and any positive integer N, there exist integers p and q with 1 ≤ q ≤ N such that

|α − p/q| < 1/(qN) ≤ 1/q²

A corollary is that every irrational number α has infinitely many rational approximations p/q with |α − p/q| < 1/q². The theorem is a foundational result in the theory of Diophantine approximation and an early application of Dirichlet's pigeonhole principle.

Proof

The standard proof considers the N + 1 fractional parts {0}, {α}, {2α}, …, {Nα} lying in the interval [0, 1), which is divided into N subintervals of length 1/N. By the pigeonhole principle, two of these fractional parts, {jα} and {kα} with j < k, fall in the same subinterval, so their difference (k − j)α is within 1/N of an integer p. Setting q = k − j gives the result. This argument — the "Dirichlet box principle" — is often cited as the theorem's most significant legacy.

Significance and extensions

The exponent 2 in 1/q² is sharp in the sense that for some numbers (the "badly approximable" numbers, such as those with bounded continued-fraction partial quotients) the constant cannot be improved indefinitely; Hurwitz's theorem refines the constant to 1/(√5 q²), which is best possible because of the golden ratio. Dirichlet's theorem generalizes to simultaneous approximation of several real numbers: given reals α₁, …, α_d and N, there is an integer q ≤ N such that each qα_i is within N^(−1/d) of an integer. The theorem underlies Dirichlet's unit theorem context in algebraic number theory and has applications in the study of small divisors in dynamical systems.
