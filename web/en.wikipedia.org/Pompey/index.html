Pompey

Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (29 September 106 BC – 28 September 48 BC), known in English as Pompey or Pompey the Great, was a general and statesman of the Roman Republic. He played a significant role in the transformation of Rome from republic to empire. For a time he was a political ally and then an enemy of Julius Caesar.

Early career

Pompey rose to prominence serving under his father and then under the dictator Sulla, whose cause he supported during the civil wars of the 80s BC. Though too young for the Senate, he raised his own legions and won victories in Sicily and Africa, earning a triumph and the cognomen Magnus ("the Great"). He later suppressed the revolt of Sertorius in Spain and helped finish the slave war against Spartacus in 71 BC, then held the consulship with Crassus in 70 BC.

Commands in the East

In 67 BC the Lex Gabinia gave Pompey an extraordinary command against the pirates infesting the Mediterranean, whom he swept from the sea in a matter of months. The following year he took over the war against Mithridates VI of Pontus, defeating him and reorganising Rome's eastern provinces, annexing Syria and making Judea a client territory. His settlement of the East vastly increased Rome's revenues and his own wealth and prestige.

Triumvirate and civil war

Frustrated by senatorial obstruction of his veterans' land grants, Pompey joined Caesar and Crassus in the informal alliance known as the First Triumvirate in 60 BC, sealed by his marriage to Caesar's daughter Julia. After Julia's death and Crassus's death at Carrhae, the alliance collapsed. Appointed sole consul in 52 BC, Pompey aligned with the Senate against Caesar. When Caesar crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC, Pompey and the Senate fled Italy. Defeated at the Battle of Pharsalus in 48 BC, Pompey fled to Egypt, where he was murdered on the orders of Ptolemy XIII's advisers as he came ashore.

Legacy

Pompey built Rome's first permanent stone theatre, completed in 55 BC, and was celebrated as one of the Republic's greatest generals, though his political judgment faltered late in his career. His sons Gnaeus and Sextus continued resistance to Caesar and the triumvirs after his death.
