Cochinchina

Cochinchina corresponds roughly to the southern third of modern Vietnam

Cochinchina or Cochin-China (Vietnamese: Nam Kỳ) is a historical exonym for the southern third of Vietnam, and was the name of a French colony from 1862 to 1949. It comprised the fertile Mekong Delta and the region around Saigon (now Ho Chi Minh City). Together with Annam (central Vietnam), Tonkin (northern Vietnam), Cambodia and Laos, it formed French Indochina.

Name

The name combines "Cochin" — derived from the Indian port of Kochi (Cochin), by which early Portuguese navigators identified the region westward — with "China", reflecting its position on the route to China. The term was used loosely in Europe for centuries for parts of Vietnam before being fixed to the south; the Cochin chicken breed owes its name to this era of Far Eastern trade, though the birds came from Shanghai.

French colony

France seized Saigon in 1859 and by treaties of 1862 and 1867 obtained the southern provinces, organizing them as the directly ruled colony of Cochinchina — in contrast to the protectorates of Annam and Tonkin. Saigon became a major port and showcase of French colonial urbanism, and the Mekong Delta was converted into one of the world's great rice-exporting regions through canal drainage schemes.

During the First Indochina War the colony's status was contested; in 1949 Cochinchina was formally merged into the State of Vietnam. After the Geneva Accords of 1954, the territory formed the southern part of the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam) until reunification in 1976. Like other colonial creations, it functioned as a subordinate partner of its imperial power, akin to a client state of the modern era.
