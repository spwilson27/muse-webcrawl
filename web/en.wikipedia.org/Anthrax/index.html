Anthrax

Anthrax is an infection caused by the bacterium Bacillus anthracis, a Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacterium that forms hardy spores. It occurs naturally in soil and commonly affects domestic and wild animals around the world. People can become infected through contact with infected animals or animal products, inhalation of spores, ingestion, or injection.

Forms and symptoms

The disease takes four main forms. Cutaneous anthrax, the most common, begins as a small blister that develops into a painless ulcer with a characteristic black centre (eschar). Inhalation anthrax starts with flu-like symptoms and can progress to severe breathing problems and shock, and is frequently fatal without early treatment. Gastrointestinal and injection forms are rarer.

History and weaponization

Anthrax was historically associated with the wool and hide industries ("woolsorter's disease"). Because its spores are stable and easily dispersed, B. anthracis has been developed as a biological weapon; in the 2001 anthrax attacks in the United States, letters containing spores killed five people and infected 17 others. Vaccines exist for people at high risk, and antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin and doxycycline are effective when given promptly.
