The Armenian genocide was the systematic destruction of the Armenian people and identity in the Ottoman Empire during World War I. Spearheaded by the ruling Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), it was implemented primarily through the mass murder of around one million Armenians during death marches to the Syrian desert and the forced Islamisation of others, primarily women and children.

Background

The Ottoman Empire's roughly two million Armenians were a Christian minority whose position worsened amid the empire's territorial losses and the rise of Turkish nationalism. In the late 19th century, the Hamidian massacres (1894–96) killed tens of thousands. After the CUP seized power, and especially after Ottoman defeats by Russia in early 1915, the regime portrayed Armenians as an internal threat allied with the enemy.

Course of events

The genocide began with the disarming and execution of Armenian soldiers in the Ottoman army. On 24 April 1915, hundreds of Armenian intellectuals and community leaders were arrested in Constantinople — the date now commemorated as Genocide Remembrance Day. The Tehcir (deportation) law of May 1915 authorised empire-wide removals; deportees were marched toward camps in Syria (notably Deir ez-Zor) with little food or water, and many were massacred by the paramilitary Special Organization, gendarmes and local actors. Armenian property was confiscated and redistributed.

Aftermath and recognition

The genocide effectively destroyed the Armenian population of the eastern Ottoman provinces and created a global Armenian diaspora. Trials in 1919 convicted leading CUP figures, several in absentia. The scholarly consensus — including the International Association of Genocide Scholars — recognises the events as genocide, as do dozens of states and, in 2021, the United States formally. The Republic of Turkey rejects the term, a longstanding source of diplomatic dispute. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the word "genocide", cited the Armenian case as a formative example.
