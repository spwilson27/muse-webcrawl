War of 1812

The War of 1812 was fought by the United States and its Indigenous allies against the United Kingdom and its own Indigenous allies in British North America, with limited participation by Spain. It began when the United States declared war on Britain on 18 June 1812 and ended with the Treaty of Ghent, signed on 24 December 1814, which restored pre-war boundaries.

Causes

Causes included British impressment of American sailors, Britain's trade restrictions amid the Napoleonic Wars, and American expansionism toward British Canada and Indigenous lands in the Northwest Territory, where Tecumseh's confederacy resisted with British support.

Course

American invasions of Canada were repulsed, while US naval victories on Lake Erie and single-ship victories by frigates such as USS Constitution boosted morale. In 1814 British forces burned Washington, D.C., including the Capitol and White House, but were repelled at Baltimore — inspiring Francis Scott Key's "The Star-Spangled Banner" — and at Plattsburgh. The final major battle, New Orleans (January 1815), was a decisive American victory under Andrew Jackson, fought after the peace treaty had been signed but before news crossed the Atlantic.

Legacy

The war confirmed American independence, weakened Indigenous resistance east of the Mississippi, strengthened Canadian identity, and ushered in an "Era of Good Feelings" in the United States. Indigenous nations, who gained none of the promised buffer state, were the war's clearest losers.
