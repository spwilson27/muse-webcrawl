Norwegian cyclone model

The Norwegian cyclone model is a conceptual model of the development and life cycle of extratropical cyclones, developed beginning in 1918 by the Bergen School of meteorology in Norway, led by Vilhelm Bjerknes and including his son Jacob Bjerknes, Halvor Solberg, and Tor Bergeron. It was the first model to describe a mid-latitude cyclone in terms of air masses and fronts, and it revolutionised weather forecasting.

The model

In the model, a cyclone begins as a wave on a stationary front separating cold polar air from warm subtropical air (the polar front). As the wave amplifies, warm air pushes poleward east of the low centre, forming a warm front, while cold air pushes equatorward to the west, forming a faster-moving cold front. The region of warm air between them is the warm sector. Because the cold front advances faster than the warm front, it eventually catches up near the low centre, lifting the warm sector aloft and forming an occluded front; the cyclone then reaches maturity and gradually dissipates as it becomes cut off from its warm air supply.

Legacy

The Bergen school's frontal analysis, worked out from surface observations during and just after the First World War, gave forecasters a physical picture of storm development that remains the foundation of synoptic meteorology teaching, though later models such as the Shapiro–Keyser model refined its treatment of frontal fracture and warm seclusion.
