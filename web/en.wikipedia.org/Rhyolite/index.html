Rhyolite

TypeIgneous, volcanic (extrusive)

Rhyolite is an extrusive (volcanic) igneous rock of felsic composition, the volcanic equivalent of granite. It consists mainly of quartz, alkali feldspar and plagioclase, with more than 68% silica by weight. Rhyolite is typically fine-grained or glassy because its lava cools quickly at the surface, and it is often light-coloured — white, pink, grey or pale brown.

Formation

Rhyolitic magma is highly viscous owing to its high silica content and polymerised melt structure. It therefore tends to erupt explosively, producing ash flows, ignimbrites and pumice, or to ooze slowly into lava domes and short, thick flows. Some of the largest volcanic eruptions known — the caldera-forming "super-eruptions" of Yellowstone and Toba — involved enormous volumes of rhyolitic magma. Flow banding, spherulites and lithophysae are characteristic textures; the glassy form of rhyolite is obsidian, and its frothy, vesicular form is pumice.

Occurrence

Rhyolites are found in continental volcanic provinces and island arcs worldwide, including Iceland, the Taupō Volcanic Zone of New Zealand, the Yellowstone Plateau, the Lipari Islands (from which the related name liparite derives) and many parts of the western United States.

Uses

In prehistory, rhyolite was quarried and knapped for stone tools where it occurred in fine-grained, flint-like varieties. Today it is used locally as aggregate and decorative stone.
