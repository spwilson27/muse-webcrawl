Maurice of Nassau, Prince of Orange (Dutch: Maurits van Oranje; 14 November 1567 – 23 April 1625) was stadtholder of most of the provinces of the Dutch Republic and, as captain-general, commander of the Dutch States Army during the Eighty Years' War against Spain. A brilliant military reformer, he is regarded as one of the great captains of the age and an architect of the Dutch Republic's survival.

Early life

Maurice was born at Dillenburg, the son of William the Silent, Prince of Orange, and Anna of Saxony. After his father's assassination in 1584 he studied at Leiden University and, at only eighteen, was elected stadtholder of Holland and Zeeland in 1585. He later gained the stadtholderships of Utrecht, Gelderland and Overijssel.

Military reforms

Working with his cousin William Louis of Nassau-Dillenburg and advised by the humanist Justus Lipsius, Maurice rebuilt the States Army on classical Roman models: professional long-service soldiers, systematic drill, smaller tactical units, standardized weapons, volley fire by rank, and improved siegecraft supported by engineering and mathematics. These "Maurician reforms" influenced armies across Europe, notably that of Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden.

Campaigns

In a celebrated decade of conquest (1590–1600) Maurice captured Breda (by the famous peat-boat stratagem), Zutphen, Deventer, Nijmegen, Steenwijk, Groningen and other strongholds, largely securing the Republic's borders. In 1600 he defeated a Spanish army in open battle at Nieuwpoort in Flanders. His last years were marked by the Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621), a bitter political and religious conflict with the Land's Advocate Johan van Oldenbarnevelt — who was executed in 1619 — and renewed war after the truce expired. Maurice died at The Hague in April 1625, during the siege of Breda, and was succeeded by his half-brother Frederick Henry.
