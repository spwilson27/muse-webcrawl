Active immunity

Active immunity is immunity that develops after exposure to an antigen, either through natural infection or through vaccination. In active immunity, the body's own immune system produces antibodies and activates lymphocytes specific to the antigen, and it forms immunological memory, so subsequent encounters with the same antigen provoke a faster and stronger response.

Mechanism

When an antigen is encountered, antigen-presenting cells stimulate helper T cells, which in turn activate B cells to proliferate and differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies, and into long-lived memory B cells. Cytotoxic T cells are also activated against infected cells. The primary response takes days to weeks to develop, but the secondary response upon re-exposure is rapid and robust, which is the basis of long-term protection.

Comparison with passive immunity

Active immunity contrasts with passive immunity, in which ready-made antibodies are transferred to a person — for example, from mother to infant across the placenta or in breast milk, or by injection of immune globulin. Passive immunity provides immediate protection but is temporary, because the recipient's own immune system is not engaged and no memory is formed.
