Mooring

Mooring
DefinitionSecuring a vessel or floating structure to a fixed object or anchor system
ComponentsAnchor or ground tackle, chain/rode, buoy, lines
TypesPermanent moorings, swinging moorings, pile moorings, dockside berthing

A mooring is any permanent structure to which a seagoing vessel may be secured, and the act of securing the vessel to it. Examples include quays, wharves, jetties, piers, anchor buoys, and mooring buoys. A permanent mooring system for boats at anchor in a harbour typically consists of a heavy anchor or block on the seabed, a chain rising to a mooring buoy, and a pendant line to which the vessel ties.

Types of mooring

In a swinging mooring, a single ground tackle allows the boat to swing with wind and tide around the buoy. Fore-and-aft moorings secure bow and stern between two buoys or piles, restricting movement in crowded anchorages; Mediterranean mooring involves backing stern-to onto a quay with an anchor laid out ahead. Large ships moor alongside berths using heavy mooring lines managed by winches and bollards, and offshore oil and gas platforms use spread mooring systems of many anchor legs, or dynamic positioning.

Mooring equipment and safety

Mooring lines are made of synthetic fibres such as nylon, polyester, and high-modulus polyethylene, chosen for strength and elasticity. Snap-back of a parted line under tension is a serious hazard, and mooring operations are a leading cause of injury aboard ships. Scientific moorings — anchored arrays of instruments used in oceanography — share the name and are deployed and recovered by research vessels worldwide.
