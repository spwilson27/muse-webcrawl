A lighthouse is a tower, building, or other type of physical structure designed to emit light from a system of lamps and lenses and to serve as a beacon for navigational aid for maritime pilots at sea or on inland waterways. Lighthouses mark dangerous coastlines, hazardous shoals, reefs, rocks, and safe entries to harbours; they also assist in aerial navigation.

History

The most famous ancient lighthouse was the Pharos of Alexandria in Egypt, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, built in the 3rd century BC. Modern lighthouse construction flourished from the 18th century as trade expanded; notable engineers include John Smeaton, whose Eddystone Lighthouse (1759) pioneered the interlocking stone tower, and Robert Stevenson and his family in Scotland. Augustin-Jean Fresnel's stepped lens, first installed in the 1820s, greatly increased the power and range of lighthouse optics.

Technology and operation

A lighthouse's light characteristic — its pattern of flashes or occultations and its colour — identifies it on charts. Lights were successively fuelled by wood, coal, whale oil, kerosene, and acetylene before electrification. Automation in the 20th century removed resident keepers from nearly all stations; the last manned lighthouses in many countries were automated in the 1990s. Although GPS and electronic navigation have reduced their navigational role, lighthouses remain maintained as backup visual aids and are valued as cultural heritage, many protected as historic monuments.
