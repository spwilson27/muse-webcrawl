Argo is an international programme that uses free-drifting profiling floats to observe the temperature, salinity, and currents of the upper ocean. Named after the ship of Jason and the Argonauts — and deliberately echoing the Jason satellite altimetry missions with which its data are combined — Argo maintains an array of roughly 4,000 robotic floats distributed across the world ocean, managed by more than 30 countries.

How it works

Each float drifts at a parking depth of about 1,000 metres, then every ten days descends to 2,000 metres and rises to the surface, measuring temperature, salinity, and pressure profiles on the way up. At the surface it transmits its data and position by satellite before sinking again. The floats run on batteries for about four to five years. The resulting data are freely available in near real time and have transformed the monitoring of ocean heat content, sea level rise, and the global water cycle.

Extensions and science

Newer programme branches include Biogeochemical Argo, which carries sensors for oxygen, nitrate, pH, chlorophyll, and other variables, and Deep Argo, whose floats profile to 4,000–6,000 metres. Argo data underpin estimates of ocean warming used in climate assessments and have provided the majority of subsurface ocean profiles in recent decades.
