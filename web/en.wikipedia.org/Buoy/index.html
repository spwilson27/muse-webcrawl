Buoy


A port-hand lateral buoy marking a navigation channel.

A buoy (pronounced /ˈbuːi/ or /ˈbɔɪ/) is a floating device anchored at a specific location, used among other things to mark channels and hazards for navigation, to moor vessels, and to carry instruments that measure weather and ocean conditions. The word comes from Middle Dutch boeye, originally referring to a float or a shackle. Buoys range from small plastic floats marking lobster pots to steel structures tens of metres tall carrying lights, radar reflectors, bells or solar-powered instruments, and some drift freely rather than being anchored.

Navigation buoys

The most familiar buoys are aids to navigation. The International Association of Marine Aids to Navigation and Lighthouse Authorities (IALA) Maritime Buoyage System standardises their shapes, colours, topmarks and light rhythms. Lateral marks indicate the sides of a channel: in IALA Region B (the Americas, Japan, Korea and the Philippines) a red conical or can buoy marks the right side when returning from sea — the mnemonic "red, right, returning" — while Region A (Europe, Africa, most of Asia and Australasia) uses the opposite colour convention for the same shapes. Other standard marks include cardinal marks, which indicate safe water by reference to the compass directions relative to a hazard; isolated-danger marks, coloured black with red bands; safe-water marks in red and white; and special marks, usually yellow, denoting areas such as cable zones or recreation areas. Many are lit or fitted with radar reflectors, and historically fog signals such as bells and whistles sounded by the motion of the waves.

Other types

Mooring buoys provide a point to which ships and boats tie up, taking the strain on a heavy ground chain and sinker instead of the vessel's own anchor. Weather buoys, operated by agencies such as NOAA's National Data Buoy Center, carry sensors for wind, pressure, air and sea temperature and wave height, transmitting data by satellite; tropical moored arrays such as TAO/TRITON in the Pacific are central to El Niño forecasting. Oceanographic and drifting buoys — including the thousands of Argo floats that profile the upper ocean — drift with the currents and report by satellite. Specialised buoys include tsunami-detection DART buoys anchored in deep water, sonar buoys (sonobuoys) dropped by anti-submarine aircraft, marker buoys for racing courses and fishing gear, and rescue and lifebuoy floats. The first lightships and later light buoys took over station where building a lighthouse was impracticable.

Design and operation

A buoy's hull must provide buoyancy and stability in waves; traditional materials were wood and steel, while modern small buoys are often rotationally moulded polyethylene with foam filling so they survive collision and ice. Anchor systems typically consist of chain and a concrete or cast-iron sinker sized to local depths and currents. Lights are now almost universally LED powered by solar panels and batteries, replacing acetylene gas and filament lamps. Coast guards and port authorities maintain buoy fleets with dedicated tenders that lift, service and reposition the marks.
