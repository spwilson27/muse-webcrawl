Allan Ramsay

Allan Ramsay (15 October 1686 – 7 January 1758) was a Scottish poet (or makar), playwright, publisher, librarian and impresario of early Enlightenment Edinburgh. Born in Leadhills, Lanarkshire, he was apprenticed as a wig-maker in Edinburgh and became a member of the Easy Club, a literary society, before turning to bookselling and publishing.

Ramsay wrote in both Scots and English. His pastoral comedy The Gentle Shepherd (1725) was a landmark of Scots literature, and his collections The Tea-Table Miscellany (1724–1737) and The Ever Green (1724) preserved and popularised both his own poems and older Scots verse, helping to revive vernacular Scottish poetry. He established one of Britain's first circulating libraries in Edinburgh in 1725 and set up the Carrubber's Close theatre, which was forced to close under the Licensing Act. His son, also Allan Ramsay, became a celebrated portrait painter to George III.

Works

Ramsay's output includes poems such as "The Monk and the Miller's Wife" and "Elegy on Maggy Johnston", as well as songs, fables and epistles exchanged with fellow poets. His editions of older Scots literature drew on the Bannatyne Manuscript, providing many readers with their first access to the medieval makars such as Robert Henryson and William Dunbar.

Legacy

Ramsay is commemorated by a monument in Greyfriars Kirkyard, Edinburgh, and by a statue on Princes Street. His preservation of Scots song and verse influenced later poets including Robert Fergusson and Robert Burns.
