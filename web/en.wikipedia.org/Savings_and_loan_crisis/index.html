Savings and loan crisis

The savings and loan crisis of the 1980s and 1990s was a wave of failures of savings and loan associations (S&Ls, or "thrifts") in the United States. Between 1986 and 1995, over a thousand S&Ls — roughly a third of the industry — failed, and the federal cleanup cost taxpayers an estimated 24 billion or more.

Causes

Thrifts traditionally took deposits and made long-term fixed-rate home mortgages. When interest rates spiked in the late 1970s, they paid more for deposits than they earned on old loans. Deregulation in 1980–82 (notably the Garn–St Germain Act) let S&Ls enter commercial real estate and other risky lending without matching supervision, while deposit insurance through FSLIC encouraged moral hazard. Fraud and insider abuse, exemplified by Charles Keating's Lincoln Savings and the "Keating Five" scandal, worsened losses.

Resolution

The Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (FIRREA) abolished FSLIC and created the Resolution Trust Corporation (RTC) to sell off the assets of failed thrifts; the RTC closed 747 institutions before dissolving in 1995. Oversight of the remaining industry passed to the Office of Thrift Supervision.
