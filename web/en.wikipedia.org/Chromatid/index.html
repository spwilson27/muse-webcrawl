Chromatid

A chromatid is one half of a duplicated chromosome. Before replication, one chromosome consists of a single DNA molecule; during the synthesis (S) phase of the cell cycle the DNA is copied, and the chromosome thereafter consists of two identical copies, called sister chromatids, joined together at a constricted region called the centromere by cohesin proteins.

Role in cell division

During mitosis, sister chromatids condense, align at the metaphase plate, and are then pulled apart when cohesin is cleaved, so that each daughter cell receives one copy of every chromosome. In meiosis the process is more elaborate: homologous chromosomes pair, and crossing over between non-sister chromatids exchanges genetic material, generating new combinations of alleles. Sister chromatids separate in the second meiotic division.

Sister and non-sister chromatids

The two chromatids of one duplicated chromosome are sisters; chromatids belonging to the two different homologues of a pair are non-sister chromatids. Genetic recombination during prophase I of meiosis occurs between non-sister chromatids and produces chiasmata, the visible points of exchange.
