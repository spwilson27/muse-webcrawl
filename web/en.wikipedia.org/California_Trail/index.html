California Trail

The California Trail was an emigrant trail of about 3,000 kilometres (1,950 miles) across the western half of North America, used from the 1840s to the late 1860s by more than 250,000 settlers and gold seekers travelling to California. It shared much of its eastern course with the Oregon Trail before branching westward across the Great Basin and over the Sierra Nevada.

Route

Wagon trains outfitted at Missouri River towns such as Independence and St. Joseph, followed the Platte River across the Great Plains to Fort Laramie, crossed the Continental Divide at South Pass in present-day Wyoming, and continued past Fort Bridger. From there emigrants could circle the Great Salt Lake or take the Hastings Cutoff directly across the salt flats — a shortcut whose delays contributed to the Donner Party disaster of 1846–47. The main trail followed the Humboldt River across present-day Nevada, then crossed the Sierra Nevada by passes such as Donner Pass and Carson Pass into the Sacramento Valley.

History

The first overland emigrant party to reach California by this route was the Bidwell–Bartleson Party of 1841. Traffic grew after the United States acquired California in 1848 and exploded with the California Gold Rush of 1849, when tens of thousands of "forty-niners" made the five-to-six-month journey in a single season. Use declined sharply after the completion of the first transcontinental railroad in 1869. Today much of the trail is preserved by the National Park Service as the California National Historic Trail, and wagon ruts remain visible in many places.
