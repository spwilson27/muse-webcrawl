Class-D amplifier

A class-D amplifier converts the input to a pulse train, then filters it.

A class-D amplifier or switching amplifier is an electronic amplifier in which the amplifying devices (usually MOSFETs) operate as electronic switches, being either fully on or fully off, rather than in the linear mode used by other amplifier classes. The input signal is converted into a sequence of pulses whose average value is proportional to the signal — most often by pulse-width modulation (PWM) — amplified by the switching output stage, and then passed through a low-pass filter to recover the amplified waveform and remove the high-frequency switching components.

Operation

Because the output transistors spend almost no time in the lossy transition region between on and off, theoretical efficiency approaches 100%, and practical designs commonly exceed 90%. This contrasts with class-A amplifiers (maximum theoretical efficiency 25–50%) and class-AB designs (up to about 78.5% for sine waves). The low waste heat allows small heatsinks and compact, lightweight products. Typical switching frequencies range from tens of kilohertz in subwoofer amplifiers to several hundred kilohertz or more in full-range designs.

Advantages and drawbacks

High efficiency and small size have made class-D the dominant topology in battery-powered and portable audio, mobile phones, televisions, powered speakers, car audio and high-power public-address systems. Drawbacks include the need for output filtering, potential electromagnetic interference from the switching stage, distortion from dead time and power-supply effects, and historically a reputation for inferior sound quality in high-fidelity applications — a gap that modern designs using feedback and faster semiconductor devices (including gallium nitride transistors) have largely closed.

History

The principle was proposed by British scientist Alec Reeves in the 1950s and the term "class D" was introduced in 1955; practical designs awaited suitable switching transistors. Commercial products appeared from the 1960s (such as the Sinclair X-10) and 1980s onward, and integrated class-D amplifier chips became widespread in the 2000s. The "D" in class-D does not stand for "digital", though class-D stages are often combined with digital inputs. See also amplifier classes and pulse-width modulation.
