Parachute

TypeAerodynamic decelerator
First jump1783 (Lenormand, unverified); 1797 (Garnerin)
Main usesPersonnel descent, cargo drop, spacecraft recovery, sport

A parachute is a device used to slow the motion of an object through an atmosphere by creating drag. Parachutes are made from light, strong fabric — originally silk, now usually nylon — and are used to drop troops, supplies, and vehicles from aircraft, to recover spacecraft, to stabilise racing cars and aircraft, and as recreational and competitive equipment in the sport of parachuting (skydiving).

History

The concept is old: a conical parachute-like device appears in Chinese accounts, and Leonardo da Vinci sketched a pyramidal parachute around 1485. Louis-Sébastien Lenormand is said to have jumped from a tower in Montpellier in 1783. André-Jacques Garnerin made the first recorded descent with a frameless parachute of silk over Paris in 1797, descending from a hydrogen balloon at about 900 metres. Parachutes became practical life-saving equipment for balloonists and, during the First World War, for observation balloon crews and later for aviators. The US Army adopted backpack parachutes after the war, influenced by the work of Leslie Irvin, who made the first premeditated free-fall jump in 1919.

Design

The classic personnel parachute uses a round canopy that creates drag and descends at roughly 5 metres per second. Modern sport parachutes are usually ram-air canopies — rectangular wings that generate lift and allow the jumper to steer and flare for a soft landing. Components include the canopy, suspension lines, risers, harness and container, a deployment system (often a small pilot chute), and a reserve parachute. Automatic activation devices open the reserve if the jumper descends too fast at low altitude.

Military and space use

Mass airborne operations in the Second World War, such as the Normandy landings and Operation Market Garden, relied on parachutes to deliver paratroopers. Cargo parachutes can deliver loads of many tonnes. Spacecraft including Apollo, Soyuz, and Crew Dragon use clusters of parachutes for splashdown or landing, and parachutes have landed rovers on Mars despite its thin atmosphere.

Records

Joseph Kittinger jumped from 31,333 metres in 1960 as part of Project Excelsior. Felix Baumgartner surpassed this in 2012, jumping from 38,969 metres and becoming the first person to break the sound barrier in free fall; Alan Eustace set the current altitude record of 41,422 metres in 2014.
