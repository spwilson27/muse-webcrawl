Dying-and-rising god

A dying-and-rising god, or death-rebirth deity, is a religious motif in which a god or goddess dies — or descends into the underworld — and is subsequently resurrected or returned to life. The concept was popularized by the Scottish anthropologist Sir James George Frazer in The Golden Bough (1890–1915), who linked such deities to seasonal vegetation cycles: the god's death representing winter or harvest, and the return representing spring renewal.

Examples

Figures commonly cited include the Egyptian Osiris, the Mesopotamian Tammuz (Dumuzi) and Inanna/Ishtar's descent, the Levantine Baal, the Phrygian Attis, the Greek Adonis, Dionysus, and Persephone, and the Norse Baldr. In some traditions the deity returns cyclically each year; in others the return is a one-time event or a descent-and-return rather than a literal death.

Scholarly debate

Twentieth-century scholars increasingly criticized the category as an artificial construct that flattens distinct myths into a single pattern. Jonathan Z. Smith argued that many supposed "dying-and-rising gods" either do not die, do not rise, or do not do both. Others, such as Tryggve Mettinger in The Riddle of Resurrection (2001), defended a limited version of the category for certain ancient Near Eastern deities. The concept remains debated in comparative religion and in discussions of early Christianity.
