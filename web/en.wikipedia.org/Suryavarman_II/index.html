Suryavarman II (Khmer: សូរ្យវរ្ម័នទី២), posthumously named Paramavishnuloka, was a Khmer king from 1113 AD to c. 1145/1150. He was the builder of Angkor Wat, the world's largest religious monument, which he dedicated to the Hindu god Vishnu. His reign also saw the reunification of the Khmer Empire after a period of division and renewed warfare against neighbouring Champa and Dai Viet. Suryavarman's monumental architecture, numerous military campaigns, and extensive building program have made him one of the most celebrated Khmer monarchs.

Rise to power

Suryavarman was a young prince of a collateral royal line, descended from kings ruling in the region of Mahidharapura. Inscriptions describe him studying the sacred rules of ritual and claiming the throne after defeating his great-uncle, Dharanindravarman I, in battle — an episode he compared to Garuda slaying a serpent. He was crowned in 1113, with the aged Brahman scholar Divakarapandita presiding over his consecration, and he unified a kingdom that had been split between rival courts.

Angkor Wat

Angkor Wat, built during the first half of Suryavarman's reign, is a vast temple-mountain representing Mount Meru, surrounded by a moat some 3.6 km long. Unusually for Khmer temples, it is oriented to the west and was dedicated to Vishnu rather than Shiva, reflecting the king's personal devotion. Its bas-reliefs include a famous panel depicting Suryavarman's court procession — one of the few contemporary images of a Khmer king. Scholars generally conclude the temple also served as the king's funerary temple, consistent with his posthumous name Paramavishnuloka, "the king who has entered the supreme world of Vishnu".

Warfare and diplomacy

Suryavarman campaigned repeatedly in the east against Dai Viet and Champa. He invaded Dai Viet in 1128, 1132, and later years, and mounted large expeditions against Champa in the 1140s, briefly installing a brother-in-law as a client king at Vijaya. Champa recovered under Jaya Harivarman I, and Suryavarman may have died during a campaign in the mid-1140s. Diplomatically, he reopened relations with China, sending embassies to the Song court, which in 1116 and 1120 recognised him with honorific titles.

Legacy

Suryavarman's reign is regarded as a high point of the classical Khmer Empire. Temples built in his style include Thommanon, Chau Say Tevoda, Banteay Samre, Beng Mealea, and the early stages of Preah Khan. He was succeeded by Dharanindravarman II, a cousin, under whom the empire began a period of weaker central control that culminated, a generation later, in the Cham sack of Angkor in 1177.
