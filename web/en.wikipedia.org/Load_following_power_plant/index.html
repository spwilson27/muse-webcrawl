Load following power plant

A load following power plant, sometimes called a load follower, is a power plant that adjusts its power output as demand and price for electricity fluctuates throughout the day. Load following plants are typically in between base load and peaking power plants in efficiency, speed of start-up and shut-down, construction cost, cost of electricity and capacity factor.

Operation

Base load power plants run nearly continuously at high capacity, while peaking plants run only during periods of highest demand. Load following plants occupy the middle ground: they ramp output up and down each day to follow the changing load profile of the grid. Hydroelectric plants, natural gas combined-cycle plants, and some coal plants are commonly used in this role.

Technologies

Hydropower is well suited to load following because reservoirs can store energy and turbines can change output within minutes. Combined-cycle gas turbines can also ramp relatively quickly. Nuclear power plants in some countries, notably France, have historically operated in load-following mode, although economic and technical factors usually favour running them at constant output.

Economics

Operating a plant at partial output or cycling it on and off imposes additional maintenance costs and reduces fuel efficiency. As renewable generation with variable output grows, the value of flexible load-following capacity on electrical grids has increased.
