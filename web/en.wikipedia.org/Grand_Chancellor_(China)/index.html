Grand Chancellor (China)

Court officials of imperial China, among whom the chancellor ranked first

The Grand Chancellor (Chinese: 宰相; pinyin: zǎixiàng, among other titles) was the highest-ranking executive official of the imperial Chinese government for much of China's history, functioning in effect as the emperor's chief minister or prime minister. The precise title, powers, and number of holders changed considerably from dynasty to dynasty.

Terminology and titles

Under the Han dynasty, the senior official was the Chancellor (丞相, chéngxiàng), one of the Three Ducal Ministers. In the Tang dynasty, the heads of the Chancellery, Secretariat, and Department of State Affairs effectively shared chancellorial power, and officials could be designated "participants in deliberations" (參知政事) with equivalent rank. The Song dynasty used titles including cānzhī zhèngshì for vice-chancellors. Under the Ming dynasty, after the Hongwu Emperor abolished the chancellorship in 1380 following the Hu Weiyong case, the role was gradually assumed by the Grand Secretaries of the Grand Secretariat.

Role

The chancellor supervised the civil bureaucracy, drafted and reviewed imperial edicts, advised the emperor on policy and appointments, and coordinated the ministries. The office served as a check on imperial power in theory, since edicts ideally required chancellorial countersignature, but powerful chancellors could also dominate the throne, and emperors periodically purged or bypassed the office through inner-court institutions.

Abolition

The Hongwu Emperor's abolition of the position in 1380, and his edict forbidding its restoration, permanently altered Chinese government: later Ming and Qing rulers governed through grand secretaries and grand councilors who lacked the chancellor's formal authority, concentrating more power in the emperor's hands.
