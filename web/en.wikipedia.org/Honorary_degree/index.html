Honorary degree

An honorary degree is an academic degree for which a university has waived the usual requirements, such as matriculation, coursework, and examinations. It is typically a doctorate or master's degree conferred honoris causa ("for the sake of the honour") to recognise distinguished achievement in scholarship, the arts, public service, or philanthropy.

History

The practice dates to the late fifteenth century; Lionel Woodville is often cited as the first recipient, receiving a Doctor of Canon Law from Oxford around 1478. Universities have long used honorary degrees to honour benefactors and public figures as well as scholars.

Customs and conventions

Recipients are usually addressed without the title "Doctor" in some conventions, though practice varies by country and institution. Honorary degrees are generally not considered equivalent to earned doctorates for academic appointments. Awards to controversial figures occasionally provoke protest, and institutions have sometimes rescinded them.

Notable refusals and returns

Some recipients have declined or returned honorary degrees in protest at institutional policies. The degree is distinct from a degree awarded posthumously or ad eundem.
