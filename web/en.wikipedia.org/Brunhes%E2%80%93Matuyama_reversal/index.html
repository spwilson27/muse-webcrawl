Brunhes–Matuyama reversal

TypeGeomagnetic reversal
DateApprox. 781,000 years ago
Named afterBernard Brunhes; Motonori Matuyama

The Brunhes–Matuyama reversal was the most recent event in Earth's history in which the geomagnetic field fully reversed its polarity, so that magnetic north and south exchanged places. It occurred approximately 781,000 years ago and marks the boundary between the current Brunhes Chron of normal polarity and the preceding Matuyama Chron, which was predominantly of reversed polarity. The reversal is named after French geophysicist Bernard Brunhes and Japanese geophysicist Motonori Matuyama, pioneers in the study of palaeomagnetism.

Discovery

In 1906 Brunhes reported that some ancient lavas in France were magnetised opposite to the present field, and in the 1920s Matuyama showed that older Japanese rocks were reversely magnetised while younger ones were not. Dating of the boundary improved through the 20th century; the currently accepted age of about 781,000 years is based on argon–argon dating and astronomical tuning of marine sediment records.

Duration and character

Estimates of the reversal's duration vary: studies of lava sequences and sediments suggest the directional transition took on the order of a few thousand years, though precursor events and a weakened field may span a longer interval. During the transition the field intensity fell substantially and its geometry became complex rather than simply dipolar.

Significance

The Brunhes–Matuyama boundary is a globally synchronous marker horizon widely used in Quaternary geochronology and stratigraphy. It lies close to the boundary between the Early and Middle Pleistocene and is recorded in marine sediments, ice cores through its effect on cosmogenic isotopes, and volcanic rocks.
