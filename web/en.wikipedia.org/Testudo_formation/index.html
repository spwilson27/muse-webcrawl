Testudo formation

In Ancient Roman warfare, the testudo or tortoise formation was a type of shield wall formation commonly used by the Roman legions during battles, particularly during sieges. In the testudo formation, the men would align their shields to form a packed formation covered on the front and top with shields, like the shell of a tortoise (testudo is Latin for "tortoise").

Description

The front rank of soldiers would kneel or stand with their curved rectangular shields (scuta) held edge-to-edge in front of them. The ranks behind them would place their shields over their heads, overlapping those in front, so that the whole unit was protected from missile fire from above as well as from the front. The sides and rear were similarly protected by soldiers turning their shields outward. The formation could advance slowly as a solid block, and was especially effective against archers and other light missile troops.

Usage

The testudo was used when approaching fortified positions under fire, allowing legionaries to reach walls, gates, or siege works while minimizing casualties from arrows, stones, and javelins. It is described by Roman authors including Julius Caesar and Cassius Dio, and depicted on monuments such as Trajan's Column. Because the formation was dense and slow, it was vulnerable to heavy infantry charges and to artillery such as ballistae, and it could not be maintained while fighting hand-to-hand.

Later use

Similar shield-roof formations were used by Byzantine infantry, and the principle survives today in the "turtle" formations of riot police, who interlock shields overhead and on all sides when advancing under thrown missiles.
