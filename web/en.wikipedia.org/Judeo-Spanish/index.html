Judeo-Spanish

Judeo-Spanish (also known as Ladino, judezmo, spanyol, and in North Africa haketia) is a Romance language derived from Old Spanish and spoken by Sephardic Jews. After the expulsion of the Jews from Spain by the Alhambra Decree in 1492, exiles carried their Spanish to the Ottoman Empire — Salonica, Constantinople, the Balkans, and Anatolia — and to North Africa, where it developed independently of the Spanish of Spain, preserving many archaic features while borrowing vocabulary from Hebrew, Turkish, Greek, and other local languages.

History

In the Ottoman world, Judeo-Spanish became the lingua franca of Sephardic communities, with a rich press, literature, and oral tradition of ballads (romances) and folk songs. Traditionally written in the Hebrew alphabet (the Rashi script for print and the cursive Solitreo for handwriting), it is now usually written in Latin letters. The term "Ladino" is, strictly, also applied to a word-for-word calque register used for translating sacred texts.

Decline and revival

The language declined sharply in the 20th century through assimilation, emigration, and above all the Holocaust, which destroyed its heartland communities such as Salonica, where Jews had been a plurality of the population. Most remaining speakers are elderly, and UNESCO classifies Judeo-Spanish as severely endangered. Revival efforts include the work of Israel's National Authority for Ladino, academic programmes, dictionaries, and a growing body of music and publishing, as well as online communities of learners.

Features

Judeo-Spanish retains features of 15th-century Castilian, such as the pronunciation of letters that later changed in Spain, and has absorbed loanwords from Hebrew and Aramaic (especially in religious domains), Turkish, Greek, South Slavic languages, Arabic (in Moroccan Haketia), and French, the latter through the schools of the Alliance Israélite Universelle.
