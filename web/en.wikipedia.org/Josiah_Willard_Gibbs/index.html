Josiah Willard Gibbs

Born: February 11, 1839, New Haven, Connecticut, U.S.
Died: April 28, 1903 (aged 64), New Haven, Connecticut, U.S.
Alma mater: Yale University (PhD 1863)
Known for: Chemical thermodynamics; Gibbs free energy; statistical mechanics; vector analysis

Josiah Willard Gibbs (February 11, 1839 – April 28, 1903) was an American scientist who made significant theoretical contributions to physics, chemistry, and mathematics. His work on the applications of thermodynamics was instrumental in transforming physical chemistry into a rigorous deductive science. Together with James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann, he created statistical mechanics, explaining the laws of thermodynamics as consequences of the statistical properties of ensembles of possible states of a physical system.

Early life and education

Gibbs was born in New Haven, Connecticut, the son of a Yale professor of sacred literature, also named Josiah Willard Gibbs, known for his role in the Amistad case. The younger Gibbs studied at Yale, winning prizes in mathematics and Latin, and in 1863 received the first American doctorate in engineering. After tutoring at Yale he spent three years in Europe studying with Kirchhoff, Helmholtz, and other leading physicists.

Thermodynamics

Appointed professor of mathematical physics at Yale in 1871 — a chair he held, initially without salary, for the rest of his life — Gibbs published a series of papers culminating in "On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances" (1876–1878), described by Wilhelm Ostwald as giving thermodynamics its form and by later writers as the foundation of chemical thermodynamics. It introduced the concepts of Gibbs free energy and chemical potential and the phase rule governing equilibria among phases of matter. Published in an obscure journal, the work was at first appreciated mainly in Europe, where Maxwell championed it.

Statistical mechanics and vectors

In his Elementary Principles in Statistical Mechanics (1902), Gibbs gave the subject its name and its mathematical framework of ensembles — microcanonical, canonical, and grand canonical — placing thermodynamics on a probabilistic basis. Separately, his lectures on vector analysis, developed from Maxwell's and Hamilton's ideas, provided the modern notation of vectors widely used in physics and engineering, though it embroiled him in a public dispute with advocates of quaternions.

Legacy

Gibbs spent nearly his whole career quietly at Yale, publishing little but with extraordinary depth. Einstein called him "the greatest mind in American history." His name is attached to the Gibbs phenomenon in Fourier analysis, Gibbs entropy, and the unit of surface concentration, and he was awarded the Copley Medal in 1901.
