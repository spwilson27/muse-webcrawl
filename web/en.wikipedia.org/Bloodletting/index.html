Bloodletting

Bloodletting
Type: Historical medical practice
Theory: Humorism
Modern remnant: Therapeutic phlebotomy

Bloodletting is the withdrawal of blood from a patient to prevent or cure illness. Based on the ancient theory of humours, it was the most common medical practice performed by surgeons from antiquity until the late 19th century.

History

Physicians including Galen prescribed venesection for fevers and inflammation, and barber-surgeons made it a routine service. Methods included opening a vein, cupping with scarification, and applying leeches.

Decline

Statistical studies in the 19th century, notably by Pierre Louis, showed bloodletting did not help pneumonia patients, and germ theory undermined its rationale. A narrow form survives as therapeutic phlebotomy for conditions such as haemochromatosis and polycythaemia vera.
