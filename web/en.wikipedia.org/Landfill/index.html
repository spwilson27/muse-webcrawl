Landfill

A landfill is a site for the disposal of waste materials by burial. It is the oldest and most common form of large-scale waste disposal: waste is deposited in excavated or above-ground cells, compacted, and covered with soil or alternative daily cover. Modern regulated sites are engineered as "sanitary landfills" with liners, leachate collection, and gas management.

Operation

Waste is spread in thin layers and compacted by heavy steel-wheeled compactors to maximise capacity, then covered to reduce odour, litter, and vermin. A modern landfill cell sits on a liner of compacted clay and high-density polyethylene, with pipes collecting leachate — the contaminated liquid that drains from decomposing waste — for treatment. Decomposition of organic matter produces landfill gas, roughly half methane; captured gas may be flared or burned for electricity, while escaping methane is a potent greenhouse emission.

Environmental issues

Poorly managed landfills can pollute groundwater, emit methane and odours, and attract vermin. Regulations in the United States (under RCRA Subtitle D) and the European Union (the 1999 Landfill Directive) require siting controls, groundwater monitoring, financial assurance, and decades of aftercare after closure. Closed landfills are commonly reused as parks, solar farms, or golf courses; New York's Freshkills, once the world's largest landfill, is being transformed into Freshkills Park.

Alternatives

Waste policy generally ranks landfill last in the waste hierarchy, after reduction, reuse, recycling, composting, and incineration with energy recovery, and many jurisdictions tax landfill disposal to discourage it.
