Maser

A maser is a device that produces coherent electromagnetic waves through amplification by stimulated emission. The term is an acronym for microwave amplification by stimulated emission of radiation. The maser was the precursor of the laser, which extended the same principle to optical frequencies; in modern usage "maser" sometimes refers to any stimulated-emission oscillator, whatever its frequency.

History

The principle of stimulated emission was described by Albert Einstein in 1917. In 1951–53 Charles Townes and his students at Columbia University built the first working maser using ammonia gas, while Nikolay Basov and Alexander Prokhorov in the Soviet Union independently developed the theory of molecular oscillators. Townes, Basov, and Prokhorov shared the 1964 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. Solid-state masers, notably the ruby maser developed at Bell Labs, followed in the late 1950s.

Principle of operation

A maser requires a gain medium driven into a population inversion — more atoms or molecules in an excited state than in a lower state. A passing photon of the right energy can then stimulate an excited particle to emit a second photon identical in frequency, phase, and direction. Placed in or coupled to a resonant cavity, the medium amplifies a coherent wave, producing an extremely pure, low-noise microwave signal.

Applications

Because of their extraordinarily low noise, maser amplifiers were used in early satellite communications and in radio astronomy, including receivers associated with deep-space tracking. The hydrogen maser is used as an atomic frequency standard whose stability exceeds that of caesium clocks over short intervals, serving in very-long-baseline interferometry and deep-space navigation. Masers also occur naturally in space: astrophysical masers in star-forming regions and around evolved stars amplify microwave lines of molecules such as water, hydroxyl, and silicon monoxide, letting astronomers probe those environments.
