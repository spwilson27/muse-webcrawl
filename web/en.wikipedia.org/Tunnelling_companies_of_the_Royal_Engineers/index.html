Tunnelling companies of the Royal Engineers

Tunnellers at work beneath the Western Front

The tunnelling companies of the Royal Engineers were specialist units of the British Army formed during the First World War to conduct offensive and defensive mining operations beneath enemy lines on the Western Front.

Formation

In late 1914 and early 1915, German mining attacks exposed the weakness of improvised British counter-mining. Major John Norton-Griffiths, an engineer and Member of Parliament, pressed for the recruitment of civilian tunnellers — coal miners, sewer workers and "clay-kickers" from Britain's mines and underground railways. The first companies were formed in February 1915, and at their peak there were around 25 British tunnelling companies, alongside Canadian, Australian and New Zealand tunnelling companies.

Work

Tunnellers dug offensive mines under German positions, charged with ammonal explosive; defensive galleries and listening posts to detect enemy digging; deep dugouts, subways and shelters; and wells and water infrastructure. Work was extraordinarily dangerous: tunnels could collapse or flood, underground fighting erupted when opposing galleries met, and the enemy used camouflets — charges designed to collapse tunnels and kill their occupants without breaking the surface.

Major operations

The tunnellers' most famous achievement was the mines at Messines Ridge in June 1917, where 19 mines containing some 455 tonnes of explosive were fired at the start of the battle. At Hill 60, Vimy Ridge and in the run-up to the Somme offensive (including the huge Lochnagar and Y Sap mines at La Boisselle), tunnellers played a central role.
