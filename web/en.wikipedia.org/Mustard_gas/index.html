Mustard gas

Mustard gas, or sulfur mustard, is a chemical warfare agent with the formula bis(2-chloroethyl) sulfide, a cytotoxic and blistering (vesicant) compound. In impure form it is a yellow-brown oily liquid said to smell of garlic, mustard, or horseradish; the pure compound is nearly odourless and colourless.

Effects

Mustard gas is a potent alkylating agent that damages skin, eyes, and the respiratory tract. Symptoms are delayed, typically appearing hours after exposure: severe blistering of the skin, conjunctivitis and possible blindness, and airway injury that can be fatal. Because it attacks DNA, it is also mutagenic and carcinogenic. It penetrates clothing and rubber, and persists in the environment, especially in cool conditions.

History

First used by Germany near Ypres, Belgium, in July 1917 during the First World War — hence the name "Yperite" — mustard gas was subsequently used by both sides and in conflicts including the Rif War, the Second Italo-Ethiopian War, and the Iran–Iraq War. Studies of its effects on blood cells led, paradoxically, to the development of nitrogen mustard chemotherapy drugs such as mechlorethamine.

Regulation

Sulfur mustard is listed under Schedule 1 of the Chemical Weapons Convention (1993), and declared stockpiles have been progressively destroyed under international supervision.
