Mass DNA screening

A mass DNA screening (also called a DNA dragnet) is a police investigative technique in which DNA samples are collected from a large number of people — usually men in a defined area or group — and compared with DNA recovered from a crime scene, in order to identify a suspect or eliminate volunteers from inquiry.

History and examples

The first mass screening followed the world's first forensic DNA manhunt: after the murders of Lynda Mann (1983) and Dawn Ashworth (1986) in Leicestershire, England, some 5,000 local men gave blood samples; the screening cleared the wrongly suspected Richard Buckland and led to Colin Pitchfork, the first person convicted on DNA evidence. Later large screenings include the 1998 screening in Germany after the murder of a schoolgirl, involving about 18,000 men, then the largest ever conducted.

Controversies

Participation is nominally voluntary, but critics argue that refusal attracts suspicion, making consent effectively coerced. Civil-liberties groups also raise concerns about retention of samples from innocent people and about the cost-effectiveness of dragnets, which have a mixed record of success.
