Carp are various species of oily freshwater fish of the family Cyprinidae, native to Europe and Asia. The term most often refers to the common carp (Cyprinus carpio), one of the most widely introduced and domesticated fish in the world. Carp have been raised in aquaculture for thousands of years — in China for over 2,000 years and in Europe since Roman times, when monastic pond culture spread the species across the continent.

Species

Besides the common carp, the name applies to the grass carp, silver carp, bighead carp, black carp (collectively the "Asian carps"), crucian carp, and the Indian major carps such as catla and rohu. Ornamental koi are domesticated varieties of carp (chiefly Cyprinus rubrofuscus) bred in Japan for colour and pattern.

Biology

Common carp are large, deep-bodied fish typically 40–80 cm long, with two pairs of barbels at the mouth, large scales, and a long dorsal fin. They are omnivorous bottom feeders that tolerate low oxygen and a wide range of temperatures, which contributes to their success as an introduced species. Wild fish may live 20 years or more; the oldest known koi was claimed to be over 200 years old, though this is disputed.

As food and sport

Carp is a traditional food fish across Central and Eastern Europe — a Christmas Eve staple in Poland, Czechia and Slovakia — and throughout Asia. In Britain and elsewhere it is prized by coarse anglers as a powerful sport fish and is usually released. In North America and Australia, by contrast, carp are widely regarded as invasive pests: their feeding uproots vegetation and muddies water, and large control and eradication programmes exist in the Murray–Darling Basin and the Great Lakes region.

In culture

In Chinese and Japanese symbolism the carp represents perseverance and strength, said to swim against the current and, in legend, to transform into a dragon after leaping the falls at Longmen. Carp streamers (koinobori) are flown in Japan for Children's Day.
