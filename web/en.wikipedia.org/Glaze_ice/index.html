Glaze ice

Glaze or glaze ice is a smooth, transparent, and homogeneous coating of ice that forms when freezing rain or drizzle strikes a surface whose temperature is at or below freezing. Unlike rime ice, which is milky and rough, glaze is dense and clear, and it adheres tenaciously to whatever it coats — tree branches, wires, roads, and aircraft wings.

Formation

Glaze forms when supercooled water droplets in warm air aloft fall through a shallow layer of sub-freezing air near the ground, remaining liquid until they strike a cold surface and freeze on contact. Because the droplets spread before freezing, the resulting ice is clear and bubble-free.

Effects

Glaze storms are among the most destructive forms of winter weather. Ice accumulations of only a few millimetres make roads treacherous, while heavier accretion snaps tree limbs and brings down power lines; the 1998 North American ice storm left millions without power in Canada and the northern United States. On aircraft, glaze is hazardous because it builds rapidly over large areas of the airframe and distorts airflow. On the ground, the related phenomenon on roadways is often called black ice.
