Karlheinz Stockhausen (22 August 1928 – 5 December 2007) was a German composer widely regarded as one of the most important and controversial figures in 20th-century classical music. A pioneer of electronic music, aleatory (controlled-chance) composition, and spatial music, he exerted a lasting influence on both the classical avant-garde and popular musicians from the Beatles to techno artists.

Early life and studies

Stockhausen was born in Mödrath, near Cologne. His father, a schoolteacher, was killed in the Second World War and his mother died in a mental asylum under the Nazi regime. After the war he studied piano, musicology, philosophy, and German studies in Cologne, and in 1951 attended the Darmstadt Summer Courses, where works by Messiaen and others inspired him. In Paris he studied with Olivier Messiaen and worked with Pierre Schaeffer's musique concrète studio.

Electronic and serial works

At the WDR Studio for Electronic Music in Cologne, Stockhausen composed landmark works including Studie I and II, Gesang der Jünglinge (1955–56), which fused electronically generated sounds with a boy's recorded voice, and Kontakte (1958–60). Instrumental works such as Gruppen for three orchestras (1955–57) and the piano Klavierstücke extended total serialism. In the 1960s he explored intuitive music, live electronics (Mikrophonie I, Mixtur), and vast collages such as Hymnen (1966–67), built from national anthems.

Licht

From 1977 until 2003 Stockhausen devoted himself chiefly to Licht ("Light"), a cycle of seven operas — one for each day of the week — lasting about 29 hours in total and centred on the cosmic characters Michael, Eva, and Luzifer. Parts of it, such as the Helikopter-Streichquartett (Helicopter String Quartet), in which the four players perform in separate helicopters, became emblematic of his monumental ambitions.

Legacy

Stockhausen's teaching at Darmstadt and Cologne, his publishing house, and more than 370 individually performed works shaped generations of composers. His remark describing the 9/11 attacks as a "work of art" caused widespread outrage in 2001, though he claimed his words were taken out of context. He died at his home in Kürten in 2007.
