Naiad

Naiads
Type: Water nymphs
Abode: Springs, fountains, streams, rivers
Greek: Ναϊάδες (Naiádes)
Roman equivalent: Lymphae (in part)

In Greek mythology, the Naiads (Greek Ναϊάδες) are a type of female nature spirit, or nymph, who preside over fountains, wells, springs, streams, brooks, and other bodies of fresh water. They are distinct from the river gods (Potamoi) and from the salt-water Nereids and Oceanids.

Character and cult

Naiads were thought to inhabit the waters they personified and to be essential to their fertility and purity; if a naiad's spring dried up, she could die. They were worshipped locally with offerings of flowers, oil, and milk, and were credited with prophetic and healing powers — sick people sometimes drank or bathed in their waters. Naiads could also be dangerous: the beautiful naiads of Mysia drew Heracles' companion Hylas into their spring, and jealous naiads in myth drowned or maddened mortals who spurned them.

Types and examples

Subtypes were named for their habitats: Crinaeae (fountains), Pegaeae (springs), Potameides (rivers), Limnades (lakes), and Eleionomae (marshes). Famous individual naiads include Salmacis, whose waters merged her with Hermaphroditus, and the Cretan Britomartis. The naiads were an enduring subject in classical sculpture and later European painting, typically shown as beautiful young women reclining by urns of flowing water. See also Nymph.
