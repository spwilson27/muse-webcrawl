Angular resolution

[Image placeholder]

Angular resolution describes the ability of any image-forming device such as an optical or radio telescope, a microscope, a camera, or an eye, to distinguish small details of an object, thereby making it a major determinant of image resolution. It is used in optics applied to light waves, in antenna theory applied to radio waves, and in acoustics applied to sound waves.

The Rayleigh criterion

The closely related term spatial resolution refers to the precision of a measurement with respect to space. In scientific and engineering contexts, the resolving power of an optical system is conventionally quantified by the Rayleigh criterion: two point sources are regarded as just resolved when the centre of the diffraction pattern (the Airy disk) of one falls on the first minimum of the other. For a circular aperture of diameter D observing light of wavelength λ, the limiting angular resolution is:

θ ≈ 1.22 λ / D

where θ is in radians. The formula shows why larger apertures give sharper images, and why radio telescopes, observing at long wavelengths, must be very large or combined as interferometers to achieve good resolution.

Examples

The unaided human eye, with a pupil of about 5 mm in bright light, has an angular resolution of roughly one arcminute, corresponding to visual acuity of about 20/20. The Hubble Space Telescope achieves about 0.05 arcseconds in visible light. Interferometers such as the Very Large Array and the Event Horizon Telescope synthesise apertures thousands of kilometres across, reaching resolutions of tens of microarcseconds — sufficient to image the shadow of a supermassive black hole.
