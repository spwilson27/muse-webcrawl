Land Ordinance of 1785

The Land Ordinance of 1785 was adopted by the United States Congress of the Confederation on May 20, 1785. It set up a standardised system whereby settlers could purchase title to undeveloped land in the West. The ordinance created the rectangular survey system — the basis of the Public Land Survey System — dividing land into townships and sections.

Provisions

Before sale, land was to be surveyed into townships six miles square, each divided into 36 sections of one square mile (640 acres). Sections were numbered, and section 16 in each township was reserved for the maintenance of public schools — a foundation of American land-grant education. Other sections were reserved for federal purposes, including future sale to raise revenue.

Implementation

Surveying began at the Point of Beginning where Ohio, Pennsylvania and Virginia (now West Virginia) met, under Geographer Thomas Hutchins. The first surveyed area was the Seven Ranges of Ohio. The minimum price was set at one dollar per acre, with a minimum purchase of a full section.

Significance

The grid imposed by the ordinance still shapes the landscape, roads and field patterns of much of the Midwest and West. Together with the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, which governed territorial government, it structured American westward expansion — and the dispossession of Native American peoples from the surveyed lands.
