Contact improvisation is a form of improvised partner dancing that involves the exploration of one's body in relationship to others by using the fundamentals of sharing weight, touch, and movement awareness. It was developed by the American choreographer Steve Paxton, whose 1972 performance Magnesium with students at Oberlin College is usually taken as its starting point. Dancers move together through rolling points of contact, giving and taking weight, falling, lifting, and following momentum without set steps or music-imposed rhythm.

Practice

Contact improvisation is practised in "jams" — open sessions with no designated leader — as well as in classes and festivals worldwide. Central skills include the "small dance" of standing, rolling across the floor and over a partner's body, counterbalancing, and safely giving full weight. Paxton and early collaborators such as Nancy Stark Smith, who founded the journal Contact Quarterly, framed the form as both an art sport and a social, egalitarian practice open to all bodies.

Influence

The form profoundly influenced postmodern dance and contemporary choreography, feeding into the work of companies and choreographers internationally. It also generated debate: its informal jam culture has prompted ongoing discussion of consent, touch, and safety within the community, leading many organizations to adopt explicit guidelines.
