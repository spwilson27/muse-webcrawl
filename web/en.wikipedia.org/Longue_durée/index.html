Longue durée

The longue durée (French for "long duration") is an approach to historical study developed by the French Annales School of historians, most famously Fernand Braudel. It prioritizes slow-moving, long-term historical structures — geography, climate, demography, mentalities, and economic systems — over short-term events (événements) or medium-term cycles (conjonctures).

Origins

Braudel introduced the concept in his 1949 work The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II, which organized history into three temporal layers: the almost motionless history of humanity's relationship with the environment (the longue durée), the slowly changing history of social and economic structures, and the rapid history of events, which he compared to surface foam on deep ocean currents. He elaborated the theory in his 1958 essay "Histoire et sciences sociales: La longue durée".

Influence

The longue durée reshaped twentieth-century historiography, encouraging historians to draw on geography, archaeology, economics, and anthropology, and to study material life and everyday structures rather than only political and military narratives. The approach influenced world-systems theory and remains a reference point in debates about historical time scales.
