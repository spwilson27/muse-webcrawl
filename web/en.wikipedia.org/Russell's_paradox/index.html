In set theory, Russell's paradox (also known as Russell's antinomy) is a paradox discovered by the British philosopher and mathematician Bertrand Russell in 1901. It shows that every set theory that includes an unrestricted comprehension principle leads to contradictions.

The paradox

Consider the set R of all sets that are not members of themselves: R = {x | x ∉ x}. If R is a member of itself, then by its own definition it is not a member of itself. If R is not a member of itself, then by the same definition it is a member of itself. Either way a contradiction follows, so the "set of all sets not containing themselves" cannot exist in a consistent theory that permits its formation.

Impact

Russell communicated the paradox to Gottlob Frege in a letter of 16 June 1902, just as the second volume of Frege's Grundgesetze der Arithmetik was in press; Frege appended a famous acknowledgement that the discovery undermined his system. Ernst Zermelo had discovered the same antinomy independently but did not publish it.

Responses

Solutions include Russell's own theory of types, which stratifies sets so a set can never contain itself, and Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, whose axiom of separation restricts comprehension to subsets of already-given sets. The popular "barber paradox" — a barber who shaves exactly those who do not shave themselves — is a widely used informal illustration.
