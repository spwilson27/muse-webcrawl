Transhumance

A flock being moved along a traditional drove road to high summer pasture.

Transhumance is a type of pastoralism or nomadism, a seasonal movement of livestock between fixed summer and winter pastures. In montane regions (vertical transhumance), it implies movement between higher pastures in summer and lower valleys in winter; herders have a permanent home, typically in the valleys. In horizontal transhumance, movement occurs between pastures at similar altitudes over longer distances across plains and steppes.

Unlike fully nomadic pastoralism, transhumant communities have fixed settlements, and only the herders and livestock move, traditionally on foot along drove roads known variously as cañadas in Spain, tratturi in Italy, and drailles in France.

Practice worldwide

Transhumance has been practised for millennia around the Mediterranean, in the Alps, Pyrenees, Balkans, Anatolia, the Caucasus, Iran, and the highlands of East Africa and Asia. In Spain, the Mesta—the powerful association of migratory sheep owners chartered in 1273—drove millions of merino sheep along a protected network of cañadas reales. In the Alps, cattle are still ceremonially driven up to alpine pastures (Alpaufzug) in spring and down (Alpabzug) in autumn.

Benefits and decline

Transhumance exploits seasonal grazing resources that cannot be used year-round, produces high-quality meat, milk, and cheese, and maintains open landscapes of high biodiversity: many semi-natural grasslands depend on continued grazing. The practice has declined sharply since the mid-20th century due to motorised transport, sedentarisation policies, land enclosure, and the economics of intensive livestock farming. In 2019, UNESCO inscribed transhumance on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity for several Mediterranean and Alpine countries.
