Ionia was an ancient region on the western coast of Anatolia, in present-day Turkey, north of the Maeander River and south of the Hermus. It was inhabited by the Ionians, one of the principal divisions of the ancient Greeks, who founded a group of prosperous city-states there beginning around the 11th–10th centuries BC.

The Ionian cities

Twelve cities formed the Ionian League, a religious and cultural confederation centred on the Panionion sanctuary: Miletus, Myus, Priene, Ephesus, Colophon, Lebedos, Teos, Clazomenae, Erythrae and Phocaea on the mainland, plus the island cities of Samos and Chios. Smyrna, originally Aeolian, later joined. The cities prospered through trade and founded colonies around the Mediterranean and Black Sea; Phocaea founded Massalia (Marseille), and Miletus planted dozens of colonies.

Culture and thought

Ionia was the birthplace of early Greek philosophy and rational inquiry. Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes of Miletus sought natural explanations of the world; Heraclitus taught at Ephesus, and the mathematician Pythagoras came from Samos. The Ionian dialect became the standard language of early Greek prose, including the works of Herodotus.

History

The Ionian cities fell under Lydian and then Persian rule in the 6th century BC. Their revolt against Persia in 499–494 BC — the Ionian Revolt — helped trigger the Greco-Persian Wars. After the Greek victories the cities joined the Athenian-led Delian League. In later centuries Ionia passed through Hellenistic kingdoms and became part of the Roman province of Asia; Ephesus grew into one of the largest cities of the Roman world.
