Saccharometer

A saccharometer is an instrument used to measure the concentration of sugar in a solution, most commonly in brewing and in the sugar industry. It is a type of hydrometer: a sealed, weighted glass float with a graduated stem that floats in the liquid, sinking to a depth determined by the liquid's density. Because dissolved sugar increases the density of a solution, the reading on the scale indicates the sugar content directly.

Use in brewing

In brewing, the saccharometer measures the specific gravity of wort before fermentation (original gravity) and of beer afterwards (final gravity); the difference allows the brewer to estimate the alcohol content produced. Thomas Thomson's saccharometer, introduced in the early 19th century and adopted by British excise authorities, was widely used to assess beer for taxation. Scales used with the instrument include Brix, Plato, and Balling, all of which express sugar concentration by weight.

Principle

The device works by Archimedes' principle of buoyancy. Readings are temperature-dependent, so saccharometers are calibrated for a reference temperature (commonly 20 °C) and correction tables are used when the sample differs. Modern alternatives include refractometers and digital density meters, but the glass saccharometer remains common in small-scale brewing and winemaking.
