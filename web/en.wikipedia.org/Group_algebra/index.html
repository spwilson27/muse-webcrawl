In mathematics, the group algebra of a group G over a field K, denoted K[G], is the vector space of all formal finite linear combinations of elements of G with coefficients in K, equipped with a multiplication that extends the group operation linearly. Modules over the group algebra correspond exactly to linear representations of G, so the group algebra provides the central algebraic tool of representation theory: studying a group through its group algebra lets one bring the techniques of linear algebra and ring theory to bear on group-theoretic questions.

Definition

An element of K[G] is a formal sum Σg∈G ag g with ag ∈ K and only finitely many ag nonzero. Addition is componentwise; multiplication is defined by (Σ ag g)(Σ bh h) = Σg,h ag bh (gh), extending the product in G.

When G is finite, the elements of G form a basis, so K[G] is a finite-dimensional algebra of dimension |G|. The construction generalises to a group ring over an arbitrary ring; for infinite groups in analysis, one instead considers completions such as the ℓ¹ group algebra or the group C*-algebra.

Examples

  • For the trivial group, K[G] ≅ K.
  • For the infinite cyclic group, the group algebra is the ring of Laurent polynomials K[x, x−1].
  • For a finite cyclic group of order n, ℂ[G] ≅ ℂ[x]/(xn − 1), which by the theory of roots of unity splits as a direct sum of n copies of ℂ — corresponding to the n one-dimensional representations.
  • The group algebra of the Klein four-group over ℝ decomposes into four copies of ℝ, while the quaternion group yields a copy of the quaternions ℍ among its components.

Structure theory

By Maschke's theorem, if G is finite and the characteristic of K does not divide |G|, then K[G] is semisimple; by the Artin–Wedderburn theorem it is then a direct sum of matrix algebras over division rings, and its irreducible modules are precisely the irreducible representations of G. When the characteristic divides |G| (the modular case), the algebra is no longer semisimple and its study — modular representation theory — is substantially harder. The centre of the group algebra has a basis given by conjugacy-class sums, and its dimension equals the number of conjugacy classes, hence the number of irreducible complex representations.

Applications

Group algebras underlie character theory, block theory, and the use of representations in physics and chemistry; in probability, random walks on groups are analysed through convolution in the group algebra; and the Kaplansky zero-divisor and unit conjectures — the latter disproved in 2021 by Giles Gardam for group rings over 𝔽₂ — concern the structure of group rings of torsion-free groups.
