Norse mythology is the body of myths belonging to the North Germanic peoples, stemming from Old Norse religion and continuing after the Christianisation of Scandinavia into modern folklore. The mythology is principally preserved in Old Norse literature composed in Iceland during the Middle Ages, above all the Poetic Edda, a compilation of anonymous poems, and the Prose Edda, written in the 13th century by the Icelandic scholar Snorri Sturluson.

Sources

Most surviving texts were written down in Iceland in the 13th century, two centuries or more after the conversion to Christianity, though the poems and skaldic verses they preserve are in part much older. Additional evidence comes from runic inscriptions, picture stones, place names, and the accounts of outside observers such as Tacitus and Adam of Bremen. Because the material was recorded by Christian authors, the degree to which it faithfully represents pre-Christian belief is debated by scholars.

Cosmology

At the centre of the Norse cosmos stands Yggdrasil, a gigantic ash tree whose branches and roots connect the various realms of gods, humans, giants, and the dead. The cosmos originated from the primordial void Ginnungagap, between the fiery realm of Muspell and the icy realm of Niflheim; from their meeting arose the first giant, Ymir, from whose dismembered body the gods fashioned the world. Humans, Ask and Embla, were created from driftwood given life by Odin and his brothers.

Deities

The gods fall chiefly into two groupings: the Æsir, associated with war and governance, and the Vanir, associated with fertility and prosperity, who fought a war in the distant past before uniting. Chief among the Æsir is Odin, the one-eyed seeker of wisdom who hung upon Yggdrasil and sacrificed an eye for knowledge. His son Thor, wielder of the hammer Mjölnir, defends the world against giants. Other major figures include the goddess Freyja of the Vanir, the watchman Heimdall, the doomed god Baldr, and the ambiguous trickster Loki, whose children — the wolf Fenrir, the serpent Jörmungandr, and Hel — are fated to oppose the gods.

Ragnarök

Norse myth is distinguished by its eschatology: the gods know in advance that they will face destruction at Ragnarök, a catastrophic final battle preceded by the Fimbulvetr, a winter of three years without summer. Odin will be devoured by Fenrir, Thor will slay Jörmungandr and fall to its venom, and the world will sink beneath the sea — after which a renewed earth rises, inhabited by a surviving pair of humans and a new generation of gods.

Legacy

Norse mythology has profoundly influenced modern literature and popular culture, from Wagner's Ring cycle and Tolkien's legendarium to contemporary comics, film, and video games. Days of the week in English — Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday — preserve the names of the gods Týr, Odin, Thor, and Frigg.
