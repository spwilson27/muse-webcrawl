A university is an institution of higher education and research which awards academic degrees in several academic disciplines. Universities typically offer both undergraduate and postgraduate programs, and the word derives from the Latin universitas magistrorum et scholarium, meaning "community of teachers and scholars".

History

The modern university has its origins in the medieval European institutions that emerged in the 11th and 12th centuries, notably Bologna (1088), Paris, and Oxford. These grew out of cathedral schools and guilds of masters and students, and were granted charters by popes and monarchs. Older centers of learning existed elsewhere — such as al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (859), al-Azhar in Cairo, and Nalanda in India — though their institutional forms differed from the European corporate model. During the 19th century the German research university model associated with Wilhelm von Humboldt, combining teaching with research, spread worldwide.

Organization

Universities are usually divided into faculties, schools, or colleges corresponding to academic disciplines, and departments within them. Governance typically involves a chancellor or president, academic senates, and governing boards. Funding comes from tuition, government appropriations, endowments, and research grants; universities may be public or private.

Degrees and study

Undergraduate study usually leads to a bachelor's degree; postgraduate study leads to master's degrees and doctorates such as the PhD. Admission, curricula, and quality assurance are regulated by accreditation systems that vary by country.

Global presence

There are tens of thousands of universities worldwide. Higher education participation has expanded dramatically since the mid-20th century, and universities play central roles in research, professional training, and public life. International rankings and cross-border student mobility are prominent features of the modern system.
