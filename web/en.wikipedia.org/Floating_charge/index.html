Floating charge

A floating charge is a security interest over a fund of changing assets (e.g. stocks and inventory) of a company or other artificial person. Unlike a fixed charge, which attaches to a specific asset, a floating charge "hovers" over a class of assets that the company may use and dispose of in the ordinary course of business. On the occurrence of certain events — typically insolvency proceedings or default — the charge crystallises and becomes a fixed charge over the assets then held.

Origins and use

The floating charge was developed by the English courts of equity in the nineteenth century, notably in cases such as Holroyd v Marshall (1862) and Re Panama, New Zealand and Australian Royal Mail Co (1870). It became a staple of corporate lending, commonly combined in a single debenture with fixed charges over specific property, and allowed companies to borrow against their whole undertaking while continuing to trade.

Crystallisation

Crystallisation occurs on liquidation, the appointment of a receiver or administrator, cessation of business, or any event specified in the charge instrument (automatic crystallisation clauses). Upon crystallisation the company may no longer deal freely with the charged assets.

Priority and criticism

In insolvency, floating charge holders rank behind fixed charge holders and preferential creditors, and in England and Wales a portion of floating-charge realisations (the "prescribed part") is set aside for unsecured creditors. The device's breadth has attracted criticism that it leaves too little for ordinary creditors, prompting reforms such as the restrictions on administrative receivership in the Enterprise Act 2002.
