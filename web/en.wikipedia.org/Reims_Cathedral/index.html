Reims Cathedral

Reims Cathedral
Location: Reims, France
Style: High Gothic
Construction: 1211 – c. 1275
Designated UNESCO World Heritage Site (1991)

Reims Cathedral (French: Cathédrale Notre-Dame de Reims) is a Roman Catholic cathedral in Reims, France, built in the High Gothic style. It was the traditional coronation church of the kings of France, commemorating the baptism of Clovis I by Bishop Remigius around 496, traditionally held to have taken place on the site.

History

The present building was begun in 1211 after a fire destroyed the earlier Carolingian and Romanesque church. Master builders including Jean d'Orbais and Bernard of Soissons gave the cathedral its celebrated west front, decorated with more than 2,300 statues, and its luminous nave. Twenty-five royal coronations were held here, most famously that of Charles VII in 1429, attended by Joan of Arc.

The cathedral was severely damaged by German shelling in the First World War — the image of the burning cathedral became an emblem of cultural loss — and was restored over decades, aided by donations including from the Rockefeller family. Its modern stained glass includes windows by Marc Chagall (1974). Reims Cathedral, the former Abbey of Saint-Rémi and the Palace of Tau were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1991.
