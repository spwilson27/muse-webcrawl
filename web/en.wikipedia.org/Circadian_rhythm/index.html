Circadian rhythm

A circadian rhythm (from Latin circa, "around", and diem, "day") is a natural, internal process that regulates the sleep-wake cycle and other physiological functions, repeating roughly every 24 hours. Circadian rhythms are endogenous — generated within the organism — but are entrained to the local environment by external cues called zeitgebers, of which light is the most important.

Mechanism

In mammals, the master clock resides in the suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus, which receives light input directly from the retina. At the molecular level, circadian rhythms are generated by transcription-translation feedback loops of clock genes, including PER, CRY, CLOCK, and BMAL1. Peripheral clocks in nearly every tissue are synchronized by the central clock. The 2017 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash, and Michael Young for elucidating this mechanism in fruit flies.

Physiology

Circadian rhythms govern sleep, body temperature, hormone secretion (such as melatonin and cortisol), blood pressure, metabolism, and cognitive performance. The human free-running period averages slightly over 24 hours — about 24.2 hours — so daily light exposure is required to keep the clock aligned with the solar day.

Disruption and health

Disruption of circadian rhythms through jet lag, shift work, or irregular light exposure is associated with sleep disorders, metabolic syndrome, mood disorders, and increased cancer risk. Circadian rhythm sleep disorders include delayed sleep phase disorder and non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder. Chronotherapy and timed light exposure are used to treat several of these conditions.
