Shakespearean comedy

In the First Folio, the plays of William Shakespeare were categorised into three genres: comedy, history and tragedy. Shakespearean comedy comprises the fourteen plays listed as comedies in the Folio, although modern scholars sometimes dispute the boundaries of the category — The Tempest, for instance, is now often classed among the late romances, and Troilus and Cressida was listed neither with the comedies nor, unambiguously, with the tragedies.

Characteristics

Although "comedy" in Shakespeare's time did not necessarily mean a humorous play, the Shakespearean comedies share a set of common features:

Happy endings, usually involving marriage. Where tragedy ends in death, comedy ends in union: As You Like It concludes with four weddings, Twelfth Night with three couples. Mistaken identity and disguise drive the plots — heroines such as Rosalind, Viola and Portia dress as men, generating confusion and self-discovery. Multiple interwoven plots mirror and comment on one another, and clever wordplay, puns and comic prose servants (the "clowns" Touchstone, Feste and the gravediggers' cousins in comedy) set high wit against low humour. Many comedies move from a restrictive court or city to a liberating natural world — the Forest of Arden, the woods outside Athens — a pattern the critic Northrop Frye called the "green world", before returning to society transformed.

The plays

The Folio comedies are: The Tempest, The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Merry Wives of Windsor, Measure for Measure, The Comedy of Errors, Much Ado About Nothing, Love's Labour's Lost, A Midsummer Night's Dream, The Merchant of Venice, As You Like It, The Taming of the Shrew, All's Well That Ends Well, Twelfth Night and The Winter's Tale. Cymbeline, though listed among the tragedies in the Folio, is also often grouped with the late romances.

The problem plays

The critic F. S. Boas coined the term "problem plays" for All's Well That Ends Well, Measure for Measure and Troilus and Cressida: comedies by structure whose tone is bitter, cynical or morally unresolved — marriages are forced, forgiveness is uneasy, and virtue goes unrewarded. Modern criticism reads them as evidence that Shakespeare was stretching the comic form to its limits in the years around 1600.

Influence

The Shakespearean comedy established conventions — the witty heroine, the separated lovers, the festive conclusion — that run through Restoration comedy, the novels of Jane Austen, and modern romantic comedy in film and theatre.
