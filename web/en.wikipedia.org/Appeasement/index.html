Appeasement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Chamberlain returning from Munich, 1938

Appeasement, in an international context, is a diplomatic policy of making political, material, or territorial concessions to an aggressive power in order to avoid conflict. The term is most often applied to the foreign policy of the British governments of Prime Ministers Ramsay MacDonald (in office 1929–1935), Stanley Baldwin (in office 1935–1937) and (most notably) Neville Chamberlain (in office 1937–1940) towards Nazi Germany (from 1933) and Fascist Italy (from 1935) between 1935 and 1939.

Under British pressure, appeasement of Nazism and Fascism also played a role in French foreign policy of the period, but was always much less popular there than in the United Kingdom.

The Munich Agreement

The policy's defining episode was the Munich Agreement of September 1938, in which Britain and France accepted the German annexation of the Sudetenland from Czechoslovakia without Czech participation. Chamberlain returned home proclaiming "peace for our time". Germany occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939, and Britain and France responded with guarantees to Poland; when Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, they declared war, and appeasement was abandoned.

Motives and judgement

Appeasement rested on several motives: horror of another world war among a public scarred by 1914–1918, belief that some German grievances against the Treaty of Versailles were legitimate, military unreadiness, economic constraints, and hope that concessions would satisfy Hitler's ambitions. The wartime polemic Guilty Men (1940) fixed a negative verdict, but later historians have debated how far the policy bought time for rearmament and whether alternatives were realistically available.

"Appeasement" has since become a pejorative term in political debate, invoked — often controversially — against negotiations with aggressive states, and the "lessons of Munich" remain a recurring argument in foreign policy.
