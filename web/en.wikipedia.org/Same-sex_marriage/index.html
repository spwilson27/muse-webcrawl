Same-sex marriage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legal recognition of same-sex marriage worldwide

Same-sex marriage, also known as gay marriage, is the marriage of two people of the same legal sex. As of the mid-2020s, marriage between same-sex couples is legally performed and recognised in more than 35 countries, with the Netherlands (2001) the first to extend marriage to same-sex couples by legislation. Legalisation has come about by court decisions, legislative votes, and popular referendums, most prominently the United States Supreme Court ruling in Obergefell v. Hodges (2015), which guaranteed the right nationwide.

History

Denmark introduced the world's first registered partnerships in 1989, a stepping stone in many European states. The Netherlands opened civil marriage in 2001, followed by Belgium (2003), Spain and Canada (2005), and South Africa (2006). Ireland in 2015 became the first country to legalise same-sex marriage by popular referendum. Taiwan in 2019 was the first place in Asia, and Thailand followed in 2025.

Debate

Supporters argue from equality, civil rights, and the legal and social benefits of marriage, pointing to research consensus that children raised by same-sex couples fare as well as those of opposite-sex couples. Opponents have argued from religious doctrine, tradition, and procreation-based definitions of marriage; public opinion in most Western countries has shifted steadily toward support over recent decades.

Recognition

Recognition varies widely: some jurisdictions offer civil unions or registered partnerships instead; others do not recognise same-sex unions performed elsewhere, and a number criminalise homosexuality itself. The trend since 2000 has nonetheless been toward broader recognition, especially in Europe and the Americas.
