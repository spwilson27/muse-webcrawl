English contract law

English contract law is the body of law that regulates legally binding agreements in England and Wales. With its genesis in the lex mercatoria and the activism of the judiciary during the industrial revolution, it has been a model for contract law around the world, particularly in Commonwealth jurisdictions. Broadly, English contract law follows the principle that parties are free to bargain and that agreements should be kept (pacta sunt servanda), tempered by statutory protection — especially for consumers and employees.

Formation

A contract generally requires an offer, acceptance, consideration (something of value exchanged), and an intention to create legal relations. The objective test asks what a reasonable person would understand the parties to have meant. Leading cases include Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893], which established that an advertisement can be a unilateral offer to the world, and Entores v Miles Far East on the timing of acceptance of instantaneous communications.

Terms and interpretation

Terms may be express or implied by fact, law or custom. Courts classify terms as conditions, warranties or innominate terms, with different consequences for breach. Exclusion clauses are controlled at common law and by statute, notably the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 and, for consumers, the Consumer Rights Act 2015.

Vitiating factors

Contracts may be void or voidable for mistake, misrepresentation, duress, undue influence or illegality. The doctrine of frustration, developed in cases such as Taylor v Caldwell (1863), discharges a contract when a supervening event makes performance impossible or radically different.

Remedies

The normal remedy for breach is damages, assessed on the principles of Hadley v Baxendale (1854) concerning remoteness. Equitable remedies — specific performance and injunction — are discretionary and exceptional. Parties may agree liquidated damages clauses, subject to the rule against penalties restated in Cavendish Square Holding v Makdessi [2015].
