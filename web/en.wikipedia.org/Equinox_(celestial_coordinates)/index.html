Equinox (celestial coordinates)

The March equinox point defines the origin of right ascension in the equatorial coordinate system.

In astronomy, an equinox is either of two places on the celestial sphere at which the ecliptic intersects the celestial equator. Because of precession and nutation, the positions of the equinoxes shift slowly over time, so a celestial coordinate system must specify the date for which the equinox is defined — the epoch of the coordinates. A coordinate pair given without qualification is therefore incomplete; catalogues state positions "at equinox J2000.0" or "at equinox B1950.0", for example.

Usage

The March (vernal) equinox defines zero right ascension: the hour circle through that point is the origin from which right ascension is measured eastward. Star catalogues and atlases are published for a standard equinox and epoch. Observers converting catalogue coordinates to the sky of the current date apply precession (and, for precise work, nutation) corrections, a process now usually handled by telescope control software and astrometry libraries.

Standard equinoxes

The most common standard equinoxes are B1950.0 (the Besselian epoch at the start of 1950, used by older catalogues and radio astronomy charts) and J2000.0 (the Julian epoch 2000, noon Terrestrial Time on 1 January 2000), which is the present standard for most catalogues such as the Hipparcos and Gaia catalogues and for the International Celestial Reference System. The difference between B1950 and J2000 coordinates for the same object can amount to a degree or more, so mixing equinoxes is a classic source of pointing errors.

Mean and apparent equinox

Astronomers distinguish the mean equinox, which accounts only for the smooth motion of precession, from the apparent or true equinox, which also includes the short-period wobble of nutation. Coordinates "of date" use the apparent equinox of the observation date.
