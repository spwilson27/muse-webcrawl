Synoptic problem

Synoptic problem
FieldBiblical studies, textual criticism
Texts involvedGospels of Matthew, Mark, and Luke
Leading solutionTwo-source hypothesis (Mark + Q)

The Synoptic problem is the question of the specific relationships among the three Synoptic Gospels — Matthew, Mark, and Luke. These gospels share much of their content, including long stretches of nearly identical wording, yet also differ in wording, order, and material. The problem is to explain this pattern: the sources the evangelists used, the order in which they wrote, and how they edited their materials.

Evidence

Scholars note the "triple tradition" (material in all three), the "double tradition" (material shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark), and material unique to Matthew ("M") or Luke ("L"). Almost all of Mark appears in Matthew, and much in Luke, often in the same order — strong evidence of literary dependence rather than independent reliance on oral tradition alone.

Proposed solutions

  • Two-source hypothesis (the majority view): Mark was written first and used by both Matthew and Luke, who independently supplemented Mark with a hypothetical sayings source, Q (from German Quelle, "source").
  • Farrer hypothesis: Mark first, then Matthew, then Luke using both — making Q unnecessary.
  • Two-gospel hypothesis: Matthew first, then Luke, with Mark as a conflation of the two.
  • Augustinian hypothesis: the traditional order Matthew, Mark, Luke, with each later evangelist using the earlier ones.

Significance

Resolving the Synoptic problem shapes how scholars read each gospel's theology (redaction criticism), reconstruct Q and the earliest Jesus traditions, and assess the historical reliability of the gospels' accounts.
