Hokusai


The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c. 1831), Hokusai's most famous print

Katsushika Hokusai (Japanese: 葛飾 北斎; c. 31 October 1760 – 10 May 1849) was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist of the Edo period, celebrated as the author of the woodblock print series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, which includes the iconic The Great Wave off Kanagawa. Active for over seven decades, Hokusai produced an estimated 30,000 works — prints, paintings, picture books and drawing manuals — and changed his art name more than thirty times. His work profoundly influenced European artists of the Japonisme movement, including Van Gogh, Monet and Degas.

Life

Hokusai was born in Katsushika, Edo (now Tokyo), possibly the son of a shogunal mirror-maker. He began painting around age six, worked in a bookshop and woodblock carver's workshop, and at eighteen entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō, master of actor prints. Expelled after his teacher's death for studying rival schools, he endured poverty, working as a peddler and picture-book illustrator under names such as Shunrō and Sōri. From about 1800 the name Hokusai became established. A public drawing spectacle in 1804, in which he painted a gigantic portrait of Daruma, made his reputation. He moved house compulsively (by some accounts 93 times) and was struck by lightning in his fifties. His daughter Katsushika Ōi was also a noted artist.

Major works

Between 1814 and 1878 the fifteen volumes of the Hokusai Manga — thousands of sketches of people, animals, landscapes and the supernatural — taught generations of artists and remain a landmark of Japanese drawing. His landscape masterpieces came late: Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (c. 1830–32), made when he was about seventy, used imported Prussian blue pigment to striking effect; The Great Wave off Kanagawa shows boats struggling beneath a towering wave with Fuji small in the distance. Other major series include A Tour of the Waterfalls of the Provinces and Thirty-six Views successors by his great rival Hiroshige, whose fame Hokusai's series helped create.

Old age and legacy

Hokusai signed late works "Gakyō Rōjin Manji" — "the old man mad about art". A studio fire in 1839 destroyed much of his work, yet he continued painting until his death in 1849 at about eighty-eight, reportedly lamenting that with ten more years he might have become "a real painter". His prints were exhibited in Paris from the 1860s and collected avidly in the West; the Great Wave became one of the most reproduced images in the world, and Hokusai the only Japanese artist ranked in some lists of the most influential artists of the second millennium. The Sumida Hokusai Museum opened in Tokyo in 2016 near his birthplace.
