Woolly mammoth

The woolly mammoth (Mammuthus primigenius) is an extinct species of mammoth that lived during the Pleistocene epoch across northern Eurasia and North America. It is among the best-known prehistoric animals, thanks to frozen carcasses preserved in Siberian permafrost, abundant skeletal remains, and depictions in Ice Age cave art.

Description

Roughly the size of a modern African elephant, the woolly mammoth stood about 2.7 to 3.4 metres at the shoulder. It was covered in a double coat of long guard hairs and dense underwool over a layer of fat, with small ears and a short tail that minimised heat loss and frostbite. Its huge tusks, up to about 4 metres long, curved strongly upward and were used for display, fighting and sweeping aside snow to reach vegetation. A high-crowned skull, a hump of fat behind the head, and highly ridged molars suited to grinding coarse grasses completed its cold-adapted form.

Extinction

Most mainland populations died out around 10,000 years ago as the climate warmed and the mammoth steppe shrank, with human hunting a debated contributing factor. Isolated island populations survived much longer: mammoths on St. Paul Island in Alaska persisted until about 5,600 years ago, and those on Wrangel Island in the Arctic Ocean until roughly 4,000 years ago — within the era of the Egyptian pyramids. Ancient DNA studies have reconstructed the species' genome, fuelling discussion of "de-extinction" and mammoth-like cold-resistant elephants.
