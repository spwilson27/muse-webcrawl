Monophyly

Monophyly is a property of a group of organisms (a taxon) that consists of a common ancestor and all of its descendants. Such a group is called monophyletic, or a clade. Monophyly is the central criterion of modern phylogenetic systematics (cladistics), founded by Willi Hennig, which holds that formal taxa should correspond to complete branches of the tree of life.

Definitions

A monophyletic group is defined by shared derived characteristics, or synapomorphies, inherited from the group's most recent common ancestor. Two related concepts are:

  • Paraphyly: a group containing a common ancestor but only some of its descendants — for example, "reptiles" when birds (descended from dinosaurs) are excluded.
  • Polyphyly: a group whose members derive from two or more separate ancestors and do not include their most recent common ancestor — for example, "warm-blooded animals" combining birds and mammals.

Examples

Mammals (Mammalia), birds (Aves), and the flowering plants are monophyletic. Humans and chimpanzees together with their common ancestor form a clade, as do all tetrapods. By contrast, traditional groupings such as "fish" and "algae" are paraphyletic or polyphyletic and are not recognised as formal taxa in cladistic classifications.

Testing monophyly

Monophyly is tested by phylogenetic analysis of morphological or molecular data. A robustly supported clade on a phylogenetic tree, diagnosed by synapomorphies, is presumed monophyletic; hypotheses are revised as new data accumulate.
