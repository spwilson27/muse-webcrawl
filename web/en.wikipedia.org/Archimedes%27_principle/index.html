Archimedes' principle

Archimedes' principle (also spelled Archimedes's principle) states that the upward buoyant force that is exerted on a body immersed in a fluid, whether fully or partially, is equal to the weight of the fluid that the body displaces. Archimedes' principle is a law of physics fundamental to fluid mechanics. It was formulated by Archimedes of Syracuse.

The buoyant force equals the weight of the displaced fluid

Explanation

In On Floating Bodies, Archimedes suggested that any floating object displaces its own weight of fluid, and that an object wholly or partially submerged experiences an upward force equal to the weight of the displaced fluid. In modern form:

Fb = −ρ · V · g

where ρ is the fluid density, V the volume of displaced fluid, and g gravitational acceleration. If the object's weight exceeds the maximum buoyant force (when fully submerged), it sinks; if less, it floats with a fraction submerged such that the displaced fluid's weight equals its own.

The crown story

According to the famous anecdote retold by Vitruvius, Archimedes discovered the principle when asked by King Hiero II to determine whether a votive crown was pure gold or adulterated with silver. Realizing while bathing that the water level rose as he entered, he supposedly ran naked through Syracuse shouting "Eureka!" ("I have found it!"). By measuring the crown's displacement and weight, its density — and hence its composition — could be checked without damaging it.

Applications

The principle explains why ships float, governs the design of submarines and hot-air balloons (for which the "fluid" is air), underlies hydrometers used to measure liquid density, and is essential to naval architecture and offshore engineering.
