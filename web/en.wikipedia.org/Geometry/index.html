Geometry (from the Ancient Greek geōmetría, "earth measurement") is a branch of mathematics concerned with properties of space, including the distance, shape, size, and relative position of figures. One of the oldest mathematical sciences, it arose from practical needs in surveying, astronomy, and construction, and was systematised by Euclid around 300 BC in the Elements.

History

Early geometry was developed in Egypt, Mesopotamia, India, China, and Greece. Euclid's axiomatic treatment — deriving theorems about points, lines, angles, and circles from a small set of postulates — dominated the subject for two millennia. In the 17th century, René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat introduced analytic geometry, linking geometry with algebra through coordinates. The 19th century saw the discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries by Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky, and Riemann's general theory of curved spaces, which later underpinned Einstein's general theory of relativity.

Modern branches

Contemporary geometry includes differential geometry (calculus on manifolds), algebraic geometry (solutions of polynomial equations), topology (properties preserved under continuous deformation), discrete and computational geometry, and fractal geometry. Felix Klein's Erlangen program characterised geometries by their symmetry groups, a viewpoint that continues to organise the field.

Applications

Geometry is used throughout science and engineering: in physics (spacetime, crystallography), computer graphics and robotics, architecture, cartography, and data analysis.
