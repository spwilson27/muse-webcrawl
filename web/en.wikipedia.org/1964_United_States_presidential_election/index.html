The 1964 United States presidential election was the 45th quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, 3 November 1964. Incumbent Democratic President Lyndon B. Johnson, who had succeeded to the presidency following the assassination of John F. Kennedy less than a year earlier, defeated Republican Senator Barry Goldwater of Arizona in one of the most lopsided landslides in American history.

Background

Johnson, a Texan and former Senate Majority Leader, assumed office on 22 November 1963 after Kennedy's assassination in Dallas. He pledged continuity with Kennedy's programme, pushing the Civil Rights Act of 1964 through Congress and declaring a "War on Poverty". At the Democratic convention in Atlantic City he selected Senator Hubert Humphrey of Minnesota as his running mate. The Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party's challenge to the state's segregated delegation produced a nationally televised controversy.

Goldwater, a leader of the Republican Party's conservative wing, won his party's nomination at the Cow Palace in San Francisco, defeating moderate rivals Nelson Rockefeller and William Scranton. In his acceptance speech he declared that "extremism in the defense of liberty is no vice", a line that alarmed moderates and became a byword of the campaign.

Campaign

The Johnson campaign portrayed Goldwater as a dangerous extremist who might use nuclear weapons in Vietnam, an image reinforced by the famous "Daisy" advertisement, broadcast once on 7 September 1964, which showed a young girl counting petals before dissolving into a nuclear explosion. Goldwater's support for states' rights against the Civil Rights Act cost him nationally but won him the Deep South. Johnson ran on the Great Society programme and national unity.

Results

Johnson won 61.1 percent of the popular vote — the highest share since the unopposed re-election of James Monroe in 1820 — and 486 electoral votes to Goldwater's 52. Goldwater carried only Arizona and five Deep South states: Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and South Carolina. The result was a milestone in the political realignment of the South, as African American voters newly enfranchised in the region backed Johnson overwhelmingly while many white Southerners began their migration to the Republican Party.
