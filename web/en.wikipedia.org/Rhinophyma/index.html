Rhinophyma

Rhinophyma is a condition causing development of a large, bulbous nose associated with granulomatous infiltration. It is due to hypertrophy (overgrowth) of the sebaceous glands and connective tissue of the nose, and is regarded as the most severe, end-stage form of phymatous rosacea. It commonly affects older, fair-skinned men, and is much more frequent in men than women.

Rhinophyma was once incorrectly associated with alcoholism, which led to the colloquial and stigmatizing names "whiskey nose," "gin blossom," and "rum nose" — as famously applied to the actor W. C. Fields. In fact alcohol may worsen flushing in rosacea but does not cause rhinophyma; many sufferers drink little or nothing.

Signs and symptoms

The condition develops gradually over years. The nose becomes enlarged, red, and lumpy, with thickened, waxy skin, prominent dilated pores (patulous follicles), and sometimes nodules that distort the nasal contour and may obstruct the nostrils or interfere with vision in advanced cases. Telangiectasias (small dilated blood vessels) are often present across the surface. The chin (gnathophyma), ears, eyelids, and forehead can be affected by analogous but rarer phymatous changes.

Causes

Rhinophyma is the phymatous subtype of rosacea; its pathogenesis involves chronic inflammation, vascular hyper-reactivity, sebaceous gland hyperplasia, and fibrosis. The Demodex mites that inhabit hair follicles have been implicated in rosacea generally, though their precise role is debated. Not everyone with rosacea develops phymatous change, and the factors determining progression are incompletely understood.

Treatment

Early phymatous changes may be partially moderated with oral isotretinoin or other rosacea therapies, but established rhinophyma requires surgical reshaping. Techniques include scalpel sculpturing, dermabrasion, electrosurgery, cryosurgery, and laser ablation — commonly with CO2 or erbium:YAG lasers — in which excess tissue is removed down to a level that preserves the deeper pilosebaceous units, allowing the surface to re-epithelialize with acceptable scarring. Treatment results are usually good, though recurrence is possible.

Epidemiology

Rhinophyma occurs most often in men aged 50 and above of Northern European ancestry, paralleling the distribution of rosacea; reported male-to-female ratios range from about 5:1 to 30:1. The psychosocial burden can be considerable because of the visible deformity and the false public association with alcohol abuse.
