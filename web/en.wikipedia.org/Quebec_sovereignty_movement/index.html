Quebec sovereignty movement

The Quebec sovereignty movement (French: mouvement souverainiste du Québec) is a political movement whose objective is to achieve the independence of Quebec from Canada. Supporters argue that Quebecers constitute a distinct nation — predominantly French-speaking, with its own culture, history, and institutions — and should exercise self-determination. Opponents maintain that Quebec is stronger within the Canadian federation.

History

The modern movement emerged during the Quiet Revolution of the 1960s, when Quebec's government expanded rapidly and French-speaking Quebecers asserted control over their economy and institutions. René Lévesque founded the Parti Québécois (PQ) in 1968, and the Front de libération du Québec's bombing campaign culminated in the October Crisis of 1970. The PQ won the 1976 provincial election and held a referendum in 1980 on "sovereignty-association," which was defeated 60% to 40%.

The 1995 referendum

After the failures of the Meech Lake and Charlottetown accords, a second referendum was held on 30 October 1995 under Premier Jacques Parizeau and Bloc Québécois leader Lucien Bouchard. The sovereignty option lost by the narrowest of margins: 50.58% No to 49.42% Yes. The close result led the federal government to seek a Supreme Court reference on unilateral secession (1998) and to pass the Clarity Act (2000).

Later developments

Support for sovereignty declined in the 2000s and 2010s, and the PQ suffered historic defeats, but the question remains a defining axis of Quebec politics, with the Bloc Québécois representing sovereigntist interests in the federal Parliament in Ottawa.
