Birefringence

Birefringence is the optical property of a material having a refractive index that depends on the polarization and propagation direction of light. These optically anisotropic materials are said to be birefringent (or birefractive). The birefringence is often quantified as the maximum difference between refractive indices exhibited by the material. Crystals with non-cubic crystal structures are often birefringent, as are plastics under mechanical stress.

Explanation

Birefringence is responsible for the phenomenon of double refraction, whereby a ray of light, when incident upon a birefringent material, is split by polarization into two rays taking slightly different paths. This effect was first described by Danish scientist Rasmus Bartholin in 1669, who observed it in calcite. In a uniaxial material, one ray (the ordinary ray) follows Snell's law, while the other (the extraordinary ray) experiences a refractive index that varies with direction relative to the crystal's optic axis.

Sources

Birefringence may arise from the intrinsic anisotropy of a crystal lattice, from alignment of molecules in liquid crystals and stretched polymers, from mechanical stress (photoelasticity), or from applied electric and magnetic fields (Kerr and Cotton–Mouton effects). Many everyday materials, including cellophane and ice, are birefringent.

Applications

Birefringent materials are used in waveplates and polarizing optics, in liquid-crystal displays where electrically controlled birefringence modulates light, and in polarized-light microscopy for studying minerals, biological tissues and stresses in transparent materials. In geology, the interference colours of minerals between crossed polarizers are a standard diagnostic tool.
