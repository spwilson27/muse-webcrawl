Postcyberpunk

Postcyberpunk is a subgenre of science fiction that emerged in the 1990s as a successor and response to cyberpunk. Where classical cyberpunk (exemplified by William Gibson's Neuromancer, 1984) featured alienated loners and marginalised hackers struggling against dystopian corporate orders, postcyberpunk typically features protagonists who are integrated members of society — engineers, officials, parents — seeking to preserve or improve the social order, explore its complexities, or repair its faults, in worlds where advanced technology is an ordinary fact of daily life.

Characteristics

The term was promoted by critic Lawrence Person, whose "Notes Toward a Postcyberpunk Manifesto" (1998) argued that a new generation of writers had moved beyond cyberpunk's default dystopia while keeping its dense, technology-saturated world-building. Postcyberpunk settings take for granted the information revolution: ubiquitous computing, biotechnology, and nanotechnology are woven into ordinary life, and the drama arises from how people live with them rather than from rebellion against them. The tone is often less nihilistic and more interested in social and political consequences at a systemic level.

Examples

Works frequently cited as postcyberpunk include Neal Stephenson's The Diamond Age (1995) and Snow Crash (1992, transitional), Bruce Sterling's Holy Fire (1996) and Schismatrix Plus, Greg Egan's novels, Ken MacLeod's Fall Revolution sequence, and much of the later work of cyberpunk's own founders. In film and anime, Ghost in the Shell: Stand Alone Complex is often cited: its protagonists are state security professionals working within, rather than against, their society.
