Rhyme scheme

A rhyme scheme is the pattern of rhymes at the end of each line of a poem or song. It is usually referred to by using letters to indicate which lines rhyme; lines designated with the same letter all rhyme with each other.

Notation

An example of the ABAB rhyming scheme, from "To Anthea, who may Command him Anything", by Robert Herrick:

Bid me to weep, and I will weep (A) While I have eyes to see; (B) And having none, yet I will keep (A) A heart to weep for thee. (B)

In contrast, a more complicated scheme appears in the Shakespearean sonnet: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG, comprising three quatrains and a final rhyming couplet.

Common schemes

  • Alternate rhyme: ABAB — lines rhyme in alternation.
  • Couplet: AA, BB, CC — pairs of successive lines rhyme.
  • Enclosed rhyme: ABBA — the first and fourth lines rhyme, enclosing the second and third.
  • Monorhyme: AAAA — every line rhymes with every other.
  • Chain rhyme: schemes such as ABA BCB CDC, used in terza rima.

Function

Rhyme schemes impose structure on verse, aid memorisation, and create expectations in the reader that the poet may fulfil or subvert. Fixed forms such as the villanelle, rondeau and ballade depend on strict, repeating rhyme schemes.
