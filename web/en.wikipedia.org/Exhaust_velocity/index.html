Exhaust velocity

Exhaust velocity, in the study of rocket propulsion, is the speed at which exhaust gases leave a rocket engine's nozzle relative to the rocket. It is a key measure of engine efficiency: the higher the exhaust velocity, the more thrust is produced per unit of propellant consumed.

Relation to specific impulse

Exhaust velocity is closely related to specific impulse. When specific impulse is expressed as a time (in seconds), multiplying it by standard gravity (g0 ≈ 9.80665 m/s²) yields the effective exhaust velocity, which accounts for pressure effects at the nozzle exit: ve = Isp × g0. The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, Δv = ve ln(m0/mf), shows that a vehicle's achievable change in velocity grows linearly with exhaust velocity but only logarithmically with mass ratio — which is why high exhaust velocity is so valuable in spaceflight.

Typical values

  • Solid rocket propellants: ~2.0–2.8 km/s
  • Liquid kerosene/oxygen engines: ~3.0–3.4 km/s
  • Liquid hydrogen/oxygen engines: ~4.4 km/s (e.g., the RS-25 Space Shuttle main engine)
  • Ion thrusters: 20–50 km/s, but with extremely low thrust

Chemical engines are limited by the energy available from chemical bonds and by the temperature their combustion chambers and nozzles can withstand, which is why electric propulsion systems, though feeble in thrust, are preferred for missions requiring very large total velocity changes.
