Charles François du Périer Dumouriez (26 January 1739 – 14 March 1823) was a French general who, together with Kellermann, won the Battle of Valmy (1792) and then conquered the Austrian Netherlands at Jemappes. In 1793, following defeat at Neerwinden, he defected to the Austrians — the most sensational betrayal of the early French Revolutionary Wars.

Early career

Dumouriez joined the army at eighteen and fought in the Seven Years' War, where he was wounded and captured at Rossbach while serving as a volunteer. He later undertook confidential missions for the French crown in Corsica, Poland (advising the Bar Confederation), and Spain, and spent years in semi-disgrace, including imprisonment in the Bastille on suspicion of misusing funds, before Louis XVI recalled and promoted him. At the Revolution he joined the Jacobin Club, cultivated the Girondins, and served briefly as Minister of Foreign Affairs in 1792, in which role he pushed for the declaration of war on Austria.

1792 campaigns

Given command of the Army of the North, Dumouriez invaded the Austrian Netherlands but was hurriedly recalled when the Prussian army advanced on Paris. Joining Kellermann, he manoeuvred into the Argonne — the "Thermopylae of France" — and checked Brunswick's invasion at Valmy on 20 September 1792, a stand that saved the Revolution. Dumouriez then returned north and crushed the Austrians at Jemappes (6 November 1792), overrunning Belgium.

Defection

His second invasion, of the Dutch Republic in early 1793, ended with his defeat at Neerwinden (18 March 1793). Facing arrest by agents of the Convention and hostile to the Jacobin turn of the Revolution, Dumouriez attempted to march his army on Paris to restore a constitutional monarchy; when his troops refused, he fled to the Austrian lines on 5 April 1793, handing the Convention's commissioners over to the enemy. The defection deepened the paranoia of the Terror and destroyed the Girondins, his political patrons.

Exile and death

Dumouriez spent thirty years in exile, wandering through Europe before settling in England, where the British government granted him a pension and consulted him on military matters during the Napoleonic Wars. He wrote extensive memoirs defending his conduct. He died at Turville Park near Henley-on-Thames in 1823.
