Control chart

Control charts are graphical tools used in quality control to determine if a manufacturing or business process is in a state of control. Introduced by Walter A. Shewhart in the 1920s while working at Bell Telephone Laboratories, they are also known as Shewhart charts or process-behaviour charts.

Structure

A control chart plots a quality characteristic measured over time, together with a centre line representing the process average and upper and lower control limits, typically set at three standard deviations (3σ) from the mean. Points falling outside the limits, or showing non-random patterns, signal that the process may be affected by special causes of variation.

Common and special causes

Shewhart distinguished variation due to common causes, inherent in the process, from variation due to special (assignable) causes, which should be identified and eliminated. Acting on common-cause variation as though it were special — "tampering" — generally makes a process worse, a point later emphasised by W. Edwards Deming.

Types of charts

Variable charts include the X-bar and R charts and X-bar and S charts for subgrouped measurements, and the individual/moving-range (I-MR) chart. Attribute charts include the p-chart and np-chart for proportions, and the c-chart and u-chart for counts of defects.

Rules for detecting signals

Supplementary run rules, such as the Western Electric rules and the Nelson rules, detect trends, shifts, and cycles, increasing sensitivity to small process changes at the cost of more false alarms.
