Oil sands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Oil sands, also known as tar sands, are a type of unconventional petroleum deposit: loose sand or partly consolidated sandstone saturated with a dense, extremely viscous form of petroleum technically called bitumen. The largest deposits are the Athabasca oil sands in northeastern Alberta, Canada, and the Orinoco Belt in Venezuela. Together they account for a very large share of the world's proven oil reserves.

Extraction

Shallow deposits are surface-mined: the oil sand is excavated with giant shovels and trucks, crushed, and mixed with hot water so the bitumen can be separated and skimmed. Deeper deposits are produced in situ, most commonly by steam-assisted gravity drainage (SAGD), in which injected steam liquefies the bitumen so it drains to a producing well. Raw bitumen is too viscous to flow in pipelines; it is either upgraded into synthetic crude oil or blended with diluent ("dilbit") for transport to refineries.

Environmental issues

Oil sands production is energy- and water-intensive and has a larger carbon footprint per barrel than conventional oil. Surface mining strips boreal forest and creates large tailings ponds; in-situ projects fragment habitat and consume natural gas for steam. Proponents point to reclamation obligations, falling emissions intensity and the sector's economic importance to Canada. See also Athabasca oil sands, bitumen and unconventional oil.
