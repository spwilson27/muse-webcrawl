Catch (music)

A catch is a musical composition for a group of voices, usually three or more, in which the singers perform the same melody as a round or canon, with lyrics designed so that the overlapping lines create wordplay, jokes, or hidden meanings not apparent in any single part. Catches were especially popular in England during the 17th and 18th centuries.

History

The catch flourished in the Restoration period and the Georgian era, when catch clubs met in taverns to sing them socially. Composers such as Henry Purcell wrote many catches, ranging from drinking songs and patriotic pieces to bawdy humour. The collection Pammelia (1609) and later publications by John Playford helped spread the form.

Characteristics

Unlike the glee, a related part-song, the catch is strictly canonic: each voice sings identical music delayed by a fixed interval. The wit of the form lies in the text — phrases recombine as the voices overlap, producing puns and sometimes ribald double meanings that made catches a favourite of convivial musical societies.
