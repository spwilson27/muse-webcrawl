Yi (philosophy)

Yi (Chinese: 義; pinyin: yì) is a central concept in Chinese philosophy, especially Confucianism, usually translated as righteousness, justice, or appropriateness — the moral disposition to do what is right, and the practical wisdom to enact it in a given situation.

In Confucianism

Confucius elevated yi alongside ren (benevolence) and li (ritual propriety). In the Analects, the exemplary person (junzi) "takes yi as the essence" and is characterised by acting from righteousness rather than profit: "The junzi understands yi; the small person understands profit (li)." Mencius made yi one of the four innate sprouts (duan) of virtue, rooted in the heart's sense of shame and deference, and insisted that even life itself should be surrendered if it conflicts with yi — as in his parable of the beggar who would not accept food given with contempt.

Contrast with li (profit)

Classical debates — especially between Mencius and the Mohists and later Legalists — often pivot on the tension between yi and personal or state advantage. The famous opening of the Mencius, chiding King Hui of Liang for speaking of profit rather than benevolence and righteousness, frames yi as the proper foundation of government. Xunzi treated yi as a learned norm: human nature is bad, and righteousness is cultivated through ritual and education.

Later usage

Yi permeates later Chinese culture: sworn brotherhoods, secret societies, and chivalric tales celebrate yi qi — loyalty to one's obligations to friends. In modern Chinese the character still conveys justice and meaning (as in yiyi, significance) and forms part of words for obligation, morality, and adoption.
