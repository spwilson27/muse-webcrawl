Ecological niche

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Species occupy distinct niches along gradients of temperature, moisture and other resources.

In ecology, a niche is the match of a species to a specific environmental condition. It describes how an organism or population responds to the distribution of resources and competitors (for example, by growing when resources are abundant, and when predators, parasites and pathogens are scarce) and how it in turn alters those same factors (for example, limiting access to resources by other organisms, acting as a food source for predators and a consumer of prey).

History of the concept

The term was coined by the naturalist Roswell Hill Johnson but Joseph Grinnell was probably the first to use it in a research program in 1917, in his paper "The niche relationships of the California Thrasher". The concept was developed further by Charles Sutherland Elton, who in 1927 described the niche as the functional role of an organism in its community. G. Evelyn Hutchinson in 1957 formalised the niche as an n-dimensional hypervolume defined by the range of environmental variables within which a species can survive and reproduce.

Fundamental and realized niches

Hutchinson distinguished the fundamental niche, the full set of conditions under which an animal could survive and reproduce, from the realized niche, the set of conditions actually used after interactions with other species — competition and predation — are taken into account. The realized niche is almost always smaller than the fundamental niche.

Competitive exclusion

According to the competitive exclusion principle, two species competing for the same limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely in the same niche; one will eventually outcompete the other unless they differentiate their niches. Niche differentiation can occur through resource partitioning, character displacement, or spatial and temporal separation.

Niche construction

Organisms can modify their own and other species' niches through niche construction, as when beavers build dams that create wetland habitats, or earthworms change the structure and chemistry of soil.
