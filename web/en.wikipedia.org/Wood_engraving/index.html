Wood engraving

Wood engraving is a printmaking technique in which an artist works an image into a block of wood cut across the end grain. Fine tools called burins are used to incise lines into the hard end-grain surface, typically boxwood; when inked, the cut-away areas print white against the black of the untouched surface. The technique is a form of relief printing and is distinct from the older woodcut, in which the block is cut along the grain.

Detail of a nineteenth-century wood engraving

History

Wood engraving was developed in the late eighteenth century, principally by the English artist Thomas Bewick (1753–1828), whose A History of British Birds demonstrated the technique's capacity for fine detail. Because end-grain boxwood blocks could withstand the pressure of printing presses and could be set alongside type, wood engraving became the dominant method of book and periodical illustration throughout the nineteenth century.

Technique

The engraver works on a polished block of boxwood, lemonwood or a modern synthetic substitute, cutting with gravers and tint tools. White lines and textures are built up through cross-hatching; the finer the tools and the harder the wood, the greater the tonal range achievable. Blocks were often divided among several engravers in commercial workshops, each cutting a section of a large illustration, which was then bolted together for printing.

Decline and revival

Photographic methods such as the halftone process displaced wood engraving for commercial illustration by the 1890s. In the twentieth century artists including Eric Gill, Robert Gibbings and Clare Leighton revived it as a fine-art medium, often combined with private-press book production. Contemporary societies of wood engravers continue to sustain the craft.
