Pork barrel

Pork barrel, or simply pork, is a metaphor for the appropriation of government spending for localised projects secured primarily to bring money to a representative's district. The usage originated in American English. In election campaigns, the phrase is used in a derogatory manner, suggesting that such projects are wasteful or designed mainly to aid a legislator's re-election rather than to serve the general public.

Origin of the term

The expression derives from the practice of distributing salted pork from a barrel to enslaved people, and later came to denote any largesse from a government store. By the late nineteenth century, American newspapers used "pork barrel" for rivers-and-harbours bills and other legislation distributing federal funds to local projects.

Mechanism and debate

In the United States, pork-barrel spending was typically delivered through earmarks in appropriations bills. Defenders argue that members of Congress are best placed to know their districts' needs and that local projects are a legitimate part of representative government. Critics contend that such spending is allocated on political rather than merit-based criteria, encourages logrolling, and wastes public money. Notorious examples cited by watchdogs include Alaska's proposed "Bridge to Nowhere." A moratorium on earmarks was adopted in Congress in 2011; a modified system of "community project funding" returned in 2021.
