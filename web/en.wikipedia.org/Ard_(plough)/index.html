Ard (plough)

The ard, also called a scratch plough or crook ard, is a light plough without a mouldboard, one of the earliest forms of the plough. Rather than turning the soil over, the ard scratches a shallow furrow, loosening the surface so that seed can be sown. It has been in use since the Neolithic period and remains in use in parts of the world where soils are light and dry.

Construction

A simple ard consists of a stout shaft or beam pulled by draft animals, a vertical or near-vertical share (originally of wood, antler, stone, or later iron) that cuts the soil, and a handle (or two) by which the ploughman guides it. Main types include the bow ard, in which the share passes through a curved beam; the body ard; and the crook ard, in which the beam and share are made from a single bent piece of wood.

History

Evidence for ard use — including preserved wooden ards from European bogs, rock carvings from Scandinavia and the Alps, and ard marks preserved beneath burial mounds — dates back thousands of years. In Britain and northern Europe, criss-cross ploughing with an ard produced the small square "Celtic fields" of prehistory. Because the ard does not bury weeds or deepen the soil as a mouldboard plough does, seed was often sown in cross-ploughed plots.

Use

The ard suits the thin, dry soils of the Mediterranean and the Middle East, where deep inversion of the soil would lose moisture. It is still used in Ethiopia — where the maresha is a form of ard — and in other regions of small-scale dryland farming.
