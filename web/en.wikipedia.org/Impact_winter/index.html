Impact winter

An impact winter follows a major asteroid or comet strike, when ejecta darkens the sky worldwide.

An impact winter is a hypothesized period of prolonged cold weather caused by the impact of a large asteroid or comet on the Earth's surface. Such an impact throws enormous quantities of dust, pulverized rock and sulfurous aerosols into the upper atmosphere, where they spread around the globe and block incoming sunlight, causing temperatures to plunge, photosynthesis to collapse and food chains to fail.

Mechanism

When a body several kilometres across strikes the Earth at tens of kilometres per second, the energy released vaporizes the impactor and a large volume of target rock. Fine particles and sulfur-rich vapours are lofted above the stratosphere, where, unlike ordinary weather, they are not rained out and can persist for months to years. The resulting veil reduces the sunlight reaching the surface by a large fraction — in extreme scenarios to a few percent of normal levels or less.

Land temperatures can fall by 10 °C or more within weeks, with subfreezing conditions reaching into the tropics at night. Because the oceans retain heat longer than the land, coastal and marine climates cool more slowly, producing sharp temperature contrasts. Plants die back, herbivores starve, and predators and scavengers follow — a cascading ecological collapse.

The Chicxulub impact

The best-known impact winter followed the strike that formed the Chicxulub crater on the Yucatán Peninsula about 66 million years ago. The target rocks were unusually rich in sulfur-bearing minerals (anhydrite), so the impact injected vast amounts of sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere. The event is widely held responsible for the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event, which eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs and roughly three-quarters of all species. Evidence includes a worldwide layer of iridium-rich clay, shocked quartz and soot deposits consistent with global wildfires followed by darkness and cold.

Modelling and smaller impacts

Climate models suggest that an impactor roughly 1 km in diameter could produce a regionally devastating event with measurable global cooling, while bodies of 10 km or more threaten a true impact winter. Historical analogues include the "Year Without a Summer" of 1816, caused by the eruption of Mount Tambora — a volcanic winter that gives a partial preview of the crop failures and famines an impact winter would bring on a much larger scale.
