Haskell Brooks Curry (1900–1982) was an American mathematician and logician. He is best known for his foundational work on combinatory logic and formal logic, and three important concepts are named after him: currying (transforming a function of several arguments into a sequence of functions of one argument), Curry's paradox, and the Curry–Howard correspondence between proofs and programs. The programming language Haskell is named in his honour.

Life

Curry was born in Millis, Massachusetts, and studied at Harvard University, initially intending to study medicine before switching to mathematics. He earned his PhD in 1930 at the University of Göttingen under David Hilbert, with a dissertation on combinatory logic, building on work by Moses Schönfinkel. He spent most of his career at Pennsylvania State University, and from 1966 held the chair in logic at the University of Amsterdam previously held by L. E. J. Brouwer.

Work

Curry's magnum opus, written with Robert Feys, is Combinatory Logic (1958), which systematically developed a formal system intended to eliminate the notion of variable from logic. His Foundations of Mathematical Logic (1963) became a standard text. Curry's paradox shows that certain naive logical systems allowing self-reference and implication are inconsistent. The Curry–Howard correspondence, noticed independently by Curry and William Alvin Howard, states that types in programming languages correspond to propositions in logic, and programs to proofs — a cornerstone of modern type theory and proof assistants.

Legacy

Beyond the Haskell language, Curry's name lives on in everyday functional programming practice, where "currying" is a standard technique, and in logic, where the Curry–Howard isomorphism underpins the design of languages and systems such as Coq and Agda.
