Taxonomy

Taxonomy classifies organisms into a hierarchy of ranked groups (taxa).

Taxonomy is the science of defining and naming groups of biological organisms on the basis of shared characteristics. Organisms are grouped into taxa (singular: taxon) and these groups are given a taxonomic rank; groups of a given rank can be aggregated to form a more inclusive group of higher rank, thus creating a taxonomic hierarchy.

Linnaean system

The modern system originates with the works of Carl Linnaeus, who in the 18th century established binomial nomenclature — the two-part Latin name (genus and species) for each organism — and a hierarchy of ranks. The principal ranks in current use are domain, kingdom, phylum (or division in botany), class, order, family, genus, and species. The Swedish botanist's Systema Naturae (1735) and Species Plantarum (1753) are the conventional starting points of modern zoological and botanical nomenclature.

Modern developments

Since the mid-20th century, taxonomy has been reshaped by evolutionary thinking and molecular data. Willi Hennig's cladistics (phylogenetic systematics) classifies organisms strictly by common descent, recognizing only monophyletic groups. DNA sequencing and the three-domain system of Carl Woese placed classification on a phylogenetic footing, and approaches such as the PhyloCode propose rank-free naming based on clades. Rules of naming are governed by international codes, including the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature and the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants.
