Gardian

Image placeholder: a gardian herding Camargue bulls

A gardian is a mounted herdsman of the Camargue region of southern France, comparable to the American cowboy, the Hungarian csikós, and the Spanish vaquero. Gardians ride the small white Camargue horses to herd the region's black bulls (raço di biòu) across the marshy delta of the Rhône, where the animals are raised semi-wild in herds called manades.

Role and traditions

Gardians manage the bulls used in the Provençal bullfighting traditions — the course camarguaise, in which the animals are not killed, and bull-running festivals such as the abrivado and bandido, when bulls are escorted through village streets between pasture and arena. They also work with Camargue horses, assist at brandings (ferrades), and take part in the annual pilgrimage of Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer. The Confrérie des Gardians, founded in 1512, is among the oldest such brotherhoods in France and helps preserve gardian dress, customs, and the Provençal language.

Equipment and dress

The gardian's characteristic tool is the trident (ferri), a long wooden shaft tipped with a small iron trident used to guide and sort the bulls. Traditional dress includes a wide-brimmed felt hat, a velvet jacket, a brightly patterned shirt, and moleskin trousers. The Camargue saddle and the horse's traditional tack are distinctive to the region.

In culture

The gardian was romanticised in Provençal literature, notably by the poet Frédéric Mistral and in the work of the Marquis Folco de Baroncelli-Javon, who codified many gardian traditions in the early twentieth century and promoted the Camargue as a French "Wild West."
