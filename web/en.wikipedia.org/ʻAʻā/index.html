ʻAʻā

TypeVolcanic, mafic
CompositionBasalt
TextureRough, clinkery, spinose
An ʻaʻā flow: a jumbled mass of rough, spinose clinker over a dense core

ʻAʻā (Hawaiian: [ʔəˈʔaː]; also spelled aa, aʻa, ʻaʻa, and in English sources as ah-ah) is one of three basic types of flow lava. ʻAʻā is basaltic lava characterised by a rough or rubbly surface composed of broken lava blocks called clinker. The Hawaiian word was introduced as a technical term in geology by Clarence Dutton.

The loose, broken, and sharp, spiny surface of an ʻaʻā flow makes walking across it extremely difficult and slow. The clinkery surface actually covers a massive dense core, which is the most active part of the flow. As pasty lava in the core travels downslope, the clinkers are carried along at the surface. At the leading edge of an ʻaʻā flow, however, these cooled fragments tumble down the steep front and are buried by the advancing flow. This produces a layer of lava fragments both at the bottom and top of an ʻaʻā flow.

Characteristics

Accretionary lava balls as large as 3 metres (10 feet) are common on ʻaʻā flows. ʻAʻā typically has a higher viscosity than pāhoehoe, the smooth, ropy form of basaltic lava. Pāhoehoe, which is the characteristic lava of shield volcanoes, can change into ʻaʻā as it moves away from its vent, as a consequence of heat loss and increased viscosity, or when the rate of strain exceeds the ability of the lava to flow plastically. The transition is one-way under normal circumstances: ʻaʻā does not change back into pāhoehoe.

The texture of ʻaʻā flows makes them poor radar reflectors in some respects but very strong scatterers; they appear bright in radar imagery, which has allowed scientists to identify similar flows on other worlds, including Venus and Mars.

Etymology

The term comes from the Hawaiian word ʻaʻā, with several proposed derivations. One popular account derives it from the exclamation of pain ("ah! ah!") made by barefoot Hawaiians walking on the sharp surface; Dutton himself suggested it comes from a Hawaiian root meaning "stony" or "rough lava", also used metaphorically for burning or blazing. The word entered the worldwide geological vocabulary from Hawaiʻi, alongside pāhoehoe.

Basaltic andesite and block lava

Flows of more silicic composition, such as basaltic andesite, tend to form what is called "block lava", which resembles ʻaʻā but is made of more regular, smooth-sided, polyhedral blocks rather than spinose clinker. Andesite and dacite flows commonly take this blocky form.
