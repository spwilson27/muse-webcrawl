Supply (economics)

In economics, supply is the amount of a product per unit of time that producers are willing to sell at various given prices when all other factors are held equal (ceteris paribus). The law of supply states that, other things equal, an increase in price results in an increase in quantity supplied.

The supply curve

The relationship is commonly represented by an upward-sloping supply curve on a graph with price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis. A change in the good's own price causes a movement along the curve, while changes in other determinants — input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, and the number of sellers — shift the whole curve.

Elasticity and market supply

The price elasticity of supply measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to price changes; supply tends to be more elastic in the long run, when firms can adjust capacity. Market supply is the horizontal sum of individual producers' supply. In models of perfect competition, the firm's supply curve is its marginal cost curve above the shutdown point. Together with demand, supply determines market equilibrium price and quantity.
