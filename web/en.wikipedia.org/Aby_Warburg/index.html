Aby Warburg

Born: 13 June 1866, Hamburg, German Empire

Died: 26 October 1929, Hamburg, Germany

Known for: Iconology, Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg, Mnemosyne Atlas

Aby Moritz Warburg (13 June 1866 – 26 October 1929) was a German art historian and cultural theorist who founded the Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg (Library for Cultural Studies) in Hamburg. He pioneered the study of iconography and iconology, tracing how images and symbols from antiquity survived and were transformed in Renaissance art and later culture.

Life

Born into the wealthy Hamburg banking family of Warburg, Aby famously renounced his claim to the family business to his younger brother Max in exchange for a promise to buy him all the books he would ever need. He studied art history, history and philology at Bonn, Munich and Strasbourg, writing his dissertation on Botticelli. A journey to the American Southwest in 1895–96, where he witnessed Hopi rituals, deepened his interest in the psychology of symbolism. After a severe mental breakdown following the First World War, he spent several years in sanatoria, returning to scholarship in the 1920s.

Work and institute

Warburg's private library, organised by thematic "good neighbourhood" rather than discipline, became a research institute frequented by scholars including Ernst Cassirer and Erwin Panofsky. His final project, the Mnemosyne Atlas (1924–1929), arranged nearly a thousand images on large black panels to map the afterlife (Nachleben) of classical gestures and symbols — his concept of the Pathosformel, or pathos formula. After the Nazi seizure of power, the library was moved to London in 1933, where it became the Warburg Institute, now part of the University of London.
