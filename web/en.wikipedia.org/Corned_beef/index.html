Corned beef

Corned beef is a salt-cured beef product, typically made from brisket. The term "corned" refers to the coarse grains — "corns" — of rock salt used in the curing process. The meat is usually brined with salt, sugar, and curing salts (sodium nitrite, which gives it its characteristic pink colour), often with pickling spices, then slowly simmered until tender.

History

Salted beef has been produced for centuries as a way of preserving meat. Ireland became a major producer and exporter of corned beef from the seventeenth century, shipping it throughout the British Empire and to the Americas, even though most Irish people could not afford to eat it themselves. In the United States, corned beef became associated with Irish-American culture and is traditionally eaten with cabbage on Saint Patrick's Day. Jewish delicatessens in New York also made corned beef (and its smoked cousin, pastrami) a signature sandwich meat.

Forms

Fresh corned beef is sold as a brined brisket (flat or point cut) for home cooking. Canned corned beef, produced largely in South America — famously in Fray Bentos, Uruguay — became a military and household staple in Britain in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and is used for corned beef hash, a fried mixture of chopped corned beef, potatoes and onions.

In the Philippines and elsewhere

In the Philippines, shredded canned corned beef sautéed with garlic and onions (corned beef silog) is a common breakfast. In Hawaii, corned beef and cabbage and corned beef hash are diner staples.
