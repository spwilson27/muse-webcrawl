Cholecystitis

Cholecystitis is inflammation of the gallbladder. Symptoms include right upper abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and occasionally fever. Often gallbladder attacks (biliary colic) precede acute cholecystitis; the pain lasts longer in cholecystitis than in a typical gallbladder attack. Without appropriate treatment, recurrent episodes are common. Complications of acute cholecystitis include gallstone pancreatitis, common bile duct stones, and inflammation of the common bile duct.

Signs and symptoms

The classic pain of acute cholecystitis is in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen, often radiating to the right shoulder or back, and lasting more than six hours. It is frequently accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and low-grade fever. On examination, Murphy's sign — inspiratory arrest when the examiner palpates under the right costal margin — is characteristic. A minority of patients develop jaundice, suggesting common bile duct obstruction.

Cause

In about 90% of cases (calculous cholecystitis), a gallstone becomes impacted in the cystic duct, causing bile stasis, gallbladder distension, wall ischaemia, and bacterial colonisation. The remaining cases (acalculous cholecystitis) occur mainly in critically ill patients. Risk factors for gallstones are summarised by the "five Fs": fat, female, fair, fertile, and forty.

Diagnosis

Abdominal ultrasound is the first-line test, showing gallstones, a thickened gallbladder wall (>3 mm), pericholecystic fluid, and a sonographic Murphy's sign. Blood tests typically show raised white cell count and C-reactive protein. If the diagnosis remains uncertain, a HIDA scan (cholescintigraphy) showing non-filling of the gallbladder is highly sensitive.

Treatment

Treatment consists of nil-by-mouth, intravenous fluids, antibiotics, and analgesia, followed in most patients by laparoscopic cholecystectomy — ideally early, during the same admission. Patients unfit for surgery may be managed with percutaneous cholecystostomy drainage.
