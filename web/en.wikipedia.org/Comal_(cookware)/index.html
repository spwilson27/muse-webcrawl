Comal (cookware)

A comal is a smooth, flat griddle used in Mexico and Central America for cooking tortillas and other foods. Traditionally made of unglazed earthenware clay, modern comals are commonly cast iron, steel or aluminium, sometimes with a non-stick coating. The word comes from the Nahuatl comalli. Comals have been used since pre-Hispanic times and remain essential in Mexican kitchens and street food stalls.

Design and use

A comal is a shallow, round (occasionally rectangular or oval) plate with a flat or slightly convex cooking surface and a low rim or handles. Placed over a fire, gas burner or charcoal brazier, it is used to cook corn and flour tortillas, toast dried chillies and spices, roast tomatoes, onions and tomatillos for salsas (developing charred spots called tatemado), and prepare foods such as quesadillas, tlayudas, memelas and gorditas.

Seasoning and care

Clay comals are traditionally cured before first use, sometimes by rubbing with a lime (cal) solution or simply by cooking tortillas, to reduce sticking and prevent cracking. Cast-iron comals are seasoned like other cast-iron cookware. The comal's dry, even heat is key to properly puffing corn tortillas and achieving the characteristic speckled browning.
