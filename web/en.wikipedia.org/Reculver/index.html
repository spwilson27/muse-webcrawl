Reculver is a village and coastal resort in Kent, England, about 3 miles (5 km) east of Herne Bay. It is best known for the ruined church of St Mary, whose twin towers stand dramatically at the cliff edge, and for the remains of the Roman fort of Regulbium, one of the Saxon Shore forts. Much of the ancient site has been lost to coastal erosion.

History

The Romans built a fort at Regulbium in the early third century AD to guard the northern entrance to the Wantsum Channel, the waterway that then separated the Isle of Thanet from the mainland. After the Roman period, the site became a royal estate of the kings of Kent; in 669 King Ecgberht granted it for the foundation of a monastery, and a church was built within the fort walls in the seventh century, its fabric incorporating Roman material.

By the medieval period the sea, which in Roman times had been some distance away, was eroding the cliff. The church was remodelled in the twelfth century, when the distinctive twin towers were added. In 1805, with the cliff edge threatening the building, most of the church was demolished; the vicar, with the encouragement of the landowner, had the towers preserved — and their spires rebuilt by Trinity House — as a navigational aid for shipping. Further losses to erosion followed, but sea defences now protect the remaining ruins, which are in the care of English Heritage.

Second World War

Reculver played a role in the development of the "bouncing bomb" used in the 1943 Dambusters raid: the quiet, shallow waters off Reculver were used for test drops of Barnes Wallis's prototype bombs in the weeks before Operation Chastise, and recovered test bombs are displayed in the village and at the nearby RAF Manston museum.

Today

Modern Reculver is a small resort and residential area adjoining the Reculver Country Park, noted for birdwatching on the marshes and for its geology and fossils. The Kent Coast Path passes the towers, and the village has a pub, caravan parks and a small number of shops serving visitors.
