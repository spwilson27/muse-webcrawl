Dissimilation

In phonology, dissimilation is a phenomenon whereby similar consonant or vowel sounds in a word become less similar. In English, the sound change that turned the Latin word peregrīnus ("foreigner") into the French pèlerin and English pilgrim is an example: one of two similar /r/ sounds changed to /l/. Dissimilation is the opposite of assimilation, in which sounds become more similar to one another.

Types

Regressive dissimilation occurs when a sound changes under the influence of a following sound, while progressive dissimilation follows the influence of a preceding sound. Dissimilation may also be classified by distance: contact dissimilation affects adjacent sounds, while dissimilation at a distance (such as the liquid dissimilation of /r/ and /l/ in Latin suffixes -ālis vs. -āris, as in lunāris) operates across intervening segments.

Causes

Dissimilation is often analysed as a repair strategy enforcing the Obligatory Contour Principle, which disfavours sequences of identical features. Perceptual explanations hold that listeners may misparse a repeated feature and attribute it to a single source, "undoing" its effect on one segment — a process related to hypercorrection in the listener-based theory of sound change proposed by John Ohala.

Examples

  • Latin arbor → Spanish árbol (r → l).
  • English "February" commonly pronounced with the first /r/ lost or changed.
  • Greek Grassmann's law (also called Grassmann's law of dissimilation): aspirated consonants lose aspiration when followed by another aspirate in the next syllable.
  • Latin suffix pair -alis/-aris conditioned by a preceding /l/.
