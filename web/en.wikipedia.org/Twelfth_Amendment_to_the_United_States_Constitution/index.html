Twelfth Amendment to the United States Constitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Twelfth Amendment (Amendment XII) to the United States Constitution provides the procedure for electing the President and Vice President. It replaced the procedure provided in Article II, Section 1, Clause 3, by which the Electoral College originally functioned. The amendment was proposed by Congress on December 9, 1803, and was ratified by the requisite three-fourths of state legislatures on June 15, 1804.

Background

Under the original system, each elector cast two undifferentiated votes; the candidate with the most votes became President and the runner-up Vice President. The rise of political parties broke the system: in 1796 John Adams (Federalist) became President and Thomas Jefferson (Democratic-Republican) Vice President, and in 1800 Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr tied at 73 electoral votes each, throwing the election into the House of Representatives and producing a constitutional crisis.

Provisions

The amendment requires electors to cast separate ballots for President and Vice President. If no presidential candidate receives a majority of electoral votes, the House of Representatives chooses among the top three, voting by state delegation; if no vice-presidential candidate has a majority, the Senate chooses between the top two. It also provides that a person constitutionally ineligible for the presidency is ineligible for the vice presidency.

Effects and later elections

The amendment institutionalised the party-ticket system and made divided executive outcomes almost impossible. Its contingent-election provisions were used by the House in 1824 (choosing John Quincy Adams) — under the new rules — and by the Senate in 1836 to select Vice President Richard Mentor Johnson.
