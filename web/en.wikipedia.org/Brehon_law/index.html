Brehon law is the common name for the legal system of early Ireland, preserved in Old Irish law tracts written down mainly in the seventh and eighth centuries. It was administered by professional jurists called brehons (brithemain), who memorised the law, gave judgements, and advised kings. The system governed Ireland, in whole or part, until the English conquest was completed in the seventeenth century.


Medieval Irish legal manuscripts preserve the tracts.

Characteristics

Brehon law emphasised compensation rather than capital or corporal punishment: offences were settled by fines (such as the éric body-fine for homicide) calibrated to the victim's status, known as honour-price. Society was graded minutely by rank, and legal capacity, suretyship, distraint of property, and fosterage were all carefully regulated. The system had no state enforcement machinery in the modern sense; judgement relied on sureties and social obligation.

Important texts

The great compilation known as the Senchas Már grouped some fifty tracts covering topics from bee-keeping judgements (Bechbretha) to sick-maintenance and the law of persons. Later commentaries and glosses accumulated around the canonical texts, showing the law adapting over centuries.

Decline

English administrations from the Statutes of Kilkenny (1366) onward sought to suppress the law in the Pale, and after the Tudor conquest leading cases such as the Case of Tanistry (1608) declared Irish customs void, ending the brehons' jurisdiction.
