Bumper sticker

A bumper sticker is an adhesive label or sticker with a message, intended to be attached to the bumper of an automobile and to be read by the occupants of other vehicles — although they are often stuck onto other objects or surfaces. Most bumper stickers are about 8.5 by 2.75 inches (about 21.6 by 7.0 cm) and are made of weather-resistant vinyl or PVC. They express political views, humour, commercial advertising, religious beliefs, support for sports teams and universities, and tourist destinations.

History

Before bumpers became common, similar slogans were displayed on horse-drawn carriages and on early automobiles as placards. The modern bumper sticker emerged after World War II, aided by advances in adhesives and screen printing; Kansas silk-screener Forest P. Gill is often credited with an early version in the 1940s. Bumper stickers became widespread during the 1952 U.S. presidential election and have remained a fixture of American political campaigning and road culture ever since.

Purpose and variations

Stickers may promote candidates, causes, or businesses, commemorate places visited ("I ❤ NY"-style variants), or display humour and identity. Variations include window decals, magnetic signs, and the oval country-code stickers modelled on European international registration plates (such as "OBX" for the Outer Banks). In some jurisdictions, stickers that obstruct windows or contain obscenities have raised legal free-speech questions.
