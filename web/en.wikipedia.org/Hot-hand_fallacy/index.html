Hot-hand fallacy

The hot-hand fallacy, also known as the hot-hand phenomenon or "hot hand", is the belief that a person who has experienced success with a seemingly random event has a greater chance of further success in additional attempts. The concept is most often applied to sports, especially basketball, where a player who has made several shots in a row is said to have a "hot hand" and is believed more likely to make the next shot.

Research

A landmark 1985 study by Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone, and Amos Tversky analysed basketball shooting data and found no evidence for the hot hand: the probability of making a shot was essentially independent of previous outcomes, and apparent streaks were consistent with chance. The authors attributed the belief to the clustering illusion and a misperception of random sequences, and for decades the "hot-hand fallacy" was a staple example of cognitive bias.

Reassessment

Later work complicated the picture. A 2015 reanalysis by Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo showed that the original statistical method contained a subtle selection bias — in finite sequences, the expected proportion of successes following streaks is biased downward even for random data. Correcting for this bias, the original data actually showed a substantial hot-hand effect. Subsequent studies and meta-analyses have found modest evidence of streak effects in several sports, and the topic remains an active area of debate in statistics and psychology.

Related concepts

The hot-hand belief is closely related to the gambler's fallacy, its mirror image, in which a run of one outcome is believed to make the opposite outcome more likely.
