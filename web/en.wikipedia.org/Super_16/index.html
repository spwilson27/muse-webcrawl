Super 16

Super 16 is a motion picture film format that extends the image area of standard 16 mm film by using the space normally reserved for the optical soundtrack. It produces a wider image with a native aspect ratio of approximately 1.66:1, making it well suited to blow-up to 35 mm and to transfer for widescreen television.

Technical description

Super 16 uses single-perforation 16 mm stock; because no soundtrack is printed on the film (sound is recorded separately), the picture is widened into the soundtrack area and slightly increased in height, giving about 40% more usable image area than standard 16 mm. Cameras require a modified gate, viewfinder and lens centring for the shifted optical axis. Prints are not projected directly; the format is an origination format intended for optical blow-up to 35 mm or for scanning.

History and use

Swedish cinematographer Rune Ericson developed the format in 1969, and it was standardised in the early 1970s. Super 16 became a mainstay of independent film, documentaries and television drama because it offered a cinematic depth of field and grain structure closer to 35 mm at far lower cost in cameras, stock and crew. Notable productions shot wholly or partly in Super 16 include the television series The Walking Dead (early seasons) and films such as Leaving Las Vegas and The Hurt Locker's contemporary independent features; many Academy Award–nominated productions have used it.

Digital era

With digital intermediate workflows, Super 16 negatives are scanned at 2K or higher resolution, and the format remains in use by filmmakers seeking a filmic texture, including for music videos and features. The term "Super 16" has also been informally applied to digital sensors and 16:9 crops of similar image size.
