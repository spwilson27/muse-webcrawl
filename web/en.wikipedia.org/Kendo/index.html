Kendo (剣道, kendō, "way of the sword") is a modern Japanese martial art descended from kenjutsu, the swordsmanship of the samurai. Practitioners use bamboo swords called shinai and wear protective armour called bōgu, allowing full-contact practice with a strong emphasis on etiquette, spirit and self-discipline.

History

Kendo developed from the kenjutsu schools of feudal Japan. In the 18th century, padded armour and bamboo practice swords were introduced, making sparring safer, and during the Meiji period sword arts were reorganised for physical education. After a ban during the Allied occupation, kendo was revived in the 1950s under the All Japan Kendo Federation, with the "Concept of Kendo" formally stated in 1975: "to discipline the human character through the application of the principles of the katana."

Practice and equipment

The bōgu consists of a helmet (men), gauntlets (kote), torso protector (dō) and skirt-like hip protector (tare), worn over the keikogi and hakama. Valid scoring strikes (yūkō datotsu) are delivered to the head, wrists, torso or throat with correct posture, spirit shout (kiai) and follow-through (zanshin). Training includes kata performed with wooden or metal swords, basic drills (suburi, kirikaeshi) and free sparring (jigeiko).

Competition and grades

Matches (shiai) are usually decided by the first competitor to score two points within the time limit, judged by three referees. Ranks run from kyū grades for beginners through eight dan grades. The World Kendo Championships have been held every three years since 1970 under the International Kendo Federation, which has more than 50 affiliated countries; South Korea (where the art is called kumdo) is Japan's chief rival.
