High-frequency trading

High-frequency trading (HFT) is a type of algorithmic trading that uses sophisticated technological tools and computer algorithms to rapidly trade securities. HFT uses proprietary trading strategies carried out by dedicated, high-speed computers to move in and out of positions in seconds or fractions of a second. Firms compete on speed, co-locating servers at exchanges and using direct market feeds and microwave or laser networks to minimise latency.

Characteristics and strategies

High-frequency trading firms typically trade large volumes, hold positions for very short periods (often ending the day flat), and earn small profits per trade. Common strategies include electronic market making, statistical arbitrage, index arbitrage, latency arbitrage, and order anticipation. Well-known HFT firms include Citadel Securities, Virtu Financial, Jump Trading, Tower Research Capital, and Hudson River Trading.

Growth and regulation

HFT expanded rapidly in the 2000s as markets became electronic and fragmented; by some estimates it accounted for around 70% of US equity volume at its peak around 2009–2010, declining thereafter. HFT has been a subject of controversy: critics link it to events such as the Flash Crash of May 6, 2010, and allege practices such as quote stuffing and spoofing, while proponents argue it narrows bid–ask spreads and improves liquidity. Regulators including the SEC and European authorities under MiFID II have introduced rules on algorithmic trading, circuit breakers, and market access. Michael Lewis's 2014 book Flash Boys brought the debate to a wide audience.
