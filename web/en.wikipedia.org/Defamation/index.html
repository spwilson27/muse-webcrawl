Defamation
Defamation law balances protection of reputation against freedom of speech.
TypesLibel (written), Slander (spoken)
Legal areaTort law, civil law
Key defenceTruth, honest opinion, privilege

Defamation is the communication of a false statement of fact that harms the reputation of an individual person, business, product, group, government, religion, or nation. In common law jurisdictions, defamation communicated in writing or published form is called libel, while defamation communicated by spoken words or gestures is called slander.

Under common law, to constitute defamation, a claim must generally be false and must have been made to someone other than the person defamed. Some common law jurisdictions also distinguish between libel and slander, with libel treated as more serious because it is in a fixed form and therefore potentially more damaging.

Elements

A plaintiff in a defamation case typically must prove that the defendant made a false statement purporting to be fact, published or communicated it to a third party, acted at least negligently as to its falsity, and that the statement caused actual harm. In the United States, public officials and public figures must prove "actual malice" — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth — following the Supreme Court's decision in New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964).

Defences

The most complete defence to a defamation claim is that the statement is true. Other defences include statements of opinion rather than fact, absolute and qualified privilege (for example, statements made in legislative or judicial proceedings), and consent.

Defamation around the world

Most jurisdictions allow legal action, civil and/or criminal, to deter various kinds of defamation. In many countries, criminal defamation laws have been criticised by free-expression advocates and international bodies for chilling legitimate speech.
