Border control

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Border control comprises measures taken by governments to monitor and regulate the movement of people, animals, and goods across land, air, and maritime borders. Its purposes include enforcing immigration law, collecting customs duties, preventing smuggling and trafficking, and protecting against the spread of disease, pests, and invasive species.

Functions

At official ports of entry, border officers check passports and visas, inspect luggage and cargo, and assess goods for customs. Between ports of entry, border patrols surveil frontier areas to detect unauthorized crossings. Many states also impose exit controls and pre-clearance arrangements, such as the United States preclearance facilities at certain foreign airports.

History

Controls on movement are ancient — city gates, toll stations, and walled frontiers all served border-control functions — but systematic passport controls are largely a product of the First World War, when states made the wartime requirement of identity documents permanent. The late 20th and early 21st centuries saw the introduction of biometric passports, passenger name records, advance passenger information systems, and electronic travel authorizations.

Border control by region

The Schengen Area in Europe abolished internal border checks among its members while maintaining common external border rules, administered with the support of the Frontex agency. The United States enforces its borders through Customs and Border Protection and the Border Patrol, while the United Kingdom operates Border Force. Australia is notable for maritime interdiction policies directed at unauthorized boat arrivals.

Quarantine and biosecurity

Border agencies also enforce quarantine: inspecting food, plants, and animals; requiring declarations of agricultural products; and imposing health measures during epidemics, as was widespread during the COVID-19 pandemic.
