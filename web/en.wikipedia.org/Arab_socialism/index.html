Arab socialism

Arab socialism (Arabic: الاشتراكية العربية, al-Ishtirākīya al-ʿArabīya) is a political ideology based on the combination of pan-Arabism and socialism. It is distinct from the much broader tradition of socialist thought in the Arab world that predates it. The term was coined by Michel Aflaq, one of the founders of the Ba'ath Party, in order to distinguish his version of socialist ideology from the Marxist socialism of Eastern Europe and East Asia.

Principles

For Aflaq and the Ba'ath, Arab socialism was a necessary component of the struggle for Arab unity and freedom, but it rejected Marxist class struggle and materialism, emphasising instead national unity and a spiritual dimension. Land reform, public ownership of major industries and natural resources, and state-led development were its usual policy content.

Variants

In Egypt, President Gamal Abdel Nasser implemented an "Arab socialist" programme through the Arab Socialist Union, nationalising the Suez Canal Company in 1956 and much of Egyptian industry in the early 1960s. Variants were also pursued in Syria and Iraq under Ba'athist governments, in Algeria after independence, and in Libya under Muammar Gaddafi's "Third Universal Theory" set out in The Green Book.

The defeat of Egypt in the Six-Day War of 1967 badly damaged the prestige of Arab socialism, and during the 1970s many governments, beginning with Anwar Sadat's infitah (open-door) policy in Egypt, moved toward economic liberalisation.
