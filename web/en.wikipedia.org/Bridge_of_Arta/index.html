Bridge of Arta

The Bridge of Arta (Greek: Γεφύρι της Άρτας) is a stone arch bridge over the Arachthos River at the entrance to the city of Arta in northwestern Greece. Originally built in its present form in 1612, during the Ottoman period, on much older foundations that may go back to the Roman era or the Despotate of Epirus, the bridge is one of the most famous in Greece — celebrated not for its engineering alone but for the dark folk ballad attached to it.

Description

The bridge consists of four large semicircular arches and a series of smaller relieving arches, with a total length of about 130 metres (430 ft) and a width of about 4 metres (13 ft). Its irregular, asymmetrical profile, rising unevenly over the river, is characteristic of Ottoman-era stone bridges in the Balkans. The structure was damaged during fighting in the Greek War of Independence and again in the Second World War, and has been restored several times; it now carries pedestrians only.

The ballad

The bridge is the subject of one of the best-known Greek folk songs, "The Bridge of Arta." In the ballad, forty-five master builders and sixty apprentices work all day building the bridge, but each night what they have built collapses. A supernatural voice declares that the bridge will stand only if the master builder sacrifices his wife by walling her into the foundations. She comes bringing lunch, is tricked into the foundations, and as the masonry rises around her she curses the bridge — then, remembering her brother may cross it, softens her curse into a blessing. Versions of this "foundation sacrifice" ballad occur across Balkan folklore, from the Serbian "Building of Skadar" to the Romanian legend of Meșterul Manole.
