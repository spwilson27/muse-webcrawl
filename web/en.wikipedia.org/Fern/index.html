Fern

Fern fronds and an uncurling fiddlehead

Ferns (class Polypodiopsida) are a group of vascular plants that reproduce via spores and have neither seeds nor flowers. They differ from mosses by being vascular — having xylem and phloem — and from seed plants by reproducing through spores. Ferns have complex leaves called megaphylls, and the young leaves typically unroll from a coiled form known as a fiddlehead.

Life cycle

The familiar fern is the diploid sporophyte generation. Spores produced in sporangia on the underside of fronds germinate into small, heart-shaped gametophytes (prothalli) that produce eggs and sperm; the sperm swim to fertilise the egg, from which a new sporophyte grows.

Distribution and uses

There are roughly 10,500 living species of ferns, found worldwide but most diverse in the tropics; some are trees, others epiphytes. Ferns were dominant plants in the Carboniferous period and contributed greatly to coal deposits. Today many species are grown as ornamentals, and fiddleheads of some species are eaten as a vegetable.
