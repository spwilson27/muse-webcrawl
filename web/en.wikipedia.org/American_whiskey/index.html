American whiskey is a distilled beverage produced in the United States from a fermented mash of cereal grain. Among the most common types are bourbon whiskey, rye whiskey, Tennessee whiskey, corn whiskey, and blended whiskey, each defined by federal regulations governing mash composition and aging.

Types

Bourbon must be made from at least 51% corn and aged in new charred oak barrels. Straight whiskey must be aged at least two years and contain no added coloring or flavoring. Tennessee whiskey follows the bourbon rules but is filtered through sugar maple charcoal (the Lincoln County Process). Rye whiskey uses at least 51% rye grain.

History

Distilling in America began with colonial rum and applejack before westward-moving settlers turned to corn and rye. The Whiskey Rebellion of the 1790s, Prohibition (1920–1933), and the postwar consolidation of distilleries shaped the industry. A 1964 act of Congress declared bourbon a distinctive product of the United States.

Regulation

American whiskeys are regulated under the Federal Alcohol Administration Act and standards of identity maintained by the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB).
