Vulgar Latin

Vulgar Latin (Latin: sermo vulgaris) is the set of non-literary registers of Latin spoken from the late Roman Republic through the early Middle Ages, from which the Romance languages developed. "Vulgar" here means "of the common people" rather than "crude": the term covers the everyday speech of soldiers, merchants, settlers, and the urban population of the Roman world, as distinct from the standardized literary Classical Latin of authors such as Cicero and Virgil.

Sources

Vulgar Latin was seldom written deliberately, so it is reconstructed from indirect evidence: graffiti (notably at Pompeii), misspellings in inscriptions, glossaries and school texts, grammatical works correcting "errors"—such as the Appendix Probi, which lists correct forms against common spoken forms (speculum non speclum, "mirror, not 'speclum'")—letters and documents in a colloquial style, and comparison of the daughter Romance languages (comparative reconstruction).

Features

Phonology

Classical Latin's system of long and short vowels collapsed in most regions into a quality-based system, and diphthongs monophthongized (ae → e). Unstressed syllables weakened; final -m was lost early, and consonant clusters simplified. Syncope of unstressed internal vowels produced forms like caldus for calidus ("hot"), the ancestor of Italian caldo and Spanish caldo.

Grammar

The six-case noun system eroded as final syllables weakened; prepositions with the accusative took over the functions of the genitive, dative, and ablative (compare French de and à constructions). Verb forms were regularized, the synthetic passive was replaced by reflexive and periphrastic constructions, and a new future tense formed from the infinitive plus habeo ("I have")—e.g., cantare habeo → French chanterai, "I will sing". The demonstratives ille and ipse developed into definite articles.

Vocabulary

Everyday words often replaced their classical equivalents: caballus ("nag") replaced equus ("horse"), giving French cheval, Spanish caballo, Italian cavallo; bucca ("cheek") replaced os ("mouth"), giving French bouche, Spanish boca; testa ("pot") came to mean "head" (French tête).

Development into Romance

As the Western Roman Empire fragmented, regional varieties of spoken Latin diverged. By the 9th century, documents such as the Strasbourg Oaths (842) show a Romance vernacular distinct enough to be written differently from Latin; the following centuries saw the emergence of written Italian, Spanish, French, Portuguese, Catalan, Romanian, and other Romance languages, all direct descendants of Vulgar Latin.
