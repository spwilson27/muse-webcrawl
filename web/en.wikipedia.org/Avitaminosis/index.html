Avitaminosis

Avitaminosis is a group of conditions caused by a deficiency of one or more vitamins in the diet or by impaired absorption. The term contrasts with hypervitaminosis, illness from vitamin excess, and with hypovitaminosis, sometimes used for milder deficiency.

Examples

Classic avitaminoses include scurvy from lack of vitamin C; beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome from thiamine (B1) deficiency; pellagra from niacin deficiency; rickets and osteomalacia from vitamin D deficiency; night blindness and xerophthalmia from vitamin A deficiency; and pernicious anaemia from vitamin B12 deficiency, often caused by malabsorption rather than diet. The concept took shape in the early twentieth century as Casimir Funk and others identified "vitamines" whose absence explained diseases that had plagued sailors, prisoners and populations dependent on polished rice.

Causes

Deficiency may result from inadequate intake, malabsorption (as in coeliac disease or after bariatric surgery), increased requirements, or certain drugs. Treatment is replacement of the missing vitamin and correction of the underlying cause; food fortification and varied diets have made severe avitaminoses uncommon in high-income countries, though they persist where diets are restricted.
