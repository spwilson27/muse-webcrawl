Groundwater


Groundwater in an aquifer system

Groundwater is the water present beneath Earth's surface in rock and soil pore spaces and in the fractures of rock formations. About 30 percent of all readily available freshwater in the world is groundwater. A unit of rock or an unconsolidated deposit is called an aquifer when it can yield a usable quantity of water. The depth at which soil pore spaces or fractures and voids in rock become completely saturated with water is called the water table. Groundwater is recharged from precipitation, and it flows from the surface naturally to outlets such as springs and seeps, and can form oases or wetlands. Groundwater is also often withdrawn for agricultural, municipal, and industrial use by constructing and operating extraction wells.

Characteristics

Historically, groundwater has attracted less attention than surface water, because it is hidden from view and its movement is slow—typically centimetres to metres per day. Groundwater in an aquifer moves under the influence of gravity and pressure gradients, following Darcy's law, which relates flow rate to hydraulic conductivity and the hydraulic gradient. Porosity and permeability of the geological material govern how much water can be stored and transmitted.

Aquifers may be unconfined, where the water table forms the upper boundary, or confined, where groundwater is held under pressure between impermeable layers; wells drilled into confined aquifers may flow artesian, rising above the top of the aquifer without pumping.

Recharge and discharge

Groundwater is recharged naturally by rain and snowmelt infiltrating through soil and rock, and artificially by irrigation return flows and managed aquifer recharge. It discharges into streams, lakes, wetlands and the ocean, sustaining baseflow in rivers during dry periods. Residence times range from days in shallow systems to tens of thousands of years in deep, arid-zone aquifers such as the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System.

Use and overexploitation

Groundwater provides drinking water for roughly two billion people and supplies a large share of irrigation water worldwide. Excessive pumping—overdraft—can lower water tables, dry up wells and springs, induce land subsidence, and cause saltwater intrusion in coastal aquifers. Notable depletion has occurred in the Ogallala Aquifer of the North American Great Plains, in northern India, and in the North China Plain.

Pollution

Contaminants from landfills, septic systems, agricultural fertilisers and pesticides, leaking fuel tanks, and industrial spills can pollute groundwater. Because it moves slowly and is difficult to observe, contamination may persist for decades and remediation is costly. Naturally occurring arsenic and fluoride affect aquifers in many regions, notably Bangladesh and West Bengal.
