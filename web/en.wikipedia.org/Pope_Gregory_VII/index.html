Pope Gregory VII (Latin: Gregorius VII; c. 1015 – 25 May 1085), born Hildebrand of Sovana, was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 22 April 1073 to his death. A central figure of the Gregorian Reform, he is best known for the Investiture Controversy with Emperor Henry IV, which he prosecuted in defence of the liberty and supremacy of the Church.

Reform

As the adviser of successive popes Hildebrand drove the reform programme against simony, clerical marriage and lay investiture — the appointment of bishops by secular rulers. Elected pope by acclamation in 1073, he set out his claims in the Dictatus papae (1075), 27 propositions asserting, among other things, that the pope alone could depose emperors and that the Roman Church had never erred. His decrees enforcing clerical celibacy and forbidding lay investiture brought him into open conflict with the German king Henry IV.

Investiture Controversy

In 1076 Henry procured Gregory's deposition at Worms; Gregory responded by excommunicating and deposing Henry, releasing his subjects from their allegiance. Facing revolt, Henry crossed the Alps and stood for three days in the snow outside the castle of Canossa in January 1077, where Gregory absolved him — the famous "Walk to Canossa". The settlement was temporary: war resumed, Gregory excommunicated Henry again in 1080, and Henry installed an antipope and occupied Rome. Rescued by the Normans under Robert Guiscard — whose troops then sacked Rome — Gregory withdrew to Salerno, where he died in 1085, reputedly saying, "I have loved justice and hated iniquity, therefore I die in exile." He was canonised in 1728.
