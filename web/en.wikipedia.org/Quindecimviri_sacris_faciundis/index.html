The quindecimviri sacris faciundis ("fifteen men for the performance of sacred rites") were one of the four major colleges of priests (collegia) in ancient Roman religion. Their principal duty was the custody and interpretation of the Sibylline Books, a collection of prophetic utterances in Greek hexameters that the Roman state consulted in times of crisis.

Roman priests at sacrifice; the quindecimviri advised the state on prodigies

History

Tradition attributed the acquisition of the Sibylline Books to the last king of Rome, Tarquinius Superbus, who purchased them from the Cumaean Sibyl. Under the monarchy and early Republic, two men (duumviri sacris faciundis) guarded them. In 367 BC, as part of the Licinian-Sextian settlement, the college was enlarged to ten (decemviri), with five patricians and five plebeians, opening the office to plebeians. The dictator Sulla increased the number to fifteen in the 80s BC, after which the college took the name quindecimviri. Membership was for life and was held by leading senators; emperors were members ex officio, and from Augustus onward emperors could appoint supernumerary members.

Duties

The books were kept in stone chests in the vault of the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitoline Hill, and later moved by Augustus to the Temple of Apollo on the Palatine. The quindecimviri could consult them only on the order of the Senate, typically after reports of prodigies — plagues, earthquakes, monstrous births, or military disasters. Their reports prescribed ritual remedies, which often involved introducing new cults to Rome, including those of Ceres, Apollo, and the Magna Mater (Cybele) in 204 BC. The college also supervised the worship of Greek-rite cults and organized celebrations such as the Secular Games, notably those of Augustus in 17 BC, for which Horace composed the Carmen Saeculare.

Decline

The college survived into the Christian empire but lost its function as public pagan cult was suppressed. In the late fourth century, the emperor Theodosius I is said to have ordered the Sibylline Books burned; the general Stilicho was later accused of destroying them. With their loss, the quindecimviri disappeared from history.
