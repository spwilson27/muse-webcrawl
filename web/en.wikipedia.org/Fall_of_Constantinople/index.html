The fall of Constantinople, also known as the conquest of Constantinople, was the capture of the capital of the Byzantine Empire by the Ottoman Empire on 29 May 1453. The twenty-one-year-old Sultan Mehmed II led an army of some 50,000–80,000 men against a garrison of perhaps 7,000–10,000 defenders commanded by Emperor Constantine XI, who died in the fighting. The city's capture ended the Byzantine Empire after more than eleven centuries and is widely regarded as a landmark between the medieval and early modern worlds.

Background

By 1453 the empire had shrunk to little more than the city itself, a few Aegean islands and part of the Peloponnese. Mehmed, determined to succeed where his predecessors had failed, built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı on the Bosphorus, prepared a fleet, and commissioned enormous bombards from the Hungarian founder Urban, the largest some 8 metres long, capable of firing stone balls of roughly 600 kg against the Theodosian Walls.

The siege

The siege began on 6 April 1453. Ottoman cannon battered the land walls while the fleet tried to force the Golden Horn, blocked by a great chain. Mehmed famously transported some seventy ships overland on greased logs into the Horn, outflanking the chain. After weeks of bombardment and repelled assaults, a final attack in the early hours of 29 May overwhelmed the exhausted defenders; according to tradition, a small gate — the Kerkoporta — was found left open, hastening the breakthrough, though historians debate the story. Constantine XI died fighting, and the city was subjected to the customary three days of plunder, after which Mehmed entered and ordered the Hagia Sophia converted into a mosque.

Aftermath

Mehmed made Constantinople his capital and styled himself Kayser-i Rûm, "Caesar of the Romans". He repopulated the city, guaranteed the Orthodox Church under Patriarch Gennadios II, and made it the centre of a multi-ethnic empire. The fall reverberated through Europe: Greek scholars fleeing westward carried manuscripts that fed Renaissance humanism, and the event is often used (though debated) as an end-date for the Middle Ages.
