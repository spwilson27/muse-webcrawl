T cell

A T cell is a type of lymphocyte, a white blood cell that plays a central role in cell-mediated immunity. T cells mature in the thymus — hence the name — and can be distinguished from other lymphocytes by the presence of a T-cell receptor (TCR) on their surface.

T cell
Type: Lymphocyte
Origin: Bone marrow progenitors
Maturation: Thymus
Key marker: CD3, T-cell receptor
Subtypes: Helper, cytotoxic, regulatory, memory

Development

Hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow give rise to progenitors that migrate to the thymus. There they undergo positive and negative selection, ensuring they can recognize self major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules but do not react strongly against self antigens. Only a small percentage survive.

Types

Helper T cells (CD4+) activate other immune cells by releasing cytokines. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) kill virus-infected and cancerous cells. Regulatory T cells suppress immune responses and maintain tolerance. Memory T cells persist after infection and mount faster responses on re-exposure.

Activation

Activation requires recognition of a peptide antigen presented on MHC by an antigen-presenting cell such as a dendritic cell, plus co-stimulatory signals. Activated T cells proliferate by clonal expansion and differentiate into effector cells.

Clinical significance

HIV infects and destroys CD4+ helper T cells, leading to AIDS. Defects in T-cell function cause immunodeficiency, while overactivity contributes to autoimmune disease. Engineered CAR T cells are used in cancer immunotherapy. See also Immune system, B cell and Taiwan for unrelated topics.
