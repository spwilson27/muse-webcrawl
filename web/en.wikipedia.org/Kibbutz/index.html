Kibbutz

A kibbutz (Hebrew: קִבּוּץ, lit. "gathering, clustering"; plural kibbutzim) is a collective community in Israel that was traditionally based on agriculture. The first kibbutz, Degania Alef, was established in 1910 by Jewish pioneers in Ottoman Palestine. Combining socialism and Zionism in a form of practical Labour Zionism, the classical kibbutz held property in common, practised equality of consumption, and rotated jobs among members; children were raised communally in children's houses.

History

Kibbutzim were founded by idealistic youth-movement pioneers (halutzim) of the Second and Third Aliyot, who sought to create a new Jewish society of free labour on the land. Degania (1910), Kfar Giladi, and later Ein Harod and the tower-and-stockade settlements of the 1930s made the kibbutz a tool of settlement and defence; kibbutz members were central to the Palmach and to Israel's founding leadership — prime ministers David Ben-Gurion, Golda Meir and others had kibbutz ties. By the late 20th century about 270 kibbutzim housed a small but influential share of Israel's population and produced a disproportionate share of its agricultural and industrial exports.

The classical model

Members worked in the kibbutz's branches — field crops, orchards, dairy and poultry, later plastics, electronics and tourism — receiving no wages; the community provided housing, food in the communal dining hall, clothing, healthcare and education "to each according to his needs". Decisions were made in the weekly general assembly. Children slept in children's houses, a practice largely abandoned by the 1990s.

Crisis and change

The debt crisis of the 1980s, inflation and generational change pushed most kibbutzim toward reform: differential budgets, privatised services, hired labour, outside employment and "renewed kibbutz" models with personal budgets and partial private ownership. A minority retain the fully communal ("collective") model. Kibbutzim today operate hotels, high-tech firms and renewable-energy ventures alongside farms.
