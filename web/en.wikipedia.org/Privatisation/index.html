Privatization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A public share offer during a privatisation programme

Privatization (also privatisation in British English) can mean several different things, most commonly referring to moving something from the public sector (government) into the private sector. It is also sometimes used as a synonym of deregulation when a heavily regulated private company or industry becomes less regulated. Government functions and services may also be privatised: in this case, private entities are tasked with the implementation of government plans and the provision of services previously provided by state-owned enterprises.

Mass privatisation campaigns were carried out from the 1980s onwards, notably in the United Kingdom under Margaret Thatcher, where industries such as British Telecom, British Gas, British Steel and the railways were sold, and in the post-communist states of Central and Eastern Europe, where voucher schemes transferred state assets to citizens on an enormous scale in the 1990s.

Methods

Governments may privatise through public share offerings on stock exchanges, trade sales to strategic investors, management or employee buyouts, voucher distributions, or the contracting out of services while retaining ownership of assets. The stated motivations include raising revenue, increasing efficiency through competition, reducing state borrowing, and widening share ownership. Results have been mixed: studies find efficiency gains in competitive industries but weaker outcomes in natural monopolies lacking effective regulation.

Criticism

Critics argue that privatisation can lead to higher prices, job losses, reduced accountability and the transfer of public wealth to private investors at undervalued prices. Debates around water, energy, rail and health services continue in many countries, and some services have been brought back into public ownership ("renationalisation" or "remunicipalisation").
