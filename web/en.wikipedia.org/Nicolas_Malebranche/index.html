Nicolas Malebranche (August 6, 1638 – October 13, 1715) was a French Oratorian priest and rationalist philosopher. A leading follower of René Descartes, he developed the doctrines of occasionalism—the view that God is the only true cause, and created things merely provide 'occasions' for divine action—and of the 'vision in God', the theory that we perceive ideas directly in God.

Malebranche studied philosophy and theology at the Sorbonne and joined the Oratory in 1660. Reading Descartes's Treatise on Man in 1664 converted him to philosophy, and his masterpiece The Search After Truth (1674–75) combined Cartesianism with Augustinian theology.

Philosophy

Because Malebranche could see no necessary connection between finite minds and bodies, he held that God acts on every occasion of apparent interaction: my willing to move my arm is the occasion, God the cause. Ideas, being eternal and intelligible, exist in God, and the mind sees them there—the doctrine of the vision in God. His views provoked famous controversies with Antoine Arnauld and influenced Berkeley, Leibniz, Hume and Locke, who wrote an examination of his philosophy.
