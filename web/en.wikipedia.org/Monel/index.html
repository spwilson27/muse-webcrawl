Monel

Monel is a group of nickel alloys, primarily composed of nickel (up to about 67%) and copper, with small amounts of iron, manganese, carbon and silicon. Monel was created by David H. Browne, chief metallurgist at International Nickel Co., and named for the company's president, Ambrose Monell, with one "l" dropped because surnames were not allowed as trademarks at the time. It was patented in 1906.

Properties

Monel alloys are considerably stronger than pure nickel or copper and are noted for exceptional resistance to corrosion by seawater, acids and alkalis. Unlike most stainless steels, Monel is resistant to rapidly flowing seawater and to hydrofluoric acid, and it resists pitting and stress-corrosion cracking in many environments. Monel 400, the original and most common grade, contains roughly 63–70% nickel and 28–34% copper. Other grades include Monel K-500, which adds aluminium and titanium and can be age-hardened for much higher strength, and Monel R-405, a free-machining variant.

Applications

Because of its seawater resistance, Monel is used extensively in marine engineering — propeller shafts, pump components, valves and fasteners — and in chemical and hydrocarbon processing equipment, including heat exchangers and tanks for hydrofluoric acid alkylation in petroleum refining. It was used for the roofing of the original Pennsylvania Station in New York and for the internal parts of the first industrial gas turbines. Monel also finds niche use in musical instruments, including valve assemblies on high-quality brass instruments and strings for electric guitars and mandolins.
