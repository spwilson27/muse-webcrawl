Différance

The "a" of différance is visible in writing but silent in speech.

Différance is a French term coined by the philosopher Jacques Derrida and central to his project of deconstruction. It is a deliberate misspelling of différence ("difference"): the substitution of a for e can be seen in writing but not heard in speech, a fact Derrida used to challenge the traditional priority of speech over writing in Western philosophy.

Meaning

The spelling fuses the two senses of the French verb différer: to differ — to be distinct in space — and to defer — to postpone in time. Différance names the process by which meaning is produced: a sign means what it does only by differing from other signs, and its full meaning is perpetually deferred along an endless chain of further signs, never arriving at a final, self-present meaning. Derrida insisted that différance is "neither a word nor a concept", but rather the condition of possibility of signification itself.

Background

The idea builds on Ferdinand de Saussure's structuralist linguistics, in which language is a system of differences without positive terms, and on critiques of Husserl's phenomenology and Heidegger's ontology. Derrida first presented the concept in a 1968 lecture to the Société française de philosophie; the essay was later collected in Margins of Philosophy (1972).

Influence and criticism

Différance became one of the most discussed — and contested — notions in late twentieth-century thought, influencing literary theory, architecture, and theology. Analytic philosophers and some linguists criticised it as obscure wordplay; defenders reply that the neologism performs exactly the instability of meaning it describes, and that the silent letter dramatises how writing exceeds and unsettles spoken presence.
