The Mauna Loa Observatory (MLO) is an atmospheric baseline station on the north flank of Mauna Loa volcano on the island of Hawaii. Operated by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), it has been a premier site for monitoring long-term changes in the composition of the atmosphere since the 1950s, thanks to its remote location, high altitude, and air largely undisturbed by local pollution or vegetation.

The Keeling Curve

The observatory is most famous for the continuous record of atmospheric carbon dioxide begun in March 1958 under Charles David Keeling of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography. Plotted over time, the measurements form the Keeling Curve, which shows both the seasonal breathing of the Northern Hemisphere's vegetation and the relentless year-on-year rise of CO₂ — from about 315 parts per million in 1958 to more than 420 ppm in the 2020s. The record is considered the iconic evidence of human-caused climate change.

Other measurements

MLO also measures methane, carbon monoxide, ozone, nitrous oxide, solar radiation, aerosols, and volcanic gases, and hosts instruments of numerous international agencies and universities, including solar observation equipment. Occasional volcanic outgassing from Mauna Loa itself must be filtered from the data.

Interruptions

Measurements were interrupted by the 1984 eruption of Mauna Loa and again by the November–December 2022 eruption, when lava flows cut the access road and power line, forcing a months-long pause during which NOAA relied on a temporary station on Mauna Kea. The CO₂ record from Mauna Loa remains the longest continuous instrumental record of atmospheric carbon dioxide in the world.
