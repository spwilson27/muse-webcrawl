Cell division

TypesMitosis, meiosis, binary fission
Part ofCell cycle
OutcomeTwo (mitosis) or four (meiosis) daughter cells

Cell division is the process by which a parent cell divides into two or more daughter cells. It is how organisms grow, repair tissue, and reproduce: in eukaryotes, cells usually divide as part of the cell cycle, either by mitosis, producing two genetically identical diploid daughters, or by meiosis, producing four genetically distinct haploid gametes. Prokaryotes divide by binary fission.


Stages of mitosis, the principal form of cell division in eukaryotes

Mitosis

Mitosis divides the duplicated chromosomes equally between two nuclei. Its phases are prophase (chromosomes condense, spindle forms), metaphase (chromosomes align at the equator), anaphase (sister chromatids separate), and telophase (new nuclei form), followed by cytokinesis, the division of the cytoplasm — by a cleavage furrow in animal cells or a cell plate in plant cells.

Meiosis

Meiosis consists of two successive divisions after a single round of DNA replication. Crossing over and independent assortment generate genetic variation in the resulting gametes (or spores), which is the cellular basis of sexual reproduction.

Regulation and disease

Progression through the cell cycle is controlled by cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases and monitored at checkpoints that detect DNA damage or spindle errors. Failure of these controls can allow damaged cells to proliferate, a hallmark of cancer; conversely, many chemotherapy drugs target dividing cells.
