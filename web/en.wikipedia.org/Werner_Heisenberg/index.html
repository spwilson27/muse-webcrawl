Werner Karl Heisenberg (5 December 1901 – 1 February 1976) was a German theoretical physicist and one of the main pioneers of quantum mechanics. He published his breakthrough paper in 1925, creating matrix mechanics, the first complete formulation of quantum mechanics, and in 1927 formulated the uncertainty principle, which states that the position and momentum of a particle cannot both be measured with arbitrary precision. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1932 "for the creation of quantum mechanics".

Early work

Heisenberg studied under Arnold Sommerfeld in Munich and worked with Max Born in Göttingen and Niels Bohr in Copenhagen. His 1925 "reinterpretation" paper, developed on the island of Heligoland, replaced classical orbits with arrays of observable transition quantities; Born and Pascual Jordan recognised these as matrices. Together with Bohr he became a principal architect of the Copenhagen interpretation. He also contributed the concept of isospin, foundational work on ferromagnetism and turbulence, and the theory of the atomic nucleus following Chadwick's discovery of the neutron (1932).

Wartime role

Heisenberg remained in Germany under the Nazi regime and, during the Second World War, became the leading scientific figure in the German nuclear weapons programme (the Uranverein). The programme never came close to a bomb, and historians continue to debate Heisenberg's motives, competence on the project and his 1941 meeting with Bohr in Copenhagen — later dramatised in Michael Frayn's play Copenhagen. He was detained by the Allies at Farm Hall in England in 1945, where his recorded reaction to the Hiroshima bombing has been much analysed.

Later life

After the war Heisenberg rebuilt German physics, directing the Max Planck Institute for Physics, championing international cooperation (he was involved in founding CERN), and pursuing a unified field theory. His popular book Physics and Philosophy (1958) and his memoir Physics and Beyond (1969) influenced wide audiences.
