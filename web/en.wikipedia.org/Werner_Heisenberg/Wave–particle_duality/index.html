Wave–particle duality

Wave–particle duality: quantum objects propagate like waves and interact like particles.

Wave–particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities of the universe, like photons and electrons, exhibit particle or wave properties according to the experimental circumstances. It expresses the inability of the classical concepts such as particle or wave to fully describe the behavior of quantum objects. During the 19th century it became accepted that light is a wave.

Albert Einstein postulated that light itself consists of localized particles (quanta), which explained the photoelectric effect in 1905. In 1924, Louis de Broglie proposed waves of matter, predicting that particles such as electrons should diffract, which was confirmed in 1927. Werner Heisenberg's uncertainty principle of 1927 placed a fundamental limit on simultaneously knowing complementary variables, underscoring that the duality is not merely a limitation of instruments but of nature.

Historical debates

Whether light is made of particles or waves was long debated by preeminent scientists: Huygens' wave theory and Newton's corpuscular theory competed until Young and Fresnel's interference experiments favored waves, and James Clerk Maxwell's equations identified light as an electromagnetic wave. The particle aspect returned with Planck's quanta, Einstein's photons, and Compton scattering.

Wave-particle duality and measurements

The duality plays a central role in interpretations of quantum mechanics. In the Copenhagen interpretation associated with Bohr and Heisenberg, a quantum system must be described by the experimental arrangement used to probe it. In modern quantum field theory, the duality is superseded: fields are fundamental, and both wave-like propagation and particle-like detection emerge naturally.
