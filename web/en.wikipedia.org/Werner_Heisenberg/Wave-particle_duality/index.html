Wave-particle duality

Single electrons arrive as particles yet accumulate into a wave-like interference pattern.

Wave-particle duality is the concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities of the universe, like photons and electrons, exhibit particle or wave properties according to the experimental circumstances. Werner Heisenberg's matrix mechanics (1925) and his uncertainty principle (1927) gave this duality its first rigorous mathematical form: the formalism dispenses with classical trajectories altogether, and conjugate quantities such as position and momentum obey the commutation relation [x, p] = iℏ.

Background

For centuries, theories of light alternated between particle (corpuscular) and wave models: Newton's corpuscles, Huygens' waves, Young's interference, and Fresnel's diffraction theory successively favored first one picture, then the other. Einstein's 1905 photon hypothesis for the photoelectric effect, Compton's 1923 scattering experiments, and de Broglie's 1924 matter-wave hypothesis forced the conclusion that both aspects coexist.

The Heisenberg picture

Heisenberg developed his theory in collaboration with Max Born and Pascual Jordan in Göttingen and through correspondence with Wolfgang Pauli. In his 1927 paper he derived the uncertainty relations from the formalism itself, arguing — with the famous gamma-ray microscope thought experiment — that any measurement of position disturbs momentum. Niels Bohr responded with the broader doctrine of complementarity, which Heisenberg came to accept: the wave and particle descriptions are mutually exclusive yet jointly necessary.

Modern view

In quantum field theory, particles are quantised excitations of fields that propagate according to wave equations; duality is thus a feature of approximation and measurement rather than an inconsistency. Experiments with electrons, neutrons, atoms, and large molecules continue to exhibit interference, confirming the principle across scales.
