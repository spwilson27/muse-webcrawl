East European Plain

The East European Plain (also called the Russian Plain) is a vast plain in Eastern Europe, the largest landscape region of Europe, extending roughly 4 million square kilometres from the Ural Mountains in the east to the Baltic Sea and the borders of Central Europe in the west, and from the Arctic Ocean's White and Barents Seas south to the Black and Caspian Seas.

Geography

The plain covers much of European Russia and extends into Ukraine, Belarus, the Baltic states, Moldova, Poland and parts of Kazakhstan and Romania. Its relief is mostly low and rolling, with an average elevation of about 170 metres; the Valdai Hills reach about 346 metres. It is crossed by some of Europe's longest rivers, including the Volga, Dnieper, Don, Dvina and Pechora, and includes uplands such as the Central Russian Upland, the Volga Upland and the Timan Ridge, together with lowlands such as the Caspian Depression, which lies below sea level.

Climate and vegetation

Climate ranges from tundra in the far north through taiga (coniferous forest), mixed forest and forest-steppe to arid steppe near the Black and Caspian Seas. The fertile black earth (chernozem) belt of the south is one of the world's great grain-producing regions.

Historical significance

The openness of the plain has shaped the history of Eastern Europe: it offered a corridor for migrations and invasions from the steppe, provided no natural barrier to the expansion of Kievan Rus' and later Russia, and was the theatre of vast campaigns in the Napoleonic Wars and the Second World War.
