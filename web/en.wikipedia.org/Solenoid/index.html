Solenoid

A solenoid (from the Greek solen, "pipe, channel") is a type of electromagnet formed by a helical coil of wire whose length is substantially greater than its diameter. When an electric current passes through the wire, the coil generates a controlled, nearly uniform magnetic field in the volume it encloses. Solenoids were first studied by André-Marie Ampère in the 1820s.

Magnetic field

For a long solenoid with n turns per unit length carrying current I, the magnetic field inside is approximately B = μ₀nI, directed along the axis, and is nearly zero outside. Adding a ferromagnetic core, such as iron, greatly increases the field strength by concentrating magnetic flux.

Applications

In engineering, "solenoid" also denotes an electromechanical actuator: a coil with a movable ferromagnetic plunger which is drawn into the coil when current flows, converting electrical energy into linear motion. Such devices operate valves (solenoid valves), door locks, relays, starter motors and fuel injectors. Solenoidal coils are also used as inductors in electronics, in MRI scanners and particle accelerators (where superconducting solenoid magnets focus beams), and in scientific instruments.
