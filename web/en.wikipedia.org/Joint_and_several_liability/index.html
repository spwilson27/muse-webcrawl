Joint and several liability

Joint and several liability
Legal areaTort law; contract law
EffectEach liable party is responsible for the whole obligation
ContrastSeveral (proportionate) liability

Joint and several liability is a legal doctrine under which two or more parties that are liable for the same loss are each responsible for the full amount of the obligation. The claimant may recover the entire sum from any one of them, leaving that party to seek contribution from the others.

Operation

In tort law, where multiple defendants contribute to a single indivisible injury, the traditional common-law rule allows the plaintiff to collect the whole judgment from the wealthiest or most solvent defendant (sometimes called the "deep pocket"). That defendant may then sue co-defendants for contribution according to their share of fault. In contract, partners in a partnership and co-signatories of an obligation are commonly jointly and severally liable.

Reform

Because the rule can impose the full burden on a defendant only slightly at fault, many jurisdictions in the United States, Australia and elsewhere have reformed it — replacing it wholly or partly with several liability, under which each defendant pays only its proportionate share, or with hybrid thresholds based on percentage of fault.

Rationale

Supporters argue the doctrine protects plaintiffs from bearing the risk of an insolvent or absent wrongdoer; critics argue it is unfair to minor contributors and encourages litigation aimed at wealthy defendants.
