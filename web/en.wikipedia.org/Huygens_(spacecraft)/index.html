Huygens was an atmospheric entry probe developed by the European Space Agency and carried to Saturn's moon Titan by NASA's Cassini spacecraft. Named after the Dutch astronomer Christiaan Huygens, who discovered Titan in 1655, it landed on Titan on 14 January 2005—the most distant landing ever made by a spacecraft—and transmitted data from the surface for over an hour.

Mission

The Cassini–Huygens mission was launched on 15 October 1997 and entered orbit around Saturn on 1 July 2004. Huygens was released from the Cassini orbiter on 25 December 2004 and coasted for 20 days before entering Titan's atmosphere on 14 January 2005. Protected by a heat shield, it decelerated from about 6 km/s and deployed a sequence of parachutes. During the roughly 2-hour 27-minute descent, its six instruments measured the atmosphere's composition, temperature, pressure, and winds, and photographed the surface.

Landing and results

Huygens touched down on a surface resembling a dry lakebed strewn with icy pebbles, at a landing site later informally associated with the feature Adiri. Images showed channels and shorelines carved by liquid methane. The probe continued transmitting from the surface for at least 72 minutes until its batteries faded and Cassini passed below the horizon. A design flaw in the Cassini receiver meant that roughly half of the 700 planned images were lost, though the surviving images and the full set of in-situ measurements were scientifically rich. A software error in the Doppler wind experiment was largely recovered using radio telescopes on Earth.

Instruments

The payload comprised the Huygens Atmospheric Structure Instrument (HASI), the Doppler Wind Experiment (DWE), the Descent Imager/Spectral Radiometer (DISR), the Gas Chromatograph Mass Spectrometer (GCMS), the Aerosol Collector and Pyrolyser (ACP), and the Surface Science Package (SSP), which measured the mechanical and electrical properties of the surface at impact.
