Tweed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tweed is a rough, woollen fabric, of a soft, open, flexible texture, resembling cheviot or homespun, but more closely woven. It is usually woven with a plain weave, twill or herringbone structure. Colour effects in the yarn may be obtained by mixing dyed wool before it is spun.

History and name

Tweed originated in Ireland and Scotland as a practical cloth for crofters and outdoor work, valued for its warmth, durability and resistance to wind and moisture. The name is often said to derive from a misreading of the Scots word tweel (twill), associating it with the River Tweed; whatever its origin, the name was in commercial use by the 1830s.

Types

  • Harris Tweed — handwoven by islanders in the Outer Hebrides of Scotland from pure virgin wool; protected by the Harris Tweed Act 1993 and its Orb trademark
  • Donegal tweed — from County Donegal, Ireland, characterised by colourful flecks in the yarn
  • Cheviot tweed, Saxony tweed and district checks woven for sporting estates

Uses

Traditionally worn for shooting, fishing and country pursuits — complete Norfolk jackets, breeches and flat caps — tweed was popularised by the British aristocracy and later adopted by academics and fashion designers. Coco Chanel adapted it for women's suits, and it remains a staple of autumn and winter tailoring.
