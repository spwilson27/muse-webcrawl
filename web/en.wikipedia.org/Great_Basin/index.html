Great Basin

Great Basin
LocationWestern United States (chiefly Nevada and Utah)
AreaAbout 540,000 km² (209,000 sq mi)
Highest pointBoundary Peak, Nevada (4,007 m)
Defining featureEndorheic drainage; basin and range topography

The Great Basin is the largest area of contiguous endorheic watersheds — those with no outlet to the sea — in North America. It spans most of Nevada, much of Utah, and portions of California, Idaho, Oregon, Wyoming, and Baja California, and is bounded by the Sierra Nevada to the west and the Wasatch Range to the east.

Geography

The region is characterised by basin and range topography: parallel mountain ranges separated by broad desert valleys. Rivers such as the Humboldt, Bear, and Truckee terminate in sinks and salt lakes, most famously the Great Salt Lake, a remnant of Pleistocene Lake Bonneville. The Great Basin Desert, dominated by sagebrush steppe, is the largest desert in the United States.

History

Indigenous peoples including the Shoshone, Paiute, and Ute have lived in the region for millennia. John C. Frémont coined the name "Great Basin" after his expeditions of the 1840s recognised its internal drainage. Mormon settlers arrived in 1847, and mining booms later dotted the region with towns.

Ecology

The Great Basin hosts ancient bristlecone pines, among the oldest living organisms on Earth, and large areas are protected in Great Basin National Park, established in 1986.
