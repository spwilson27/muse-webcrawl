A camera is an optical instrument that captures an image. At its most basic it consists of a light-tight box with an opening (aperture) at one end through which light enters and a recording surface — photographic film or an electronic image sensor — at the other. Most cameras also have a lens to focus the light, a shutter to control exposure time, and a viewfinder or screen for composing the shot.

History

The camera descends from the camera obscura, described in antiquity and used by artists for centuries, in which an image of a scene is projected through a small hole into a dark room. Photography became possible in the 1820s when Nicéphore Niépce recorded a permanent image using bitumen-coated pewter; Louis Daguerre's daguerreotype (1839) and Talbot's calotype followed. George Eastman's roll-film cameras, beginning with the Kodak of 1888 ("You press the button, we do the rest"), brought photography to the mass market. The 35 mm Leica (1925), the single-lens reflex, instant cameras and finally digital sensors (from the 1990s) successively transformed the medium, and in the 21st century the smartphone became the world's dominant camera.

How a camera works

Light from the scene passes through the lens, whose aperture (expressed as an f-number) controls brightness and depth of field. The shutter opens for a set time — the exposure — during which light strikes the film or sensor. In a digital camera the sensor converts photons to electrical signals that are processed into an image file. Exposure is governed by the combination of aperture, shutter speed and sensitivity (ISO).

Types

Common types include compact (point-and-shoot) cameras, DSLRs, mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras, medium- and large-format cameras, rangefinders, twin-lens reflexes, instant cameras, action cameras and the cameras integrated into mobile phones. Motion-picture and video cameras record sequences of images instead of single frames.
