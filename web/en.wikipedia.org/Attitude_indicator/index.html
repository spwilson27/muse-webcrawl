Attitude indicator

The attitude indicator (AI), also known as the gyro horizon or artificial horizon, is a flight instrument that informs the pilot of the aircraft's orientation relative to the Earth's horizon. It shows pitch (fore and aft tilt) and bank (side-to-side tilt) and is indispensable for flying in instrument meteorological conditions, when the natural horizon cannot be seen.

Operation

Traditionally the instrument is driven by a gyroscope spun by vacuum or electricity, whose rigidity in space keeps a miniature horizon bar aligned with the true horizon. A fixed symbol representing the aircraft moves against the bar and a graduated pitch ladder, while a roll pointer indicates bank angle against markings at 10, 20, 30, 45 and 60 degrees. Modern airliners replace the mechanical gyro with solid-state attitude and heading reference systems (AHRS) feeding electronic flight displays.

History

Elmer Sperry developed early gyroscopic horizons; his son Lawrence demonstrated a practical artificial horizon in 1918, and Jimmy Doolittle used Sperry instruments for the first "blind" take-off, flight and landing in 1929. The instrument is one of the traditional "six pack" of basic flight instruments, together with the airspeed indicator, altimeter, turn and slip indicator, heading indicator and vertical speed indicator.

Limitations

Vacuum-driven gyros can tumble during extreme manoeuvres and suffer precession errors in prolonged turns; pilots are trained to cross-check the AI against other instruments, since a failed attitude indicator has contributed to accidents involving spatial disorientation.
