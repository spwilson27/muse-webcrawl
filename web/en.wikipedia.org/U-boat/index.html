U-boat

U-boat is an anglophone term for a military submarine of German origin, derived from the German word U-Boot, a contraction of Unterseeboot ("undersea boat"). U-boats were most famously used by the Imperial German Navy in the First World War and by the Kriegsmarine in the Second World War, in campaigns of commerce raiding against Allied shipping.

First World War

Germany entered the First World War with a small submarine force but quickly recognized its potential against British trade. Unrestricted submarine warfare — sinking merchant ships without warning — was declared in 1915 and again in February 1917; the sinking of the liner RMS Lusitania in May 1915, with the loss of 1,198 lives including 128 Americans, caused international outrage. The resumption of unrestricted warfare in 1917 helped bring the United States into the war. The adoption of the convoy system sharply reduced U-boat successes.

Second World War

In the Battle of the Atlantic, U-boats organized in "wolfpacks" (Rudeltaktik) coordinated by Admiral Karl Dönitz attacked convoys at night on the surface. They sank some 14 million tons of Allied shipping. The tide turned in 1943 ("Black May") through a combination of convoy escorts, long-range aircraft closing the mid-Atlantic air gap, centimetric radar, high-frequency direction finding, and signals intelligence from decrypted Enigma traffic. Of the roughly 1,162 U-boats commissioned, about 785 were lost, and some 28,000 of the 40,000 men who served in them were killed — the highest casualty rate of any German service. Late-war innovations such as the snorkel and the high-underwater-speed Type XXI "Elektroboot" arrived too late to change the outcome but influenced postwar submarine design worldwide.
