Hockey puck


A standard ice hockey puck

A hockey puck is a disk made of vulcanised rubber used in ice hockey and related sports. Standard pucks are black, 1 inch (25.4 mm) thick, 3 inches (76.2 mm) in diameter, and weigh between 5.5 and 6 ounces (156–170 g). The puck serves the role of a ball in other games: players propel it with hockey sticks toward the opposing goal. The word "puck" is attested in print from the 1870s in Montreal, where organised indoor ice hockey began; its etymology is uncertain, possibly from a Scots or Irish word for a poke or blow.

History

Early Canadian hockey used wooden balls, lumps of frozen cow dung or roughly cut wooden disks. Rubber pucks appeared in the 1870s–80s, reportedly first made by slicing a rubber ball; the flat-sided vulcanised disk soon became standard. William F. Robertson is often credited with early rubber puck manufacture. From 1996–2000, the NHL briefly used "smart pucks" with embedded electronics for television tracking (FoxTrax), and modern leagues employ pucks with sensors for player and puck tracking data. Pucks are frozen before games to reduce bouncing.

Variants and manufacture

Pucks are compression-moulded from rubber compounds; the NHL logo and league markings are silk-screened or inlaid. Lighter blue pucks (4 oz) are used for youth training, and heavier red pucks (10 oz) for strength training. Street and roller hockey use plastic pucks designed to slide on dry surfaces; underwater hockey uses a weighted puck coated in plastic. Sponge pucks serve informal play. The world record for the hardest recorded shot propelled a puck at over 110 mph (177 km/h) in skills competitions.
