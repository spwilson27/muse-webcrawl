Cogeneration

Cogeneration, also known as combined heat and power (CHP), is the simultaneous generation of electricity and useful heat from a single fuel source. By capturing heat that would otherwise be wasted in conventional power generation, cogeneration plants can achieve overall efficiencies of 80% or more, compared with roughly 50% for separate generation.

Also known asCombined heat and power (CHP)
OutputsElectricity, heat
Typical efficiency65–90%
FuelsNatural gas, biomass, coal, waste heat

How it works

A prime mover — such as a gas turbine, steam turbine, or reciprocating engine — drives a generator to produce electricity. Exhaust gases or steam pass through a heat recovery unit, supplying hot water or steam for industrial processes, district heating, or absorption cooling (trigeneration).

Applications

Cogeneration is widely used in hospitals, universities, factories, refineries, and district heating networks, notably in Denmark, Finland, and the Netherlands, where CHP supplies a large share of electricity. Small-scale micro-CHP units serve individual buildings.

Advantages and limits

Benefits include reduced fuel consumption, lower carbon emissions, and improved energy security. CHP is most economical where heat and power demands coincide year-round; excess heat in summer can limit savings.
