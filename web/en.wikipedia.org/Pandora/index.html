In Greek mythology, Pandora (Greek: Πανδώρα, from πᾶν, "all", and δῶρον, "gift", hence "all-gifted" or "all-giving") was the first human woman created by Hephaestus on the instructions of Zeus. As Hesiod related it in his Works and Days, each god cooperated in giving her unique gifts; her other name, Anesidora, means the one who "sends up gifts".

The myth

When Prometheus stole fire from heaven, Zeus took vengeance by presenting Pandora to Prometheus's brother Epimetheus. Pandora was given a storage jar (pithos) as a wedding gift and warned not to open it; driven by curiosity, she opened it, releasing all the evils of humanity — sickness, death and many other unspecified troubles — into the world. When she closed the lid again, only one thing remained inside: Hope (Elpis), which remained within, near the edge of the jar, and did not fly out.

"Pandora's box"

The phrase "Pandora's box" derives from the jar in the story: the Greek pithos was mistranslated as "box" (Latin pyxis) in the influential retelling by Erasmus in his Adagia (1508), and the mistaken term stuck. Proverbially, "opening Pandora's box" means to begin something that causes many unforeseen problems. The figure of Pandora has been compared with the biblical Eve, and her story functions as an aetiological myth explaining why humans must work and suffer.

In art and culture

Pandora appears on Greek vase painting, including scenes of her creation (sometimes shown rising from the ground, like a statue being adorned by the gods), and she has been a recurring subject in Western painting and literature, including works by Dante Gabriel Rossetti and Odilon Redon. Her name is also given to a moon of Saturn and to the asteroid 55 Pandora.
