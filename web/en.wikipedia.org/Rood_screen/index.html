Rood screen

A medieval rood screen in an English parish church

A rood screen (also called a choir screen, chancel screen, or jube) is a partition, usually richly decorated, that separated the chancel of a church from the nave in late medieval Christian churches. It was surmounted by a cross (the "rood", from Old English rod, meaning cross) flanked by statues of the Virgin Mary and St John the Evangelist.

Rood screens were common throughout western Europe from the 14th to the 16th centuries, particularly in England, France, Germany and the Low Countries. They served both a practical purpose—separating the clergy and choir from the lay congregation—and a symbolic one, marking the threshold between the earthly realm of the nave and the sacred space of the sanctuary.

Structure and decoration

The screen typically consisted of a solid lower portion rising to about waist height, topped by open tracery through which the high altar could be glimpsed. Above the screen ran a gallery or loft, from which readings might be given or music performed. The rood itself—a large crucifix—stood on the loft or hung above it, often accompanied by figures of Mary and John, and sometimes by angels bearing the instruments of the Passion.

Screens were made of stone or wood and were often painted in bright colours and gilded. Carved decoration included foliage, saints, and heraldic shields of benefactors. In England, the finest surviving examples include the screens at St Fiacre in Brittany (technically French work admired by English craftsmen), and those in Devon churches such as Atherington and Chivelstone.

History

The division of the church into separate spaces for clergy and laity has ancient roots, but the elaborate rood screen developed in the later Middle Ages alongside the growth of parish church building and the elaboration of the liturgy. The screen reinforced the mystery of the Mass, much of which was performed out of the congregation's full view.

During the English Reformation, roods and their imagery were targeted as idolatrous; most were dismantled under Edward VI and Elizabeth I, though many stone and wooden screens survived, stripped of their figures. The Victorian Gothic Revival restored interest in chancel screens, and many were reinstated or newly built in the 19th century.
