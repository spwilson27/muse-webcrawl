Dramaturgy (sociology)

Dramaturgy is a sociological perspective that analyses social interaction as if it were a theatrical performance. The approach was developed principally by the Canadian-American sociologist Erving Goffman in his book The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1956, expanded 1959).

Key concepts

In Goffman's model, individuals are actors performing roles before an audience. They manage the impression they make through their "front" — the setting, appearance, and manner of the performance. Goffman distinguishes:

  • Front stage — where the actor performs for an audience and follows conventions appropriate to the role (for example, a waiter's behaviour in a dining room).
  • Back stage — where the actor can relax, drop the role, and prepare or contradict the front-stage performance (for example, the kitchen).
  • Impression management — the efforts actors make to control how others perceive them, including concealing mistakes and "saving face".

Influence

Dramaturgy is a central concept of symbolic interactionism and has influenced communication studies, social psychology, and organisational research. Critics have noted that the metaphor can understate coercion and sincerity in social life, but the vocabulary of front stage/back stage has passed into general usage.
