Akira Kurosawa

Akira Kurosawa (23 March 1910 – 6 September 1998) was a Japanese film director and screenwriter widely regarded as one of the most important and influential filmmakers in cinema history. In a career spanning 57 years he directed 30 films, including Rashomon (1950), Ikiru (1952), Seven Samurai (1954), Throne of Blood (1957), Yojimbo (1961), Kagemusha (1980), and Ran (1985).

Career

Kurosawa entered the film industry in 1936 as an assistant director at PCL (later Toho). His international breakthrough came when Rashomon, with its innovative multi-perspective narrative, won the Golden Lion at Venice in 1951, opening Western markets to Japanese cinema. Seven Samurai is frequently ranked among the greatest films ever made and was remade in Hollywood as The Magnificent Seven; Yojimbo inspired Sergio Leone's A Fistful of Dollars, and his The Hidden Fortress influenced George Lucas's Star Wars. His signature techniques include telephoto lenses, multiple cameras, axial cuts, and dramatic use of weather.

Later years and legacy

After commercial setbacks in the late 1960s, Kurosawa returned with the Soviet co-production Dersu Uzala (1975), which won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film, and the epics Kagemusha and Ran, supported by admirers including Lucas and Francis Ford Coppola. He received an Academy Honorary Award in 1990. Kurosawa's humanism and visual dynamism profoundly shaped world cinema and generations of directors.
