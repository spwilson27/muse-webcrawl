The Dreyfus affair (French: affaire Dreyfus) was a political scandal that divided French society from 1894 until its resolution in 1906. Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery officer in the French Army, was convicted of treason in 1894 for allegedly passing military secrets to the German Empire, on the basis of a handwriting attribution to a torn memorandum (the bordereau) recovered from a wastebasket at the German embassy. He was publicly degraded and sentenced to life imprisonment on Devil's Island in French Guiana.

Exposure of the miscarriage

Evidence soon pointed to the real culprit, Major Ferdinand Walsin Esterhazy, but the army suppressed it; intelligence officer Georges Picquart, who uncovered the truth, was himself punished. When Esterhazy was acquitted in January 1898, novelist Émile Zola published his famous open letter J'Accuse…! accusing the military of a cover-up; Zola was convicted of libel and fled to England. France split into Dreyfusards and anti-Dreyfusards, with anti-Semitism poisoning the debate. Forged documents by Major Hubert-Joseph Henry, exposed in 1898, brought Henry's suicide and a retrial at Rennes in 1899, which again convicted Dreyfus with "extenuating circumstances"; he was pardoned days later.

Resolution

In 1906 the Court of Cassation annulled the Rennes verdict and fully exonerated Dreyfus, who was reinstated as a major and made a knight of the Legion of Honour. The affair reshaped French politics, strengthened the Radical and socialist left, contributed to the 1905 law separating church and state, and remains a landmark case of wrongful conviction, institutional cover-up, and antisemitism.
