Bhakti

Bhakti (Sanskrit: भक्ति) is a term used in Indian religions meaning attachment, devotion to, fondness for, and loving devotion towards a personal god or a representational god. It is a central concept in Hinduism, where it denotes devotion and love for a personal god, and appears also in Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. In Hinduism, bhakti is often described as a path to spiritual liberation (moksha) alongside the paths of knowledge (jnana) and action (karma).

Origins

The concept receives an early and influential exposition in the Bhagavad Gita, where Krishna teaches that those who set their hearts on him with faith and devotion attain the highest goal. Between the 6th and 9th centuries CE, the Tamil poet-saints — the Alvars devoted to Vishnu and the Nayanars devoted to Shiva — spread intense, emotional devotion through vernacular hymn-singing in South India.

The bhakti movement

From roughly the 7th century onward, and with great vigour from the 15th to 17th centuries, the bhakti movement swept across India. Its saint-poets — including Ramananda, Kabir, Mirabai, Tulsidas, Surdas, Guru Nanak, Chaitanya Mahaprabhu, and Tukaram — composed devotional songs in regional languages, emphasized a direct relationship with God over ritual and caste, and in some cases challenged social hierarchy. Bhakti reshaped Indian religion, literature, music, and art, and lies at the root of major traditions including Gaudiya Vaishnavism, the Varkari pilgrimage, and Sikhism.
