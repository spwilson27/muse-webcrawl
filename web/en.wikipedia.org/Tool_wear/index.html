Tool wear

FieldMachining, manufacturing engineering

In machining, tool wear is the gradual failure of cutting tools due to regular operation. It is a normal phenomenon in metal cutting, affecting the geometry of the tool edge and thereby the accuracy, surface finish and forces of the machining process. Tool wear is quantified by measuring features such as the width of the flank wear land, and tools are replaced or re-sharpened when wear reaches a criterion defined in standards such as ISO 3685.

Types of wear

Flank wear occurs on the clearance face of the tool where it rubs against the newly machined surface, and is the most commonly used wear criterion. Crater wear forms a concave depression on the rake face where the hot chip slides across it, driven largely by diffusion and abrasion at high temperatures. Notch wear develops at the depth-of-cut line, while chipping and fracture are abrupt edge failures associated with interrupted cuts, vibration or excessive load. A built-up edge — workpiece material welded to the edge — is sometimes classed with wear phenomena because it alters the effective tool geometry.

Mechanisms

The fundamental wear mechanisms are abrasion by hard particles in the workpiece, adhesion and attrition, diffusion of tool material into the chip at high interface temperatures, chemical (oxidative) wear, and plastic deformation of the edge under stress and heat. Their relative importance depends on cutting speed, feed, tool material and workpiece material: abrasion dominates at low speeds, while diffusion and plastic deformation dominate at high speeds.

Tool life

The relationship between cutting speed and tool life is classically described by the Taylor tool-life equation, V·Tn = C, where V is cutting speed, T is tool life and n and C are constants for a given tool–workpiece combination. Tool wear progresses through a break-in stage of rapid initial wear, a steady-state stage of roughly uniform wear rate, and an accelerated stage leading to failure. Managing tool wear — through tool materials and coatings, cutting fluids, optimised parameters and condition monitoring — is central to economical machining, since tool costs and tool-change downtime are major components of production cost.
