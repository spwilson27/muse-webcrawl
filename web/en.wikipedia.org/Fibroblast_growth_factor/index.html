Fibroblast growth factor

Fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) are a family of cell signalling proteins produced by macrophages and involved in a wide variety of processes, most notably as crucial elements for normal development in animal cells. Any irregularities in their function have been shown to lead to a range of developmental defects.

Family members

In humans, 22 members of the FGF family have been identified, numbered FGF1 through FGF23. They signal through four fibroblast growth factor receptors (FGFR1–FGFR4), which are receptor tyrosine kinases. Most FGFs are secreted and act locally (paracrine), while the endocrine FGFs (FGF19, FGF21, FGF23) circulate and regulate metabolism, bile acid synthesis, and phosphate homeostasis, using Klotho proteins as co-receptors.

Functions

FGFs are multifunctional proteins with a wide variety of target cells. They stimulate the proliferation of fibroblasts and endothelial cells, promote angiogenesis, and play key roles in embryonic development — including limb patterning, neural development, and lung morphogenesis — as well as in wound healing and tissue repair in the adult organism.

Clinical significance

Mutations in FGFR genes cause skeletal disorders such as achondroplasia (FGFR3) and craniosynostosis syndromes (FGFR1 and FGFR2). Aberrant FGF signalling is implicated in several cancers, and FGFR inhibitors have been approved for certain cholangiocarcinomas and urothelial cancers. The related protein filaggrin is not an FGF but another protein of importance in human disease.
