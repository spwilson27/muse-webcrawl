Zucchini

The zucchini (American English) or courgette (British English) is a summer squash — the immature fruit of certain cultivars of Cucurbita pepo, the same species that produces pumpkins and acorn squash. The name zucchini comes from Italian (a diminutive of zucca, "gourd"); courgette is the French diminutive of courge. Although botanically a fruit (a pepo, or berry with a hard rind), the zucchini is treated as a vegetable in cooking.

Description

The zucchini plant has a bushy, non-vining habit with large, dark-green, sometimes silver-mottled leaves and large golden-yellow male and female flowers, both of which are edible. The fruit is typically harvested while still immature — usually under 20 cm long — when the skin is tender and glossy and the seeds are soft. Left to grow, a zucchini can become a marrow more than a metre long. The standard fruit is cylindrical and deep green, but cultivars also come in yellow ("golden zucchini"), pale green, round, and striped forms.

History and cultivation

Squashes were domesticated in Mesoamerica thousands of years ago and brought to Europe after the Columbian exchange. However, the modern zucchini type was selected in Italy in the late 19th century, near Milan, and spread worldwide with Italian emigration. Zucchini is famously easy to grow in temperate gardens and very prolific in midsummer — a fecundity celebrated by jokes and by annual festivals, such as zucchini races and "sneak a zucchini onto your neighbour's porch" traditions in North America. The world's heaviest zucchini have weighed over 50 kg.

Culinary use

Zucchini is mild in flavour and is eaten raw in salads, grilled, sautéed, roasted, baked into breads and cakes (as zucchini bread), spiralized into "zoodles" as a pasta substitute, and combined with other summer vegetables in dishes such as ratatouille. The flowers are stuffed and fried in Italian and Mexican cuisines. Very large or stressed fruits can accumulate bitter cucurbitacins and should not be eaten if intensely bitter.
