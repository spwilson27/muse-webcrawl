Incense trade route

The Incense trade route was a network of ancient trade routes linking the Mediterranean world with the sources of frankincense and myrrh in southern Arabia and the Horn of Africa. Flourishing roughly from the 3rd century BC to the 2nd century AD, it carried aromatics northward by camel caravan through the Arabian Peninsula to ports and markets such as Gaza and Damascus, from which they reached the wider Greco-Roman world.

Route

Frankincense was harvested in Dhofar (modern Oman) and the Hadhramaut (modern Yemen), and myrrh in Arabia and Somalia. Caravans assembled at Shabwah in the Hadhramaut and travelled north through Mecca and the Hejaz to Petra, capital of the Nabataean kingdom, which grew rich taxing and servicing the traffic. From Petra the goods continued to the Mediterranean port of Gaza or inland to Damascus. A journey of some 62 stages and roughly 1,600 miles (2,600 km) could take about two months.

Decline

The route declined as maritime trade through the Red Sea expanded, allowing ships to carry incense and Indian Ocean goods directly to Egypt, and as the demand for frankincense fell with the Christianisation of the Roman Empire, which discouraged its use in funerary and pagan ritual.

World Heritage

In 2005 UNESCO inscribed the desert cities of the Negev — Haluza, Mamshit, Avdat and Shivta — as the Incense Route – Desert Cities in the Negev World Heritage Site, recognising the route's outstanding significance to ancient commerce.
