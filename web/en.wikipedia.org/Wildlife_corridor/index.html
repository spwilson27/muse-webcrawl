Wildlife corridor

A wildlife corridor is an area of habitat that connects wildlife populations separated by human activities or structures such as roads, development or logging. Corridors may allow animals to move between patches of habitat for feeding, mating, migration and recolonisation, increasing genetic diversity and the survival of populations in fragmented landscapes.

Types

Corridors range from natural features — riverbanks, hedgerows and strips of forest — to purpose-built structures such as wildlife overpasses and underpasses ("ecoducts"), fish ladders and amphibian tunnels. Landscape-scale initiatives link whole protected areas: the Yellowstone-to-Yukon Conservation Initiative in North America and the Mesoamerican Biological Corridor are prominent examples.

Benefits and debate

Experiments and monitoring show that corridors increase movement between patches, raise plant and animal diversity, and can reduce roadkill when combined with fencing. Critics have noted that corridors are costly, may spread disease, invasive species or fire between patches, and that protecting larger core habitats should take priority; scientific opinion generally supports corridors as part of a broader conservation strategy.
