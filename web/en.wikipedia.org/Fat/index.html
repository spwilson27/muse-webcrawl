In nutrition, biology and chemistry, fat usually refers to any ester of fatty acids, or a mixture of such compounds, most commonly those that occur in living beings or in food. Chemically, fats are triglycerides — triesters of glycerol and any of several fatty acids. Fats may be solid or liquid at room temperature, depending on their structure and composition; those solid at room temperature are often called fats, while liquid ones are called oils.

Types of fatty acids

Fatty acids are classified as saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated according to the number of double bonds in their carbon chains. Saturated fats, common in meat and dairy products, have no double bonds and tend to be solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fats, abundant in vegetable oils, nuts and oily fish, contain one or more double bonds. Industrial partial hydrogenation produces trans fats, whose consumption is associated with increased cardiovascular risk and which many jurisdictions now restrict.

Biological role

Fats are one of the three main macronutrient groups in human food, alongside carbohydrates and proteins. They are the most energy-dense macronutrient, providing roughly 9 kilocalories per gram. In the body, fat stored in adipose tissue insulates against heat loss, cushions organs, and serves as a long-term energy reserve. Dietary fat is also required for the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) and provides essential fatty acids such as omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids that the body cannot synthesise itself.
