Philosophical fiction

Philosophical fiction is fiction in which a significant portion of the work is devoted to the exploration of philosophical questions — such as the nature of reality, knowledge, morality, free will, and the meaning of life — through narrative rather than exposition. The category includes the philosophical novel, in which characters debate or embody ideas at length.

Characteristics

Where other fiction may merely touch on philosophical themes, philosophical fiction makes the testing of ideas central to the plot: thought experiments are dramatized, and characters often function as spokespeople for rival positions. The tradition reaches from Plato's dialogues and Lucretius's poem De rerum natura through medieval allegory to the conte philosophique of the Enlightenment.

Examples

Canonical examples include Voltaire's Candide (1759), an attack on Leibnizian optimism; Diderot's Jacques the Fatalist; Dostoevsky's novels, especially The Brothers Karamazov, which stage conflicts of faith, reason, and morality; Thomas Mann's The Magic Mountain; Sartre's Nausea and Camus's The Stranger, vehicles of existentialism; Ayn Rand's Atlas Shrugged; and Robert M. Pirsig's Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance. Science fiction often serves philosophical ends, as in the works of Stanisław Lem and Ursula K. Le Guin.
