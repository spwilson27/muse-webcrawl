Nicholas II (Nikolai Aleksandrovich Romanov; 18 May 1868 – 17 July 1918) was the last Emperor of Russia, ruling from 1894 until his abdication in March 1917, during the February Revolution. His reign saw Imperial Russia transformed from one of the foremost great powers into a state collapsing under military defeat and revolution.

Reign

Nicholas succeeded his father Alexander III in 1894 and married Princess Alix of Hesse (Alexandra). His coronation festivities were marred by the Khodynka Tragedy, a stampede that killed more than a thousand people. Determined to preserve autocracy, he nonetheless oversaw rapid industrialisation under ministers such as Sergei Witte. Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) and the shooting of peaceful petitioners on Bloody Sunday (1905) ignited the Revolution of 1905, forcing him to issue the October Manifesto and create the Duma — concessions he later curtailed.

First World War and fall

The imperial family's reliance on the mystic Grigori Rasputin, valued for his apparent help with the haemophilia of Nicholas's son Alexei, deeply damaged the monarchy's prestige. In 1915 Nicholas took personal command of the army in the First World War, leaving Alexandra and Rasputin's influence dominant at home. Military failure, food shortages and inflation brought the February Revolution of 1917; Nicholas abdicated on 15 March 1917, ending three centuries of Romanov rule.

Death and legacy

After the Bolsheviks seized power, the family was exiled to Siberia and then Yekaterinburg, where Nicholas, Alexandra, their five children and four retainers were shot and bayoneted by their guards on the night of 16–17 July 1918. Their remains were identified in the 1990s and reburied in St Petersburg's Peter and Paul Cathedral. The Russian Orthodox Church canonised the family as passion bearers in 2000.
