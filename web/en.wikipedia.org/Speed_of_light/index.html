The speed of light in vacuum, denoted c, is a universal physical constant: exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. According to special relativity, c is the upper limit for the speed at which matter, energy or information can travel, and it is the same for all observers regardless of their motion.

History of measurement

Galileo tried and failed to measure light's speed with lanterns. Ole Rømer first demonstrated in 1676, from the timing of eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io, that light travels at a finite speed. Fizeau (1849) and Foucault obtained good terrestrial measurements; Michelson refined them over decades. Since 1983 the metre has been defined as the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 of a second, making c exact.

Role in physics

c appears throughout physics: in Maxwell's equations it relates the permittivity and permeability of free space; in relativity it links space and time and enters the mass–energy equivalence E = mc². Light slows in transparent media according to the refractive index, but no signal within the medium can exceed c.

Communication delays

The finite speed of light causes the ~1.3-second delay in Earth–Moon communication and delays of up to about 22 minutes (one way) to Mars probes, and lets telescopes observe distant galaxies as they were billions of years ago.
