Phalloidin

Fluorescently labelled phalloidin reveals actin filaments (in red) in a cell.

Phalloidin is a bicyclic heptapeptide belonging to the phallotoxin family of toxins isolated from the death cap mushroom Amanita phalloides. It was among the first of the phallotoxins to be characterized, isolated by Wieland and co-workers in the 1930s. Phalloidin binds with high affinity and specificity to filamentous actin (F-actin) and stabilizes it against depolymerization.

Phalloidin's unusual structure includes a cross-link between a cysteine and a tryptophan (a tryptathionine bond) forming a bicyclic peptide. Its binding site lies at the interface between adjacent actin subunits in a filament, which both explains its stabilizing effect and why it does not appreciably bind monomeric G-actin. Unlike the amatoxins from the same mushroom (such as α-amanitin), phalloidin is poorly absorbed from the gut, so it contributes little to human death cap poisoning when ingested; it is, however, highly toxic when injected into the bloodstream, where it damages the liver.

Use in research

Phalloidin conjugated to fluorescent dyes (such as FITC, rhodamine, or the Alexa Fluor dyes) is one of the most widely used probes in cell biology. Because it stains F-actin selectively, fluorescent phalloidin is routinely used in fluorescence microscopy to visualize the actin cytoskeleton, stress fibers, and actin-rich structures in fixed and permeabilized cells. It cannot normally cross the membranes of living cells, limiting its use to fixed specimens.
