Epicureanism is a system of philosophy founded around 307 BC by the Greek philosopher Epicurus. It teaches that the purpose of life is to attain pleasure — understood chiefly as the absence of physical pain (aponia) and mental disturbance (ataraxia) — through modest living, friendship, and the avoidance of fear, above all the fear of the gods and of death. Despite its later reputation for indulgence, Epicureanism advocated a simple, even ascetic, life.

History

Epicurus taught in Athens in a house and garden — the school became known simply as "the Garden" — and admitted women and enslaved people as members, which was unusual for the time. He wrote voluminously, but most of his works are lost; his doctrines survive in three letters and collections of sayings preserved by Diogenes Laertius, and in the great didactic poem De Rerum Natura ("On the Nature of Things") by the Roman poet Lucretius. Carbonised papyrus scrolls from the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum preserve works by the Epicurean philosopher Philodemus.

Epicureanism flourished in the Roman world for some six centuries before declining under Christian criticism, which caricatured it as a doctrine of gluttony and denial of providence. It was revived in the seventeenth century by Pierre Gassendi and influenced early modern materialism and utilitarian thought.

Doctrine

Epicurean physics is atomism inherited from Democritus: everything, including the soul, is made of atoms moving in the void, and the universe is unguided by purpose. The gods exist but dwell in blissful detachment and do not intervene in human affairs. Death is nothing to us, since where we are, death is not, and where death is, we are not.

Ethically, Epicureans classified desires as natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, or vain; only the first should be pursued without limit. The tetrapharmakos, or "four-part cure", summarises the teaching: God is nothing to fear; death is nothing to worry about; what is good is easy to have; what is terrible is easy to endure.
