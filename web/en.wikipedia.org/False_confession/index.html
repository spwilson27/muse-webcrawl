False confession

A false confession is an admission of guilt for a crime for which the confessor is not responsible. False confessions can be induced through coercion, exhaustion, deception or the vulnerability of the suspect, and are a documented cause of wrongful convictions: DNA exoneration studies have found false confessions in a substantial minority of overturned convictions.

Types

Following the psychologist Saul Kassin, false confessions are usually classified as voluntary (offered without pressure, sometimes to protect another person or from a desire for notoriety), coerced-compliant (given to escape an aversive interrogation or gain a promised benefit, while the suspect privately knows they are innocent), and coerced-internalized (in which a vulnerable suspect comes to believe in their own guilt or confabulate memories).

Risk factors and cases

Risk factors include lengthy interrogations, false-evidence ploys, minimization tactics, youth, intellectual disability and mental illness. Notorious examples include the Central Park Five case in New York, in which five teenagers confessed after long interrogations and were later exonerated by DNA evidence. Reforms proposed and increasingly adopted include mandatory recording of interrogations, limits on deceptive techniques with juveniles, and expert testimony on confession evidence at trial.
