The Sistine Chapel ceiling (Italian: La volta della Cappella Sistina), painted in fresco by Michelangelo between 1508 and 1512, is a cornerstone work of High Renaissance art. Commissioned by Pope Julius II, it covers the barrel-vaulted ceiling of the Sistine Chapel in the Vatican — some 500 square metres — and is perhaps the most famous fresco cycle in the world.

Commission and execution

Michelangelo, who considered himself primarily a sculptor, accepted the commission reluctantly and initially proposed a scheme of the Twelve Apostles; he then negotiated a far more ambitious programme, which he devised with theologians of the papal court. He built a novel flat scaffold that allowed work without holes in the ceiling, and painted from the entrance wall toward the altar between May 1508 and October 1512, dismissing most of his assistants and working largely alone in physical discomfort, lying on his back. The ceiling was unveiled on All Saints' Eve, 31 October 1512.

Iconography

Nine central panels run the length of the vault narrating episodes from the Book of Genesis, from the Separation of Light from Darkness through the Creation of Adam — whose near-touching hands of God and Adam in The Creation of Adam have become one of the most reproduced images in art — to the Drunkenness of Noah. These are framed by a fictive architecture peopled by seven Hebrew prophets and five pagan sibyls, monumental seated figures alternating with medallions of Old Testament scenes; between them recline twenty nude young men, the Ignudi, of debated meaning. In the corner spandrels appear dramatic episodes of Jewish salvation — Judith beheading Holofernes, David killing Goliath, the Brazen Serpent, and the hanging of Haman — while the lunettes depict the ancestors of Christ. In all, the ceiling contains over 300 figures.

Technique and restoration

The frescoes display Michelangelo's sculptural conception of the human body and a colour palette of remarkable brightness that was long obscured by centuries of candle soot and glue-based coatings. A major restoration (1980–1994), funded by the Nippon Television Network, removed the accumulated grime and revealed vivid, almost acidic colours; it remains debated, some critics arguing that it stripped intentional shading applied by Michelangelo in a secco. In 1536–41 Michelangelo returned to the chapel to paint The Last Judgment on its altar wall.
