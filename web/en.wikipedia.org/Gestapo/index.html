Gestapo

The Gestapo (German: Geheime Staatspolizei, "Secret State Police") was the official secret police of Nazi Germany and in German-occupied Europe. Founded in April 1933 by Hermann Göring in Prussia, it was soon merged with the corresponding political police forces of the other German states and placed under Heinrich Himmler's control. From 1939 it formed Amt IV of the Reich Security Main Office (RSHA), headed by Heinrich Müller.

Operating largely outside judicial oversight, the Gestapo had the power of "protective custody" (Schutzhaft), allowing it to imprison people without charge in concentration camps. It became notorious for its role in suppressing resistance, persecuting Jews and other groups, and enforcing Nazi rule through terror.

History

Göring created the Gestapo in 1933 from the Prussian political police, purging officials not loyal to the Nazi regime. In 1934 control passed to Himmler, who extended the model across Germany. In 1936 the Gestapo was formally combined with the Kripo (criminal police) into the Sicherheitspolizei under Reinhard Heydrich. With the creation of the RSHA in September 1939, the Gestapo became its Department IV, responsible for counter-intelligence and the suppression of opponents of the regime.

Organisation and methods

Although relatively small — around 40,000 officials at its peak — the Gestapo relied heavily on denunciations from the public. Its officers investigated treason, espionage, sabotage and attacks on the Nazi Party, and administered the machinery of repression against Jews, Roma, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses, resistance movements and forced labourers. In occupied Europe, Gestapo offices coordinated deportations to extermination camps and reprisals against partisans.

After the war

The Gestapo was declared a criminal organisation by the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg. Many of its leading officials were tried, though Müller disappeared in 1945 and his fate remains unknown. The Gestapo remains a byword for police-state terror.
