BIND

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

BIND (Berkeley Internet Name Domain) is a suite of software for the Domain Name System (DNS), including the most widely used DNS server program, named. Originally written at the University of California, Berkeley in the early 1980s and long maintained by the Internet Systems Consortium (ISC), BIND implements authoritative and recursive name service and has served as the reference implementation of the DNS protocols.

History

BIND began as a graduate-student project at Berkeley under DARPA sponsorship, taking its name from a student paper titled "The Berkeley Internet Name Domain Server." BIND 4 and BIND 8 became ubiquitous on Unix systems; BIND 9, a ground-up rewrite begun in the late 1990s, added DNSSEC validation, IPv6 support, views, and improved security architecture, and remains the mainstream release line.

Components and features

The suite includes the named daemon, resolver libraries, and diagnostic tools such as dig, host and nslookup. BIND supports primary and secondary authoritative service, recursive resolution and caching, response policy zones, split-horizon "views," dynamic updates, zone transfers (AXFR/IXFR), and DNS-over-TLS/HTTPS in later versions. Historically it ran a large share of the Internet's DNS infrastructure, including several root name servers, though alternatives such as NSD, Knot DNS and PowerDNS have diversified the ecosystem.

Security

Given its ubiquity, BIND vulnerabilities attract wide attention; the ISC issues regular security releases, and DNSSEC signing and validation support was a major focus of BIND 9 development.
