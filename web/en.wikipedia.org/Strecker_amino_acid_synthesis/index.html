Strecker amino acid synthesis

The Strecker amino acid synthesis, also known simply as the Strecker synthesis, is a method for the synthesis of amino acids from an aldehyde or ketone. First reported by the German chemist Adolph Strecker in 1850, it was the first laboratory synthesis of an amino acid (alanine) and remains a standard textbook route to α-amino acids.

Reaction

In the classical procedure, an aldehyde is treated with ammonium chloride and potassium cyanide (or, in modern variants, ammonia and hydrogen cyanide). The aldehyde first condenses with ammonia to form an imine, which is attacked by cyanide to give an α-aminonitrile. Acidic hydrolysis of the nitrile then yields the α-amino acid together with ammonium salts. Starting from acetaldehyde, the product is alanine; other aldehydes give the amino acid whose side chain corresponds to the aldehyde's organic group.

Significance

Because the product is formed through a planar intermediate, the classical Strecker synthesis produces a racemic mixture of D- and L-amino acids; asymmetric variants using chiral auxiliaries or catalysts were developed later. The reaction is also notable in origin-of-life research: amino acids can form by Strecker chemistry from simple precursors such as hydrogen cyanide, ammonia, and aldehydes in water, and the reaction has been invoked to explain amino acids found in meteorites and in prebiotic chemistry experiments of the Miller–Urey type.
