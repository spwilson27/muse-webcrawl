The Spirits' Book (French: Le Livre des Esprits) is a book on the philosophy of Spiritism published on 18 April 1857 by the French educator Hippolyte Léon Denizard Rivail under the pen name Allan Kardec. It is the founding text and first of the five basic works of Spiritism, known collectively as the Spiritist Codification.

Contents

The book is arranged as a series of 1,019 questions posed by Kardec to mediums, together with the answers he attributed to communicating spirits, which he says he cross-checked across multiple mediums for consistency. Its four parts treat: (1) first causes — God, the elements of the universe and the vital principle; (2) the world of spirits — their origin, incarnation and reincarnation, the mission of spirits and life after death; (3) moral laws — natural law, labour, reproduction, progress, equality, liberty, justice, love and charity; and (4) hopes and consolations — earthly sorrows, the loss of loved ones, and heaven and hell reinterpreted as states of mind.

Doctrine

The work sets out the core Spiritist tenets: the existence of God, the immortality of the soul, the plurality of inhabited worlds, and above all reincarnation as the mechanism of moral and intellectual progress, together with the possibility of communication between the living and the dead. Kardec presented Spiritism not as a revealed religion but as a "science of observation" and moral philosophy compatible with Christianity, which he described as the doctrine of Jesus clarified and completed.

Influence

The book went through expanded editions (notably 1860) and was followed by Kardec's The Mediums' Book (1861), The Gospel According to Spiritism (1864), Heaven and Hell (1865) and The Genesis (1868). Spiritism spread widely in France, Spain and above all Brazil, where Kardecist Spiritism counts millions of adherents and The Spirits' Book remains continuously in print.
