Armand Hippolyte Louis Fizeau (23 September 1819 – 18 September 1896) was a French physicist best known for being the first person to measure the speed of light by a terrestrial (non-astronomical) method, in 1849. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.

Speed of light

In his celebrated 1849 experiment, Fizeau shone a beam of light through the gaps of a rapidly rotating toothed wheel to a mirror 8.6 km away at Montmartre and back. By adjusting the wheel's speed until the returning light was blocked by the next tooth, he calculated a value of about 315,000 km/s — within a few percent of the modern value. With Léon Foucault he also took the first detailed photographs of the Sun's surface and investigated infrared interference.

Other work

Fizeau's 1851 experiment on the speed of light in moving water measured the partial dragging of light by a moving medium (the Fresnel drag), a result later understood as a precursor of special relativity. Independently of Doppler, he proposed in 1848 that electromagnetic waves shift in frequency due to the motion of their source — the Doppler–Fizeau effect used to measure stellar radial velocities. He became a member of the Académie des Sciences in 1860 and president of the French Physical Society.
