Pericardiocentesis

Pericardiocentesis is a medical procedure in which excess fluid is aspirated from the pericardium, the fibrous sac surrounding the heart. It is most commonly performed to relieve cardiac tamponade, a life-threatening condition in which accumulated pericardial fluid compresses the heart and prevents it from filling properly.

Indications

The principal indication is pericardial effusion causing haemodynamic compromise (cardiac tamponade). Pericardiocentesis may also be performed diagnostically to obtain fluid for analysis when infection, malignancy or another specific cause of an effusion is suspected.

Procedure

The procedure is usually performed at the bedside under ultrasound (echocardiographic) guidance, which has greatly improved its safety and success compared with the historical blind technique. After local anaesthesia, a needle is advanced — commonly by the subxiphoid, parasternal or apical approach — into the pericardial space while continuous ECG monitoring watches for signs of myocardial contact. Once fluid is aspirated, a flexible catheter (pigtail drain) is often left in place over a guidewire to allow continued drainage, and the fluid is sent for cytology, culture and chemistry.

Complications

Possible complications include laceration of the myocardium or a coronary artery, pneumothorax, puncture of the liver or other abdominal organs, arrhythmias and infection. With image guidance, major complications occur in roughly 1–2% of cases. Surgical drainage via a pericardial window is preferred for loculated effusions, clotted haemopericardium, or recurrent effusions requiring biopsy.
