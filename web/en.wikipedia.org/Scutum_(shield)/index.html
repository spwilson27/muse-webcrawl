Scutum (shield)

The scutum (Latin for "shield") was the large, curved rectangular shield carried by Roman legionaries. Made of layers of wood glued together like plywood and covered with canvas and leather, the scutum curved around the bearer's body, offering protection from the knees to the shoulders. A central metal boss (umbo) protected the hand grip and could be used to punch opponents.

Use in battle

The scutum was central to Roman tactics. Its size — roughly 1.2 metres tall in the imperial period — allowed legionaries to form the famous testudo (tortoise) formation, overlapping shields on all sides and overhead to protect against missiles. Behind its cover, the legionary thrust with the short gladius sword. Shields were often painted with unit insignia, such as Jupiter's eagle and lightning bolts.

History

Earlier Roman shields were oval; the curved rectangular form became standard around the late Republic and early Empire. By the later Roman Empire the scutum was gradually replaced by flatter oval or round shields. The only surviving complete example was found at Doncaster in England, with fragments from Fayum in Egypt.
