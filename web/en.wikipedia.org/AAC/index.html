Advanced Audio Coding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Advanced Audio Coding (AAC) is an audio coding standard for lossy digital audio compression. Designed to be the successor of the MP3 format, AAC generally achieves higher sound quality than MP3 encoders at the same bit rate of encoding. It was first specified in 1997 as part of the MPEG-2 family of standards and has since been extended in MPEG-4.

AAC has been standardized by ISO and IEC as part of the MPEG-2 and MPEG-4 specifications. Part of AAC, HE-AAC ("High Efficiency AAC"), is also part of MPEG-4 Audio and is commonly used in digital radio and streaming at low bit rates.

History

AAC was developed with the cooperation and contributions of several companies, including AT&T Bell Labs, Fraunhofer IIS, Dolby Laboratories, LG, NEC, Nokia, and Sony Corporation. It was officially declared an international standard in April 1997. In 1999 the MPEG-2 AAC profile received additions and was declared an international standard once again, followed by the MPEG-4 versions in 2000 and later amendments.

Design

AAC achieves its efficiency through several techniques, including modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT) with increased window lengths, temporal noise shaping, prediction, and joint stereo coding. Typical AAC-LC encoding supports sampling frequencies from 8 kHz to 96 kHz and up to 48 channels.

Usage

AAC is the default audio format for Apple's iTunes and iPhone/iPad products, is used by YouTube, is the standard audio format in Nintendo and PlayStation consoles, and is widely used in terrestrial and satellite digital radio (DAB+ and Digital Radio Mondiale).

Profiles

The standard defines several profiles, including the Main profile, Low Complexity (LC), Long Term Prediction (LTP), Scalable Sample Rate (SSR), and the High Efficiency profiles HE-AAC and HE-AAC v2, which add spectral band replication and parametric stereo.
