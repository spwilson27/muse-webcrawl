Edward Mills Purcell (August 30, 1912 – March 7, 1997) was an American physicist who shared the 1952 Nobel Prize for Physics for his independent discovery (published 1946) of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) in liquids and in solids. From its early beginnings, NMR has had numerous applications in physics, chemistry and medicine, most notably magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Purcell spent most of his career at Harvard University. He also worked on radar at MIT's Radiation Laboratory during the Second World War, and made important contributions to radio astronomy, including the first detection of the 21-centimetre hydrogen line from interstellar space.

Biography

Purcell was born in Taylorville, Illinois. He studied electrical engineering at Purdue University (BS 1933), then physics at Harvard, earning his PhD in 1938. After war work on radar, he joined Harvard's faculty in 1945, becoming professor in 1949 and serving in senior academic posts; he also advised several U.S. presidents on science.

Scientific work

  • NMR — in December 1945 Purcell, with Henry Torrey and Robert Pound, detected resonant absorption by protons in paraffin, independently of Felix Bloch's group at Stanford; the two groups shared the 1952 Nobel Prize.
  • 21-cm line — in 1951 his group (with Harold Ewen) first detected the hyperfine hydrogen emission line, enabling mapping of the Galaxy's spiral structure.
  • Smith–Purcell effect — radiation emitted when an electron beam passes over a diffraction grating (1953).
  • Purcell effect — enhancement of spontaneous emission in a resonant cavity, key to cavity quantum electrodynamics.
  • Scallop theorem — a celebrated argument in low-Reynolds-number fluid mechanics, from his lecture "Life at Low Reynolds Number" (1977).

Textbooks and legacy

His textbook Electricity and Magnetism, volume 2 of the Berkeley Physics Course, is renowned for its relativistic treatment and remains widely used; a revised edition by David Morin appeared in 2013. Purcell received the National Medal of Science in 1979.
