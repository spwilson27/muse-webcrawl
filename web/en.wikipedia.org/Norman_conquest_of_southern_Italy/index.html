Norman conquest of southern Italy
Date999–1139
LocationSouthern Italy and Sicily

The Norman conquest of southern Italy was the piecemeal takeover, between roughly 999 and 1139, of the Italian Mezzogiorno and Sicily by Norman adventurers — originally mercenaries and pilgrims from Normandy — at the expense of the Byzantine Empire, the Lombard principalities, and the Muslim emirates of Sicily. It culminated in 1130 with the coronation of Roger II as the first king of Sicily.

Arrival of the Normans

Normans first appeared in southern Italy around 999–1017, fighting as mercenaries for Lombard lords against the Byzantines. Rainulf Drengot received the county of Aversa in 1030, the first Norman lordship. The sons of Tancred de Hauteville — including William Iron Arm, Robert Guiscard, and Roger — arrived from the 1040s and carved out territories in Apulia and Calabria, defeating a papal army at Civitate in 1053.

Conquest of Sicily and the mainland

In 1059 Pope Nicholas II invested Robert Guiscard as Duke of Apulia, Calabria, and future Sicily. Guiscard and his brother Roger invaded Muslim Sicily in 1061, taking Messina and, after a thirty-year campaign, completing the conquest at Noto in 1091. Bari, the last Byzantine stronghold, fell in 1071, and Salerno in 1076/77.

Kingdom of Sicily

Roger II of Sicily inherited the mainland duchy in 1127 and in 1130 was crowned king, uniting southern Italy and Sicily into a single, remarkably centralised Norman kingdom that endured, through Hohenstaufen and Angevin succession, in its Sicilian and Neapolitan successor states for centuries.
