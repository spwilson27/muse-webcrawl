Bioaccumulation

Bioaccumulation is the gradual accumulation of substances, such as pesticides or heavy metals, in an organism. It occurs when an organism absorbs a substance at a rate faster than it can be lost or metabolised, so concentrations in tissues build up over the organism's lifetime.

Mechanism

Persistent, fat-soluble (lipophilic) substances are especially prone to bioaccumulate because they are stored in fatty tissues rather than excreted. Classic examples include the pesticide DDT, polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), and methylmercury. The older and larger an animal, the greater the accumulated burden tends to be.

Biomagnification

Bioaccumulation is related to biomagnification, the increase in concentration of a substance at successively higher levels of a food chain. Top predators such as tuna, eagles, and polar bears may carry concentrations far higher than those in the surrounding environment, with toxic effects.

Assessment

Regulators evaluate substances for bioaccumulation potential using measures such as the bioconcentration factor and the octanol–water partition coefficient (log Kow) as part of chemical safety assessments.
