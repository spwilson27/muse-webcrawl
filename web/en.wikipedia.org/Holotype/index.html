Holotype

A holotype is the single physical specimen (or illustration) designated by the author of a scientific name as the name-bearing type of a species or other taxon when the name is formally published. The holotype anchors the application of the name: whatever population the holotype belongs to is, by definition, the species to which that name applies. Holotypes are among the most scientifically valuable objects in natural history museums and herbaria, and are typically marked with a red label and stored with special care.

Definition and use

Under the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature, the holotype is "the single specimen upon which a new nominal species-group taxon is based" in the original publication. Modern codes require or strongly recommend that authors explicitly designate a holotype and state the collection in which it is deposited. All other specimens in the original type series are paratypes. If a species is later split, the name stays with the part containing the holotype; if two names turn out to refer to the same species, priority is decided by publication date, with the holotypes serving as the objective points of comparison.

Replacement types

If no holotype was designated — common in older literature, where authors worked from a series of syntypes — a later researcher may select a lectotype from among the syntypes to serve as the single name-bearer. If all original material is lost or destroyed (as happened to many collections in the Second World War), a neotype may be designated, under strict conditions, to fix the name's application. In botany, a duplicate of the holotype is an isotype; an epitype may be designated when the holotype is demonstrably ambiguous and cannot be critically identified for precise application of the name.

Botany and zoology compared

The concept is shared across codes but details differ: the botanical code (ICN) permits a type to be an illustration in some older cases and allows types of plant names to be preserved specimens in herbaria, while zoology generally requires an actual specimen or part of one for newer names. For prokaryotes, types are living strains deposited in culture collections. Palaeontology follows the same principles with fossils, some of immense fame — the London specimen of Archaeopteryx is the holotype of Archaeopteryx lithographica, and "Sue" the Tyrannosaurus is not a holotype but a celebrated referred specimen (the T. rex holotype is a partial skeleton in Pittsburgh).

Contemporary issues

Debates continue over whether new species should ever be described from photographs alone without a collected holotype — a practice some taxonomists defend for rare or protected animals and others condemn as unverifiable. Digitisation projects increasingly image holotypes in high resolution, making the definitive reference specimens accessible worldwide while reducing handling of fragile originals.
