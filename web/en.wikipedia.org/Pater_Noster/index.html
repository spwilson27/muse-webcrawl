The Pater Noster is the Latin form of the Lord's Prayer.

The Pater Noster — Latin for "Our Father" — is the Latin version of the Lord's Prayer, the central Christian prayer taught by Jesus to his disciples. It takes its name from its opening words and has been recited in Latin in the Western Church for well over a millennium, in the Mass, the Liturgy of the Hours, the rosary and private devotion. The text follows the longer Matthean version (Matthew 6:9–13), in the form transmitted by the Vulgate.

Text

Pater noster, qui es in caelis, sanctificetur nomen tuum. Adveniat regnum tuum. Fiat voluntas tua, sicut in caelo et in terra. Panem nostrum quotidianum da nobis hodie, et dimitte nobis debita nostra, sicut et nos dimittimus debitoribus nostris. Et ne nos inducas in tentationem, sed libera nos a malo. Amen.

("Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread, and forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil. Amen.")

Use and study

In the Roman Rite the Pater Noster is sung or said at every Mass during the Communion rite, introduced by the invitation Praeceptis salutaribus moniti. The prayer has generated an enormous commentary tradition — Tertullian called it a summary of the whole Gospel — and expositions by Cyprian, Augustine, Thomas Aquinas and the Catechism of the Catholic Church. Because Latin grammars and primers traditionally taught it by heart, the Pater Noster has also served linguists as a standard sample text: collections of the prayer in hundreds of languages were among the earliest works of comparative philology, and it was used as specimen text in early printed grammars of newly documented languages.
