Disco

Disco is a genre of dance music and a subculture that emerged in the 1970s from the United States' urban nightlife scene. Its sound is typified by four-on-the-floor beats, syncopated basslines, string sections, horns, electric piano, synthesizers, and electric rhythm guitars.

Origins and peak

Disco grew out of the discothèques of New York City, where DJs such as David Mancuso at The Loft and Francis Grasso pioneered seamless mixing for audiences drawn heavily from African-American, Latino, Italian-American, and gay communities, and from funk, soul, Motown, and Latin music. By the mid-1970s, acts including the Bee Gees, Donna Summer, Gloria Gaynor, Chic, ABBA, and KC and the Sunshine Band dominated charts worldwide. The film Saturday Night Fever (1977) and New York's Studio 54 symbolised the fad at its height.

Backlash and decline

A backlash — often criticised as carrying racist and homophobic undertones — culminated in the "Disco Demolition Night" promotion at Chicago's Comiskey Park in July 1979, after which disco rapidly collapsed commercially in the United States. Major labels dropped disco acts and the word itself became toxic in marketing.

Legacy

Disco never truly died: it evolved into house music in Chicago, Hi-NRG, and Eurodisco, and underpins modern dance and pop music. DJ and remix culture, the 12-inch single, and extended dance mixes all originated in disco. Revivals, sampling, and acts such as Daft Punk have repeatedly returned disco's sound to the charts.
