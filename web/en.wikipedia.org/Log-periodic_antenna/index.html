Log-periodic antenna

Log-periodic antenna
Type: directional broadband antenna
Invented: 1952 (John Dunlavy); developed by Dwight Isbell and Raymond DuHamel, 1957–58
Typical gain: 8–10 dBi
Bandwidth: up to 10:1 or more
Uses: television reception, EMC testing, HF/VHF/UHF communications

A log-periodic antenna (LP), also known as a log-periodic array, is a multi-element directional antenna designed to operate over a wide band of frequencies. Its electrical characteristics — impedance, gain and radiation pattern — vary periodically with the logarithm of frequency, hence the name. The most common form is the log-periodic dipole array (LPDA), a row of dipole elements of progressively decreasing length mounted along a boom.

A log-periodic dipole array for UHF television reception

Design

In an LPDA, pairs of metal rods form dipoles whose lengths and spacings decrease geometrically along the array, each successive element scaled by a constant factor τ (tau). The dipoles are connected to a balanced feed line with alternating phase reversals between adjacent elements. At any frequency, only the few elements near resonance — about λ/2 long — radiate effectively; this "active region" slides along the array as frequency changes, giving nearly constant performance across the band. The antenna is fed at its short (high-frequency) end and radiates toward that end.

History

John Dunlavy filed the first log-periodic patent in 1952. At the University of Illinois, Raymond DuHamel and Dwight Isbell developed the concept in 1957–58, producing the LPDA and the log-periodic folded slot and spiral variants. Isbell's array became a standard broadband antenna and remains in production largely unchanged.

Applications

Because of their wide bandwidth and moderate gain, log-periodic antennas are widely used for terrestrial television reception (especially combined VHF/UHF rooftop aerials), for electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) emissions and immunity testing, in HF communications installations, and as feed elements and measurement antennas in anechoic chambers.
