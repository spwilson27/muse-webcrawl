Colonnade

In classical architecture, a colonnade is a long sequence of columns joined by their entablature, often free-standing, or part of a building. In pairs, columns may be coupled, and colonnades are often used to form porticoes, stoas, and covered walkways.

History

Colonnades appear in ancient Egyptian temples, Greek stoas such as the Stoa of Attalos in Athens, and Roman fora and basilicas. St Peter's Square in Vatican City features Bernini's vast curved colonnades (1656–1667), symbolically embracing visitors.

Types

A colonnade at the front of a building is a portico; along a street it forms an arcade when arched. The spacing between columns is called intercolumniation. Colonnades can be single or double-aisled and may enclose courtyards as a peristyle.

Modern use

Neoclassical, Beaux-Arts, and modernist buildings continue to use colonnades for shade, procession, and civic monumentality, from university campuses to memorials such as the Lincoln Memorial.
