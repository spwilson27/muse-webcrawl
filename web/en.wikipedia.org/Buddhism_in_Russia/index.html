Buddhism in Russia

Main regionsBuryatia, Kalmykia, Tuva
TraditionTibetan Buddhism (Gelug school)
Key institutionsIvolginsky Datsan; Buddhist Traditional Sangha of Russia

Buddhism in Russia is one of the country's officially recognised traditional religions. It is historically concentrated among three ethnic groups of Mongolian and Turkic heritage: the Buryats of Siberia, the Kalmyks of the lower Volga steppe — the only traditionally Buddhist ethnic group native to Europe — and the Tuvans of southern Siberia. The form practised is Tibetan Buddhism, mainly of the Gelug school, transmitted from Mongolia from the 17th century onward.

History

Buddhism spread among the Buryats around Lake Baikal in the 17th and 18th centuries, and in 1741 Empress Elizabeth issued a decree formally recognising the Buddhist church in the Russian Empire. Monasteries called datsans became centres of learning, medicine and printing. Kalmyk Buddhists, descendants of Oirat Mongols who migrated to the Volga in the 17th century, built temples on the steppe. The early 20th century saw a Buddhist temple built in Saint Petersburg (consecrated 1915), championed by the scholar and diplomat Agvan Dorzhiev.

Soviet repression and revival

Under Joseph Stalin, Buddhism was savagely repressed: monasteries were destroyed, lamas executed or imprisoned, and the entire Kalmyk population was deported to Siberia in 1943. A single monastery, Ivolginsky Datsan, was permitted to reopen after World War II. Since the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, Buddhism in Russia has undergone a revival, with temples rebuilt in Buryatia, Kalmykia and Tuva, including the Golden Abode of Buddha Shakyamuni in Elista, one of the largest Buddhist temples in Europe.
