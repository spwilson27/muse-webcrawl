Last universal common ancestor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The last universal common ancestor (LUCA) is the hypothesized common ancestral cell from which the three domains of life — Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya — originated. It is suggested that LUCA lived around 3.5 to 4.3 billion years ago, in the Paleoarchean or early Hadean era of Earth.

Characteristics

By reconstructing genes shared across all modern lineages, researchers infer that LUCA already possessed DNA, the standard genetic code, ribosomes and protein synthesis, ATP-based metabolism, and probably a cell membrane. A 2016 study identified 355 genes likely present in LUCA and suggested it was an anaerobic, hydrogen-dependent organism living in a geochemically active environment rich in hydrogen, carbon dioxide and iron — leading to the hypothesis that it inhabited deep-sea hydrothermal vents.

Concept

LUCA was not necessarily the first living organism, nor a single cell: it is best understood as the most recent population of organisms from which all extant life descends, itself the product of earlier evolution. Charles Darwin had already speculated in On the Origin of Species (1859) that all living things might descend from "one primordial form". Modern phylogenetic methods, beginning with Carl Woese's ribosomal RNA trees, gave that idea a precise molecular meaning.
