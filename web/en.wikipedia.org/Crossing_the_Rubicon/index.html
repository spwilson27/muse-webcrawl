Crossing the Rubicon

Crossing the Rubicon is an idiom meaning to pass a point of no return. It derives from an event in January 49 BC, when Julius Caesar led his army across the Rubicon, a small river that marked the boundary between his province of Cisalpine Gaul and Italy proper. Roman law forbade a general from bringing his army into Italy, so the act was one of insurrection and treason — and it precipitated Caesar's civil war against Pompey and the Roman Senate.

Historical account

According to Suetonius, Caesar hesitated at the riverbank before declaring "the die is cast" (alea iacta est, itself a quotation from the Greek playwright Menander) and crossing with Legio XIII. Plutarch gives a similar account. Caesar then advanced rapidly on Rome, which Pompey and most senators abandoned. The exact location of the ancient Rubicon is uncertain; in 1933 the river formerly called the Fiumicino in Emilia-Romagna was officially renamed Rubicone and is generally identified with Caesar's river.

Legacy

The phrase "crossing the Rubicon" entered English in the 17th century and remains a common way of describing any irreversible step. "The die is cast" is likewise proverbial for a decision that cannot be undone.
