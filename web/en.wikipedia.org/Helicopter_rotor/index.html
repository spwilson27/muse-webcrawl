Helicopter rotor

ComponentsBlades, hub, mast, swashplate, pitch links
Head typesTeetering, fully articulated, hingeless/rigid
FunctionLift, propulsion and control

A helicopter rotor is the rotating part of a helicopter that generates lift. It consists of a mast, hub and two or more long, narrow aerofoil blades. Unlike a fixed-wing propeller, the helicopter rotor simultaneously produces lift, forward thrust and the forces used to control the aircraft, and its disc can be tilted by the pilot through the swashplate.

Design

Rotor heads are classified by how the blades attach to the hub. A teetering (seesaw) two-blade rotor, long associated with Bell designs, lets the blades rock as a unit. Fully articulated rotors give each blade hinges to flap, lead-lag and change pitch independently. Hingeless and bearingless designs replace hinges with flexible elements. Because the advancing blade in forward flight experiences much higher airspeed than the retreating blade, blades flap (or flex) to equalise lift across the disc; if airflow conditions cause the retreating blade to stall, the helicopter reaches a fundamental speed limit.

Configurations

Most helicopters pair a single main rotor with a tail rotor that counters torque. Alternatives include coaxial rotors (as in Kamov designs), intermeshing rotors (Kaman), tandem rotors (Chinook), transverse rotors, and the fenestron and NOTAR systems. In an engine failure the rotor can keep turning by autorotation, allowing a controlled descent and landing.
