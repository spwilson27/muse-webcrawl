In Chinese philosophy, yin and yang (English: /jɪn/ and /jæŋ/; Chinese: 陰陽, lit. 'dark–bright', 'negative–positive') describes an opposite but interconnected, self-perpetuating cycle, yielding a composed state not meant in terms of good or bad. A concept of dualism, it posits that contrary forces may actually be complementary, interconnected and interdependent in the natural world, and how they may give rise to each other as they interrelate to one another.

Principles

The philosophy rests on several linked ideas: opposition — all things have a counterpart that is not absolute but relative (hot/cold, day/night, active/passive); interdependence — neither pole can exist without the other, as there can be no shadow without light; mutual consumption and support — the two are in dynamic equilibrium, so that when one grows the other recedes; and inter-transformation — at its extreme each turns into the other, as the longest day of summer contains the beginning of winter's return. Each pole contains the seed of the other, represented by the small dots in the taijitu symbol. Common pairings include yin as dark, receptive, earth, female, moon and rest, and yang as bright, active, heaven, male, sun and movement — associations of the shady (yin) and sunny (yang) sides of a hill in the characters' original senses.

History

The characters 陰 and 陽 appear in early texts including the I Ching (Book of Changes) and the Tao Te Ching. The concept was systematised by the School of Yin-Yang, one of the Hundred Schools of Thought of the Warring States period, whose leading thinker Zou Yan (c. 305–240 BC) linked yin-yang to the Five Phases (Wu Xing). During the Han dynasty, thinkers such as Dong Zhongshu integrated yin-yang into Confucian cosmology and state ideology, and the framework became fundamental to traditional Chinese medicine — where health is the balance of yin and yang in the body's qi — as well as to feng shui, martial arts, calendrical science and divination.

Symbol

The familiar taijitu — a circle divided by an S-curve into black and white halves, each with a dot of the opposite colour — became standard in the Ming dynasty, building on diagrams published by Song-dynasty Neo-Confucian philosophers. In the West the symbol has been widely adopted as a general emblem of balance and harmony, appearing in contexts from the flag of South Korea (where the red-and-blue taegeuk is a related symbol) to popular culture and alternative medicine.
