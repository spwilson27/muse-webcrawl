Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia

The Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (SFRY; Serbo-Croatian: Socijalistička Federativna Republika Jugoslavija) was a federal socialist state in Southeast and Central Europe that existed from 1945 until its dissolution in 1992. It comprised six republics — Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, Macedonia, Montenegro, Serbia (including the autonomous provinces of Vojvodina and Kosovo), and Slovenia — with its capital at Belgrade.

History

The federation grew out of the Partisan resistance led by Josip Broz Tito during the Second World War. Proclaimed in 1945, it initially aligned with the Soviet Union, but the Tito–Stalin split of 1948 pushed Yugoslavia toward a distinct path: workers' self-management at home and co-founding the Non-Aligned Movement abroad. Tito ruled until his death in 1980, after which a rotating collective presidency governed.

System

Yugoslavia's "self-managing socialism" gave workers' councils nominal control of enterprises, and its citizens enjoyed more travel and consumer freedoms than most of the communist world. The 1974 constitution devolved extensive powers to the republics, a decentralization later blamed for paralyzing federal decision-making amid the economic crisis of the 1980s.

Dissolution

Rising nationalism after Tito's death culminated in multiparty elections in 1990, declarations of independence by Slovenia and Croatia in 1991, and the Yugoslav Wars. The SFRY formally ceased to exist in April 1992, succeeded by independent states and the rump Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro).
