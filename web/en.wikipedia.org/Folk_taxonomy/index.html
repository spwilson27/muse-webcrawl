Folk taxonomy

A folk taxonomy is a vernacular naming and classification system used by a culture or community to organise the organisms and objects of its everyday world, as distinct from scientific taxonomy. Folk taxonomies arise from social knowledge and practical use, and are studied in ethnobiology and cognitive anthropology.

Structure

Research by anthropologists such as Brent Berlin showed that folk biological classifications tend to be hierarchical, with levels roughly corresponding to kingdom, life-form (such as tree, bird or fish), genus and species. The "generic" level — oak, robin, trout — appears to be the most psychologically basic and the first learned by children. Folk categories often correspond surprisingly well to scientific species and genera, though they may split organisms by use, edibility or appearance, or group unrelated organisms together (as with "worms" or "bugs").

Significance

Folk taxonomies preserve detailed local ecological knowledge, including information about habitats, behaviour and medicinal or food uses, and they are embedded in language: when languages disappear, their folk classifications and the knowledge encoded in them are lost as well. Comparing folk and scientific systems has informed debates about how humans universally categorise the natural world.
