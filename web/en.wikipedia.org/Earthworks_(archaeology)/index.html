Earthworks (archaeology)

TypeArtificial changes in land level
FormsBanks, ditches, mounds, platforms
ExamplesHillforts, burial mounds, henges, moated sites

Aerial view of an Iron Age hillfort's earthworks.

In archaeology, earthworks are artificial changes in the level of the land, usually made from piles of artificially placed or sculpted soil and rock. They are among the most common surviving remains of past human activity in the landscape, and include banks, ditches, mounds, terraces and platforms.

Types

Earthworks served many purposes. Defensive sites such as hillforts and castles were ringed with ramparts and ditches; burial mounds (barrows and tumuli) covered the dead; henges and causewayed enclosures bounded ceremonial spaces; lynchets, ridge-and-furrow and terraces are residues of farming; and hollow ways record old routeways. Large-scale examples include the serpent and geometric mounds of the North American mound-builder cultures and the Offa's Dyke frontier earthwork between England and Wales.

Study

Because earthworks are often subtle, they are studied by topographic survey, aerial photography, geophysics and lidar, which can reveal features hidden under woodland. Their profiles and relationships — for example where one ditch cuts another — help archaeologists establish relative chronology, and many earthworks are protected as scheduled monuments.
