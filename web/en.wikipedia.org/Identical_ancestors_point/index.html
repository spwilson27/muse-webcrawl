Identical ancestors point

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In population genetics, the identical ancestors point (IAP) is the most recent point in time such that every individual alive at that time is either an ancestor of every person alive today, or an ancestor of no living person. In other words, beyond this point the set of ancestors stops changing: going further back adds no new individuals to the ancestry of the present-day population.

Concept

The concept follows from pedigree collapse: the number of one's ancestors doubles each generation until it exceeds the size of the past population, so ancestors must repeat and pedigrees interweave. Statistical models by Joseph Chang (1999), extended by Douglas Rohde and others, showed that under random-mating assumptions the identical ancestors point for all living humans is surprisingly recent—on the order of a few thousand years—far more recent than the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of all humans, which the same modelling places perhaps only a few thousand years earlier.

Distinctions

The identical ancestors point is a property of pedigrees (genealogical ancestry), not of genetic ancestry: many genealogical ancestors contribute no DNA to a given descendant. It also differs from the MRCA, the single most recent person from whom everyone descends; at the IAP, typically many individuals qualify as universal ancestors simultaneously.

Estimates

Estimates depend heavily on assumptions about migration and population structure. Modelling by Rohde, Olson, and Chang (published in Nature in 2004) suggested an MRCA within the last few thousand years and an identical ancestors point for all humans perhaps 5,000–15,000 years ago, with isolated populations pushing the date earlier. The IAP for regional populations, such as Europeans, is much more recent still.
