Tariff of 1828

The Tariff of 1828 sharply raised duties on imported manufactured goods.

The Tariff of 1828, called the Tariff of Abominations in the South, was a protective tariff passed by the Congress of the United States on May 19, 1828, designed to protect industry in the Northern United States. It was labeled the Tariff of Abominations by its Southern detractors because of the effects it had on the Southern economy.

The tariff raised duties on imported goods, including raw materials such as wool, hemp, flax, and iron, to levels as high as 50 percent on some items. It was enacted during the presidency of John Quincy Adams and became a central political issue in the election of 1828.

Background

Protectionist sentiment had grown after the War of 1812, and earlier tariffs in 1816 and 1824 had already raised rates. The 1828 bill was shaped in part by political maneuvering: supporters of Andrew Jackson and of Adams each sought to construct a bill that would embarrass the other side, but the bill passed and Adams signed it.

Opposition and the nullification crisis

Southern states, whose economies depended on exporting cotton and importing manufactured goods, bore much of the tariff's cost. Vice President John C. Calhoun anonymously authored the South Carolina Exposition and Protest, arguing that a state could nullify a federal law within its borders. Opposition culminated in the Nullification Crisis of 1832–1833, when South Carolina declared the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 null and void, and President Andrew Jackson threatened to use force. A compromise tariff in 1833 gradually reduced rates and defused the confrontation.
