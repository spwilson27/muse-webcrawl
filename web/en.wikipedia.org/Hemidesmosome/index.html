Hemidesmosome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hemidesmosome
LocationBasal surface of epithelial cells, at the basement membrane
Key componentsIntegrin α6β4, plectin, BPAG1 (dystonin), BPAG2 (collagen XVII), laminin-332
FunctionAnchoring intermediate filaments of the cell to the extracellular matrix

Hemidesmosomes are very small, stud-like structures found in keratinocytes of the skin and in other stratified and some simple epithelia, which attach the basal surface of the cell to the underlying basement membrane. They are similar in appearance to desmosomes — hence "half-desmosomes" — but differ in structure and function: desmosomes join neighbouring cells to each other, whereas hemidesmosomes anchor cells to the extracellular matrix. This anchorage gives tissues such as the epidermis their resistance to mechanical stress.

Structure

The core receptor is the integrin α6β4, an unusual integrin with a very long β4 cytoplasmic tail. Inside the cell, an electron-dense plaque of the plakin proteins plectin and BPAG1 (dystonin) connects integrin β4 to the keratin intermediate-filament network. The 180-kDa bullous pemphigoid antigen BPAG2 (type XVII collagen) spans the membrane as an additional structural and signalling component. Outside, the integrin binds laminin-332 in the lamina lucida/densa of the basement membrane, which in turn links via type VII collagen anchoring fibrils to the dermal matrix. Type I hemidesmosomes (of stratified epithelia) contain plectin and BPAGs; simpler type II junctions occur in intestinal epithelium.

Clinical significance

Defects in hemidesmosomal proteins cause blistering skin diseases. Mutations in integrin α6β4, plectin or collagen XVII underlie forms of junctional epidermolysis bullosa (and, for plectin, a form with muscular dystrophy). In the autoimmune diseases bullous pemphigoid and mucous membrane pemphigoid, autoantibodies against BPAG1/BPAG2 disrupt adhesion, separating epidermis from dermis. Hemidesmosome proteins and integrin α6β4 are also implicated in the invasion and progression of squamous cell and other carcinomas, where regulated disassembly of the junctions contributes to cell migration in wound healing and metastasis.
