Deportation

Deportation is the expulsion of a person or group of people from a state, normally because they are present in its territory without legal authorisation, have overstayed or breached the conditions of admission, or have committed serious offences. The deporting state forcibly returns the person to their country of citizenship or another country willing to receive them. Deportation is an administrative or judicial measure of immigration control; it is distinct from extradition (surrender for trial or punishment in another state) and from exile.

Grounds and process

Typical grounds include unlawful entry, visa overstay, working without permission, fraud in immigration applications, and convictions for crimes. Procedures vary: most states provide for an administrative order, detention pending removal, and some right of appeal or judicial review, though the scope of these protections is politically contested. In the United States, "removal" has been the formal legal term since 1996, encompassing both deportation (of persons admitted) and exclusion (of persons seeking entry). In EU law, return decisions are governed by the Returns Directive, supplemented by readmission agreements with countries of origin.

Limits under international law

States' power to deport is limited by the principle of non-refoulement: the 1951 Refugee Convention and other instruments prohibit returning a person to a country where they face persecution, torture, or other grave harm. The European Convention on Human Rights has been applied to bar deportations that would separate families (Article 8) or expose individuals to inhuman treatment (Article 3); collective expulsion of aliens is prohibited outright. Deporting a state's own citizens is generally forbidden in modern law, though it historically occurred, and some states have deprived dual nationals of citizenship to make them deportable — a practice challenged as rendering people stateless.

History

Forced removal has a long history: the Assyrian empire deported conquered peoples en masse, as did Rome in specific cases; early modern and imperial states exiled convicts and dissidents (transportation to Australia being the largest such system); and the 20th century saw mass deportations as instruments of ethnic engineering, including the Soviet deportations of entire nationalities under Stalin, which are now widely classified as crimes. Post-war immigration enforcement in Western states made deportation a routine administrative practice; mass-removal campaigns (such as the U.S. operations of the 1930s and 1950s, which swept up many citizens) remain cautionary episodes. Contemporary deportation policy is a central and divisive issue in the politics of migration.
