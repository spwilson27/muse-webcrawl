Hypertrichosis is an abnormal amount of hair growth over the body. The two distinct types of hypertrichosis are generalised hypertrichosis, which occurs over the entire body, and localised hypertrichosis, which is restricted to a certain area. Hypertrichosis can be either congenital (present at birth) or acquired later in life. The excess growth of hair occurs in areas of the skin where hair normally does not grow or grows only minimally, and is not dependent on androgens — in contrast to hirsutism, which is excessive hair growth in women in a male pattern.

Classification

Congenital forms

Congenital hypertrichosis lanuginosa is a very rare condition in which fine lanugo hair covers the whole body; it is often linked to rearrangements on chromosome 8. Congenital generalised hypertrichosis (Ambras syndrome) presents with long, silky hair over the face and body and has been mapped to chromosome 17 in some families. Localised congenital forms include hypertrichosis over a faun-tail deformity of the spine or over melanocytic naevi.

Acquired forms

Acquired hypertrichosis may develop as a paraneoplastic syndrome (hypertrichosis lanuginosa acquisita, associated especially with lung and colorectal cancers), from malnutrition or eating disorders, or as a side effect of drugs including minoxidil, phenytoin, ciclosporin and diazoxide.

History

People with congenital generalised hypertrichosis were historically exhibited in travelling circuses and "freak shows" as "dog-men", "ape-men" or "werewolves". Famous cases include Petrus Gonsalvus (c. 1537–1618), a Canarian man employed at the courts of France and the Netherlands whose children inherited the condition, and Julia Pastrana (1834–1860), a Mexican performer. Charles Darwin cited such cases in his work on human variation, and the condition is popularly nicknamed "werewolf syndrome".

Treatment

There is no cure for congenital hypertrichosis. Management is cosmetic: temporary removal by shaving, waxing or depilatory creams, and longer-term reduction by laser hair removal and electrolysis. Acquired forms may resolve when the underlying cause — a tumour or drug — is treated.
