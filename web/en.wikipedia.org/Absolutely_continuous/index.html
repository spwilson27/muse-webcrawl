Absolute continuity

In calculus and real analysis, absolute continuity is a smoothness property of functions that is stronger than continuity and uniform continuity but weaker than continuous differentiability. The concept also extends to measures, where one measure is absolutely continuous with respect to another if it assigns zero measure to every set that the other measure considers negligible.

Functions

A function f on a compact interval is absolutely continuous if, for every ε > 0, there is a δ > 0 such that the total variation of f over any finite collection of disjoint subintervals of total length less than δ is less than ε. Such functions are exactly the indefinite integrals of Lebesgue-integrable functions: f is absolutely continuous if and only if it can be written as f(x) = f(a) + ∫ax g(t) dt for some integrable g, in which case f is differentiable almost everywhere and f′ = g almost everywhere. This makes absolute continuity the natural setting for the fundamental theorem of calculus in the Lebesgue theory.

Measures

A measure μ is absolutely continuous with respect to a measure ν (written μ ≪ ν) if ν(A) = 0 implies μ(A) = 0. The Radon–Nikodym theorem states that, under suitable conditions, such a μ has a density with respect to ν, a fact fundamental to probability theory, where densities of continuous random variables are absolute continuity statements with respect to Lebesgue measure.
