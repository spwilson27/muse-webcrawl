Edward the Confessor

Reign: 1042 – 5 January 1066

Born: c. 1003, Islip, Oxfordshire

Died: 5 January 1066, Westminster

Burial: Westminster Abbey

Spouse: Edith of Wessex

House: Wessex

Feast day: 13 October

Edward the Confessor (c. 1003 – 5 January 1066) was an Anglo-Saxon English king and saint. Usually considered the last king of the House of Wessex, he ruled from 1042 until his death in 1066. Edward was the son of Æthelred the Unready and Emma of Normandy. He succeeded Cnut's son Harthacnut and restored the rule of the House of Wessex after a period of Danish domination.

Early life and exile

Edward spent most of his first forty years in exile in Normandy, where his mother had taken him after the Danish conquest of England in 1013. He developed a deep piety and strong Norman connections during this period. He returned to England in 1041 at the invitation of Harthacnut, and was crowned at Winchester Cathedral on 3 April 1043 after Harthacnut's death.

Reign

Edward's reign was marked by the growing power of the great earls, above all Godwin, Earl of Wessex, whose daughter Edith Edward married in 1045. A crisis in 1051 led to the exile of Godwin and his sons, but they returned by force in 1052. After Godwin's death, his son Harold Godwinson became the dominant figure in the kingdom and the king's leading commander, winning notable victories in Wales.

Edward devoted much energy to the rebuilding of St Peter's Abbey at Westminster in the new Romanesque style — the first major Romanesque church in England. Westminster Abbey was consecrated on 28 December 1065, only days before the king's death.

Death and succession crisis

Edward died childless on 5 January 1066. His death without a clear heir triggered a succession crisis: Harold Godwinson was crowned the next day, but William, Duke of Normandy, and Harald Hardrada of Norway also claimed the throne. The resulting Norman Conquest, sealed at the Battle of Hastings in October 1066, transformed England permanently.

Canonisation and legacy

Edward was canonised by Pope Alexander III in 1161 and became one of England's patron saints until superseded by St George in the fourteenth century. His shrine at Westminster Abbey remains a place of pilgrimage, and his feast is kept on 13 October. His epithet "the Confessor" distinguishes him from Edward the Martyr: a confessor is a saint who suffered for the faith without dying for it.
