History of logic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Logic developed independently in several cultures, including Greece, India and China.

The history of logic deals with the study of the development of the science of valid inference (logic). Formal logics developed in ancient times in India, China, and Greece. Greek methods, particularly Aristotelian logic (or term logic), found wide application and acceptance in Western science and mathematics for millennia. The Stoics, especially Chrysippus, began the development of predicate logic.

Christian and Islamic philosophers such as Boethius (died 524), Ibn Sina (Avicenna, died 1037) and William of Ockham (died 1347) further developed Aristotelian logic in the Middle Ages, reaching a high point in the mid-fourteenth century, with Jean Buridan. The period between the fourteenth century and the beginning of the nineteenth century saw largely decline and neglect, and at least one historian of logic regards this time as barren. Empiricist rule was dominant at the time when modern methods developed.

Ancient logic

In ancient Greece, Aristotle (384–322 BC) produced the earliest surviving systematic treatises on logic in the collection of works known as the Organon. His syllogistic, presented in the Prior Analytics, dominated Western logical thought for over two thousand years. Aristotle's logic was term logic: reasoning proceeded by combining terms through propositions of the forms "All A is B", "No A is B", "Some A is B" and "Some A is not B".

The Stoics, in particular Chrysippus of Soli, developed an alternative propositional logic in which whole propositions, rather than terms, were the units of inference. Their theory of conditionals and inference schemata anticipated aspects of modern propositional calculus.

In India, logic developed within the Nyaya school, which produced a five-membered inference scheme, and in the work of Buddhist philosophers such as Dignaga and Dharmakirti, who developed sophisticated theories of inference (anumana) and the three marks of a valid reason (trairupya). In China, the later Mohists developed a form of logic concerned with definitions, distinctions and disputation.

Medieval logic

Boethius transmitted Aristotle's logic to the Latin West through translations and commentaries. In the Islamic world, al-Farabi, Avicenna and Averroes wrote extensive works on logic; Avicenna's system influenced Latin scholasticism. Medieval logicians developed theories of supposition, consequences (consequentiae), insolubles (paradoxes such as the liar) and obligations (obligationes), a formalized kind of disputation.

Modern logic

The modern era of logic began in the nineteenth century. George Boole's The Laws of Thought (1854) gave logic an algebraic form. Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) introduced quantifier notation and is widely regarded as the founding work of modern logic. Giuseppe Peano and his school developed notation still in use, and Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead attempted in Principia Mathematica (1910–1913) to derive all of mathematics from logic (logicism).

In the twentieth century, Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed fundamental limits to formal systems. The work of Alfred Tarski on truth and models founded model theory, while proof theory grew from David Hilbert's programme. Logic became central to computer science through the work of Alonzo Church and Alan Turing on computability, and later through programming languages, databases and artificial intelligence.
