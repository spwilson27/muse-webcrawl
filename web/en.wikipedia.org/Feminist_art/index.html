Feminist art is a category of art associated with the late 1960s and 1970s feminist movement. It seeks to challenge the social and political inequalities within society and the art world, and to bring women's experience into cultural production. Feminist artists used a variety of mediums — painting, performance art, photography, video and crafts traditionally dismissed as "women's work", such as needlework — to contest the male-dominated canon.

History

The movement gained a theoretical catalyst in Linda Nochlin's 1971 essay "Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?", which attributed women's absence from art history to institutional barriers rather than talent. Judy Chicago and Miriam Schapiro founded the Feminist Art Program at the California Institute of the Arts, whose Womanhouse exhibition (1972) transformed a Hollywood mansion into installations on domestic female experience. Chicago's monumental The Dinner Party (1974–79) commemorated 1,038 women from history. In the 1980s the anonymous Guerrilla Girls turned to poster activism about sexism in the art market.

Themes

Recurring concerns include the body and its representation, domestic labour, the "male gaze", craft and decoration (the Pattern and Decoration movement), biography and recovered histories of earlier women artists.
