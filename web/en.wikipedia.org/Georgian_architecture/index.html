Georgian architecture

Georgian architecture
Period1714–1830 (including Regency)
RegionBritain, Ireland, British colonies
InfluencesPalladianism, Neoclassicism
HallmarksSymmetry, proportion, sash windows, panelled doors

Georgian architecture is the name given to the architectural styles current in the English-speaking world between about 1714 and 1830, during the reigns of the first four British monarchs of the House of Hanover. It is characterised by symmetry, careful proportion based on classical models, and restrained ornament.

Characteristics

Georgian buildings are typically of brick or stone, five bays wide in grander examples, with a panelled front door centred in the façade, often crowned by a decorative fanlight and flanked by pilasters. Sash windows, divided into six panes over six, are arranged in strict symmetry, diminishing in size on upper floors. Roofs are usually hidden behind a parapet, and classical orders govern cornices and doorcases.

Development

Early Georgian design continued the Palladian tradition of Inigo Jones and Lord Burlington, reacting against the Baroque. From the 1760s, Robert Adam popularised a lighter Neoclassicism with delicate interior decoration. The late Georgian or Regency period brought stucco terraces, crescents such as those at Bath and in Nash's London, and the picturesque planning of squares and circuses. The style spread throughout the British world, shaping the townhouses of Dublin, Edinburgh's New Town, and the federal-style buildings of the early United States.

Legacy

Georgian principles of proportion and urban terraces influenced town planning for two centuries, and "Georgian revival" styles recurred in the twentieth century. Conservation of Georgian districts became a founding cause of the modern preservation movement.
