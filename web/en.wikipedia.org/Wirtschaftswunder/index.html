Wirtschaftswunder

The Wirtschaftswunder (German for "economic miracle") is the term for the rapid reconstruction and sustained growth of the economies of West Germany (and Austria) after World War II. From a devastated, divided country in 1948, West Germany grew into one of the world's leading industrial and export powers within a decade.

PeriodRoughly 1948 – early 1960s
LocationWest Germany (and Austria)
Key figuresLudwig Erhard, Konrad Adenauer
ModelSocial market economy (Soziale Marktwirtschaft)

Causes

The turning point was the currency reform of June 1948, which replaced the Reichsmark with the Deutsche Mark and, together with Economics Minister Ludwig Erhard's abolition of price controls, ended the black-market economy almost overnight. The Marshall Plan supplied aid and confidence; the Korean War boom boosted demand for German machinery. West Germany also had a large pool of skilled labor, including millions of expellees from the east, and adopted Erhard's "social market economy" — free competition combined with a welfare state and an independent central bank.

Course and effects

Industrial production more than doubled between 1948 and 1951, and unemployment fell from about 10 percent in 1950 to near zero by the early 1960s, prompting recruitment of Gastarbeiter ("guest workers") from Italy, Turkey, and elsewhere. Consumer goods such as the Volkswagen Beetle became symbols of renewed prosperity, and exports earned Germany persistent trade surpluses.

Legacy

The miracle legitimized the West German state and its integration into the Western alliance and the European Economic Community. The term has since been applied to other postwar booms, notably Japan's and South Korea's.
