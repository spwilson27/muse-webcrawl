Valor Ecclesiasticus

The Valor Ecclesiasticus (Latin for "Church Valuation") was a survey of the finances of the Church in England, Wales and the English-controlled parts of Ireland carried out in 1535 on the orders of King Henry VIII. It recorded the income and property of every benefice, monastery and ecclesiastical institution in the kingdom, and it provided the fiscal basis for the Dissolution of the Monasteries that followed.

Background

Following the break with Rome, the Act for First Fruits and Tenths (1534) redirected to the Crown the payments clergy had previously made to the papacy, requiring an accurate valuation of all ecclesiastical livings. Commissioners were appointed in every county in January 1535 and completed their returns with remarkable speed, reporting by the summer of the same year.

Contents and consequences

The survey recorded the gross and net income of some 8,000–9,000 benefices and around 800 religious houses, revealing that the Church held a substantial share of the landed wealth of England — its total annual income was estimated at over £300,000, considerably more than the Crown's ordinary revenue. The figures supplied Thomas Cromwell with the evidence used to justify the suppression of the lesser monasteries under the Act of 1536, which targeted houses with incomes below £200 a year, and informed the wider dissolution completed by 1540.

Historical significance

The returns, preserved in record volumes and published by the Record Commission in the early nineteenth century, remain an essential source for historians of the English Reformation, local history and the medieval Church, comparable in importance to Domesday Book for the study of ecclesiastical wealth on the eve of the Dissolution.
