Simchat Torah (Hebrew: שִׂמְחַת תּוֹרָה‎, "Rejoicing with the Torah") is a Jewish holiday that celebrates and marks the conclusion of the annual cycle of public Torah readings, and the beginning of a new cycle. It is a component of the biblical Jewish holiday of Shemini Atzeret ("Eighth Day of Assembly"), which follows immediately after the festival of Sukkot in the month of Tishrei (falling in September or October on the Gregorian calendar).

The main celebrations of Simchat Torah take place in the synagogue during evening and morning services. The Torah scrolls are removed from the ark and carried around the sanctuary in a series of seven circuits known as hakafot, accompanied by singing and dancing. Children often participate carrying flags and are given treats.

Observances

On Simchat Torah, the final portion of the Book of Deuteronomy is read, completing the annual cycle, and the first chapter of Genesis is then read immediately afterward, beginning the cycle anew. The person honored with the final reading of Deuteronomy is called the Hatan Torah ("Groom of the Torah"), and the one who begins Genesis is the Hatan Bereishit ("Groom of Genesis").

In the evening service and again the following morning, all the Torah scrolls are taken from the ark and paraded around the synagogue. In many communities the celebrations, featuring song and dance with the Torah scrolls, can last for several hours.

History

The custom of concluding and restarting the Torah reading on Shemini Atzeret developed in the Babylonian Jewish community during the early medieval period, where the annual reading cycle was completed in a single year, in contrast to the older triennial cycle used in the Land of Israel. The name "Simchat Torah" came into use later, during the geonic period.
