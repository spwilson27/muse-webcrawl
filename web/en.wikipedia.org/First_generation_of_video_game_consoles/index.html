First generation of video game consoles

The Magnavox Odyssey (1972), the first commercial home video game console.

The first generation of video game consoles lasted from 1972 to 1983. The generation began with the release of the Magnavox Odyssey, the first home video game console, in 1972, and was defined by dedicated consoles: systems built with discrete transistor–transistor logic circuits that contained one or a small number of built-in games and no microprocessor.

Characteristics

First-generation systems used hard-wired analogue and discrete digital circuitry rather than software on ROM. Games were selected by switches, jumpers, or plug-in cards that altered circuit connections, and graphics were extremely simple — blocks, lines, and dots rendered on a television screen, sometimes supplemented by plastic screen overlays, playing cards, and paper money as accessories. Sound, if present, was a simple beep produced by the console.

Major systems

The Magnavox Odyssey, designed by Ralph H. Baer and his team, launched in 1972 with games including a table-tennis game that inspired Atari's arcade hit Pong. Atari's home version of Pong (1975), sold through Sears, popularised home gaming, and dozens of dedicated Pong consoles followed from companies including Coleco (the Telstar series). The era also included the APF TV Fun and numerous clones produced worldwide. The generation faded as microprocessor-based consoles with interchangeable cartridges — the second generation, led by the Fairchild Channel F (1976) and Atari 2600 (1977) — took over the market, though dedicated consoles continued to be sold into the early 1980s.
