Transistor model


The hybrid-pi small-signal model

Transistor models are mathematical and circuit representations used in electronic design to predict the behaviour of bipolar junction transistors (BJTs) and field-effect transistors (FETs). Because real transistor behaviour is nonlinear and depends on operating point, temperature and frequency, engineers use a hierarchy of models of increasing complexity, most often inside circuit simulators such as SPICE.

Types of model

Large-signal (DC) models describe the terminal currents and voltages over the full operating range; the classic example is the Ebers–Moll model of the BJT, which represents the transistor as two coupled diodes with current gain. The Gummel–Poon model extends it with effects such as base-width modulation and high-level injection. For MOSFETs, models range from the simple Shichman–Hodges square-law model to the industry-standard BSIM family used for integrated-circuit design.

Small-signal models linearise the device around a bias point for AC analysis. The BJT's hybrid-pi model represents the base-emitter junction by a resistance rπ and a voltage-controlled current source gmvbe; the earlier hybrid (h-parameter) model expresses the device with input and output impedances and current and voltage gains. Similar small-signal models exist for MOSFETs, incorporating transconductance gm and output resistance ro.

Choosing a model is a trade-off between accuracy and simplicity: hand analysis uses idealised small-signal models, while simulation models may include dozens of parameters extracted from measurements of real devices.
