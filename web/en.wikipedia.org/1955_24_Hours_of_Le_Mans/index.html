1955 24 Hours of Le Mans

The 1955 24 Hours of Le Mans (23e Grand Prix d'Endurance les 24 Heures du Mans) was held on 11–12 June 1955 at the Circuit de la Sarthe, France. The race is remembered above all for the worst accident in motor racing history: the "Le Mans disaster", in which Pierre Levegh's Mercedes-Benz 300 SLR was launched into the crowd, killing Levegh and 83 spectators and injuring well over a hundred more.

The disaster

Early in the third hour, as Mike Hawthorn's Jaguar braked for a pit stop, Lance Macklin's slower Austin-Healey swerved to avoid him and into the path of Levegh's Mercedes travelling at roughly 200 km/h. The Mercedes struck the rear of the Austin-Healey and was launched over an embankment into a packed grandstand area; its engine and front axle ploughed through the crowd and the car burst into flames, its magnesium-alloy body burning fiercely. Levegh was killed instantly.

The race continues

Organisers controversially decided the race should continue, partly to keep spectators from clogging roads needed by ambulances. Mercedes withdrew its remaining cars in the early hours at the request of its board. The Jaguar D-Type of Mike Hawthorn and Ivor Bueb won, covering a record distance.

Aftermath

The disaster prompted sweeping safety reforms and temporary bans on motor racing in several countries; Switzerland's ban on circuit racing lasted for decades. Mercedes-Benz withdrew from motorsport entirely and did not return to top-level racing until the 1980s. Spectator protection, pit-lane design and circuit layouts were permanently changed by the lessons of 1955.
