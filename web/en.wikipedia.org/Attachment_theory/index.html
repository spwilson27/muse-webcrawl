Attachment theory

Attachment theory is a psychological framework concerning the relationships between humans, particularly the importance of early bonds between children and their caregivers. It was formulated by psychiatrist and psychoanalyst John Bowlby in the mid-20th century and developed empirically by Mary Ainsworth.

Bowlby's theory

Bowlby proposed that infants are biologically predisposed to seek proximity to attachment figures in times of distress, and that the quality of early care shapes internal working models of relationships. Children deprived of consistent care may suffer lasting difficulties, as Bowlby argued in his work on maternal deprivation.

Ainsworth's patterns

Using the "Strange Situation" laboratory procedure, Mary Ainsworth identified attachment patterns: secure, anxious-avoidant, and anxious-ambivalent/resistant; Mary Main later added disorganized attachment. These styles are associated with later social and emotional development.

Adult attachment

Later research extended the theory to adult romantic relationships, describing attachment styles along dimensions of anxiety and avoidance, and to clinical practice, education, and social work.
