The Papal States (Italian: Stato Pontificio) were a series of territories in central Italy under the direct sovereign rule of the Pope from 756 until 1870. At their height they stretched across the Italian peninsula from the Tyrrhenian to the Adriatic, encompassing much of the modern regions of Lazio, Umbria, Marche and Emilia-Romagna, and made the papacy one of the major political powers of Italy for more than a thousand years.

Origins

The temporal power of the popes grew as Byzantine authority in Italy collapsed in the 6th–8th centuries and the bishops of Rome assumed civil administration of the city. In 756 the Frankish king Pepin the Short, having defeated the Lombards, granted the pope the former Byzantine territories around Rome and Ravenna — the "Donation of Pepin" — which Charlemagne confirmed in 774. A forged document, the Donation of Constantine, was later used to bolster papal claims.

Medieval and Renaissance state

Through the Middle Ages papal control over the provinces was often nominal, contested by local nobles, communes and the Holy Roman Emperors. Renaissance popes such as Alexander VI, Julius II and their successors reasserted direct rule, and under Julius II — the "warrior pope" — the Papal States became a leading Italian power and patron of the arts, rebuilding Rome and St Peter's Basilica. The states survived the upheavals of the Reformation and the Napoleonic era, when they were twice abolished and restored.

Unification and end

During the Risorgimento, the Papal States shrank as Romagna, Marche and Umbria were annexed by Piedmont-Sardinia in 1860. Rome itself was protected by a French garrison, but when the Franco-Prussian War forced its withdrawal, Italian troops breached the walls at Porta Pia on 20 September 1870 and Rome became the capital of the Kingdom of Italy. Pope Pius IX rejected the settlement and declared himself a "prisoner in the Vatican"; the resulting "Roman Question" was resolved only in 1929, when the Lateran Treaty created the sovereign micro-state of Vatican City.
