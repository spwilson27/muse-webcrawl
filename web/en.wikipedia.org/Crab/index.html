Crabs are decapod crustaceans of the infraorder Brachyura, which typically have a very short projecting tail and a thick exoskeleton and a single pair of pincers. They live in all the world's oceans, in freshwater, and on land, are generally covered with a thick exoskeleton, and have a single pair of pincers on their front legs (chelipeds).

About 7,000 species have been described. They range in size from the pea crab, a few millimetres wide, to the Japanese spider crab, with a leg span of up to 4 metres (13 ft). Many crabs are important food species; global fisheries land over a million tonnes annually.

Anatomy and behaviour

The crab body is protected by a broad carapace; the abdomen is reduced and folded beneath the thorax. Crabs typically walk sideways, because of the articulation of their legs, though some can run forward and others swim with flattened paddle-like rear legs. Most are omnivorous scavengers. Males often possess larger claws, used in display and combat.

Crabs communicate by drumming or waving their pincers. Many species undergo complex courtship rituals. Females carry fertilised eggs under the abdomen until they hatch into free-swimming zoea larvae.

Carcinisation

The crab-like body form has evolved independently many times among decapods — a classic example of convergent evolution known as carcinisation — giving rise to "false crabs" such as king crabs and porcelain crabs among the Anomura.
