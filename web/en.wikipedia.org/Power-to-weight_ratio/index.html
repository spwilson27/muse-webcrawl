Power-to-weight ratio

Power-to-weight ratio (or specific power, power-to-mass ratio) is a calculation commonly applied to engines and mobile power sources to enable comparison of one unit or design to another. It is the power output of an engine (or the tractive force of a motor) divided by the mass it must move, and is widely used as a measure of the performance potential of vehicles, aircraft, and athletes.

Units and calculation

The ratio is expressed in units such as watts per kilogram (W/kg) in SI, or horsepower per pound or per tonne in automotive practice. For a vehicle, one divides the engine's peak power by the vehicle's kerb weight. Higher values indicate greater potential acceleration and climbing ability, though actual performance also depends on gearing, traction, aerodynamics, and drivetrain losses.

Examples

Typical passenger cars achieve roughly 60–120 W/kg; high-performance sports cars exceed 300 W/kg, and Formula One cars and superbikes approach or exceed 700–1,000 W/kg. In aviation, power-to-weight (or thrust-to-weight for jets) determines climb and manoeuvrability. In human-powered performance, elite cyclists can sustain about 6 W/kg for an hour, and the ratio is a standard predictor of climbing speed in cycling. For batteries and energy storage, the related concept of specific power describes the rate at which energy can be delivered per unit mass.
