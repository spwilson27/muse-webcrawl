Execution of Charles I

The execution of Charles I took place on 30 January 1649 outside the Banqueting House in Whitehall, London. Charles, king of England, Scotland, and Ireland, had been tried by a High Court of Justice created by the Rump Parliament after the Second English Civil War, and convicted of high treason for waging war against his own people. His beheading was an unprecedented act — the first public execution of a reigning European monarch by his own subjects after a formal trial.

Trial

Charles refused to plead, denying the court's legitimacy: "I would know by what power I am called hither." Over a week of proceedings, the court led by John Bradshaw heard witnesses to the king's conduct in the civil wars. On 27 January he was sentenced to death as a "tyrant, traitor, murderer and public enemy." Fifty-nine commissioners signed the death warrant.

The execution

On a bitterly cold day, Charles walked under guard through the Banqueting House — beneath Rubens's ceiling celebrating his father's reign — to a scaffold draped in black. He wore two shirts so that shivering from cold would not be mistaken for fear. He addressed the crowd, declaring himself "the martyr of the people," forgave his executioner, and prayed. He was beheaded with a single stroke; the masked executioner's identity remains disputed. Groans reportedly rose from the crowd, and some dipped handkerchiefs in the royal blood.

Aftermath

The monarchy and House of Lords were abolished and England declared a Commonwealth. Charles was canonized by the Church of England in 1660 after the Restoration, the only saint formally added to its calendar. The legal machinery of the trial — an executor of a death warrant among the signatories — remains a defining precedent in the history of regicide and constitutional law.
