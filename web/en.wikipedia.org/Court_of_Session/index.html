Court of Session

The Court of Session (Scottish Gaelic: Cùirt an t-Seisein) is the supreme civil court of Scotland and constitutes, together with the High Court of Justiciary, the Supreme Courts of Scotland, which sit in Parliament House in Edinburgh. It was established in 1532 by King James V as the College of Justice, and its judges — Senators of the College of Justice — also sit as judges of the High Court of Justiciary, Scotland's supreme criminal court.

Parliament House, Edinburgh.

History

The court owes its foundation to a bull of Pope Paul III and an Act of the Parliament of Scotland in 1532, which created a permanent college of fifteen lords — half clerical, half lay — presided over by the Lord President, initially the Abbot of Cambuskenneth, Alexander Mylne. It replaced the earlier, irregular judicial sittings of the Lords of Council. The Treaty of Union of 1707 expressly preserved the Court of Session "in all time coming", though appeals in civil cases came to be heard by the House of Lords and, since 2009, by the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom — except in devolution and certain other matters where the UK Supreme Court's role differs.

Structure

The court is divided into the Outer House, a court of first instance in which a single judge (a Lord Ordinary) hears cases, sometimes with a civil jury, and the Inner House, which hears appeals ("reclaiming motions") from the Outer House and from lower courts, sitting in two divisions of equal authority. The First Division is presided over by the Lord President — the head of the Scottish judiciary — and the Second Division by the Lord Justice Clerk.

Jurisdiction and procedure

The Court of Session has jurisdiction over the whole of Scotland in civil matters, including judicial review of administrative action, commercial disputes, personal injury claims and family actions such as divorce in defended cases. Its decisions shape Scots law, a mixed legal system drawing on Roman-Dutch civilian tradition and common law precedent. Advocates (members of the Faculty of Advocates) enjoy rights of audience, alongside solicitor-advocates.
