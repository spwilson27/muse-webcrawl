Antenna (radio)

TypeElectromagnetic transducer
FunctionConverts electric currents to radio waves and vice versa

In radio engineering, an antenna is the interface between radio waves propagating through space and electric currents moving in metal conductors, used with a transmitter or receiver. In transmission, a radio transmitter supplies an electric current oscillating at radio frequency to the antenna's terminals, and the antenna radiates the energy as electromagnetic waves. In reception, an antenna intercepts some of the power of a radio wave in order to produce an electric current at its terminals, which is then applied to a receiver to be amplified.

Antennas are essential components of all radio equipment and are used in radio broadcasting, broadcast television, two-way radio, communications receivers, radar, mobile phones, satellite communications, and wireless networking.

History

The first antennas were built by Heinrich Hertz in 1888 during his experiments proving the existence of electromagnetic waves predicted by James Clerk Maxwell. Guglielmo Marconi adapted antennas for long-distance communication around 1900–1901, achieving the first transatlantic radio transmission. The theoretical treatment was advanced by workers including Lord Rayleigh, Oliver Lodge, and later Harold Beverage, Hidetsugu Yagi, and John Kraus.

Operation

An antenna works because accelerating electric charges radiate electromagnetic fields. Alternating current in the antenna elements causes electrons to oscillate, producing fields that detach and propagate at the speed of light. The size of an efficient antenna is generally comparable to the wavelength of the radio waves used; a resonant dipole, for example, is approximately half a wavelength long.

Types

  • Dipole antenna — two conductive elements fed at the centre; the most fundamental resonant antenna.
  • Monopole antenna — a single element over a ground plane, such as a car whip antenna.
  • Yagi–Uda antenna — a directional array of a driven element, reflector, and directors, common for television.
  • Loop antenna — a coil of wire, often used with ferrite cores in AM receivers.
  • Parabolic reflector — a dish antenna giving high gain, used for satellite and radar work.
  • Patch and phased-array antennas — planar elements used in mobile phones, Wi-Fi, and modern radar.

Characteristics

Antenna performance is described by its radiation pattern (the directional distribution of radiated power), gain relative to an isotropic radiator (dBi) or a dipole (dBd), input impedance and the standing wave ratio in its feed line, bandwidth over which it operates effectively, and polarisation of the emitted wave.
