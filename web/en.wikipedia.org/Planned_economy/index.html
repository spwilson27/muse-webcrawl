Planned economy

Five-year plans were the hallmark of Soviet-style planning

A planned economy is a type of economic system where the distribution of goods and services or the investment, production and allocation of capital goods takes place according to economic plans that are either economy-wide or limited to a category of goods and services. A planned economy may use centralised, decentralised, participatory or Soviet-type forms of economic planning. The level of centralisation or decentralisation in decision-making and participation depends on the specific type of planning mechanism employed.

Central planning

In a centrally planned or command economy, a central authority — typically a state planning agency such as the Soviet Gosplan — sets production targets, allocates resources and fixes prices, and state-owned enterprises carry out the plan. The Soviet Union adopted a series of five-year plans from 1928, emphasising rapid industrialisation and heavy industry; the model was later extended to Eastern Europe, China and other socialist states. Planners used material balances to match inputs and outputs across thousands of products.

Criticism and debate

Critics such as Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek argued that comprehensive planning faces an economic calculation problem: without market prices, planners lack the information needed to allocate resources rationally, and the dispersed knowledge of millions of actors cannot be centralised — arguments central to the socialist calculation debate. Supporters countered that planning could mobilise resources for rapid industrialisation, full employment and public goods, and pointed to wartime mobilisations in market economies as evidence of effective planning. In practice, Soviet-type economies achieved fast early industrial growth but suffered chronic shortages, poor incentives, soft budget constraints and weak innovation, and most were dismantled or reformed toward market mechanisms from the 1980s onward. Elements of planning — industrial policy, indicative planning and public provision — persist within many otherwise market economies.
