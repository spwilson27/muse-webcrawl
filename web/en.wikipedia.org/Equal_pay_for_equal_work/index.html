Equal pay for equal work

Equal pay for equal work is the concept of labour rights that individuals in the same workplace be given equal pay. It is most commonly used in the context of sexual discrimination, in relation to the gender pay gap. The principle holds that men and women in the same workplace, performing equal work, must be paid equally.

History

The demand for equal pay formed part of the early trade union movement and was advanced by the women's suffrage campaign. The International Labour Organization adopted the Equal Remuneration Convention (Convention No. 100) in 1951, committing member states to equal remuneration for men and women for work of equal value. In the United States, the Equal Pay Act of 1963 amended the Fair Labor Standards Act to prohibit sex-based wage discrimination. In the United Kingdom, the Equal Pay Act 1970 was passed after industrial action by women sewing machinists at the Ford plant in Dagenham.

The gender pay gap

The gender pay gap is the average difference between the remuneration of men and women who are working. It is measured both as an unadjusted figure (comparing overall median or mean earnings) and an adjusted figure (accounting for differences in occupation, hours, tenure and experience). Even after adjustment, a residual gap persists in most economies, which economists attribute in part to discrimination and in part to unmeasured factors.

Legislation

Article 157 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union enshrines the principle of equal pay. Several countries have introduced pay transparency laws requiring employers to report gender pay gaps. Campaigns such as Equal Pay Day symbolise the point in the year from which women effectively work for free relative to men.
