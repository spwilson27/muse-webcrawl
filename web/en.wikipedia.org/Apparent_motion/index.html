Apparent motion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldPerceptual psychology / Gestalt psychology
Key figureMax Wertheimer (1912)
Related phenomenaPhi phenomenon; beta movement; persistence of vision

Apparent motion is the perception of movement produced by a sequence of stationary stimuli presented in rapid succession at slightly different positions — although nothing actually moves, the observer sees a single object travelling through space. It is the perceptual basis of cinema, animation and many display technologies.

History and types

In 1912 Max Wertheimer's experiments on the phi phenomenon founded Gestalt psychology: two lights flashed in alternation at the right interval are seen as one light in motion, and at optimal timing produce pure, objectless movement. The closely related beta movement gives the vivid impression of an object moving continuously between positions. Psychologists distinguish short-range processes, driven by low-level motion detectors, from long-range processes that match objects across frames.

Applications

Film at 24 frames per second, television, flip books, scrolling LED signs and stroboscopic effects all exploit apparent motion. Its timing laws — summarised in Korte's laws relating distance, interval and intensity — guide animation and interface design.
