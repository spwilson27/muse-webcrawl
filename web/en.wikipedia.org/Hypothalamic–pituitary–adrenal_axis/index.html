Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis

Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis
A major neuroendocrine system controlling reactions to stress and regulating digestion, immunity, mood and energy storage.

The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (HPA axis or HTPA axis) is a complex set of direct influences and feedback interactions among three components: the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland and the adrenal glands. It is a central part of the neuroendocrine system and governs the body's stress response.

Mechanism

When the brain perceives stress, the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and vasopressin into the portal circulation. These stimulate the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which travels through the blood to the adrenal cortex and prompts release of glucocorticoids, principally cortisol in humans. Cortisol mobilises glucose, modulates immunity and then feeds back to suppress CRH and ACTH, closing the loop.

Rhythm and regulation

HPA activity follows a circadian rhythm — cortisol peaks shortly after waking and declines through the day — and is driven upward by stressors including illness, pain, fear and blood loss, so the axis participates in the response to emergencies such as hypovolemia and severe infection.

Disorders

Overproduction of cortisol causes Cushing's syndrome, while adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) reflects failure of the axis's output. Chronic dysregulation of the HPA axis has been implicated in depression, post-traumatic stress disorder and metabolic disease. The dexamethasone suppression test probes its feedback integrity. The HPA axis is distinct from the reproductive hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, though both share the same hypothalamic–pituitary control architecture.
