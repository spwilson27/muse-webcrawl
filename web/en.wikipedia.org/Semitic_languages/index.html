Semitic languages

The Semitic languages are a branch of the Afroasiatic language family. They include Arabic, Amharic, Tigrinya, Aramaic, Hebrew, Maltese and numerous other ancient and modern languages. They are spoken by more than 330 million people across much of West Asia, North Africa, the Horn of Africa, Malta and in large immigrant and expatriate communities in Europe and the Americas.

The terminology was first used in the 1780s by members of the Göttingen School of History; the name derives from Shem, a son of Noah in the Book of Genesis.

History

The earliest attested Semitic language is Akkadian of Mesopotamia, recorded in cuneiform from about the 25th century BCE. Other ancient Semitic languages include Eblaite, Ugaritic, Phoenician, Biblical Hebrew, and Old Aramaic. Aramaic became a lingua franca of the Near East under the Persian Empire, and Arabic spread widely with the early Islamic conquests in the 7th century CE. In Ethiopia and Eritrea, languages such as Ge'ez and its modern descendants Amharic and Tigrinya have been spoken for over two millennia.

Classification

Semitic languages are commonly divided into East Semitic (Akkadian, Eblaite—extinct) and West Semitic, the latter subdivided into Central Semitic (Arabic, Northwest Semitic including Hebrew and Aramaic) and South Semitic (the Modern South Arabian languages and the Ethiopian Semitic languages). The details of the subgrouping are debated.

Characteristics

Semitic languages are characterised by root-and-pattern morphology: words are typically built on consonantal roots, most often of three consonants, into which vowel patterns and affixes are interdigitated to form related words (e.g., Arabic k-t-b in kitāb "book", kātib "writer", kataba "he wrote"). Many also feature emphatic (pharyngealized) consonants and guttural consonants such as pharyngeals.

Writing systems

Semitic languages have been written in several scripts: cuneiform, the consonantal alphabets (abjads) descended from Proto-Sinaitic—including the Phoenician, Aramaic, Hebrew, Syriac and Arabic scripts—and the Ethiopian Ge'ez script, an abugida. Maltese is written in the Latin alphabet.
