Action potential

Action potential
Resting potential: about −70 mV
Threshold: about −55 mV
Peak: about +30 to +40 mV
Duration: ~1 ms in neurons

An action potential (also called a nerve impulse or spike) is a rapid, transient change in the membrane potential of an excitable cell — such as a neuron or muscle cell — that travels along the cell's membrane. It is the fundamental unit of electrical signalling in the nervous system.

Phases

At rest, the membrane sits near −70 mV. When a stimulus depolarises the membrane past a threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and Na+ rushes in, driving the potential up sharply (depolarisation). Voltage-gated potassium channels then open and sodium channels inactivate, so K+ leaves the cell and the potential falls (repolarisation), often overshooting into a brief hyperpolarisation. The sodium–potassium pump and leak channels restore the resting state. Because a stretch of membrane cannot immediately fire again, the signal propagates in one direction; this is the refractory period.

Propagation and significance

In myelinated axons, action potentials jump between nodes of Ranvier — saltatory conduction — greatly increasing speed, up to about 120 m/s. The "all-or-none" character of the spike means information is encoded in firing frequency and timing rather than amplitude. The ionic mechanism was worked out by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley in experiments on the squid giant axon, published in 1952, for which they received the Nobel Prize in 1963.
