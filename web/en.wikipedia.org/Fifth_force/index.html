Fifth force

In physics, the fifth force is a hypothetical fundamental interaction beyond the four known fundamental forces: gravitation, electromagnetism, the strong interaction and the weak interaction. Many theories beyond the Standard Model — including some theories of dark matter, dark energy and modified gravity — predict new, very weak forces acting over ranges from sub-millimetre distances to astrophysical scales.

History

Interest in a fifth force surged in 1986, when Ephraim Fischbach and collaborators reanalysed the classic Eötvös experiment data and reported evidence for a composition-dependent force with a range of roughly 100 metres, which would imply a slight violation of the equivalence principle. The claim prompted a wave of experiments using torsion balances, gravimeters in mines and towers, and free-fall measurements. By the early 1990s the consensus was that the original signal was an artefact, and no fifth force of that strength and range was confirmed.

Experimental searches

Modern searches test the inverse-square law of gravity at short range with torsion balances and micro-cantilevers, look for deviations from Newtonian gravity with atom interferometry, and test the equivalence principle with ever greater precision — including the MICROSCOPE satellite mission, which compared the free fall of different materials in orbit. Proposed mediators include light scalar particles such as dilatons and chameleons, and hypothetical particles such as the "X17" anomaly reported in some nuclear transition experiments, which remains unconfirmed.
