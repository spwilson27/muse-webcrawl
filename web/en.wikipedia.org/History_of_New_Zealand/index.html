History of New Zealand


Artist's impression of the first Māori landfall in Aotearoa

The history of New Zealand (Aotearoa) is the story of human settlement of one of the last major landmasses on Earth to be populated. Polynesian voyagers settled the islands between about 1250 and 1300 CE, developing the distinctive Māori culture. The first European to sight the country was the Dutch explorer Abel Tasman in 1642, and sustained European contact began after Captain James Cook's voyages from 1769. In 1840 representatives of the British Crown and Māori chiefs signed the Treaty of Waitangi, and New Zealand became a British colony. The colony became self-governing in the 1850s, a dominion in 1907, and fully independent in practice over the following decades, while remaining a constitutional monarchy within the Commonwealth.

Māori settlement

Archaeological and genetic evidence indicates that Polynesians from East Polynesia — probably the Society Islands region — settled New Zealand in several waves of waka (canoe) voyages between roughly 1250 and 1300. The settlers adapted to a cooler temperate environment, cultivating kūmara (sweet potato) in the north and relying heavily on hunting, fishing and gathering in the south. The giant flightless moa were hunted to extinction within about two centuries. Māori society was organised into iwi (tribes) and hapū (sub-tribes), and competition for resources led to the construction of fortified pā, particularly during the period sometimes called the Classic Māori period from about 1500.

European contact

Abel Tasman's expedition sighted the west coast of the South Island in December 1642; a violent encounter with Māori at Golden Bay, in which four of Tasman's crew were killed, discouraged further exploration. The Dutch named the land Staten Landt and later Nieuw Zeeland, after the Dutch province of Zeeland. James Cook charted the coastline extensively in 1769–70 and again on later voyages, opening the way for sealers, whalers, traders and missionaries. From the 1790s, Māori traded flax, timber and food for European goods, including muskets; the intertribal Musket Wars of the early 19th century caused thousands of deaths and major population displacement.

Treaty and colonisation

On 6 February 1840, Lieutenant-Governor William Hobson and Māori chiefs signed the Treaty of Waitangi. The Māori and English texts differ in important respects: the English text ceded sovereignty to the Crown, while the Māori text granted kāwanatanga (governorship) while reserving tino rangatiratanga (full chieftainship) over lands and treasures. Systematic colonisation followed under the New Zealand Company and its settlements at Wellington, Nelson, New Plymouth, Dunedin (1848) and Christchurch (1850). Gold rushes in Otago (1861) and the West Coast boosted population. Disputes over land sales erupted into the New Zealand Wars (1845–1872), after which the Native Land Court and confiscations (raupatu) transferred much Māori land to settlers.

Self-government and social reform

A constitution act in 1852 established representative government, and responsible government followed in 1856. Under Premier Richard Seddon (1893–1906) New Zealand gained a reputation as a "social laboratory": it was the first self-governing country to grant women the vote (1893), and introduced old-age pensions in 1898. Refrigerated shipping from 1882 made frozen meat and dairy exports to Britain the backbone of the economy. New Zealand forces served in the South African War, and in the First World War some 100,000 men served overseas from a population of about 1.1 million; the Gallipoli landing of 25 April 1915 became central to national commemoration as Anzac Day.

Depression, welfare state and war

The Great Depression hit export incomes hard. The first Labour government, elected in 1935 under Michael Joseph Savage, expanded state housing, introduced comprehensive social security (1938) and renationalised the central bank. New Zealand declared war on Germany in 1939; its forces fought in Greece, Crete, North Africa and Italy, and in the Pacific. Māori served in the celebrated 28 (Māori) Battalion.

Post-war era

Post-war New Zealand prospered as a pastoral economy closely tied to Britain. Urbanisation accelerated, including large-scale Māori migration to the cities. Britain's entry into the European Economic Community in 1973 forced economic diversification. The 1975 Treaty of Waitangi Act created the Waitangi Tribunal to investigate Māori grievances; from 1985 it could examine historical claims, leading to major settlements in the 1990s. The fourth Labour government (1984–1990) under David Lange introduced sweeping free-market reforms ("Rogernomics") and a nuclear-free policy that led to suspension from the ANZUS alliance after the 1985 bombing of the Rainbow Warrior by French agents in Auckland. In 1996 New Zealand adopted the MMP electoral system. Notable 21st-century events include the Canterbury earthquakes (2010–2011), the Christchurch mosque shootings of 15 March 2019, and the country's COVID-19 elimination response from 2020.
