Minnesota Starvation Experiment

The Minnesota Starvation Experiment, also known as the Minnesota Semi-Starvation Experiment, was a clinical study performed at the University of Minnesota between November 19, 1944, and December 20, 1945. Led by physiologist Ancel Keys, the investigation studied the effects of prolonged dietary restriction and the effectiveness of dietary rehabilitation strategies, in order to guide relief efforts for famine victims in Europe and Asia at the end of the Second World War.

Design

Thirty-six young men, selected from more than 400 conscientious objectors who volunteered, were chosen for their physical and mental health. The study had three phases: a three-month control period on a normal diet of about 3,200 calories per day; a six-month semi-starvation period of about 1,570 calories per day based on European famine foods such as potatoes, rutabagas, turnips, bread and macaroni, designed to produce a loss of roughly 25 per cent of body weight; and a three-month rehabilitation phase with varying levels of refeeding.

Findings

During semi-starvation the men experienced dramatic decreases in strength, stamina, body temperature, heart rate and sex drive, together with depression, hysteria, hypochondriasis, intense preoccupation with food, hoarding of utensils and cookbooks, and in some cases self-mutilation. Refeeding proved slow: recovery of strength lagged far behind weight gain, and the study concluded that effective rehabilitation required far more calories — around 4,000 per day — than planners had assumed.

Publication

The full results were published in 1950 in the two-volume work The Biology of Human Starvation by Keys and colleagues. The experiment remains a landmark reference in the study of starvation, eating disorders and famine relief, and is frequently cited in discussions of research ethics.
