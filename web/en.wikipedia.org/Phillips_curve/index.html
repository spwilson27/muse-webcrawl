Phillips curve

The Phillips curve is an economic theory, named after William Phillips, describing an inverse relationship between rates of unemployment and corresponding rates of inflation within an economy. Put simply, decreased unemployment in an economy will correlate with higher rates of inflation.

History

Phillips, in his 1958 paper "The Relation between Unemployment and the Rate of Change of Money Wage Rates in the United Kingdom, 1861–1957", plotted wage inflation against unemployment in the UK and found a stable downward-sloping relationship. Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow recast the relationship in terms of price inflation for the United States.

Stagflation and expectations

The stagflation of the 1970s, with simultaneously high inflation and unemployment, challenged the simple trade-off. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that the trade-off vanishes in the long run once inflation expectations adjust: the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment (NAIRU), leading to the expectations-augmented Phillips curve.

Modern forms

The New Keynesian Phillips curve relates inflation to expected future inflation and real marginal cost. The apparent flattening of the curve in many advanced economies since the 1990s remains debated.
