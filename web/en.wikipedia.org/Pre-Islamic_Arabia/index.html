Pre-Islamic Arabia

Pre-Islamic Arabia (Arabic: شبه الجزيرة العربية قبل الإسلام) is the Arabian Peninsula before the rise of Islam in the 630s CE. It includes various civilizations and kingdoms, including the South Arabian kingdoms of Saba, Himyar, Qataban, and Ma'in; the Nabataean kingdom in the north; and the trading cities of Mecca and Medina in the Hejaz.

Society and religion

Much of Arabia was inhabited by nomadic and semi-nomadic Arab tribes, alongside settled agricultural communities, especially in the well-watered south (modern Yemen), famous for frankincense and myrrh trade. Religion in pre-Islamic Arabia consisted of indigenous polytheistic beliefs, with deities such as Hubal, al-Lat, al-Uzza, and Manat, alongside communities of Jews and Christians, particularly in the south and along the Byzantine and Sasanian frontiers. Mecca's Kaaba was a major pilgrimage sanctuary housing many idols.

Kingdoms and empires

The peninsula lay between the Byzantine and Sasanian empires, which competed for influence through Arab client states: the Ghassanids served Byzantium and the Lakhmids served Persia. South Arabia's Himyarite kingdom adopted Judaism in the 4th century and was later conquered by the Aksumites and then by Sasanian Persia in the 6th century.

Language and poetry

Pre-Islamic Arabia produced a celebrated oral tradition of poetry, including the Mu'allaqat ("suspended odes"), composed in the classical Arabic that later became the language of the Quran and of Islamic scholarship.
