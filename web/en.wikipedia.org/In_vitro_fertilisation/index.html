In vitro fertilisation (IVF) is the most common form of assisted reproductive technology, in which an egg is combined with sperm outside the body, in vitro ("in glass"). The process involves monitoring and stimulating a person's ovulatory cycle, removing eggs from the ovaries, fertilising them with sperm in a culture medium in a laboratory, culturing the resulting embryos for several days, and transferring one or more embryos into the uterus with the intention of establishing a successful pregnancy.

History

Decades of animal research preceded human success: Walter Heape reported embryo transfer in rabbits in 1890, and Gregory Pincus and Miriam Menkin claimed early fertilisations of human eggs in the 1940s. In England, gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe and physiologist Robert Edwards developed the modern technique through the 1970s. After an ectopic pregnancy in 1976, they achieved the birth of Louise Brown on 25 July 1978 at Oldham General Hospital—the first baby born from IVF. Alastair MacDonald's birth followed months later. Edwards received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010 (Steptoe having died in 1988). IVF subsequently spread worldwide, with refinements including cryopreservation of embryos, donor eggs, and gestational surrogacy.

Procedure

A typical cycle involves ovarian stimulation with gonadotropin injections over roughly two weeks to mature multiple follicles, timed maturation with an hCG or GnRH-agonist trigger, and transvaginal ultrasound-guided retrieval of eggs under sedation. In the laboratory, eggs are inseminated conventionally or by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), in which a single sperm is injected into an egg—widely used for male-factor infertility since 1992. Embryos are cultured to the cleavage or blastocyst stage (day 3 or day 5–6), assessed, and transferred transcervically; surplus embryos may be frozen. Preimplantation genetic testing can screen embryos for chromosomal abnormalities or specific genetic diseases. Success rates per cycle vary strongly with age, exceeding 30–40% per embryo transfer in younger patients in many registries and declining sharply after 40.

Risks and debates

Risks include ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, multiple pregnancy (reduced by elective single-embryo transfer), and procedural complications. Ethical debates concern the moral status of embryos, their storage and disposition, sex selection, cost and access, and the regulation of surrogacy and gamete donation, which vary greatly by country.
