The Ulm School of Design (German: Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm, HfG Ulm) was a college of design founded in 1953 in Ulm, Germany, by Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher, and Max Bill. It was one of the world's most progressive design schools after the Bauhaus, and became a key model for industrial design education worldwide before closing in 1968.

History

The school was conceived as a memorial to Hans and Sophie Scholl, members of the White Rose resistance executed by the Nazis, and was funded partly with American Marshall Plan-era support (the Geschwister-Scholl-Stiftung). Max Bill, a Bauhaus alumnus, designed the school building (opened 1955) and served as first rector. After Bill's departure in 1957, the school under Tomás Maldonado shifted decisively toward science-based, systematic design method.

Approach

HfG Ulm replaced the Bauhaus emphasis on the artist-craftsman with a curriculum integrating ergonomics, semiotics, operations research, sociology, and engineering. Its design methodology — analytical, interdisciplinary, and socially responsible — shaped the corporate design of Braun (through Hans Gugelot and his students, including Dieter Rams) and the Lufthansa corporate identity designed by Otl Aicher's group. Aicher's pictograms for the 1972 Munich Olympics emerged from the school's visual communication work.

Closure and legacy

Political and funding disputes with the Baden-Württemberg state government led to the withdrawal of support, and the school closed in 1968. The Ulm model of design education was exported internationally, notably to India (National Institute of Design) and Latin America, and remains a touchstone in design theory.
