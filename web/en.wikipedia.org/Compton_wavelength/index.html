Named afterArthur Compton

The Compton wavelength is a quantum mechanical property of a particle, created by Arthur Compton in 1923 in his explanation of the scattering of photons by electrons (the Compton effect). It is equal to the wavelength of a photon whose energy is the same as the rest energy of the particle, and it sets the length scale at which relativistic quantum effects become essential for that particle.

Compton scattering: the shift depends on the electron's Compton wavelength

Formula

For a particle of mass m, the Compton wavelength is λC = h / (mc), where h is the Planck constant and c the speed of light. For the electron it is about 2.426 × 10−12 m. A related quantity, the reduced Compton wavelength ƛ = ħ/(mc), appears naturally in relativistic wave equations such as the Dirac and Klein–Gordon equations.

Physical significance

In Compton scattering, the change in a photon's wavelength when it scatters off a stationary particle at angle θ is Δλ = λC(1 − cos θ), so the Compton wavelength directly measures the effect that gave it its name. More fundamentally, attempting to localize a particle within a region smaller than roughly its (reduced) Compton wavelength requires so much energy that particle–antiparticle pair creation becomes significant, marking the limit of single-particle relativistic quantum mechanics and the onset of quantum field theory. It can also be viewed as the characteristic scale where the particle's rest energy equals the energy of a photon confined to that scale.

Relations

The Compton wavelength sits between two other fundamental lengths associated with a charged particle: the classical electron radius is smaller by a factor of the fine-structure constant α, while the Bohr radius is larger by 1/α; specifically, re = α ƛ, and a0 = ƛ/α.
