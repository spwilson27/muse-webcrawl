Declarative theory of statehood


State flags at the United Nations

The declarative theory of statehood is a doctrine in international law holding that an entity becomes a state as soon as it meets certain objective criteria for statehood, regardless of whether other states recognize it. It is contrasted with the constitutive theory of statehood, under which recognition by other states is itself a requirement.

Montevideo criteria

The criteria are codified in Article 1 of the Montevideo Convention of 1933, which states that a state should possess: (a) a permanent population; (b) a defined territory; (c) a government; and (d) the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Article 3 of the convention provides that the political existence of a state is independent of recognition by other states.

Application

The declarative theory is generally regarded as reflecting customary international law, though in practice recognition remains politically important for entities such as Kosovo, Taiwan, and Somaliland, whose status is disputed. Capacity to enter into foreign relations is often interpreted as independence from other states.
