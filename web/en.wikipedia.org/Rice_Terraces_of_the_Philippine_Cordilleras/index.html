The Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras are extensive systems of stepped rice paddies carved into the mountains of Ifugao Province on the island of Luzon in the Philippines. In 1995 five terrace clusters were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List — the first cultural landscape so honoured in the category of "evolved, living cultural landscapes".

Description

The terraces climb steep slopes at elevations above 1,000 metres, retained by walls of stone and mud and fed by intricate irrigation systems drawing water from protected watershed forests (muyong) above the paddies. The amphitheatre-shaped Batad terraces and the village-embedded Bangaan cluster are the best known. The terraces are inseparable from Ifugao culture: their construction, maintenance, and the rituals, songs (such as the hudhud epics), and communal labour that sustain them have been transmitted across generations.

Age and construction

Popular accounts long described the terraces as more than 2,000 years old. More recent archaeological research, including radiocarbon dating associated with the Ifugao Archaeological Project, suggests the massive terrace systems expanded substantially in the centuries after about 1600 AD, possibly as Indigenous communities reorganised settlement into the highlands in response to Spanish colonisation of the lowlands, intensifying wet-rice production for growing populations. Either way, they represent an enduring feat of hand-built engineering executed with minimal tools.

Threats and conservation

Out-migration of young people, the pull of wage labour, giant earthworm damage to terrace walls, and the costs of upkeep led UNESCO to place the site on the List of World Heritage in Danger from 2001 to 2012, during which Philippine authorities and local communities implemented conservation and livelihood programmes. The terraces remain both a working agricultural system and a national symbol, appearing on Philippine currency and passports, and are popularly called the "Eighth Wonder of the World".
