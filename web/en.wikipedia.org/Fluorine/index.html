Fluorine is a chemical element with the symbol F and atomic number 9. It is the lightest halogen and exists at standard conditions as a highly toxic, pale yellow diatomic gas. As the most electronegative element, it is extremely reactive, reacting with all other elements except for the light noble gases helium, neon, and argon. Among the elements, fluorine ranks 24th in universal abundance and 13th in terrestrial abundance.

Fluorite, the primary mineral source of fluorine, was first described in 1529; as it was added to metal ores to lower their melting points for smelting, the Latin verb fluo meaning "flow" gave the mineral and the element their names. Proposed as an element in 1810, fluorine proved difficult and dangerous to separate from its compounds, and several early experimenters died or sustained injuries from their attempts. Only in 1886 did French chemist Henri Moissan finally isolate elemental fluorine using low-temperature electrolysis, a process still employed for modern production. Moissan received the 1906 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this achievement.

Characteristics

Fluorine atoms have nine electrons, one fewer than neon, and electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁵. The second electron shell, which contains the valence electrons, is held close to the nucleus, giving fluorine the highest electronegativity of any element (3.98 on the Pauling scale) and a small atomic radius. The F–F bond in elemental fluorine is unusually weak, which — combined with the great strength of the bonds fluorine forms with other elements — accounts for its extreme reactivity.

Compounds

Fluorine forms compounds with every other element except helium, neon and argon. With metals it forms ionic fluorides such as sodium fluoride and calcium fluoride (fluorite). Its compounds with nonmetals are covalent; notable examples include hydrogen fluoride, sulfur hexafluoride (an extremely potent greenhouse gas), and the fluorocarbons. Hydrofluoric acid, the aqueous solution of hydrogen fluoride, etches glass and is used in industry and laboratories. Fluoride ions in trace amounts are essential for dental health, strengthening tooth enamel as fluorapatite, and many jurisdictions add fluoride to drinking water.

Applications

Principal uses of fluorine and its compounds include uranium enrichment (as uranium hexafluoride), the manufacture of fluoropolymers such as polytetrafluoroethylene (Teflon), refrigerants, sulfur hexafluoride for electrical insulation, and pharmaceuticals — roughly a fifth of modern drugs contain fluorine. Elemental fluorine itself is produced by electrolysis of a potassium fluoride/hydrogen fluoride mixture and is used in rocket propellant research and semiconductor manufacturing (often as plasma or via compounds such as nitrogen trifluoride).

Safety

Elemental fluorine is a corrosive, highly toxic oxidizer that ignites many materials on contact, including water. Hydrogen fluoride and hydrofluoric acid are uniquely hazardous because fluoride ions penetrate tissue and bind calcium and magnesium, causing deep burns and potentially fatal hypocalcemia. Exposure requires immediate specialized treatment, typically with calcium gluconate.
