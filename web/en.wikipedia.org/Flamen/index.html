Flamen

A flamen (plural flamines) was a priest of ancient Rome assigned to one of fifteen deities with official cults during the Roman Republic. The most important three were the flamines maiores ("greater flamines"), who served Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus; the twelve flamines minores served lesser-known gods including Vulcan, Flora, Pomona, and Carmenta. The flamens were among the most archaic institutions of Roman religion, traditionally said to have been created by King Numa Pompilius, and their rites preserved elements of very early Italic practice.

The flamen Dialis

The highest-ranking flamen was the flamen Dialis, priest of Jupiter. His life was hedged by an extraordinary set of taboos recorded by Aulus Gellius and other antiquarians: he wore the apex, a distinctive spiked cap, and a heavy woollen cloak (the laena); he could not ride a horse, touch iron, swear an oath, look upon an army marshalled for battle, spend more than a limited number of nights outside Rome, or allow his hair to be cut except by a free man with a bronze knife. He had to be married by the archaic rite of confarreatio to the flaminica Dialis, and if his wife died he was obliged to resign — a rule that left the office vacant for decades in the late Republic until Augustus filled it. The constraints made the flamen Dialis a living ritual object rather than a political actor: although he held a seat in the Senate, he rarely participated in public life.

Other flamines and history

The flamen Martialis served Mars and the flamen Quirinalis served Quirinus; the minor flamines included those of Ceres, Falacer, Flora, Furrina, Neptune, Palatua, Pomona, Portunes, Volcanus, Volturnus, and Carmentis. In 44 BC a flamen was appointed for the deified Julius Caesar (Mark Antony was designated, though never inaugurated), and under the Empire the institution was extended to the imperial cult: flamines served deified emperors in Rome and, in the provinces and municipalities, local flamines Augustales became a prestigious office for wealthy citizens, including, unusually, some women as flaminicae. The pagan flamines survived into the late fourth century AD before disappearing with the Christianisation of the Empire.
