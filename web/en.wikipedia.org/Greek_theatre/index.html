Greek theatre

Greek theatre — the theatre of ancient Greece — developed from religious festivals honouring the god Dionysus, and flourished in Athens from about the 6th to the 3rd century BC. It gave rise to the genres of tragedy, comedy and the satyr play, and its dramatic conventions, terminology and architecture profoundly influenced Western theatre. Athens exported the festival form throughout the Greek world, and the stone theatres built across the Mediterranean remain among the most recognisable monuments of classical antiquity.

Origins

Drama grew out of the dithyramb, a choral hymn to Dionysus. Tradition credits Thespis (c. 534 BC) with stepping out of the chorus to become the first actor, speaking dialogue with the chorus leader. At the City Dionysia in Athens, competitions in tragedy and comedy became central civic events, judged before audiences of many thousands.

The building

The classical theatre comprised three parts: the orchestra, a circular dancing floor for the chorus; the theatron ("seeing place"), a semicircular bank of tiered seats rising on a hillside; and the skene, a stage building whose façade served as scenery and dressing space, later fronted by a raised stage (logeion). Devices included the mechane, a crane that lowered gods onto the stage — the origin of the phrase deus ex machina — and the ekkyklema, a wheeled platform displaying interior scenes such as corpses. The theatre of Epidaurus, famed for its acoustics, held about 14,000 spectators.

Performance

All performers were male; actors wore masks with exaggerated features identifiable at a distance, padded costumes and, in tragedy, elevated boots (cothurni). A play used at most three speaking actors who doubled roles, supported by a chorus of 12–15 (tragedy) or 24 (comedy) who sang and danced odes between episodes. Wealthy citizens called choregoi funded productions as a public duty.

Genres and playwrights

The three great tragedians whose works survive are Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides; of the comedians, Aristophanes' Old Comedy survives complete, and Menander exemplifies New Comedy. Aristotle's Poetics analysed tragedy — its plot, character, catharsis and the unity of action — and remains foundational to dramatic theory.
