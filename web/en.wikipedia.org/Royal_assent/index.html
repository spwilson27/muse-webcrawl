Royal assent

Royal assent is the method by which a monarch formally approves an act of a legislature, either by signing it or by some other formal act, making it law. In constitutional monarchies such as the United Kingdom and the other Commonwealth realms, royal assent is the final step in the legislative process: a bill passed by parliament becomes an act only when assent is granted in the monarch's name.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, royal assent is granted by the sovereign or by Lords Commissioners appointed for the purpose. Since the Royal Assent Act 1967, it is usually signified by letters patent and announced to Parliament by the Speakers of the two Houses. By long-standing convention, assent is never refused: the last refusal of royal assent in Britain was by Queen Anne in 1708, for the Scottish Militia Bill. Although bills are formally enacted with the words "Le Roy le veult" ("The King wills it") in Norman French, in practice the monarch's role is ceremonial, acting on ministerial advice.

Commonwealth realms and elsewhere

In Commonwealth realms outside the UK, assent is given by the governor-general as the monarch's representative; in Canadian and Australian provinces and states, by lieutenant governors and governors respectively. Other monarchies have similar procedures — for example, the King of the Netherlands signs laws, and in Spain the king sanctions and promulgates laws under the 1978 constitution. In some countries the head of state's refusal or delay of assent has occasionally been politically significant, though in most parliamentary monarchies assent is automatic.
