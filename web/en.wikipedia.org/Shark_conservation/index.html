Shark conservation

A reef shark, an apex predator of coral ecosystems

Shark conservation is the effort to protect sharks and their habitats from overexploitation and decline. Sharks are exceptionally vulnerable to fishing pressure because most species grow slowly, mature late, and produce few young. Studies published since the 2000s have estimated that commercial and artisanal fisheries kill on the order of 100 million sharks per year, and a landmark 2021 assessment in Nature found that oceanic shark and ray populations had fallen by about 71% since 1970, with three-quarters of oceanic species threatened with extinction. Over a third of all chondrichthyan species (sharks, rays, and chimaeras) are now assessed as threatened on the IUCN Red List.

Threats

  • Targeted fishing for fins, meat, liver oil, gill plates (in rays), and cartilage.
  • Bycatch in tuna longline, trawl, and gillnet fisheries, often exceeding target catches in weight.
  • Shark finning — removing fins at sea and discarding the body — driven by demand for shark fin soup, widely condemned and now banned by many jurisdictions and regional fisheries bodies, usually under "fins naturally attached" rules.
  • Habitat loss, including mangrove destruction, coastal development, and climate-driven changes to nursery areas.
  • Recreational fishing and shark control programmes (nets and drumlines at beaches), which kill sharks and other marine life.

Why it matters

Many sharks are apex or meso-level predators; their removal can trigger trophic cascades — for example, declines of large sharks have been linked to increases in mid-level predators and collapses of shellfish fisheries. Sharks also support valuable dive tourism: studies have estimated that a single reef shark can be worth orders of magnitude more alive to local tourism over its lifetime than the one-time value of its fins and meat.

Measures

International

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization adopted an International Plan of Action for Sharks in 1999; the Convention on Migratory Species lists migratory shark species and adopted a memorandum of understanding on shark conservation in 2010; and CITES has progressively listed commercially important sharks (whale shark, basking shark, great white shark, oceanic whitetip, hammerheads, porbeagle, silky and thresher sharks, and the requiem shark family listings agreed in 2022) on Appendix II, requiring export permits based on sustainability findings.

National and regional

Shark sanctuaries — entire exclusive economic zones closed to shark fishing — have been declared by Palau (2009, the first), the Maldives, Honduras, the Bahamas, the Marshall Islands, French Polynesia, and others, now covering millions of square kilometres. The European Union, the United States (Shark Conservation Act of 2010 and a federal fin-trade ban enacted in 2022), and many states have banned finning and/or the sale of fins. Catch limits, minimum sizes, protected species lists, and marine protected areas form the standard management toolkit.

Public campaigns

Campaigns by organizations such as Sharkproject, WildAid, the Shark Trust, and Sea Shepherd — including celebrity-fronted campaigns in China — are credited with sharp declines in shark fin consumption and trade in the 2010s, alongside government austerity rules banning shark fin soup at official banquets. Media portrayal of sharks, long dominated by the "man-eater" image popularized by Jaws (whose author Peter Benchley later became a conservation advocate), has gradually shifted toward appreciation of sharks' ecological role.
