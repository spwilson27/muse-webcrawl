Giant isopod

Scientific genusBathynomus
FamilyCirolanidae
OrderIsopoda
HabitatDeep ocean, typically 170–2,140 m
SizeTypically 19–36 cm; largest species up to c. 50 cm

A giant isopod is any of the roughly twenty species of large isopod crustaceans of the genus Bathynomus. They are abundant in the cold, deep waters of the Atlantic, Pacific, and Indian Oceans. The best-known species, Bathynomus giganteus, is one of the largest known isopods — close relatives of the familiar terrestrial woodlice (pill bugs) — and a classic example of deep-sea gigantism.

Description

Giant isopods have a flattened, segmented exoskeleton of overlapping plates, large compound eyes with widely spaced facets adapted to dim blue light, seven pairs of walking legs (the first modified into mouthparts), and paddle-like pleopods used for swimming and respiration. Adults of the "supergiant" species B. kensleyi, B. giganteus, and B. yucatanensis can reach 36–50 cm in length.

Behaviour and diet

Giant isopods are benthic scavengers and opportunistic predators, feeding on dead whales, fish, and squid that sink to the sea floor as well as slow-moving prey such as sea cucumbers and sponges. They can endure very long periods without food — a captive individual in Japan famously survived more than five years (over 1,800 days) without eating. They are generally caught in baited traps set by deep-sea fishermen, and are occasionally eaten in parts of Asia, where the flesh is compared to crab or lobster. Females brood their eggs in a pouch (marsupium), and the young emerge as miniature adults ("mancas") lacking the last pair of legs.

History

The genus was described in 1879 by Alphonse Milne-Edwards after a specimen was caught in the Gulf of Mexico during the Blake expeditions. Giant isopods have become popular aquarium exhibits in Japan and elsewhere.
