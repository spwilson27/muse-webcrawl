Invasive species in New Zealand

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Invasive species in New Zealand are alien organisms — plants, animals, fungi and micro-organisms — introduced by humans that have established and spread at the expense of New Zealand's native ecosystems. Having evolved in isolation for tens of millions of years with no terrestrial mammals except bats, New Zealand's flora and fauna are exceptionally vulnerable to introduced predators, herbivores and weeds. Invasive species are considered the leading threat to many of the country's endemic species, a large proportion of which are endangered.

Introduced mammals

Māori settlers brought the Polynesian rat (kiore) and the kurī (dog) around the thirteenth century. European colonisation added ship rats, Norway rats, mice, cats, ferrets, stoats and weasels (introduced in the 1880s to control rabbits), brushtail possums from Australia for the fur trade, rabbits, hares, deer, goats, pigs, chamois and Himalayan tahr. Stoats and other predators are responsible for catastrophic declines in native birds such as kiwi, kākāpō and takahē; possums, numbering in the tens of millions at their peak, browse native forest canopy and prey on eggs and chicks. Predator control, island eradications and fenced mainland sanctuaries have become central tools of conservation.

Introduced plants

Hundreds of garden escapes and forestry or erosion-control plantings have naturalised. Notorious weeds include gorse, broom, old man's beard (Clematis vitalba), wilding pines (self-sown conifers invading tussock grasslands), tradescantia, and aquatic weeds such as lagarosiphon. Weeds invade forest understories, wetlands and coastal habitats and can alter fire regimes and succession.

Aquatic and other invaders

Freshwater pests include didymo ("rock snot"), introduced pest fish such as koi carp and gambusia, and invasive invertebrates. Marine biofouling species and pathogens such as kauri dieback (Phytophthora agathidicida) and myrtle rust further threaten native ecosystems.

Management

New Zealand developed some of the world's strictest biosecurity law (the Biosecurity Act 1993) and pioneered the eradication of invasive mammals from offshore islands, restoring them as refuges. In 2016 the government launched Predator Free 2050, an ambitious programme aiming to eradicate rats, stoats and possums nationwide, supported by research into trapping, toxins such as 1080, and potential genetic technologies.
