Hausdorff space

In a Hausdorff space, any two distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods.

In topology and related branches of mathematics, a Hausdorff space (also called a separated space or T2 space) is a topological space in which any two distinct points can be separated by disjoint open neighbourhoods. The property is named after Felix Hausdorff, who included it among the axioms in his foundational 1914 treatise Grundzüge der Mengenlehre.

Definition

A topological space X is Hausdorff if for every pair of distinct points x and y in X there exist open sets U and V with x ∈ U, y ∈ V, and U ∩ V = ∅. Equivalently, the diagonal is closed in the product topology, and limits of sequences (more generally, of nets and filters) are unique when they exist.

Examples and properties

All metric spaces are Hausdorff, as are all manifolds, Euclidean spaces, and most spaces arising in analysis and geometry. The Hausdorff condition is one of the standard separation axioms, sitting between T1 (points are closed) and normality in the hierarchy; it is implied by regularity together with T1 in many formulations of the T3 axiom. Non-Hausdorff spaces nonetheless arise naturally, for example the Zariski topology on the spectrum of a ring in algebraic geometry, the line with many origins, and topologies on quotient spaces.

Important consequences include: compact subsets of a Hausdorff space are closed; continuous functions into a Hausdorff space are determined by their values on a dense subset; and a compact Hausdorff space is normal.

History

Hausdorff introduced the axiom as part of the first rigorous axiomatic treatment of topological spaces. Although other separation conditions had been considered earlier by Fréchet and others, the Hausdorff property became the default assumption in most of topology; Kurt Gödel and others later studied weaker and stronger variants systematically.
