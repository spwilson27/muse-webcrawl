Alice Stokes Paul (11 January 1885 – 9 July 1977) was an American Quaker, suffragist, feminist and women's rights activist, and one of the principal leaders of the campaign for the Nineteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, ratified in 1920, which prohibited sex-based disenfranchisement. She went on to write the Equal Rights Amendment, first proposed in Congress in 1923.

Early life and British suffragettes

Paul was raised in a Quaker family in New Jersey committed to gender equality, graduated from Swarthmore College, and earned a PhD in sociology from the University of Pennsylvania. While studying in England she joined the militant Women's Social and Political Union of Emmeline Pankhurst, was arrested repeatedly, imprisoned, and force-fed during hunger strikes — experiences that shaped her later tactics.

The suffrage campaign

Returning to the United States, Paul organised the great Woman Suffrage Procession in Washington, D.C., on 3 March 1913, the day before Woodrow Wilson's inauguration. She founded the Congressional Union and then the National Woman's Party (1916), which from 1917 picketed the White House as the "Silent Sentinels". Arrested pickets were imprisoned at Occoquan Workhouse and brutally treated on the "Night of Terror" (14–15 November 1917); the resulting public sympathy strengthened the suffrage cause. The Nineteenth Amendment passed Congress in 1919 and was ratified in August 1920.

Later activism

Paul earned three law degrees and devoted the rest of her life to legal equality, drafting the original Equal Rights Amendment (the "Lucretia Mott Amendment") in 1923 and campaigning for it for fifty years; it passed Congress in 1972 but fell short of state ratification. She also helped secure a reference to women in the UN Charter and in the U.S. Civil Rights Act of 1964. In 2016 she appeared on the redesigned ten-dollar-bill proposal's reverse imagery honouring the suffrage movement, and President Obama designated the Belmont–Paul Women's Equality National Monument in Washington in her honour.
