North Magnetic Pole

The north magnetic pole is a point on the surface of the Northern Hemisphere of Earth at which the planet's magnetic field points vertically downwards (in other words, if a magnetic compass needle were allowed to rotate on a vertical axis, it would point straight down). It is the point towards which the north-seeking end of a compass needle is attracted. The north magnetic pole is distinct from the geographic North Pole, and it is drifting steadily over time because of changes in Earth's magnetic field.

Terminology

Strictly in physics terms, the magnetic pole near Earth's geographic north is actually a magnetic south pole, because opposite poles attract: the north pole of a compass magnet is drawn towards it. By convention it is nevertheless called the north magnetic pole.

History

The pole was first located on 1 June 1831 by James Clark Ross on the Boothia Peninsula in what is now Nunavut, Canada. Roald Amundsen found it in a slightly different position in 1903, demonstrating its movement. Surveys by the Canadian government traced it across the Arctic islands during the twentieth century. Having wandered slowly for decades, the pole accelerated in the 1990s, moving from about 15 km per year to around 50–60 km per year northwards across the Arctic Ocean towards Siberia. Its rapid motion contributed to an out-of-cycle update of the World Magnetic Model in 2019.

Expeditions and definition

The magnetic pole is defined by measurements of the field, and models such as the World Magnetic Model and the International Geomagnetic Reference Field give its position for any date. The related geomagnetic north pole — the pole of the best-fitting dipole approximation of Earth's field — lies near the coast of north-west Greenland.
