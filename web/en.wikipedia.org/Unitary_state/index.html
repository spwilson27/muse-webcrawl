Unitary state

A unitary state is a sovereign state governed as a single entity in which the central government is the supreme authority. The central government may create or abolish administrative divisions, and any powers exercised by such divisions are delegated by the centre and may be withdrawn. Unitary states contrast with federations, in which constituent units share sovereignty and their powers are constitutionally protected. Most of the world's states — including France, Japan, China, and the United Kingdom — are unitary.

Devolution within unitary states

Many unitary states delegate substantial autonomy to regions. In the United Kingdom, devolution has given Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland their own legislatures with differing competences, while the state remains legally unitary because Westminster retains ultimate authority. Spain's "State of Autonomies" and Italy's regions are other examples in which a unitary framework accommodates extensive regional self-government.

Centralised and decentralised forms

Unitary systems lie on a spectrum. Highly centralised states concentrate decision-making in the capital, while decentralised unitary states assign wide responsibilities to provinces and municipalities — sometimes approaching federations in practice. The distinction from federalism is constitutional: in a federation, the division of powers cannot be altered unilaterally by the central government.

History

The unitary model became dominant with the rise of the modern nation-state, notably in post-revolutionary France, where the National Assembly replaced historic provinces with uniformly administered departments. Federations more often arose by the coming together of previously separate polities, as in the United States, Switzerland, and Germany.
