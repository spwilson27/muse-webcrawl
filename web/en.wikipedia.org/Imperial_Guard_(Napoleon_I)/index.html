The Imperial Guard (French: Garde impériale) was the elite military formation of Napoleon I's Grande Armée. Created in 1804 from the Consular Guard, it served simultaneously as the emperor's personal bodyguard, a strategic reserve of veteran troops, and a model and school for the rest of the army. Its members received better pay, rations, and prestige than line troops, and entry required proven service.

Organisation

The Guard comprised infantry, cavalry, artillery, engineers, and support services. The infantry was divided into the Old Guard — the grenadiers and chasseurs à pied, veterans famous for their bearskin caps and their refusal to retreat — the Middle Guard, and the Young Guard of promising younger soldiers. Its cavalry included the horse grenadiers, chasseurs à cheval (Napoleon's usual escort), dragoons, and Polish lancers; its artillery included the celebrated horse artillery. Guard units were usually held in reserve and committed at the decisive moment of battle.

History

The Guard fought in almost every major campaign of the Napoleonic Wars. Its reputation reached its height at battles such as Austerlitz and Jena, and it provided the rearguard and the shrunken core of the army in the retreat from Russia in 1812. At Waterloo in 1815 the repulse of the Middle Guard's final attack marked the battle's turning point and passed into legend with the (probably apocryphal) exchange that "the Guard dies but does not surrender". The Guard was disbanded after Napoleon's second abdication; a new Imperial Guard was briefly recreated under Napoleon III.
