Heliopolis (Greek for "City of the Sun"; ancient Egyptian Iunu, "the Pillars") was one of the oldest and most important religious cities of ancient Egypt. Located northeast of modern Cairo, near the present-day suburbs of Ain Shams and Matariya, it was the principal cult centre of the sun god Ra and the creator god Atum.

Heliopolis was the theological home of the Ennead, the group of nine deities — Atum, Shu, Tefnut, Geb, Nut, Osiris, Isis, Seth and Nephthys — whose creation myth became one of the most influential cosmologies of the pharaonic world. Its priesthood was renowned for learning, and Greek writers such as Herodotus described the Heliopolitans as the most learned of the Egyptians.

History

The city was already a major sacred centre in the Predynastic period and grew in importance during the Old Kingdom, when the cult of Ra became central to royal ideology and pyramid complexes were aligned with the city. The high priest of Heliopolis bore the title "Chief of Seers". During the Middle and New Kingdoms the great temple of Ra received dedications from many pharaohs, including Senusret I, whose obelisk still stands at Matariya — the oldest surviving royal obelisk in Egypt.

Several famous obelisks originated at Heliopolis and were later moved abroad, among them the pair known as Cleopatra's Needles, now in London and New York, and the Lateran Obelisk in Rome. The city declined in the late period and under Persian, Greek and Roman rule, and its stones were extensively quarried for the building of medieval Cairo.

Theology

According to the Heliopolitan creation myth, the self-created god Atum emerged on the primeval mound (the benben) from the waters of Nun and produced the first divine pair, Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture). The sacred benben stone kept in the temple of Ra was linked to the Bennu bird, associated with rebirth and the sun. Heliopolitan theology was later combined with the Memphite and Theban systems and influenced the development of the Aten cult under Akhenaten.

Archaeology

Little survives above ground apart from Senusret I's obelisk and scattered temple remains, because the ancient city lies beneath modern suburbs and its water table is high. Excavations by Egyptian and German missions at Matariya and Ain Shams have uncovered temple walls, statues, workshops and cemeteries spanning three millennia, including a large necropolis of sacred Mnevis bulls, the earthly manifestation of Ra.
