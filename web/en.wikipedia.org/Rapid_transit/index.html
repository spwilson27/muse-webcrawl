A rapid transit train — the highest-capacity form of urban transport.

Rapid transit, or mass rapid transit (MRT), is a type of high-capacity public transport generally found in urban areas, characterised by electric passenger trains running on exclusive, grade-separated rights-of-way — underground, elevated or fenced at grade — with frequent service and widely spaced stations. It is commonly called a metro, subway or underground depending on the city.

History

The first rapid transit line was London's Metropolitan Railway, opened in 1863 with steam trains; electric traction and deep-level tube tunnels followed from 1890. Systems spread to Budapest (1896), Paris (1900), Berlin (1902) and New York (1904). After mid-20th-century expansion in the Soviet Union, Japan and elsewhere, metro construction accelerated in Asia: Shanghai and Beijing now operate the world's longest networks, and more than 200 cities have metros.

Characteristics

Rapid transit trains run in multiple-unit formations at headways as short as 90 seconds, carrying up to 30,000–80,000 passengers per hour per direction — far beyond road or bus capacity. Systems use dedicated signalling, level boarding and integrated fares. Because construction, especially tunnelling, is expensive, metros are typically built where density justifies the cost; automated (driverless) lines such as Copenhagen's and Singapore's are increasingly common.
