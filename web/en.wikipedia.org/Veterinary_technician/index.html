Veterinary technician

A veterinary technician is a credentialed professional who provides technical and nursing support to veterinarians in the care of animals, performing tasks comparable to those of a nurse, surgical assistant, anaesthetist, laboratory technician and radiographer combined. In the United States the titles include Registered (RVT), Licensed (LVT) and Certified (CVT) Veterinary Technician, depending on the state; in the United Kingdom the equivalent role is the Registered Veterinary Nurse (RVN).

Duties

Veterinary technicians obtain patient histories, restrain animals safely, collect blood, urine and other samples, run laboratory tests, take radiographs and other diagnostic images, induce and monitor anaesthesia, assist in surgery, place intravenous catheters, administer medications and fluids, perform dental prophylaxis, bandage wounds, and provide nursing care and client education. By law they may not diagnose conditions, prescribe medication, or perform surgery, which remain the responsibility of the veterinarian.

Education and credentialing

In the United States, technicians typically complete a two-year associate degree (or, increasingly, a four-year bachelor's degree) at a program accredited by the American Veterinary Medical Association, then pass the Veterinary Technician National Examination (VTNE) and meet state requirements. Veterinary technologist usually refers to a graduate of a four-year program. Technicians may earn specialist (VTS) certification in fields such as anaesthesia and analgesia, emergency and critical care, dentistry, internal medicine, behaviour, and clinical laboratory science, under academies recognised by NAVTA.

Work settings and outlook

Most veterinary technicians work in private small-animal practice, but others are employed in specialty and emergency hospitals, zoos, aquariums, wildlife rehabilitation, biomedical research, universities, the military, public health and industry. The occupation has grown rapidly with spending on pet care, though it is also noted for modest pay relative to its responsibilities and for high rates of burnout and compassion fatigue.
