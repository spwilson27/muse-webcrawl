Digital library

A digital library (also called an online library, an internet library, a digital repository, a library without walls, or a digital collection) is an online database of digital objects that can include text, still images, audio, video, digital documents, or other digital media formats or a library accessible through the internet. Objects can consist of digitised content like print or photographs, as well as originally produced digital content like word processor files or social media posts. In addition to storing content, digital libraries provide means for organising, searching, and retrieving the content contained in the collection.

Digital libraries can vary immensely in size and scope, and can be maintained by individuals or organisations. The digital content may be stored locally, or accessed remotely via computer networks. These information retrieval systems are able to exchange information with each other through interoperability and sustainability.

History

The early history of digital libraries is ill-documented, but several key thinkers are connected to the emergence of the concept. Precursors include Paul Otlet and Henri La Fontaine's Mundaneum, an attempt to gather and catalogue the world's printed knowledge, begun in 1895. Vannevar Bush's hypothetical Memex, described in his 1945 essay "As We May Think", anticipated hyperlinked personal knowledge stores. Large-scale projects of the 1990s included the US Digital Libraries Initiative, and mass digitisation accelerated in the 2000s with Google Books, Internet Archive, HathiTrust, Europeana and national digital libraries.

Types and terminology

The terms digital library, institutional repository and digital archive overlap but differ in emphasis: institutional repositories collect the scholarly output of a single institution; digital archives emphasise long-term preservation; digital libraries typically combine collection, organisation and user services. Digital library systems rely on descriptive metadata standards such as Dublin Core and MARC, structural standards such as METS, and interoperability protocols including OAI-PMH for harvesting metadata.

Digitisation and preservation

Digitisation converts physical materials into digital form through scanning or photography, often followed by optical character recognition (OCR) to make text searchable. Digital preservation addresses the fragility of digital media through format migration, emulation, checksums and redundant storage, guided by reference models such as OAIS. Born-digital materials pose additional challenges of scale, format diversity and legal deposit.

Issues

Copyright and licensing constrain what digital libraries can make openly available, particularly for in-copyright "orphan works". The digital divide affects access, and questions of quality control, cataloguing standards and long-term funding remain ongoing concerns for the field.
