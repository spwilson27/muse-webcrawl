The urea cycle converts ammonia into urea in the liver

The urea cycle (also known as the ornithine cycle) is a cycle of biochemical reactions that produces urea, (NH2)2CO, from ammonia (NH3). Animals that use this cycle, mainly amphibians and mammals, are called ureotelic. The cycle converts highly toxic ammonia into urea for excretion; it occurs predominantly in the liver and to a lesser extent in the kidneys.

Steps

The cycle spans two cellular compartments. In the mitochondrion, carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I condenses ammonia and bicarbonate into carbamoyl phosphate, which reacts with ornithine to form citrulline. Citrulline moves to the cytosol, where it combines with aspartate to form argininosuccinate; this is cleaved to arginine and fumarate, and arginase finally hydrolyses arginine to urea and regenerates ornithine. The overall process consumes four high-energy phosphate bonds per urea molecule. The fumarate produced links the cycle to the citric acid cycle, a connection sometimes called the "Krebs bicycle".

Discovery

The urea cycle was described in 1932 by Hans Adolf Krebs and his student Kurt Henseleit, working in Freiburg. It was the first metabolic cycle to be discovered, five years before Krebs described the citric acid cycle.

Clinical significance

Defects in any of the cycle's enzymes — most commonly ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC) deficiency — cause urea cycle disorders in which ammonia accumulates in the blood (hyperammonemia), leading to vomiting, lethargy, and potentially coma and death if untreated. Treatment involves a low-protein diet, ammonia-scavenging drugs such as sodium benzoate and sodium phenylbutyrate, and in severe cases haemodialysis or liver transplantation.
