Occupation of Araucanía

Occupation of Araucanía
Date: 1861–1883
Location: Araucanía, south-central Chile
Result: Chilean victory; incorporation of Mapuche territory into Chile

The Occupation of Araucanía (1861–1883) was a series of military campaigns, agreements, and colonisation efforts by the Chilean state that brought the autonomous Mapuche territories of Araucanía — which had resisted Spanish and Chilean rule since the Arauco War — under Chilean sovereignty. In Chilean historiography the process was long euphemistically called the "Pacification of Araucanía".

Background

South of the Biobío River, the Mapuche had maintained de facto independence through the colonial period and after Chilean independence in 1818. In the 1850s Chilean settlers and speculators began pushing into the frontier, and the proclamation in 1861 of a "Kingdom of Araucanía and Patagonia" by the French adventurer Orélie-Antoine de Tounens alarmed the Chilean government and helped precipitate intervention.

Campaigns

The occupation proceeded in phases under officers including Cornelio Saavedra, who founded a line of forts — including Angol, Negrete, and a rebuilt Villarrica — advancing the frontier from the Biobío to the Malleco and then the Traiguén and Cautín rivers. Mapuche resistance included the great uprising of 1881, during the War of the Pacific, which was defeated by 1883.

Consequences

Afterwards the Mapuche were confined to small reservations (reducciones), losing the great majority of their land, much of which passed to Chilean and European colonists. Land loss, poverty, and forced assimilation had lasting effects, and the historical memory of the occupation remains central to present-day Mapuche claims and the conflict in Araucanía.
