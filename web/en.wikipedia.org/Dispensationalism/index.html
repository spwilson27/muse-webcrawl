Dispensationalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dispensationalism is a theological framework of interpreting the Christian Bible and the nature of God's dealings with humanity. It emerged in the 1830s in the theology of John Nelson Darby and the Plymouth Brethren movement in the United Kingdom. Dispensationalists understand the Bible as divided into distinct "dispensations" — periods or ages in which God relates to humanity in different ways — commonly counted as seven: Innocence, Conscience, Human Government, Promise, Law, Grace (the present church age), and the future Millennial Kingdom.

Key tenets

Central tenets include a consistently literal (grammatical-historical) interpretation of Scripture, especially of unfulfilled prophecy; a sharp distinction between Israel and the Church, which are seen as two distinct peoples of God with separate destinies; and the expectation of a pre-tribulation Rapture of the Church before a seven-year tribulation, followed by Christ's return and a literal thousand-year reign on earth (premillennialism).

History and influence

The system was popularised in America through prophecy conferences, the Niagara Bible Conference, and especially the Scofield Reference Bible (1909), whose notes presented dispensational interpretations alongside the text. In the later 20th century, books such as Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth and the Left Behind novels brought dispensational eschatology to a mass audience, and the framework remains influential within evangelical and fundamentalist Christianity, particularly in the United States. Critics, including proponents of covenant theology, argue that its Israel–Church distinction divides the people of God and reads modern events too readily into prophecy.
