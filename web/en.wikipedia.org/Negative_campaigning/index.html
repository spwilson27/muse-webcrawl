Negative campaigning is the practice of trying to win an election by attacking an opponent's character, record, or policies rather than emphasising one's own positive attributes. It ranges from factual criticism of an opponent's voting record to misleading insinuation and personal scandal-mongering, popularly called mudslinging.

History

Negative tactics are as old as electoral politics; the U.S. presidential election of 1828 saw vicious attacks between supporters of John Quincy Adams and Andrew Jackson. Television made attack advertising a central feature of modern campaigns. The 1964 "Daisy" advertisement, aired once by Lyndon Johnson's campaign, implied that Barry Goldwater might start a nuclear war. The Willie Horton ad of 1988 and the Swift Boat Veterans campaign of 2004 are frequently cited examples of damaging negative campaigns.

Effectiveness and effects

Research on the effectiveness of negative campaigning is mixed. Meta-analyses find no consistent evidence that attack ads are more persuasive than positive ones, or that they reliably demobilise voters, though some studies link heavy negativity to reduced trust in politics and lower turnout. Campaigns nonetheless continue the practice because attacks can define an opponent early, earn free media coverage, and are memorable. Scholars distinguish legitimate contrast advertising — drawing factual comparisons — from irrelevant or deceptive attacks.

Regulation

Some jurisdictions constrain campaign negativity through defamation law and broadcast rules. In the United States, the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 required candidates to "stand by your ad", stating their approval of their own advertisements, partly in response to anonymous attack ads. Outside groups such as super PACs, which may not coordinate with candidates, produce a large share of negative advertising.
