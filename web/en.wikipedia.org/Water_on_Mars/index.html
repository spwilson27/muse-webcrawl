Water on Mars
Present formsWater ice (polar caps, subsurface), water vapour, hydrated minerals, brines
Past evidenceValley networks, outflow channels, lakebeds, deltas, conglomerates

The north polar cap of Mars contains large quantities of water ice.

Almost all water on Mars today exists as ice, though it also exists in small quantities as vapour in the atmosphere. What was once thought to be low-volume liquid brines in shallow Martian soil, also called recurrent slope lineae, may instead be grains of flowing sand and dust. The surface of Mars is primarily basalt, and abundant evidence — valley networks, outflow channels, ancient lakebeds and deltas — indicates that liquid water once flowed across the surface in large quantities early in the planet's history.

History of observations

Telescopic observers noted Mars's polar caps, and Percival Lowell popularised the idea of artificial canals around 1900. Spacecraft exploration transformed understanding: Mariner 9 (1971) revealed dried riverbeds and outflow channels; the Viking orbiters imaged extensive fluvial features. In 2002 Mars Odyssey's gamma-ray spectrometer detected abundant hydrogen, indicating widespread subsurface ice. The Phoenix lander (2008) directly confirmed water ice in the high-latitude soil, and radar sounders on Mars Express and the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter have mapped buried glaciers and layered polar deposits containing as much water ice as the Greenland ice sheet or more.

Ancient water and climate

Orbital and rover evidence — including clay minerals, hematite "blueberries" found by Opportunity, conglomerates and the dried lake sediments of Gale Crater studied by Curiosity, and the river delta in Jezero Crater explored by Perseverance — shows that early Mars (the Noachian period, over 3.7 billion years ago) had a thicker atmosphere, lakes and possibly a northern ocean. Mars lost most of its surface water as its atmosphere thinned, through escape to space and sequestration into the crust and cryosphere.

Present-day water

Today liquid water is unstable on the surface because of low pressure and temperature, though transient brines may form. Frozen water is a critical resource for proposed human missions, usable for drinking, oxygen and rocket propellant via in-situ resource utilisation.
