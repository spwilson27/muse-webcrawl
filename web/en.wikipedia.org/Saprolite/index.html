Saprolite

Saprolite is a term used in geology and soil science for rock that has been thoroughly weathered and decomposed in place (in situ) by chemical weathering processes, yet retains the original structure and fabric of the parent rock. Saprolite is typically soft, friable and easily crumbled by hand, and it forms the deeper, lower part of a weathering profile above fresh bedrock and below the overlying soil and laterite horizons.

The word derives from the Greek sapros (rotten) and lithos (rock), literally "rotten rock". Saprolites develop most extensively in warm, humid tropical and subtropical climates, where high rainfall and temperature accelerate the hydrolysis of silicate minerals over long periods of geological stability.

Formation

Saprolite forms when percolating groundwater, charged with dissolved carbon dioxide and organic acids, attacks the minerals of the underlying bedrock. Feldspars are hydrolysed to clay minerals such as kaolinite, while more resistant minerals such as quartz remain largely unchanged. Iron and aluminium oxides may accumulate, giving many saprolites their characteristic red, orange and yellow colours. Because the alteration proceeds without significant transport of material, features such as bedding, joints, veins and the outlines of original crystals (pseudomorphs) are preserved in the softened rock.

In deeply weathered tropical terrains, saprolite profiles may be tens of metres thick — in parts of West Africa, Brazil, India and Australia thicknesses of 30 to 100 metres are recorded.

Significance

Saprolite is economically important as a host for several types of mineral deposit. Nickel laterite deposits form where ultramafic rocks weather to saprolite enriched in nickel; gold is commonly recovered from saprolitic material in West Africa and Australia because it can be dug and processed without drilling and blasting; and bauxite, the principal ore of aluminium, is closely associated with intense saprolitic weathering. Saprolite horizons also matter in engineering geology, since their strength, permeability and erodibility differ greatly from those of the parent rock, and in hydrogeology as water-bearing zones in basement terrains.
