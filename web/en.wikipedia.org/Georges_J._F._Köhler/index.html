Georges Jean Franz Köhler (April 17, 1946 – March 1, 1995) was a German biologist who, together with César Milstein, developed the hybridoma technique for producing monoclonal antibodies. The two shared the 1984 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Niels Kaj Jerne.

The hybridoma method fuses antibody-producing B cells with immortal myeloma cells to produce unlimited identical antibodies.

Work

Köhler studied biology at the University of Freiburg and did his doctorate in immunology under Fritz Melchers at the Basel Institute for Immunology. As a postdoctoral fellow with César Milstein at the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, he published the landmark 1975 paper in Nature describing how fusing mouse myeloma cells with antibody-producing spleen cells from an immunised mouse creates hybrid cells ("hybridomas") that grow indefinitely in culture while secreting a single, defined antibody — monoclonal antibodies of predefined specificity.

The technique revolutionised immunology, diagnostics (pregnancy tests, infectious-disease assays) and medicine: monoclonal-antibody drugs (such as rituximab, trastuzumab and adalimumab) became a major class of therapeutics. Milstein and Köhler famously did not patent the method, leaving it freely available. Köhler returned to Germany as director of the Max Planck Institute of Immunobiology in Freiburg in 1984, the year he received the Nobel Prize. He died of pneumonia in 1995, aged 48.
