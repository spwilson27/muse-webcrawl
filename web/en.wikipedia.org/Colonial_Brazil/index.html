Colonial Brazil

Colonial Brazil (Portuguese: Brasil Colonial) comprises the period from 1500, with the arrival of the Portuguese fleet commanded by Pedro Álvares Cabral, until 1815, when Brazil was elevated to a kingdom in union with Portugal. Over three centuries the Portuguese colony grew from a string of coastal trading posts into the largest and wealthiest colony in the Americas, built successively on brazilwood, sugar, gold and coffee, and on the labour of millions of enslaved Africans and Indigenous peoples.

First decades and the captaincies

After Cabral's landfall near Porto Seguro in April 1500, the crown licensed the extraction of brazilwood, a source of red dye, traded with Indigenous peoples along the coast. Alarmed by French incursions, King John III organised the territory in 1534 into hereditary captaincies granted to private donatários. Most failed, but Pernambuco and São Vicente prospered on sugar cane. In 1549 the crown created a central government, the Governorate General, seated at Salvador in Bahia.

The sugar age

From the mid-sixteenth century, sugar plantations (engenhos) worked first by enslaved Indigenous people and then, increasingly, by enslaved Africans made northeastern Brazil the world's leading sugar producer. The Dutch West India Company seized Pernambuco and much of the northeast between 1630 and 1654 before being expelled by Portuguese and colonial forces. Bandeirantes from São Paulo pushed deep into the interior in search of captives and precious metals, effectively expanding Brazil far beyond the line set by the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494), a reality recognised by the Treaty of Madrid in 1750.

Gold, reform and decline

The discovery of gold in Minas Gerais in the 1690s triggered a rush that shifted the colony's centre of gravity southward; the capital moved from Salvador to Rio de Janeiro in 1763. Diamonds followed in the 1720s. Under the Marquis of Pombal the crown tightened control, expelled the Jesuits in 1759 and encouraged mixed companies and agriculture. Gold output declined after 1760, and discontent produced conspiracies such as the Inconfidência Mineira of 1789, whose leader Tiradentes was executed in 1792.

Transfer of the court and elevation

Fleeing Napoleon's invasion of Portugal, the Prince Regent John and the royal court arrived in Rio de Janeiro in 1808, opening Brazilian ports to friendly nations and transforming the colony into the seat of the Portuguese Empire. In 1815 Brazil was raised to a kingdom, forming the United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves — formally ending its colonial status and preparing the way for independence in 1822.
