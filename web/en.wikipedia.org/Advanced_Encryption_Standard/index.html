The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), also known by its original name Rijndael, is a specification for the encryption of electronic data established by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in 2001. It is a subset of the Rijndael block cipher developed by Belgian cryptographers Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen, who submitted the design to NIST's open AES selection process in 1997.

Selection and standardisation

After a five-year international competition among fifteen candidate algorithms, NIST announced Rijndael as the winner on 2 October 2000, citing its security, performance in hardware and software, and low memory requirements. AES became effective as US federal standard FIPS 197 on 26 May 2002 and superseded the aging Data Encryption Standard (DES). In 2003 the NSA approved AES-128 for SECRET information and AES-192/256 for TOP SECRET.

Description

AES operates on a fixed 128-bit block arranged as a 4×4 state of bytes, with 10, 12 or 14 rounds for 128-, 192- and 256-bit keys respectively. Each round applies SubBytes (a nonlinear S-box derived from the multiplicative inverse in GF(2⁸)), ShiftRows, MixColumns (omitted in the final round), and AddRoundKey, using round keys expanded from the cipher key. The design is a substitution–permutation network rather than a Feistel cipher, and it lends itself to fast implementations, including the AES-NI instruction set in modern processors.

Security

Despite extensive cryptanalysis, no practical attack on full AES is known. The best public attacks on AES-128 (biclique, 2011) are only marginally faster than brute force; related-key attacks affect AES-192 and AES-256 in theoretical models. Real-world compromises generally exploit side channels or incorrect modes of operation rather than the cipher itself. AES is used worldwide in TLS, Wi-Fi (WPA2/WPA3), disk encryption, messaging applications, and government systems.
