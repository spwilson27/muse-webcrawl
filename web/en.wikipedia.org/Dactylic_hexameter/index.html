Dactylic hexameter

Dactylic hexameter (also known as heroic hexameter and the meter of epic) is a form of meter or rhythmic scheme frequently used in Ancient Greek and Latin poetry. The scheme consists of six feet per line, where a foot is generally either a dactyl (one long syllable followed by two short syllables) or a spondee (two long syllables).

Structure

In the typical scheme, the first four feet may be dactyls or spondees, the fifth foot is almost invariably a dactyl, and the sixth foot is a spondee or trochee. Lines therefore vary between twelve and seventeen syllables. A main caesura — a word break — usually falls in the third foot. The meter depends on syllable quantity (length) rather than stress accent, unlike most modern English verse.

Use in epic

Dactylic hexameter was the standard meter of Greek and Latin epic poetry. Homer's Iliad and Odyssey were composed in it, as were Hesiod's works; in Latin, Virgil's Aeneid, Ovid's Metamorphoses, and Lucretius's De rerum natura adopted the form. Later poets in other languages, including Longfellow in Evangeline, attempted adaptations of the meter to stress-based languages.
