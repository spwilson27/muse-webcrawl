Heliocentrism (also known as the heliocentric model) is the astronomical model in which the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun at the centre of the universe. Historically, heliocentrism was opposed to geocentrism, which placed the Earth at the centre. The notion that the Earth revolves around the Sun had been proposed as early as the third century BC by Aristarchus of Samos, who influenced later Copernican thought, but had received no support in the ancient world.

The Copernican model

Nicolaus Copernicus presented a fully predictive mathematical model of a heliocentric system in his book De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres), published just before his death in 1543. Although Copernicus retained circular orbits and epicycles, his model explained retrograde motion as a perspective effect of Earth's own motion, ordered the planets unambiguously by orbital period, and allowed their relative distances to be computed. The publication of the Copernican model is often taken as the starting point of the Scientific Revolution.

Acceptance

Johannes Kepler replaced circles with elliptical orbits in his laws of planetary motion (1609–1619), greatly improving accuracy. Galileo Galilei's telescopic discoveries of 1610 — the moons of Jupiter and the full set of phases of Venus — undermined the Ptolemaic system, though not Tycho Brahe's geo-heliocentric compromise. Galileo's advocacy of Copernicanism led to his trial and house arrest in 1633. Isaac Newton's Principia (1687) supplied the physical mechanism — universal gravitation — and stellar aberration (1729) and stellar parallax (1838) provided direct observational evidence of Earth's motion. In modern terms, the Sun is not the centre of the universe; planets orbit the Solar System's barycentre, and the choice of reference frame is a matter of convenience, though the heliocentric frame is far simpler dynamically.
