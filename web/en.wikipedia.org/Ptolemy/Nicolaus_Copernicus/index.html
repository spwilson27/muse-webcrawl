Nicolaus Copernicus (19 February 1473 – 24 May 1543) was a Renaissance polymath who formulated a model of the universe that placed the Sun rather than Earth at its centre. He first outlined his heliocentric theory in the Commentariolus (c. 1514) and published it in full in De revolutionibus orbium coelestium in 1543, the year of his death. The publication is often regarded as the starting point of the Scientific Revolution.

Life

Copernicus was born in Toruń (Thorn), the youngest of four children of a wealthy copper merchant. He studied at the University of Kraków, then canon law, medicine and astronomy in Italy at Bologna, Padua and Ferrara. Returning to Warmia in Royal Prussia, he served as a cathedral canon at Frombork, administering chapter estates, practising medicine, and advising on coinage reform; his economic writings anticipated the quantity theory of money and Gresham's law.

Heliocentric theory

In the Copernican model, the Sun rests near the centre of the universe, the planets (including Earth) orbit it in circles, the Moon orbits Earth, and Earth's daily rotation explains the apparent motion of the heavens. The model explained retrograde motion of the planets as a perspective effect. Copernicus retained circular orbits and epicycles, so his system was not initially much more accurate than Ptolemy's, but its conceptual simplicity transformed astronomy. His work built on earlier ideas, including those of Aristarchus of Samos.

Legacy

The "Copernican Revolution" displaced Earth from the centre of the universe and, developed by Kepler, Galileo and Newton, became modern astronomy. The element copernicium (Cn, 112) is named in his honour, as are craters on the Moon and Mars and the EU's Copernicus Programme of Earth observation.
