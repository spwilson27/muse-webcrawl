Bornc. AD 100, Egypt, Roman Empire

Claudius Ptolemy (Greek: Πτολεμαῖος, Ptolemaîos; Latin: Claudius Ptolemaeus; c. 100 – c. 170 AD) was an Alexandrian mathematician, astronomer, astrologer, geographer, and music theorist who wrote about a dozen scientific treatises, three of which were of continuing importance to later Byzantine, Islamic, and Western European science. He lived in Alexandria, Egypt, then part of the Roman Empire. Almost nothing is known of his life beyond his writings and astronomical observations dated between AD 127 and 141.

Works

The Almagest (originally the Mathematical Treatise) presents his geocentric model of the universe: the Earth stationary at the center, with the planets moving on deferents and epicycles, supplemented by the equant — a geometric device that preserved predictive accuracy while straining uniform circular motion. The Ptolemaic system dominated astronomy for over 1,400 years until displaced by Copernicus, Kepler, and Newton. His Geography compiled coordinates for some 8,000 localities and introduced systematic latitude and longitude mapping, though it underestimated the Earth's circumference and the length of Eurasia — an error with real consequences for Columbus. The Tetrabiblos codified astrology, and works on optics, harmonics, and the Handy Tables rounded out a corpus that, transmitted through Arabic scholarship, shaped medieval science.
