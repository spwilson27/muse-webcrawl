Babylonia (/ˌbæbɪˈloʊniə/; Akkadian: māt Akkadī) was an ancient Akkadian-speaking state and cultural area based in central-southern Mesopotamia (present-day Iraq). A small Amorite-ruled state emerged in 1894 BC, which contained at the time the minor administrative town of Babylon. It was merely one among a number of small city-states in Mesopotamia until Hammurabi (r. 1792–1750 BC) made it the short-lived capital of an empire. Babylonia again became a major power under the Neo-Babylonian Empire of Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar II (626–539 BC), before falling to Cyrus the Great of Persia.

History

The Old Babylonian period produced Hammurabi's law code, one of the earliest surviving codes of law, and notable advances in literature, including versions of the Epic of Gilgamesh. After Hittite and Kassite rule, Babylon endured centuries of Assyrian domination. The Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) Empire defeated Assyria, destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BC and rebuilt Babylon on a magnificent scale, with its ziggurat Etemenanki and Ishtar Gate. Cyrus conquered Babylon in 539 BC; it subsequently passed to the Macedonian, Seleucid and Parthian empires, gradually declining as cuneiform culture faded by the 1st century AD.

Culture and science

Babylonia inherited and developed Sumerian traditions in literature, law, religion and scholarship. Babylonian mathematics used a sexagesimal (base-60) place-value system — the origin of the 60-minute hour and 360-degree circle. Babylonian astronomers compiled systematic observational records (the astronomical diaries), recognised periodic phenomena such as the Saros cycle for eclipses, and produced some of the earliest mathematical astronomy; their observations and methods were later used by Greek astronomers including Hipparchus and Ptolemy, whose Almagest drew on Babylonian eclipse records stretching back to the reign of Nabonassar (747 BC).
