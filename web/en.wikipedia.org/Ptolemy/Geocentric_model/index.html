In astronomy, the geocentric model (also known as geocentrism, often exemplified specifically by the Ptolemaic system) is a superseded description of the Universe with Earth at the centre. Under most geocentric models, the Sun, Moon, stars and planets all orbit Earth. The geocentric model was the predominant description of the cosmos in many ancient Greek civilisations, as well as the Islamic world and medieval Europe.

Ancient Greek astronomy

Plato and his student Eudoxus of Cnidus proposed systems of concentric spheres centred on Earth. Aristotle's cosmology (4th century BC) put a spherical Earth at the centre of a finite universe of nested celestial spheres composed of a fifth element, aether, moved by an unmoved mover. To account for the observed motions of the planets — including retrograde motion — later astronomers such as Apollonius of Perga and Hipparchus introduced epicycles (small circles whose centres move on larger circles, the deferents) and eccentric circles.

The Ptolemaic system

In the 2nd century AD, Claudius Ptolemy of Alexandria refined the model in his treatise the Almagest. Ptolemy added the equant, a point about which a planet's epicentre moves at uniform angular speed, and produced tables that predicted planetary positions with unprecedented accuracy. The Ptolemaic system, in its various refinements, remained the standard astronomical framework for over 1,400 years and was adopted, with religious endorsement, by Islamic and Christian scholarship.

Displacement

The model was challenged by the heliocentric system of Nicolaus Copernicus (1543), refined by Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion and Galileo Galilei's telescopic observations of the phases of Venus — which are inexplicable in the pure Ptolemaic system — and finally explained by Isaac Newton's theory of universal gravitation. By the 18th century the geocentric model had been abandoned by working astronomers, though it survives in everyday language ("sunrise") and in the use of the celestial sphere for observation.
