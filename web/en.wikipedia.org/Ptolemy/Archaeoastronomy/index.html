Archaeoastronomy
Stonehenge, whose axis aligns with the solstices, is the classic archaeoastronomical site.
DisciplineInterdisciplinary: archaeology, astronomy, history
FocusAncient peoples' understanding of the sky and its role in their cultures

Archaeoastronomy (also called astroarchaeology) is the interdisciplinary study of how people in the past "understood the phenomena in the sky; how they used these phenomena; and what role the sky played in their cultures". It combines astronomy, archaeology, anthropology and ethnohistory, examining alignments of monuments, calendars, artefacts and texts for evidence of ancient astronomical knowledge.

History of the field

Interest in astronomical alignments at Stonehenge dates to William Stukeley in the 1740s. In the 1960s Gerald Hawkins' claim that Stonehenge functioned as an observatory, and Alexander Thom's surveys of British megalithic sites, popularised the field, though their more speculative conclusions drew criticism. Modern archaeoastronomy applies rigorous statistical tests to proposed alignments and draws on ethnographic evidence, distinguishing it from "fringe" claims. Scholars such as Clive Ruggles and Anthony Aveni are leading figures.

Evidence and methods

Researchers study the orientation of buildings and monuments (for example Egyptian temples aligned to solstices, Maya "E-group" complexes, and Andean ritual lines), portable artefacts such as the Nebra sky disk, rock art, and written records including Babylonian astronomical diaries and Maya codices. Methods include surveying azimuths, correcting for changes in obliquity, horizon altitude and atmospheric refraction, and testing alignment patterns against chance.

Examples

  • Stonehenge (England) — axis aligned on the midsummer sunrise and midwinter sunset.
  • Newgrange (Ireland) — a roof-box admits winter-solstice sunlight into the chamber.
  • Egyptian pyramids — oriented close to the cardinal directions.
  • Chankillo (Peru) — thirteen towers marking the annual path of the sunrise.
  • Maya sites — buildings aligned to Venus extremes and solstices; the Dresden Codex contains a Venus table.
