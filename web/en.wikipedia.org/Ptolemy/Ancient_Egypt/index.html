Ancient Egypt was a civilisation of ancient North Africa, concentrated along the lower reaches of the Nile River, situated in the place that is now the country of Egypt. It is one of the oldest and longest-lived civilisations in history, conventionally dated from the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt around 3100 BC until the Roman annexation in 30 BC following the death of Cleopatra VII. Its history is divided into periods of stable kingdoms — the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms — separated by intermediate periods of fragmentation.

History

Farming communities developed along the predictable annual flooding of the Nile, whose rich black silt supported abundant harvests. Around 3100 BC the king Narmer is traditionally credited with unifying the Two Lands. The Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC) built the great pyramids of Giza. The Middle Kingdom (c. 2055–1650 BC) saw administrative reform and literary flourishing. After the Hyksos interlude, the New Kingdom (c. 1550–1070 BC) made Egypt an empire, with pharaohs such as Hatshepsut, Thutmose III, Akhenaten and Ramesses II. Later periods brought Libyan, Nubian, Assyrian, Persian and Macedonian rule, culminating in the Ptolemaic Kingdom founded by Ptolemy I Soter, a general of Alexander the Great.

Government and society

At the apex stood the pharaoh, regarded as a divine king and intermediary between gods and humans, supported by a vizier, priests, scribes and a bureaucracy that assessed taxes in grain. Society included artisans, farmers, soldiers and enslaved people. Egyptian law and administration rested on the concept of ma'at — truth, order and cosmic balance.

Religion and culture

Egyptians worshipped many gods, including Ra, Osiris, Isis and Amun, and invested heavily in the afterlife: mummification, tombs filled with goods, and funerary texts such as the Book of the Dead. Egypt gave the world monumental stone architecture, a 365-day calendar, hieroglyphic writing, and advances in medicine, mathematics and astronomy. The decipherment of hieroglyphs using the Rosetta Stone by Jean-François Champollion in the 1820s opened the civilisation's records to modern scholarship.
