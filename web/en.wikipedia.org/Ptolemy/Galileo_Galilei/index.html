Galileo Galilei (1564–1642).

Galileo di Vincenzo Bonaiuti de' Galilei (1564–1642), commonly referred to as Galileo Galilei, was an Italian astronomer, physicist, and engineer from Pisa, sometimes described as a polymath. He has been called the "father of observational astronomy," the "father of modern physics," and the "father of the scientific method."

Telescopic discoveries

In 1609 Galileo built a telescope of about 20× magnification and turned it to the heavens, publishing his findings in the Sidereus Nuncius (1610): mountains on the Moon, countless new stars, and four moons orbiting Jupiter — the first clear evidence of bodies not circling the Earth. He also observed the phases of Venus, which strongly supported the heliocentric system of Copernicus over that of Ptolemy.

Physics

Galileo's studies of falling bodies, inclined planes, and pendulums undermined Aristotelian physics and laid groundwork for Newton's laws of motion. In his Two New Sciences (1638) he treated the strength of materials and kinematics.

Trial and legacy

His defence of heliocentrism in the Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632) led to trial by the Roman Inquisition in 1633; found "vehemently suspect of heresy," he abjured and spent his remaining years under house arrest. The affair became an enduring symbol of the conflict between science and religious authority.
