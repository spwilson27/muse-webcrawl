Geocentrism

Geocentrism is the astronomical model in which the Earth is at the centre of the universe and all other celestial bodies orbit around it. The fully developed Ptolemaic system, set out in Ptolemy's Almagest (2nd century AD), was the standard cosmology of the ancient, medieval Islamic, and medieval European worlds until the heliocentric model of Copernicus gained acceptance in the 16th–17th centuries.

The Ptolemaic model

In Ptolemy's system, each planet moves on a small circle (an epicycle) whose centre moves along a larger circle (the deferent) around a point near the Earth. Additional devices — the eccentric and the equant — allowed the model to match observed planetary positions, including retrograde motion, with impressive accuracy for naked-eye astronomy.

Historical role

Aristotle's physics gave geocentrism a natural philosophy: heavy bodies fall toward the centre of the universe, identified with Earth's centre. The model fitted common sense, the apparent daily rotation of the heavens, and the absence of observable stellar parallax, and it served calendrical, astrological, and navigational needs for over a millennium.

Displacement

Copernicus's De revolutionibus (1543) placed the Sun near the centre; Kepler's elliptical laws, Galileo's telescopic observations (phases of Venus, moons of Jupiter), and Newton's gravitation gradually established heliocentrism. Stellar parallax was finally measured by Bessel in 1838.
