History of astronomy

The history of astronomy spans from prehistoric sky-watching to the modern science of the cosmos. Astronomy is one of the oldest natural sciences: early civilisations in Babylonia, Egypt, Greece, India, China, and Mesoamerica developed systematic observations of celestial motions for calendrical, religious, and navigational purposes.

Ancient astronomy

Babylonian astronomers recorded planetary motions and developed arithmetic schemes to predict them. Greek astronomy added geometrical models: Eudoxus's homocentric spheres, Aristarchus's early heliocentric proposal, Hipparchus's discovery of precession and his star catalogue, and finally Ptolemy's comprehensive Almagest, which dominated astronomy for some 1,400 years.

Islamic and medieval astronomy

Astronomers in the Islamic world preserved and critiqued Ptolemy, building great observatories and producing accurate tables (the Maragha school, Ulugh Beg's Samarkand observatory). In China, guest stars (novae and supernovae) were meticulously recorded; Indian astronomers such as Aryabhata developed sophisticated planetary mathematics.

The Scientific Revolution

Copernicus revived heliocentrism (1543); Tycho Brahe's precise observations enabled Kepler's laws of planetary motion; Galileo's telescope (1609) revealed Jupiter's moons and the phases of Venus; and Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) unified celestial and terrestrial mechanics.

Modern astronomy

The 19th and 20th centuries added stellar parallax measurements, spectroscopy, photography, and the discovery of galaxies beyond the Milky Way (Hubble), cosmic expansion, and the cosmic microwave background. Space telescopes and multi-messenger astronomy — gravitational waves and neutrinos — define the field today.
