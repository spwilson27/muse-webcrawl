Aristotle (Greek: Ἀριστοτέλης Aristotélēs; 384–322 BC) was an Ancient Greek philosopher and polymath. His writings cover a broad range of subjects spanning the natural sciences, philosophy, linguistics, economics, politics, psychology and the arts. As the founder of the Peripatetic school of philosophy in the Lyceum in Athens, he began the wider Aristotelian tradition that followed, which ultimately set the groundwork for the development of modern science.

Life

Born in Stagira in northern Greece, Aristotle was educated for nearly twenty years at Plato's Academy in Athens. After Plato's death he travelled to Asia Minor and Lesbos, where he conducted biological research, and was then invited by Philip II of Macedon to tutor his son, the future Alexander the Great. In 335 BC he returned to Athens and founded his own school, the Lyceum. After Alexander's death in 323 BC, anti-Macedonian sentiment led Aristotle to retire to Chalcis, where he died the following year.

Works and thought

Aristotle's surviving works — treatises probably used as lecture notes — include the Organon on logic (including the syllogism and the theory of categories), the Physics and Metaphysics (form and matter, potentiality and actuality, the four causes, and the unmoved mover), the Nicomachean Ethics and Politics, the Poetics and Rhetoric, and extensive zoological works such as the History of Animals. His cosmology placed a spherical Earth at the centre of a finite universe of concentric celestial spheres — the geocentric framework later mathematised by Ptolemy and dominant until the Scientific Revolution.

Influence

Aristotle's works were preserved and developed by Hellenistic, Roman, Islamic and Jewish scholars — al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes and Maimonides — and in the Latin West, where Thomas Aquinas integrated Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology. His logical and scientific system was the foundation of medieval scholasticism; even where early modern science overturned his conclusions, his methods of classification, definition and systematic inquiry shaped intellectual history.
