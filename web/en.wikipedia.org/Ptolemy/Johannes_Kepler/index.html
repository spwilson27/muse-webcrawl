Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) was a German astronomer, mathematician, astrologer and natural philosopher. He is a key figure in the 17th-century Scientific Revolution, best known for his laws of planetary motion, as published in Astronomia nova (1609), Harmonice Mundi (1619) and the Epitome Astronomiae Copernicanae (1621). Kepler provided one of the foundations for Isaac Newton's theory of universal gravitation.

Life

Kepler was born in Weil der Stadt in Swabia and studied at the University of Tübingen under Michael Maestlin, who taught him the Copernican system. He taught mathematics at Graz, where he published the Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596), a defence of heliocentrism. In 1600 he joined Tycho Brahe in Prague; on Brahe's death in 1601 Kepler succeeded him as imperial mathematician to Rudolf II.

Laws of planetary motion

Using Brahe's precise observations of Mars, Kepler concluded that orbits are elliptical. His first two laws appeared in Astronomia nova (1609): (1) planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus; (2) a line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times. The third law — the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis — appeared in Harmonice Mundi (1619). The Rudolphine Tables (1627), based on his laws, greatly improved the accuracy of astronomical predictions.

Other work

Kepler explained the working of the eye and the telescope in works on optics (1604, 1611), observed the supernova of 1604, wrote one of the earliest works of science fiction (Somnium), and contributed to mathematics with work on infinitesimals and the packing of spheres (the Kepler conjecture). His mother Katharina was tried for witchcraft in 1620–21; Kepler's defence secured her release.
