Gift tax

Gift tax returns are filed in the United States on IRS Form 709.

A gift tax is a tax on the transfer of property by one individual to another while receiving nothing, or less than full value, in return. The tax applies whether or not the donor intends the transfer to be a gift. Gift taxes are commonly levied alongside estate or inheritance taxes to prevent people from avoiding those taxes by giving away their wealth during life.

United States

In the United States, the federal gift tax applies to the donor, not the recipient. Gifts below an annual per-recipient exclusion (US$18,000 per recipient in 2024, indexed for inflation) are not taxable, and gifts to spouses, charities, and payments of tuition or medical expenses made directly to providers are generally exempt. Taxable gifts above the annual exclusion consume part of the donor's unified lifetime estate and gift tax exemption (over US$13 million per person in 2024); the top statutory rate is 40 percent. The tax was first enacted in 1924, repealed, and re-enacted in 1932 as a backstop to the estate tax.

Other countries

Many countries tax gifts under their inheritance tax regimes rather than as a separate tax. In the United Kingdom, for example, gifts are usually exempt if the donor survives seven years ("potentially exempt transfers"). Germany and France apply gift taxes (Schenkungsteuer, droits de donation) with allowances that depend on the relationship between donor and recipient.
