Chinese divination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chinese divination has a long history, from the oracle bone divination of the Shang dynasty to the later fortune-telling systems still practiced in China and the Chinese diaspora. It encompasses a wide range of techniques aimed at answering questions about the future, interpreting the will of spirits and ancestors, and discerning patterns of cosmic order.

Oracle bone divination

The earliest known form, scapulimancy, was practiced by Shang dynasty kings around 1200 BCE. Questions were carved onto ox scapulae and turtle plastrons, which were then heated until cracks formed; the royal diviner interpreted the cracks to answer questions about warfare, harvests, and sacrifices. These oracle bones preserve the earliest known corpus of Chinese writing.

The Yijing (Book of Changes)

The Yijing (I Ching), one of the oldest of the Chinese classics, derives from Zhou dynasty milfoil (yarrow stalk) divination. The querent generates a hexagram — six lines, each broken (yin) or solid (yang) — through a random procedure, and consults the text's commentaries on that hexagram. The system rests on the interplay of yin and yang and the eight trigrams (bagua), and deeply influenced Confucian and Daoist cosmology.

Later systems

  • Bazi ("eight characters") or Four Pillars of Destiny: astrology based on the year, month, day, and hour of birth expressed in the sexagenary cycle.
  • Ziwei doushu: "purple star astrology", a complex horoscopic system popular in Taiwan and Hong Kong.
  • Liu Yao ("six lines"): a hexagram method using coins, developed in the Han dynasty.
  • Feng shui: divination applied to the siting of buildings and graves, using the compass (luopan).
  • Physiognomy (mianxiang): reading the face, palms (palmistry), and body.
  • Kau Cim: shaking numbered sticks from a tube at temples to receive a poetic oracle, common in Hong Kong and overseas communities.

Modern status

After suppression during the Cultural Revolution, many divination practices revived with reform and opening-up; fortune-telling is now a visible part of everyday life in China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and diaspora communities, though mainland authorities officially discourage "superstition".
