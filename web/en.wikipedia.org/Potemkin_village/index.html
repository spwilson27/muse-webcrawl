Potemkin village

A Potemkin village is a construction (literally or figuratively) whose purpose is to provide an external façade to a situation, to make people believe that a country or organization is better off than it actually is. In politics and economics, the term refers to elaborate, costly, and temporary constructions that give a misleading impression of prosperity or efficiency.

The term comes from stories that Grigory Potemkin, a field marshal and lover of Catherine the Great, erected fake portable settlements along the banks of the Dnieper River in 1787 in order to fool the Russian empress during her journey to Crimea.

History

Potemkin was governor-general of Russia's new southern provinces, which Russia had annexed from the Ottoman Empire. In 1787, as preparations for the Russo-Turkish War were underway, Catherine made an unprecedented six-month tour of the region accompanied by representatives from several foreign courts. Legend has it that Potemkin showed her theatrical sets: villages consisting of painted facades, bonfires visible from afar (said to be the roofs of well-to-do villagers' huts), herds of fat cattle repeatedly driven along her route, and a fake city with stools placed in the streets to simulate inhabitants.

Historians generally regard the legend as exaggerated. The German prince de Ligne, who accompanied Catherine, reported that the empress was presented with towns "built for the occasion, but which decoration made them look more flourishing than they were." Critics of Potemkin at court, particularly those envious of his favour, spread and embellished the story. Potemkin did order the construction of impressive buildings along Catherine's route and undertook genuine settlement and development of the region, including the founding of Kherson, Nikolayev, Sevastopol, and Yekaterinoslav.

Modern usage

The term has entered many languages to describe any hollow or deceptive display designed to conceal an undesirable fact or situation. It is used in politics, economics, and criticism of architecture and urban planning. Similar concepts include the idea of a "show village" built to impress foreign visitors, and in contemporary discourse the term is applied to white elephant projects, staged inspections, and corporate practices meant to give an impression of compliance or prosperity.
