Inquest


A coroner's inquest hearing evidence into a death.

An inquest is a judicial inquiry to ascertain the facts relating to an incident, most commonly the cause and circumstances of a death. In common-law jurisdictions the inquest into a death is conducted by a coroner (or, in parts of the United States, a medical examiner system operates instead), sometimes sitting with a jury. The inquest determines who the deceased was and how, when and where they died; it does not assign criminal or civil liability, which is the province of other courts.

Scope and procedure

Inquests are generally required where a death was violent or unnatural, occurred in custody or state detention, or has an unknown cause. The coroner hears evidence from witnesses, pathologists and investigators; hearings are public, and interested persons such as family members may question witnesses. Conclusions (formerly called verdicts) include unlawful killing, accident or misadventure, natural causes, suicide and open conclusions. Coroners may also issue reports to prevent future deaths.

Distinguishing features

An inquest is a fact-finding, inquisitorial proceeding rather than an adversarial trial: there are no parties in the usual sense and no one is on trial. In the United States the term survives mainly in the coroner's inquest, while in other contexts "inquest" historically described any sworn inquiry by a jury, such as the inquests of landholding recorded in Domesday Book.
