Hugh MacDiarmid is the pen name of Christopher Murray Grieve (11 August 1892 – 9 September 1978), a Scottish poet, journalist, essayist, and political figure. He was a leading force of the Scottish Renaissance, a movement to restore a distinctly Scottish modern literature, and is regarded as one of the most important Scottish poets of the twentieth century.

Life and politics

Born in Langholm, Grieve served in the Royal Army Medical Corps during the First World War. Afterwards he worked as a journalist in Montrose, where he founded literary journals and began writing poetry in a revived literary Scots ("Lallans"), synthesized from dialects and older literature. He was a founder of the National Party of Scotland (a predecessor of the SNP) and simultaneously a member of the Communist Party of Great Britain — expelled at different times from both for his combination of nationalism and communism.

Poetry

His early Scots lyrics, such as "The Watergaw," were followed by his masterpiece, A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle (1926), a long, comic-philosophical poem in Scots. Later he turned to dense, fact-laden poetry in English, including In Memoriam James Joyce (1955), aiming at a "poetry of knowledge." His slogan for the Scottish Renaissance — "Not Burns — Dunbar!" — expressed his call to return to the greater medieval makars and to Europe-wide modernism.

Legacy

MacDiarmid's output and polemics reshaped Scottish culture and letters. A memorial at Langholm and his cottage at Brownsbank near Biggar commemorate him, and his work continues to be central to debates about Scottish identity, language, and independence.
