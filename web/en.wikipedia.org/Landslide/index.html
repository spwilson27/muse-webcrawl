Landslide

A landslide is the movement of a mass of rock, debris, or earth down a slope under the influence of gravity. Landslides are a major natural hazard worldwide, occurring in forms ranging from rockfalls and topples to slides, spreads, and flows such as debris flows and mudflows.

A landslide in progress, showing the head scarp and debris runout.

TypesFalls, topples, slides, spreads, flows
Common triggersIntense rainfall, earthquakes, erosion, human activity

Causes

Slopes fail when the shear stress acting on potential failure surfaces exceeds the shear strength of the material. Contributing factors include steep slopes, weak or fractured rock, loss of vegetation, and undercutting by rivers, waves, or road construction. Water is the most common trigger: infiltration raises pore-water pressure and reduces effective stress. Earthquakes are the other great trigger — the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake in China set off tens of thousands of landslides — and volcanic eruptions can generate enormous debris avalanches, as on Mount St. Helens in 1980.

Notable events

Among the deadliest landslides in history are the 1970 Huascarán debris avalanche in Peru, triggered by an earthquake, which buried Yungay and killed more than 20,000 people; the 1963 Vajont Dam disaster in Italy, where a landslide into a reservoir displaced a wave that overtopped the dam and killed about 2,000; and the 2014 Oso slide in Washington state, the deadliest in U.S. history with 43 deaths.

Mitigation

Hazard mapping, slope monitoring with instruments and satellite radar, drainage works, retaining structures, reforestation, and land-use zoning are the main defences. Early warning systems combine rainfall thresholds with ground sensors to alert communities in high-risk areas.
