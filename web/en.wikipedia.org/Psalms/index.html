Psalms

The Book of Psalms (from Greek Psalmoi, "instrumental music" and, by extension, "the words accompanying the music"), also known as the Psalter, is the first book of the Ketuvim ("Writings"), the third section of the Hebrew Bible, and a book of the Christian Old Testament. It is a collection of 150 religious songs, prayers, and poems, many traditionally attributed to King David.

Composition

The psalms were composed over many centuries, from the early monarchy of Israel to the post-exilic period (roughly the 9th to the 5th centuries BCE). The collection is divided into five books, mirroring the five books of the Torah, each ending with a doxology. Superscriptions attribute 73 psalms to David, others to Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, and Ethan.

Genres

Form criticism, pioneered by Hermann Gunkel, classifies the psalms into hymns of praise, communal and individual laments, royal psalms, thanksgiving psalms, and wisdom psalms. Famous examples include Psalm 23 ("The LORD is my shepherd"), Psalm 51 (the Miserere, a penitential psalm), Psalm 137 ("By the rivers of Babylon"), and Psalm 150, a closing hymn of praise.

Use in worship and music

The psalms have been central to Jewish and Christian liturgy for millennia, recited in daily prayer, sung as chant, and set to music by countless composers. Metrical versions in the vernacular — such as the Genevan and Scottish psalters — shaped Protestant worship, and psalm settings range from Gregorian chant through Bach and Handel to modern worship music.
