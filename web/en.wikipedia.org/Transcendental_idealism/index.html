Transcendental idealism

[Image: Portrait of Immanuel Kant]
images/kant.png

Transcendental idealism is the philosophical doctrine developed by Immanuel Kant in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781/1787). It holds that human knowledge is limited to appearances (phenomena) — objects as structured by the mind's own forms of intuition and categories — while things as they are in themselves (noumena) cannot be known directly. Space and time, on this view, are not properties of the world in itself but a priori forms through which all experience is given.

The Copernican revolution in philosophy

Kant compared his proposal to Copernicus's revolution in astronomy: instead of assuming that our knowledge must conform to objects, we should assume that objects of experience must conform to the conditions of our knowledge. The mind contributes universal structures — space and time as forms of sensibility, and the twelve categories of the understanding, such as causality and substance — that make experience of an objective world possible. Because these structures are ours, knowledge extends only to appearances; the attempt of traditional metaphysics to reason about the soul, the world-whole, and God as things in themselves generates antinomies.

Relation to other positions

Kant combined transcendental idealism with empirical realism: within experience, objects are genuinely real and independent of any particular observer. He distinguished his view from Berkeley's subjective idealism, insisting that he did not deny the existence of outer objects — a misunderstanding he tried to answer with the "Refutation of Idealism" in the second edition of the Critique. Critics from Jacobi and Hegel onward charged that the unknowable thing-in-itself was incoherent, while two-world and two-aspect interpretations of Kant continue to be debated by scholars.

Influence

Transcendental idealism shaped German idealism (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel), Schopenhauer's philosophy, and later phenomenology; its method — seeking the conditions of possibility of experience — influenced fields from epistemology to cognitive science.
