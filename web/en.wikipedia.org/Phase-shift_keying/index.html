Phase-shift keying

Constellation diagram for QPSK, with four phase states.

Phase-shift keying (PSK) is a digital modulation process which conveys data by changing (modulating) the phase of a constant-frequency carrier wave. The modulation is accomplished by varying the sine and cosine inputs at precise times, and is widely used for wireless LANs, RFID, Bluetooth, satellite links, and broadcast standards such as DVB.

BPSK is the simplest and most robust form, using two phases separated by 180 degrees; it tolerates high levels of noise but carries only one bit per symbol. QPSK doubles spectral efficiency by using four phases, and is equivalent to two orthogonal BPSK channels (in-phase and quadrature). Higher-order PSK packs more bits into each symbol but requires a higher signal-to-noise ratio because the phase states are closer together and more easily confused.

Demodulation

Coherent demodulation requires a reference signal for phase comparison, recovered by circuits such as a Costas loop or decision-directed synchronization. Because absolute phase references can be ambiguous, many systems instead use differential PSK (DPSK), in which information is encoded in phase changes between successive symbols, simplifying receiver design at a small cost in performance. The combination of amplitude and phase modulation is called quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM).

Applications

PSK variants appear in Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11), which uses BPSK and QPSK at its lower data rates, in the DVB-S satellite standard (QPSK), in RFID and contactless payment systems, and in deep-space and telemetry links, where BPSK's robustness is valuable.
