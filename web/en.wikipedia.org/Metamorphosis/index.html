Metamorphosis

Metamorphosis is a biological process by which an animal physically develops after birth or hatching, involving a conspicuous and relatively abrupt change in the animal's body structure through cell growth and differentiation. Some insects, fish, amphibians, molluscs, crustaceans, cnidarians, echinoderms and tunicates undergo metamorphosis, which is often accompanied by a change of habitat and behaviour.

Insects

In complete metamorphosis (holometabolism), an insect passes through egg, larva, pupa and adult stages that differ greatly in form — for example a caterpillar becoming a butterfly, or a grub becoming a beetle. About most described insect species develop this way. In incomplete metamorphosis (hemimetabolism), as in grasshoppers and dragonflies, the young (nymphs) broadly resemble adults and change gradually. Inside the pupa, larval tissues are largely broken down (histolysis) and adult structures built from imaginal discs.

Amphibians

Frog and toad tadpoles transform from aquatic, gill-breathing herbivores with tails into air-breathing, tailless carnivorous adults: limbs grow, the tail is resorbed, lungs develop and the gut shortens. The change is driven by thyroid hormones, especially thyroxine.

Control and evolution

In insects, metamorphosis is controlled by ecdysteroid hormones triggering moults and by juvenile hormone, whose presence keeps the animal in a juvenile form; its fall permits the pupal and adult stages. Metamorphosis allows juveniles and adults to exploit different food sources and habitats, reducing competition between them.
