Bornhardt

A bornhardt is a dome-shaped, steep-sided, bald rock landform — a type of inselberg — typically composed of granite or gneiss. Bornhardts rise abruptly from surrounding plains, often reaching hundreds of metres in height, and have smoothly rounded, bare slopes largely free of soil and vegetation. The term honours the German geologist Wilhelm Bornhardt, who described such features in East Africa in the early 20th century.

Formation

Bornhardts are classic products of deep weathering and stripping (etching): subsurface chemical weathering attacks fractured rock along joints, and when the weathered mantle is removed, the more resistant, sparsely jointed core stands exposed as a dome. Sheet joints, curved fractures parallel to the surface, develop through pressure release (unloading) as overlying rock erodes, causing slabs to peel away in a process called exfoliation.

Examples

Famous bornhardts include Sugarloaf Mountain in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil; the bald domes of Yosemite's granitic landscape (such as Half Dome, though glacially modified); Uluru's related inselberg forms in Australia; and numerous domes across Africa, India, and Sri Lanka's dry zone.
