Catharism was a Christian dualist or Gnostic movement that thrived in parts of Southern Europe, particularly what is now southern France and northern Italy, between the twelfth and fourteenth centuries. Its adherents, called Cathars (from the Greek katharoi, "the pure"), held that the material world was the creation of an evil power, while the good God was the author of the spiritual realm alone. The Roman Catholic Church condemned Catharism as heresy and eventually destroyed it through the Albigensian Crusade and the early Inquisition.

Beliefs and organisation

Cathars distinguished between a small elite of the Perfecti — "Good Men" and "Good Women" who had received the consolamentum, a spiritual baptism by laying on of hands — and ordinary believers (credentes), who supported them and usually received the consolamentum only on their deathbeds. The Perfect lived ascetically: they abstained from meat, eggs, and dairy, practised celibacy, refused oaths and killing, and rejected Catholic sacraments, churches, and the doctrine of the Incarnation, teaching instead that Christ was an angelic being whose body was illusory. Souls were believed to undergo reincarnation until liberated by the consolamentum.

Persecution

The movement flourished in Languedoc under the protection of local nobles, to the alarm of the Church and the French crown. After the murder of the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau in 1208, Pope Innocent III proclaimed the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), which devastated the region — beginning with the massacre at Béziers in 1209 — and brought Languedoc under Capetian control. The last major stronghold, Montségur, surrendered in 1244, and more than two hundred Perfect were burned. The Inquisition pursued the remaining Cathars through the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries; the last known Occitan Cathar Perfect, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321. Inquisitorial records, notably the register of Bishop Jacques Fournier, preserve an unusually vivid picture of Cathar village life.
