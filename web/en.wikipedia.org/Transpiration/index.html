Transpiration

Transpiration is the process by which water is absorbed by plant roots, transported through the plant in the xylem, and released as water vapour from aerial parts, mainly through microscopic leaf pores called stomata. It is the plant equivalent of evaporation and perspiration combined, and it drives the ascent of sap: evaporation from leaves creates tension (negative pressure) that pulls a continuous column of water up from the roots — the cohesion–tension mechanism.

Process and regulation

Over 90% of the water a plant absorbs is lost by transpiration; only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis and growth. Guard cells surrounding each stoma open and close the pore in response to light, carbon dioxide levels, water status and the hormone abscisic acid, balancing CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis against water loss. Some water is also lost directly through the cuticle and through lenticels in bark. Rates rise with temperature, wind, light and low humidity, and fall when the air is saturated.

Significance

Transpiration cools leaves, maintains cell turgor, and carries dissolved minerals from roots to shoots. At ecosystem scale it is a major component of the water cycle: forests, especially tropical rainforests, return vast quantities of moisture to the atmosphere, influencing regional rainfall. A single large tree can transpire several hundred litres of water on a hot day, and crops may require irrigation precisely because transpiration demand exceeds soil supply. In animals, an analogous process — water loss through skin and lungs — is usually called insensible perspiration.

Measurement

Transpiration is measured with potometers, lysimeters and sap-flow sensors, and at landscape scale by eddy-covariance towers. Combined evaporation from soil and transpiration from plants is termed evapotranspiration, a key variable in agriculture, hydrology and climate models.
