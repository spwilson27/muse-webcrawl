Computer cooling

Computer cooling is the process of removing waste heat generated by computer components to keep them within permissible operating temperature limits. Components prone to overheating — principally the central processing unit (CPU) and graphics processing unit (GPU), but also power supply circuitry, memory, and storage drives — may malfunction, throttle their performance, or suffer permanent damage if cooling fails.

Air cooling

The most common method is air cooling: heat is transferred from the chip to a finned metal heat sink, often via heat pipes or a vapour chamber, and fans move air across the fins and out of the case. Thermal interface materials such as thermal paste fill microscopic gaps between the chip and the sink. Case airflow design, fan placement, and dust management strongly affect performance, and fan noise is a persistent trade-off, especially in quiet computing.

Liquid and other methods

Liquid cooling circulates coolant through water blocks mounted on hot components to a radiator, where heat is dissipated to air. It is offered both in sealed all-in-one kits for desktop PCs and in large-scale form in data centres, where direct-to-chip liquid cooling and rear-door heat exchangers increasingly supplement air conditioning. Immersion cooling submerges electronics in a dielectric fluid. More exotic methods include thermoelectric (Peltier) coolers and phase-change or sub-ambient refrigeration used by overclocking enthusiasts, as well as the passively cooled designs of many laptops, tablets, and embedded devices, which rely on conduction to the chassis.

Thermal management

Modern processors monitor their own temperature and protect themselves by reducing clock speed (thermal throttling) or shutting down. Software and firmware adjust fan speeds and power limits dynamically to balance performance, temperature, and noise.
