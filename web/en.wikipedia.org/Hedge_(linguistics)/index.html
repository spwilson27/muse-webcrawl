Hedge (linguistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, a hedge is a word, phrase, or grammatical device used to express partial commitment to, or soften, the truth or force of a statement. Hedges signal that a speaker is not fully certain of a proposition, wish to avoid appearing categorical, or want to leave room for negotiation and disagreement. Common English hedges include perhaps, maybe, probably, sort of, kind of, roughly, I think, and as far as I know.

Origin of the term

The term was introduced by the linguist George Lakoff in his 1973 paper "Hedges: A Study in Meaning Criteria and the Logic of Fuzzy Concepts", which examined words such as sort of and technically that make category membership a matter of degree — for example, in deciding whether "a robin is a bird par excellence, but a penguin is sort of a bird". Lakoff's work was an early influence on the development of fuzzy logic in semantics.

Functions

Later pragmatic research distinguishes several functions of hedging:

  • Accuracy hedges qualify how precisely a statement holds: "The meeting starts at about three."
  • Speaker-commitment hedges mark the statement as the speaker's belief rather than established fact: "I think the report is finished."
  • Politeness hedges soften requests and criticisms, mitigating face-threatening acts: "Could you possibly send it today?"

Hedging is especially frequent in academic writing, where authors conventionally qualify claims ("The results suggest that..."), and in legal and political language, where non-committal formulations may be strategic.

Hedges and boosters

Hedges are often contrasted with boosters — devices such as clearly, certainly, and definitely that strengthen a speaker's commitment. Together hedges and boosters are studied as forms of metadiscourse and stance marking.
