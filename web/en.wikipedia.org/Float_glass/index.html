Float glass

Float glass is a sheet of glass made by floating molten glass on a bed of molten metal, typically tin, a method known as the Pilkington process. Because the glass floats on the perfectly smooth surface of the molten tin, it acquires uniform thickness and very flat surfaces without the grinding and polishing that earlier plate glass required. The process was developed by Alastair Pilkington and Kenneth Bickerstaff at the British glass manufacturer Pilkington, was announced in 1959, and revolutionised the manufacture of flat glass.

Process

Glass raw materials are melted at about 1,500 °C and poured continuously onto a bath of molten tin at about 1,000 °C in a controlled nitrogen–hydrogen atmosphere, which prevents oxidation of the tin. The glass spreads out and floats, forming a ribbon of equilibrium thickness of about 6.8 mm; thickness is adjusted by controlling the speed at which the ribbon is drawn. The ribbon is cooled slowly in an annealing lehr and then cut into sheets.

Properties and uses

The side of the sheet in contact with the tin (the "tin side") absorbs a small amount of tin, which can be detected under ultraviolet light and affects coating and silvering processes. Float glass is the standard feedstock for windows, mirrors, toughened and laminated safety glass, and the display glass industry descends from the same technology.
