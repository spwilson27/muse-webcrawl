The presentation of the Augsburg Confession at the Diet of Augsburg, 1530.

The Augsburg Confession, also known as the Augustana from its Latin name, Confessio Augustana, is the primary confession of faith of the Lutheran Church and one of the most important documents of the Protestant Reformation. It was written in both German and Latin and was presented by a number of German rulers and free cities at the Diet of Augsburg on 25 June 1530.

The purpose of the confession was to present the faith of the evangelical princes and cities to Emperor Charles V, in the hope of religious reconciliation within the Holy Roman Empire. Its principal author was Philipp Melanchthon, working with the approval of Martin Luther, who remained at Coburg because he was under imperial ban.

Structure and content

The confession consists of twenty-eight articles. The first twenty-one summarise the doctrines taught in the Lutheran churches, including the Trinity, original sin, the person of Christ, justification by faith (Article IV, often regarded as the heart of the document), the ministry, the church, baptism, and the Lord's Supper. The remaining seven articles address abuses that had been corrected, such as communion in both kinds, the marriage of priests, private masses, monastic vows, and compulsory confession.

Aftermath

The Catholic response, the Confutatio Augustana, prompted Melanchthon to write the Apology of the Augsburg Confession (1531). The confession became the foundational doctrinal standard of Lutheranism and was later included in the Book of Concord of 1580. A revised version, the Variata of 1540, whose changes on the Lord's Supper made it acceptable to some Reformed theologians including John Calvin, remained a source of controversy among Lutherans.
