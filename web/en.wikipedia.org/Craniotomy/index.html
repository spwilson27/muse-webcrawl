Craniotomy

Type: Surgical procedure
Specialty: Neurosurgery
Distinguished from: Craniectomy (bone flap not replaced)

A craniotomy is a surgical operation in which a flap of bone is temporarily removed from the skull to access the brain, then replaced at the end of the procedure. It is the standard approach for many neurosurgical operations, including removal of brain tumours, clipping of aneurysms, evacuation of hematomas, and treatment of epilepsy and traumatic brain injury.

Procedure

After anaesthesia and positioning, the surgeon makes a scalp incision, drills burr holes, and uses a craniotome to cut a bone flap, which is set aside in sterile conditions. The dura mater is opened to expose the brain, the underlying operation is performed, and the dura, bone flap (fixed with small plates and screws), and scalp are closed in layers.

Types

Named approaches include the pterional (frontotemporal) craniotomy, widely used for aneurysms of the anterior circulation; suboccipital approaches to the posterior fossa; and bifrontal craniotomies. An "awake craniotomy" keeps the patient conscious during part of the operation so that speech and motor areas can be mapped and preserved. When the bone flap is deliberately left out to allow the swollen brain room to expand, the procedure is a craniectomy instead.

Risks

As with any brain surgery, risks include infection, bleeding, seizures, stroke, cerebrospinal fluid leak, and injury to neurological function; modern imaging, neuronavigation, and microsurgical technique have substantially reduced complication rates.
