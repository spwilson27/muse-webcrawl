Voice over IP

Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP), also called IP telephony, is a method and group of technologies for delivering voice communications and multimedia sessions over Internet Protocol networks, such as the Internet. Instead of the circuit-switched lines of the traditional public switched telephone network, VoIP converts speech into digital packets that are routed across packet-switched networks.

Technology

A VoIP call begins when an analogue voice signal is sampled, compressed by an audio codec such as G.711, G.729 or Opus, and divided into packets. Signalling protocols such as the Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) or H.323 set up and tear down calls, while the Real-time Transport Protocol (RTP) carries the media stream. Because packets may arrive out of order or late, jitter buffers and packet-loss concealment are used to maintain quality.

Advantages and challenges

VoIP generally costs less than traditional telephony, integrates voice with data services, and allows features such as video calling, voicemail-to-email and location-independent numbers. Its challenges include dependence on network quality — latency, jitter and packet loss degrade calls — unreliable emergency-call location, and vulnerability to power failures, since IP phones need local power unlike line-powered analogue phones.

History

Experimental packet voice networks date to the 1970s ARPANET, but commercial VoIP took off in the mid-1990s with software such as VocalTec's Internet Phone. The 2000s saw mass-market services including Skype (2003) and bundled VoIP offerings from broadband providers; by the 2010s VoIP and mobile voice-over-LTE had displaced much fixed-line telephony.
