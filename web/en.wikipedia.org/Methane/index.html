Methane (CH4) is a chemical compound with one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms — the simplest alkane and the main constituent of natural gas. It is a colourless, odourless, highly flammable gas produced naturally by the decay of organic matter in the absence of oxygen, and it is a potent greenhouse gas.

Properties and reactions

Methane is tetrahedral in shape and non-polar, sparingly soluble in water. Its principal reaction is combustion: CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O, releasing about 890 kJ/mol. It undergoes free-radical halogenation, steam reforming to hydrogen and synthesis gas, and partial oxidation to methanol.

Sources and uses

Natural sources include wetlands, termites, oceans and thawing permafrost; human sources include fossil-fuel extraction and leaks, rice paddies, landfills and livestock — ruminants produce methane by enteric fermentation. Biogas from anaerobic digestion is largely methane. Methane is used as a fuel for heating and electricity, as a feedstock for hydrogen, methanol and fertiliser production, and liquefied as LNG for transport.

Climate significance

Methane is the second most important human-caused greenhouse gas after carbon dioxide. It remains in the atmosphere only about a decade but traps heat much more strongly: over 20 years its global warming potential is about 80 times that of CO2 (about 28–30 times over 100 years), so cutting methane emissions is a fast-acting climate measure. Atmospheric concentrations have more than doubled since pre-industrial times.

Beyond Earth

Methane is abundant on the gas giants and on Saturn's moon Titan, where it forms lakes and rain; traces detected on Mars have prompted debate about geological or biological sources.
