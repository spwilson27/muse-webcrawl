Louis VIII (5 September 1187 – 8 November 1226), nicknamed the Lion (le Lion), was King of France from 1223 to 1226. As the son and successor of Philip II Augustus, he continued his father's expansion of the royal domain. Before ascending the throne, as Prince Louis, he briefly occupied London and controlled more than half of England during the First Barons' War, having been invited to take the English throne by rebellious barons.

Prince Louis and the invasion of England

Louis married Blanche of Castile, granddaughter of Henry II of England, in 1200. In 1216, during the First Barons' War against King John, English barons offered Louis the crown and he landed in Kent with an army. He was proclaimed (though not crowned) king in London and captured Winchester and much of southern England. After John's death in October 1216, support shifted to John's young son Henry III. Louis's forces were defeated at Lincoln in May 1217 and at sea off Sandwich; by the Treaty of Lambeth (September 1217) he renounced his claim and withdrew.

Reign

Louis succeeded his father in 1223 — the first Capetian king to inherit the throne as the eldest son in unbroken succession. He continued warfare against the Plantagenets, conquering Poitou and Saintonge in 1224 and reducing English-held territory in France largely to Gascony. In 1226 he launched a royal crusade against the Albigensian (Cathar) heretics of Languedoc, capturing Avignon after a three-month siege and bringing much of the south under royal control.

Death and legacy

On the return march from the south, Louis contracted dysentery and died at Montpensier in Auvergne on 8 November 1226. He was succeeded by his young son Louis IX (the future Saint Louis), under the regency of Blanche of Castile. His will established appanages for his younger sons — Artois, Poitou, Anjou and Auvergne — a policy that shaped the French monarchy for generations.
