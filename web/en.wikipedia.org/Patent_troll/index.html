Patent troll

Patent troll is a pejorative term for a person or company that enforces patents against accused infringers in an attempt to collect licensing fees, without manufacturing products or supplying services based on the patents in question. Such entities are more neutrally called non-practicing entities (NPEs) or patent assertion entities (PAEs). The term was popularised in the late 1990s by Intel counsel Peter Detkin, after the company was sued for using the earlier phrase "patent extortionist".

Business model

Patent assertion entities acquire patents — often from inventors, universities, or bankrupt firms — and approach companies whose products may infringe, demanding licence fees. Because patent litigation is expensive (defence through trial in the United States can cost millions of dollars), many targets settle for amounts below the cost of defence, a dynamic critics liken to a litigation tax. Supporters respond that NPEs provide liquidity to inventors, enforce property rights that individual inventors cannot afford to defend, and that practising companies also assert patents aggressively.

Impact and reform

Studies by the US Government Accountability Office and academic researchers found that suits by patent assertion entities grew to constitute a majority of US patent infringement filings in the early 2010s, concentrated in software. Responses included the America Invents Act of 2011, which created inter partes review at the Patent Trial and Appeal Board; the Supreme Court's Alice v. CLS Bank decision (2014), which tightened eligibility of abstract software patents; venue reform in TC Heartland v. Kraft Foods (2017), which curtailed filing concentration in the Eastern District of Texas; and state laws against bad-faith demand letters. The role of litigation funding and large portfolio aggregators, such as Intellectual Ventures (founded 2000), remains debated.
