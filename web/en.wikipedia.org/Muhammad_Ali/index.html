Muhammad Ali (born Cassius Marcellus Clay Jr.; 1942–2016) was an American professional boxer and activist, widely regarded as the greatest heavyweight boxer of all time and one of the most significant sporting and cultural figures of the 20th century. Nicknamed "The Greatest", he became heavyweight champion three times, in 1964, 1974 and 1978.

Career

Clay won Olympic gold at Rome in 1960 and, aged 22, stunned Sonny Liston in 1964 to take the world title, announcing his conversion to Islam and his new name shortly after. In 1967 he refused induction into the U.S. Army on grounds of conscience and opposition to the Vietnam War; he was stripped of his titles and exiled from boxing for over three years before the Supreme Court overturned his conviction in 1971. His legendary fights include the "Fight of the Century" (1971 loss to Joe Frazier), the "Rumble in the Jungle" in Zaire (1974, defeating George Foreman with his "rope-a-dope" tactics) and the "Thrilla in Manila" (1975, defeating Frazier). He retired in 1981.

Later life and legacy

Ali became a global ambassador for peace and humanitarian causes; he was diagnosed with Parkinson's syndrome in 1984. He lit the Olympic flame at Atlanta in 1996 and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2005. His combination of athletic brilliance, verbal wit, religious conviction and political courage made him a transcendent figure far beyond sport.
