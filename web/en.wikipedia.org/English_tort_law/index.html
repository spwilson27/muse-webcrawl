English tort law

English tort law concerns civil wrongs that cause someone else to suffer loss or harm, resulting in legal liability for the person who commits the tortious act. Its primary aims are to provide relief to injured parties, impose liability on those responsible for the harm, and deter others from committing harmful acts. The main categories include negligence, nuisance, trespass, defamation, and a set of economic and statutory torts, developed largely through the common law of England and Wales.

Negligence

Negligence is the dominant modern tort. A claimant must show that the defendant owed a duty of care, breached that duty by falling below the standard of the reasonable person, and thereby caused recoverable damage. The foundational case is Donoghue v Stevenson [1932], where Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle" established a general duty not to injure those closely affected by one's acts. Courts have since developed staged tests (notably from Caparo v Dickman) for novel duty situations, and rules on psychiatric injury, omissions, public authorities, and pure economic loss. Defences include contributory negligence, which reduces damages, and volenti non fit injuria.

Trespass and nuisance

Trespass protects direct interference with the person (battery, assault, false imprisonment), land, and goods, and is actionable without proof of damage. Nuisance covers unreasonable interference with the use or enjoyment of land — by noise, smells, pollution, and similar indirect harms — balancing the interests of neighbours; private nuisance is a tort against land, while public nuisance also has a criminal dimension.

Defamation and privacy

Defamation (libel in permanent form, slander when transient) protects reputation against false statements causing serious harm, reformed by the Defamation Act 2013. Alongside it, the tort of misuse of private information has grown from breach of confidence under the influence of the Human Rights Act 1998.

Remedies

The standard remedy is compensatory damages, measured to restore the claimant to their pre-tort position, including special and general damages for personal injury. Courts may also grant injunctions, and in limited cases exemplary (punitive) damages. Vicarious liability attaches to employers for employees' torts committed in the course of employment.
