Revelation principle

The revelation principle is a fundamental result in mechanism design in economics and game theory. It states that if a social choice function can be implemented by any mechanism, then the same function can be implemented by an incentive-compatible direct mechanism — one in which participants are simply asked to report their private information (their "types") and it is in each participant's interest to report truthfully. The principle vastly simplifies the analysis of mechanism design by reducing the search for implementable outcomes to direct, truthful mechanisms.

Statement

Suppose a mechanism — any game form together with an equilibrium concept — implements a social choice function f. Then there exists a direct mechanism in which agents report types to a central authority, the authority computes f from the reported types, and truthful reporting is an equilibrium. The proof constructs the direct mechanism by simulating the original mechanism on the agents' behalf: whatever strategies agents would have played in equilibrium are played for them by the mechanism itself, so deviating in one's report cannot improve on the equilibrium outcome.

Variants

Different versions of the principle correspond to different equilibrium concepts — dominant strategies, Bayesian–Nash equilibrium, and ex-post equilibrium among them. Key contributions are associated with Allan Gibbard, Michael Spence, Roger Myerson, and others in the 1970s; Myerson's formulation for Bayesian settings underlies modern auction theory.

Applications and limitations

The principle is used throughout auction design, optimal taxation, and regulation theory. Its limitations include the assumption that the designer can commit to the mechanism, that reports are costless and unrestricted in complexity, and that agents do not collude — assumptions that can fail in practice, motivating research on robust and simple mechanisms.
