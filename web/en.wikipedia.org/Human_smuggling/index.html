Human smuggling

Human smuggling
Also known as: People smuggling, migrant smuggling
Legal framework: UN Protocol against the Smuggling of Migrants (2000)
Distinguished from: Human trafficking

Human smuggling, also called people smuggling or migrant smuggling, is the procurement, in order to obtain a financial or other material benefit, of the illegal entry of a person into a state of which the person is not a national or permanent resident. That definition comes from the United Nations Protocol against the Smuggling of Migrants by Land, Sea and Air, supplementing the UN Convention against Transnational Organized Crime.

Distinction from trafficking

Smuggling normally involves consent: the migrant pays a smuggler to facilitate unlawful border crossing, and the relationship usually ends on arrival. Human trafficking, by contrast, involves coercion, deception or exploitation and can occur without any border crossing. In practice the categories can blur when migrants incur debts or are abused en route.

Methods and routes

Smugglers use land, sea and air routes, forged or fraudulently obtained documents, concealed compartments in vehicles, and dangerous crossings of deserts and seas. Fees vary from hundreds to tens of thousands of dollars depending on distance and risk. Well-known routes have included the Mediterranean Sea, the Mexico–United States border, and the English Channel.

Risks

Migrants face suffocation in sealed containers, drowning, dehydration and violence. Notorious incidents include the 2000 Dover lorry deaths of 58 Chinese migrants, the 2015 Parndorf van deaths in Austria, and the 2019 Essex lorry deaths of 39 Vietnamese people in England.

Responses

States respond with border enforcement, intelligence-led policing, and international cooperation, while critics argue that restrictive migration policies increase demand for smugglers and the dangers involved.
