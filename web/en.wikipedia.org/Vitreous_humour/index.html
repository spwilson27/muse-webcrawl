Vitreous humour

The vitreous body (also called the vitreous humour) is the clear gel that fills the space between the lens and the retina of the eyeball, known as the vitreous cavity. In humans it makes up about four-fifths of the volume of the eye — roughly 4 millilitres — and is composed of about 99% water together with a network of collagen fibres, hyaluronic acid, salts, sugars and a small number of cells called hyalocytes.

LocationVitreous cavity, between lens and retina
Volume~4 ml (about 80% of the eye)
Composition~99% water, collagen, hyaluronic acid
FunctionShape, support, optical transparency

Structure and function

The vitreous is formed during embryonic development and, unlike many tissues, is not significantly replenished during life. Its gel structure holds the retina in place against the back of the eye, maintains the eye's shape and transmits light to the retina with minimal scattering. It is firmly attached at a few sites, notably the vitreous base near the ora serrata and around the optic disc and lens.

Ageing and disorders

With age the gel gradually liquefies (syneresis) and its collagen fibres aggregate into visible strands — the common "floaters" that drift across vision. The shrinking gel commonly pulls away from the retina in a posterior vitreous detachment, usually harmless but occasionally tearing the retina, which can lead to retinal detachment. Blood can leak into the vitreous (vitreous haemorrhage) from diabetic retinopathy or trauma, clouding vision. In a surgical procedure called vitrectomy, the vitreous is removed and replaced with saline, gas or silicone oil to treat such conditions.
