Achilles tendon rupture is a break of the Achilles tendon, the large tendon at the back of the ankle connecting the calf muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus) to the calcaneus (heel bone). It typically occurs during recreational sport involving sudden pushing off, jumping, or abrupt dorsiflexion of the foot, and is most common in men between 30 and 50 years of age.

Signs and symptoms

People often describe a sensation of being kicked or struck in the back of the ankle, sometimes with an audible snap or pop. Walking becomes difficult; the person cannot rise onto the toes of the affected side. A gap may be palpable in the tendon, usually 2–6 cm above its insertion, the zone of relatively poor blood supply where most ruptures occur.

Diagnosis

The Thompson test (Simmonds–Thompson calf squeeze test) is positive when squeezing the calf fails to produce plantarflexion of the foot. Ultrasound can confirm the rupture and measure tendon-end apposition; MRI is used in equivocal cases. Roughly a quarter of ruptures are initially missed, often being mistaken for an ankle sprain.

Treatment

Treatment options include open or percutaneous surgical repair and non-operative management with functional bracing (an equinus boot with heel wedges, allowing early weight-bearing). Modern trials show similar re-rupture rates between surgery and functional rehabilitation, though surgery may offer faster return of strength; it carries risks of wound infection and sural nerve injury. Rehabilitation proceeds over several months, with return to sport typically after 6–12 months.

Epidemiology

Incidence is estimated at roughly 7–40 per 100,000 people per year and has risen with increased participation in recreational sport among middle-aged adults. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics and corticosteroid exposure are recognised risk factors for tendon weakening.
