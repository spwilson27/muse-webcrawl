Great Sioux Reservation

The Great Sioux Reservation was the original area of land guaranteed to the Lakota and Dakota Sioux peoples by the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868. The treaty set aside for the Sioux "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of a vast territory encompassing all of present-day South Dakota west of the Missouri River, including the sacred Black Hills, with additional hunting grounds in neighbouring territories.

Reduction and breaking up

The reservation was steadily reduced. After gold was confirmed in the Black Hills by Custer's 1874 expedition, the United States seized the Hills in 1877, contrary to the treaty. In 1889 Congress divided the remaining Great Sioux Reservation into six separate reservations — Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, Cheyenne River, Lower Brulé and Crow Creek — and opened some nine million acres of "surplus" land to non-Indian settlement.

Legal claims

The Sioux refused monetary compensation for the Black Hills, maintaining that the land was never legally ceded. In United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians (1980), the Supreme Court upheld an award of $105 million (the 1877 value plus interest), ruling the taking had been unconstitutional. The Sioux nations declined the money, which has since grown in a trust account to well over a billion dollars, insisting instead on the return of the land. The dispute remains unresolved and the Black Hills remain a potent symbol of broken treaties.
