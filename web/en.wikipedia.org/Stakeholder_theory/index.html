Stakeholder theory

The stakeholder model of the firm.

Stakeholder theory is a theory of organizational management and business ethics that accounts for multiple constituencies impacted by business entities, including not only shareholders but also employees, customers, suppliers, financiers, governmental bodies, political groups, trade associations and communities. It holds that a firm's managers should create value for all stakeholders, not solely maximize shareholder returns.

Development

The theory was brought to prominence by R. Edward Freeman in his 1984 book Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach. Freeman drew on earlier work, including a 1963 Stanford Research Institute memorandum that coined the term "stakeholder" and the systems thinking of Russell Ackoff. The theory contrasts with the shareholder primacy view associated with Milton Friedman, who argued that the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits.

Varieties

Scholars distinguish descriptive, instrumental and normative strands of stakeholder theory. The normative strand, considered the core by many, holds that stakeholders are persons or groups with legitimate interests in an enterprise and that their interests have intrinsic value. Concepts such as stakeholder salience—based on power, legitimacy and urgency—help managers prioritize competing claims.

Influence and criticism

Stakeholder ideas underpin corporate social responsibility, ESG investing and the 2019 Business Roundtable statement redefining the purpose of a corporation. Critics argue that balancing multiple objectives can leave managers unaccountable, since a theory that justifies any decision justifies none.
