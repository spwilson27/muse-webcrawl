Ecocentrism

Ecocentrism
Field: Environmental philosophy and ethics
Core claim: Ecosystems and the biosphere have intrinsic value
Contrasts with: Anthropocentrism, biocentrism

Ecocentrism is a term used in ecological political philosophy to denote a nature-centred system of values. Ecocentric thought holds that ecosystems, species and the biosphere as a whole possess intrinsic value independent of their usefulness to humans, and that environmental ethics should take the integrity of ecological wholes — not only individual organisms or humans — as its focus.

Origins

Ecocentrism draws on Aldo Leopold's "land ethic" in A Sand County Almanac (1949), which argued that a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic community. It is closely related to the deep ecology movement founded by Arne Næss, and to James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis.

Comparison

Unlike biocentrism, which centres value on individual living beings, ecocentrism locates value in ecological collectives — populations, communities, ecosystems and the Earth system. Both contrast with anthropocentrism, which treats nature as valuable chiefly as a resource for humans.
