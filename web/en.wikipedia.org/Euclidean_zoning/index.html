Euclidean zoning

Euclidean zoning is a system of land-use regulation in which a municipality divides its territory into districts ("zones") and prescribes, for each district, the uses of land that are permitted — such as single-family residential, commercial or industrial — together with standards for lot size, building height and setbacks. It is the dominant form of zoning in the United States.

Origin of the name

The term derives not from the Greek mathematician but from the village of Euclid, Ohio, whose 1922 zoning ordinance was challenged by the Ambler Realty Company. In Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (1926), the United States Supreme Court upheld comprehensive zoning as a valid exercise of the police power, establishing the constitutional basis for the practice nationwide.

Characteristics and criticism

Euclidean zoning is typically "cumulative" and use-based: each district lists permitted uses, and districts are often arranged in a hierarchy so that a "higher" use (such as housing) may be allowed in a "lower" district (such as industry) but not the reverse. Critics argue that strict separation of uses encourages sprawl, car dependence and exclusionary housing patterns, and reform movements have promoted alternatives such as form-based codes, mixed-use zoning and performance zoning.
