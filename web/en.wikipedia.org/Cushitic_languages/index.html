Cushitic languages

The Cushitic languages are a branch of the Afroasiatic language family spoken primarily in the Horn of Africa, with minorities in eastern Africa and the Nile Valley. They take their name from Cush, a figure in the Hebrew Bible. More than forty Cushitic languages are spoken by an estimated 60 million people, principally in Ethiopia, Somalia, Djibouti, Eritrea, Kenya, and Sudan.

Major languages

The most widely spoken Cushitic languages are Oromo, spoken by more than 35 million people in Ethiopia and Kenya, and Somali, the official language of Somalia and spoken by roughly 20 million people. Other significant languages include Afar, Sidamo, Hadiyya, Kambaata, and the Central Cushitic (Agaw) languages. The ancient Beja language (Bedawi), spoken along the Red Sea coast of Sudan and Egypt, is sometimes classed as a separate North Cushitic branch.

Classification

Cushitic is usually divided into Central, East, and South Cushitic branches, with Beja as a possible fourth. East Cushitic includes the Highland and Lowland groups and the Dullay and Yaaku languages. South Cushitic languages, such as Iraqw and Burunge, are spoken in Tanzania and are known for their click consonants, acquired through contact with neighbouring languages.

Characteristics

Cushitic languages are generally verb-final with subject-object-verb word order, use case marking on nouns, and have complex systems of tone and vowel length. Many use focus-marking constructions, and several have grammatical gender. Somali and Oromo are written in Latin-based scripts; Oromo uses the Qubee alphabet.
