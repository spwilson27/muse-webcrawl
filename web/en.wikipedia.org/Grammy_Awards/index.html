The Grammy Awards, stylised as GRAMMYs and often called the Grammys, are awards presented by the Recording Academy of the United States to recognise outstanding achievements in the music industry. They are regarded as one of the four major annual American entertainment awards, alongside the Academy Awards (film), the Emmy Awards (television), and the Tony Awards (theatre)—collectively known by the acronym EGOT.

History

The Grammys originated in the Hollywood Walk of Fame project of the 1950s; recording executives compiled a list of significant industry figures and decided a dedicated awards ceremony was needed. The first ceremony was held on 4 May 1959, simultaneously in Beverly Hills and New York, honouring recordings of 1958. The trophy, a gramophone, gave the awards their name (originally the "Gramophone Awards"). The ceremony was first televised live in 1971 and has most often been held in Los Angeles, including at the Crypto.com Arena.

Categories

The most prominent categories are the four General Field awards: Album of the Year, Record of the Year, Song of the Year, and Best New Artist. Dozens of further awards cover genres from pop, rock, rap, and country to jazz, classical, and Latin music; the Latin Grammy Awards have been presented separately since 2000. Beyoncé is the most awarded artist in Grammy history, with 35 wins.

Process and criticism

Voting members of the Recording Academy—musicians, producers, engineers, and other professionals—determine nominees and winners in a multi-stage ballot process. The awards have periodically faced criticism over perceived snubs, genre boundaries, and representation, prompting reforms such as the elimination of secret nominating review committees in 2021 and the addition of new categories.
