Employment testing

Employment testing is the practice of administering written, oral, or other tests as a means of determining the suitability or desirability of a job applicant. The premise is that if test scores correlate with job performance, it is economically useful for the employer to select employees based on scores from such tests.

Types of tests

Common categories include cognitive ability tests, which are among the strongest single predictors of job performance; personality tests, such as those based on the Big Five traits; integrity tests; job knowledge and work sample tests; situational judgment tests; and physical ability tests for physically demanding jobs. Assessment centres combine several exercises, including group tasks and interviews.

Validity

Meta-analyses by industrial-organizational psychologists, notably the work of Schmidt and Hunter, have found that general mental ability tests combined with interviews or integrity tests predict job performance better than interviews alone. Nevertheless, validity varies by occupation, and tests with lower validity can still create legal risk if they disadvantage protected groups.

Legal considerations

In the United States, employment tests are governed by the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Uniform Guidelines on Employee Selection Procedures. In the landmark case Griggs v. Duke Power Co. (1971), the Supreme Court held that employment practices that have a disparate impact on minority groups must be shown to be related to job performance. Other countries regulate pre-employment medical examinations, genetic testing, and drug testing through privacy and equality law.
