Michaelis–Menten kinetics

Michaelis–Menten kinetics is one of the best-known models of enzyme kinetics. It is named after German biochemist Leonor Michaelis and Canadian physician Maud Menten, who proposed the model in 1913. The model describes the rate of enzymatic reactions by relating the reaction rate, which is the rate of formation of product, to the concentration of a substrate.

v = Vmax · [S] / (Km + [S])

In this equation, Vmax is the maximum rate achieved by the system, and Km, the Michaelis constant, is the concentration of substrate at which the reaction rate is half of Vmax.

The model

The model takes the form of an equation describing the rate of enzymatic reactions, with the scheme in which an enzyme E binds a substrate S to form an enzyme–substrate complex ES, which then releases a product P and regenerates the free enzyme. Two key assumptions underlie the derivation: the steady-state approximation, proposed by G. E. Briggs and J. B. S. Haldane in 1925, which holds that the concentration of the intermediate complex remains approximately constant, and the assumption that the reverse reaction from product to substrate is negligible.

The Michaelis constant

The Michaelis constant Km is experimentally defined as the concentration at which the rate of the enzyme reaction is half of Vmax. It is often interpreted as an inverse measure of the substrate's affinity for the enzyme: a low Km indicates high affinity, meaning a low concentration of substrate is needed to approach Vmax.

Applications and limitations

Michaelis–Menten kinetics applies to many enzymes but fails for allosteric enzymes, which display sigmoidal kinetics better described by the Hill equation. It also does not account for enzyme inhibition in its basic form, though competitive, non-competitive and uncompetitive inhibition can each be incorporated into modified equations.
