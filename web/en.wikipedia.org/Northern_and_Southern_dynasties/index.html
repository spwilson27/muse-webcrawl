The Northern and Southern dynasties (Chinese: 南北朝) was a period in Chinese history that lasted from 386 to 589, following the Sixteen Kingdoms period and preceding the Sui dynasty. Though an age of civil war and political chaos, it was also a time of flourishing arts and culture, advances in technology, and the spread of Mahayana Buddhism and Daoism. China was divided between rival dynasties ruling the north and the south.

The Northern dynasties

The Northern dynasties comprised the Northern Wei, Eastern Wei, Western Wei, Northern Qi, and Northern Zhou. The Tuoba clan of the Xianbei founded the Northern Wei in 386 and unified northern China by 439. Emperor Xiaowen's reforms in the late 5th century promoted sinicisation, moving the capital to Luoyang and encouraging intermarriage and adoption of Han customs. After civil war split the dynasty in 534–535, power passed through successor states until the Northern Zhou reunified the north in 577.

The Southern dynasties

The Southern dynasties — Liu Song, Southern Qi, Liang, and Chen — ruled from Jiankang (modern Nanjing) in succession. They preserved the culture of the Eastern Jin exile regime and saw significant economic development in the Yangtze delta. The Liang dynasty of Emperor Wu, a devout Buddhist, was devastated by the rebellion of Hou Jing (548–552), from which the south never fully recovered.

Reunification

In 581 Yang Jian seized the Northern Zhou throne and founded the Sui dynasty. In 589 Sui forces crossed the Yangtze and conquered the Chen dynasty, reunifying China after nearly three centuries of division and setting the stage for the flourishing Sui and Tang eras.

Culture

The era saw the carving of the Yungang and Longmen Buddhist grottoes, advances in mathematics such as Zu Chongzhi's calculation of pi, and the compilation of the literary anthology New Songs from the Jade Terrace and Liu Xie's The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons.
