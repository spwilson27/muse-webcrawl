Polaris

Constellation: Ursa Minor
Apparent magnitude: 1.98 (variable)
Distance: approx. 447 light-years
Spectral type: F7 Ib

Polaris is a star in the northern circumpolar constellation of Ursa Minor. It is designated α Ursae Minoris (Latinized to Alpha Ursae Minoris) and is commonly called the North Star or Pole Star. With an apparent magnitude that fluctuates around 1.98, it is the brightest star in the constellation and is readily visible to the naked eye at night. The star can be seen from all points in the Northern Hemisphere and is therefore of great use in celestial navigation, since its elevation above the horizon approximates the observer's latitude.

As the closest Cepheid variable star to Earth, Polaris has been the subject of extensive study. The star is a multiple star system: the primary, Polaris Aa, is a yellow supergiant orbited by a smaller companion, Polaris Ab; the pair is in turn orbited by Polaris B. Its distance, measured by the Hipparcos and Gaia missions, is roughly 450 light-years.

Role as pole star

Polaris lies less than one degree from the north celestial pole, so it appears nearly stationary in the night sky while all other stars appear to circle around it. Because of the precession of Earth's axis, the pole star changes over millennia: Thuban served as pole star in ancient Egyptian times, and in about 12,000 years Vega will be near the pole. Polaris will make its closest approach to the pole (about 0.45°) around the year 2100.

Nomenclature and history

The name Polaris, from the Latin stella polaris ("polar star"), was coined in the Renaissance. The star has been known by many names, including Cynosura in antiquity. It marked the end of the handle of the Little Dipper asterism.
