Transition state theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transition state theory (TST) explains the reaction rates of elementary chemical reactions. The theory assumes a special type of chemical equilibrium (quasi-equilibrium) between reactants and activated transition-state complexes. TST is used primarily to understand qualitatively how chemical reactions take place, and provides a framework for the Eyring equation relating reaction rates to temperature and activation free energy.

History

The theory was developed simultaneously in 1935 by Henry Eyring at Princeton University and by Meredith Gwynne Evans and Michael Polanyi at the University of Manchester. It built on earlier work on collision theory and on the concept of the potential energy surface introduced by René Marcelin.

Basic ideas

Reactions proceed along a minimum-energy path on a potential energy surface. At the highest point along the reaction coordinate sits the transition state: a fleeting configuration of atoms that, once crossed, proceeds to products. The theory treats the activated complex as being in quasi-equilibrium with reactants, allowing its concentration to be computed with statistical mechanics.

The Eyring equation

The rate constant is given by k = κ (kBT/h) exp(−ΔG‡/RT), where κ is a transmission coefficient, kB is Boltzmann's constant, h is Planck's constant, and ΔG‡ is the Gibbs free energy of activation. Separating ΔG‡ into enthalpy and entropy of activation allows experimental analysis of both contributions.

Limitations

TST assumes no recrossing of the dividing surface and classical motion over the barrier. It must be corrected for quantum tunnelling in reactions involving light atoms at low temperatures, and dynamical effects can cause deviations in condensed phases.
