Hippolytus (Greek: Ἱππόλυτος) is an ancient Greek tragedy by Euripides, first produced in 428 BCE, when it won first prize at the City Dionysia. Based on the myth of Hippolytus, son of Theseus, it dramatises the destructive power of Aphrodite, whom Hippolytus scorns in his exclusive devotion to Artemis and chastity. The surviving play is Euripides' second treatment of the story; an earlier version, in which Phaedra shamelessly propositioned Hippolytus herself, had shocked Athenian audiences and is now lost.

Plot

Aphrodite opens the play by announcing her revenge: Phaedra, Theseus's wife, has fallen desperately in love with her stepson Hippolytus. Phaedra's nurse extracts the secret and, despite Phaedra's horror, reveals it to Hippolytus, who reacts with a famous tirade against women. Ashamed, Phaedra hangs herself, leaving a letter falsely accusing Hippolytus of rape. Theseus returns, believes the letter, and curses his son using one of three wishes granted by Poseidon; a monstrous bull from the sea panics Hippolytus's horses and he is fatally dragged. Artemis appears to reveal the truth, and father and son are reconciled before Hippolytus dies, forgiving Theseus.

Influence

The play profoundly influenced later literature, including Seneca's Phaedra, Racine's Phèdre (1677), and many modern retellings of the Phaedra myth.
