Divine light

In theology, divine light (also called divine radiance or divine refulgence) is an aspect of divine presence perceived as light during a theophany or vision, or represented as such in allegory or metaphor. Light has also been taken as the first product in some traditions—for example, in the Book of Genesis, when God declares "Let there be light" (Latin: fiat lux)—and has been a pervasive symbol of divinity, truth, goodness and illumination across many religions.

In religious traditions

Judaism

In the Hebrew Bible, light is the first of God's creations, and God's presence is associated with radiance, as in the shining face of Moses and the glory (kavod) of the Lord. In Kabbalah, the infinite divine light, the Ohr Ein Sof, is the light emanating from God before the creation of the worlds, contracted through tzimtzum to make room for the created universe.

Christianity

Christian scripture frequently identifies God and Christ with light: the Gospel of John calls Jesus the "light of the world", and the Transfiguration depicts Christ shining with uncreated light. In Eastern Orthodox theology, especially in the hesychast tradition defended by Gregory Palamas, the light of Tabor seen by the apostles at the Transfiguration is the uncreated light of God's energies, which contemplatives may experience in prayer. Western traditions likewise employ light imagery, from the "light of faith" to the illumination of the mind discussed by Augustine and Aquinas.

Islam

The Qur'an's famous Light Verse (Ayat an-Nur, 24:35) describes God as "the Light of the heavens and the earth", and light (nur) became a central image in Islamic philosophy and Sufism, notably in the illuminationist philosophy (ishraq) of Suhrawardi.

Other traditions

Light imagery appears in many other faiths: in Zoroastrianism, where light is associated with Ahura Mazda and opposed to darkness; in Buddhism, where Buddhas and bodhisattvas such as Amitabha, the Buddha of Infinite Light, radiate light; in Hinduism, where light symbolises knowledge dispelling the darkness of ignorance, celebrated at festivals such as Diwali; and in the Inner Light doctrine of Quakerism, which holds that the light of Christ is present within every person.

Symbolism

Because light makes sight possible, it serves naturally as a metaphor for knowledge, truth, goodness, life and divine presence, opposed to darkness as a symbol of ignorance, evil and death. This symbolism pervades scripture, liturgy, religious art—where halos and mandorlas depict holy figures as radiant—and architecture, where the manipulation of light, as in Gothic cathedrals, aims to evoke the divine.
