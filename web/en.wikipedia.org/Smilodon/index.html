Smilodon

Reconstruction of Smilodon fatalis, the classic saber-toothed cat

Smilodon is a genus of extinct saber-toothed cat in the subfamily Machairodontinae. It lived in the Americas during the Pleistocene epoch, from about 2.5 million years ago until its extinction roughly 10,000 years ago. Although commonly known as the saber-toothed tiger, it was not closely related to the modern tiger or other living cats. Three species are recognised: S. gracilis, S. fatalis and S. populator, the last being among the largest felids known, at up to around 280–400 kg.

Anatomy

Smilodon was robustly built, with a short tail, powerful neck and especially strong forelimbs. Its most famous feature is the pair of elongated upper canine teeth, which in S. populator could reach about 28 cm. The canines were laterally flattened and finely serrated, and comparatively fragile; the jaw could open to around 120 degrees to clear them. Its bite force was actually weaker for its size than that of a lion, and researchers believe it used its forelimbs to pin prey before delivering a precise shearing bite to the throat.

Fossils and extinction

Thousands of S. fatalis specimens have been recovered from the La Brea Tar Pits in Los Angeles, California, where predators were trapped while scavenging mire-entangled prey. Smilodon preyed on large herbivores such as bison, camels and young mammoths. It disappeared during the Quaternary extinction event at the end of the last Ice Age, along with most American megafauna, probably through a combination of climate change, the decline of its prey, and competition with newly arrived humans.
