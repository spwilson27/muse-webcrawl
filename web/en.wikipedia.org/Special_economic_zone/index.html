Special economic zone

A special economic zone (SEZ) is an area within a country that is subject to business and trade laws that are different from those in the rest of the country, typically offering tax incentives, simplified customs and regulatory procedures, and better infrastructure, in order to attract foreign direct investment, boost exports, create employment and pilot economic reforms. SEZs include free trade zones, export processing zones, free ports and industrial parks.

History

The concept descends from ancient free ports such as Delos and from modern pioneers like Shannon Free Zone in Ireland (1959), widely considered the first modern SEZ. The model expanded rapidly after 1980, when China designated its first four special economic zones — Shenzhen, Zhuhai and Shantou in Guangdong and Xiamen in Fujian, with Hainan added in 1988 — as laboratories for market-oriented reform. Shenzhen's transformation from a small fishing town into a megacity of over 17 million became the emblematic SEZ success. The World Bank estimated in 2019 that there were nearly 5,400 SEZs in more than 140 economies.

Assessment and criticism

Successful zones tend to combine liberal policies with good location, infrastructure and links to the wider economy, and to catalyse reform beyond their fences. Critics point to mixed results in many countries: zones can degenerate into tax-haven enclaves with few local linkages, spur races to the bottom in labour and environmental standards, or displace activity rather than create it. SEZs in India, established under the SEZ Act of 2005, and in Africa and Southeast Asia show widely varying outcomes.
