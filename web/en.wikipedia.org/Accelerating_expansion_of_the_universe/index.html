Accelerating expansion of the universe

The accelerating expansion of the universe is the observation that the expansion of the universe is speeding up: the distance between distant galaxies grows at an ever-increasing rate. It was discovered in 1998 by two independent teams, the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-Z Supernova Search Team, from measurements of distant Type Ia supernovae.

Type Ia supernovae serve as standardisable candles. The teams found that supernovae at high redshift were fainter — and therefore farther away — than expected in a universe whose expansion was decelerating under gravity. The implication was that some repulsive effect has come to dominate the expansion in the recent cosmic past. The discovery earned Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam Riess the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Dark energy

The acceleration is commonly attributed to dark energy, a hypothetical form of energy with negative pressure that makes up about 68% of the universe's energy content in the standard ΛCDM model. The simplest candidate is the cosmological constant (Λ), originally introduced by Einstein, corresponding to a constant energy density of empty space. Alternatives include dynamical fields ("quintessence") and modifications of general relativity, though none is yet required by the data.

Evidence

Beyond supernovae, the acceleration is supported by observations of the cosmic microwave background, baryon acoustic oscillations, and the growth of large-scale structure, which combine to give a consistent picture of a flat universe whose expansion began accelerating roughly five billion years ago, when dark energy overtook matter in density.

Fate of the universe

If the acceleration continues indefinitely, galaxies beyond the Local Group will eventually recede faster than light can cross the intervening space, and the universe will approach a cold, dilute de Sitter state — the "Big Freeze".
