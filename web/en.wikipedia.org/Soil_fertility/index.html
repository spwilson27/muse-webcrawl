Soil fertility

Soil fertility refers to the ability of soil to sustain plant growth and optimize crop yield. This can be enhanced through organic and inorganic fertilizers to the soil. Nuclear techniques provide data that enhances soil fertility and crop production while minimizing the environmental impact. A fertile soil has the following properties: the ability to supply essential plant nutrients in adequate amounts and proportions, good structure for root growth and water movement, a suitable pH, and a healthy population of soil organisms.

Plant nutrients

Plants require macronutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in the largest quantities — along with secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulfur) and micronutrients such as iron, zinc, boron and manganese. Nitrogen supports leaf growth, phosphorus supports roots and flowering, and potassium supports overall plant health and disease resistance. Nutrients are held on the surfaces of clay and humus particles and exchanged with soil water through the soil's cation-exchange capacity.

Soil properties affecting fertility

  • Texture and structure determine drainage, aeration and root penetration.
  • Organic matter improves nutrient retention, water holding and biological activity.
  • pH influences nutrient availability; most crops prefer a slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 6.0–7.0), and lime is added to raise the pH of acid soils.
  • Soil biology — bacteria, fungi, earthworms and other organisms cycle nutrients, fix atmospheric nitrogen and build soil structure.

Maintaining and restoring fertility

Fertility is maintained by returning nutrients removed in harvests, through mineral fertilizers, manure, compost, cover crops and legume rotations that fix nitrogen. Practices that deplete fertility include continuous cropping without inputs, erosion, overgrazing and salinization. Soil testing guides the precise application of amendments, reducing both cost and nutrient runoff that pollutes waterways.
