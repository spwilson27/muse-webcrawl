Mutualism (biology)

Mutualism describes the ecological interaction between two or more species in which each species benefits. It is one of the principal types of symbiosis, contrasted with commensalism (one benefits, the other is unaffected) and parasitism (one benefits at the other's expense).

Types

Mutualisms are often classified by the service exchanged: resource–resource mutualisms (such as mycorrhizal fungi trading soil minerals to plants for sugars), service–resource mutualisms (such as pollination, in which animals transport pollen in exchange for nectar), and service–service mutualisms (such as cleaner fish removing parasites from client fish while gaining food and protection). Interactions may be obligate, where neither partner can survive without the other, or facultative, where partners can live independently.

Examples

  • Pollination of flowering plants by bees, birds, and bats.
  • Seed dispersal by fruit-eating animals.
  • Mycorrhizal associations between plant roots and fungi.
  • Nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium bacteria in legume root nodules.
  • Gut flora aiding digestion in animals, including termites' cellulose-digesting symbionts.
  • The clownfish and sea anemone partnership of protection for shelter.

Evolutionary considerations

Mutualisms can break down when one partner "cheats" by taking benefits without reciprocating, and many systems include sanctions or partner-choice mechanisms that stabilize cooperation. Mutualistic networks in ecosystems tend to be nested, with specialists interacting with subsets of the partners of generalists.
