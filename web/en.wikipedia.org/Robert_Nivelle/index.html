Robert Nivelle

Robert Georges Nivelle
Born: 15 October 1856, Tulle, France
Died: 22 March 1924 (aged 67), Paris, France
Service: French Army
Known for: Commander-in-chief of the French armies (1916–1917); Nivelle Offensive

Robert Georges Nivelle (15 October 1856 – 22 March 1924) was a French artillery officer who served in the Boxer Rebellion and the First World War. He commanded the French armies on the Western Front for a brief period in 1916 and 1917, and is principally remembered for the disastrous Nivelle Offensive of April 1917, whose heavy casualties helped trigger widespread mutinies in the French Army.

Early career

Nivelle was born in Tulle to a French father and an English mother, which gave him fluency in English that later made him popular with British politicians and officers. A graduate of the École Polytechnique, he was commissioned into the artillery and served in China during the Boxer Rebellion and in Algeria. By 1914 he was a colonel commanding an artillery regiment.

First World War

Nivelle rose rapidly during the war. His artillery methods contributed to French successes, and during the Battle of Verdun in 1916 he served under Philippe Pétain, directing the operations that retook Fort Douaumont and Fort Vaux from the Germans. His achievements at Verdun, his confidence, and his promise of a decisive breakthrough won him appointment as commander-in-chief in December 1916, replacing Joseph Joffre.

Nivelle Offensive

Nivelle planned a massive Franco-British assault for spring 1917, centred on the Chemin des Dames ridge, promising a breakthrough within 48 hours using a creeping barrage and massed artillery. The plan was widely discussed before the attack — Nivelle spoke of it openly, and the Germans captured copies of the plans — and the Germans withdrew to the Hindenburg Line, disrupting parts of the scheme. When the offensive began on 16 April 1917, the French advanced only slightly at enormous cost: casualties exceeded 100,000 in the first days, and the operation failed to achieve the promised breakthrough.

Mutinies and dismissal

The failure shattered morale; beginning in late April and May 1917, mutinies and collective indiscipline spread through much of the French Army. Nivelle was removed in May 1917 and replaced by Pétain, who restored discipline with a mixture of restraint and improved conditions. Nivelle was posted to North Africa and played no further significant role. He died in Paris in 1924.
