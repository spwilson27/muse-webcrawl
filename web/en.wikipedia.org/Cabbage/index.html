Cabbage, comprising several cultivars of Brassica oleracea, is a leafy green, red (purple), or white (pale green) biennial plant grown as an annual vegetable crop for its dense-leaved heads. It is a member of the same species as broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts, and kohlrabi, all selectively bred from the wild cabbage. Cabbages weigh typically 0.5 to 4 kilograms and are a staple vegetable across Europe and Asia.

History

Cabbage was probably domesticated somewhere in Europe before 1000 BC and was prized by the Greeks and Romans, who attributed medicinal properties to it. Heading cabbages spread through Europe in the Middle Ages; Jacques Cartier planted cabbage in Canada in the 1540s, and Captain Cook used sauerkraut to protect his crews from scurvy. China is now the world's largest producer, and global production exceeds 70 million tonnes annually.

Description and varieties

The plant forms a short stem bearing a rosette of overlapping leaves that fold into a compact head. Varieties include green, red, and white cabbages; the crinkly-leaved Savoy cabbage; and loose-headed spring greens. Red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments that act as a pH indicator, turning red in acid and blue-green in alkali. Cabbage is generally hardy, stores well, and can be grown through much of the year in temperate climates, though it suffers pests such as cabbage white butterflies and diseases including clubroot.

Culinary use and nutrition

Cabbage is eaten raw in coleslaw and salads, cooked in soups and stews, stuffed, steamed, and fermented: as sauerkraut in Germany, kimchi (usually with napa cabbage) in Korea, and curtido in El Salvador. It is low in calories and a good source of vitamin C, vitamin K, folate, and fibre. Like other brassicas it contains glucosinolates, compounds studied for their effects on health.
