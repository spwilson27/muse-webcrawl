Marcus Garvey

Marcus Mosiah Garvey (17 August 1887 – 10 June 1940) was a Jamaican political activist and founder of the Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA). He campaigned for Black nationalism and Pan-Africanism, promoting the idea that people of African descent should build their own economic and political power and ultimately redeem Africa from European colonial rule. His ideas, known as Garveyism, influenced later movements including the Nation of Islam, the Black Power movement and Rastafari, in which he is regarded as a prophet.

Early life and the UNIA

Born in Saint Ann's Bay, Jamaica, Garvey worked as a printer, travelled in Central America and lived in London (1912–1914), where he encountered Pan-African ideas and read Booker T. Washington's autobiography. Returning to Jamaica in 1914, he founded the UNIA. In 1916 he moved to Harlem, New York, where the organisation grew into a mass movement claiming millions of members worldwide, with its newspaper Negro World, conventions, uniformed auxiliaries and businesses.

Enterprises and decline

Garvey's Black Star Line shipping company (1919) and the Negro Factories Corporation sought to build Black-owned commerce linking Africa and the diaspora, but both collapsed through mismanagement and fraud by others. Prosecuted by the US government — in a campaign encouraged by J. Edgar Hoover, who regarded him as a dangerous agitator — Garvey was convicted of mail fraud in 1923 in connection with Black Star Line stock sales. His sentence was commuted by President Coolidge in 1927 and he was deported to Jamaica. He continued political work there and in London, where he died in 1940.

Legacy

Garvey is a National Hero of Jamaica. His emphasis on racial pride, self-reliance and an African homeland profoundly shaped 20th-century Black politics, and his prophecy of the crowning of an African king contributed to his veneration in the Rastafari movement. In January 2025, US President Joe Biden issued Garvey a posthumous pardon.
