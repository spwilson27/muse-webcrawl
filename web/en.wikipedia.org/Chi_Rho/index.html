Chi Rho

The Chi Rho (☧) is one of the earliest forms of Christogram, formed by superimposing the first two capital letters of the Greek word Christos (ΧΡΙΣΤΟΣ) — chi (Χ) and rho (Ρ). It became a prominent symbol of Christianity and of the Roman Empire after Constantine the Great.

History

According to tradition, Constantine saw a vision of the symbol before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 with the words "in this sign, conquer", and ordered it painted on his soldiers' shields. He subsequently adopted it on his military standard, the Labarum. The Chi Rho appears widely in late antique and early medieval art, including in the Book of Kells, and remains in use in Christian liturgy and vestments.

An earlier, similar ligature was used by Greek scribes as an abbreviation marking useful passages, and a related symbol, the staurogram, combined tau and rho.
