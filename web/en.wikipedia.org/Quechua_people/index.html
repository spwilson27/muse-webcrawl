The Quechua people are Indigenous peoples of South America who speak the Quechua languages, which originated among the peoples of the central Andes. They are concentrated in Peru, Bolivia, and Ecuador, with communities in Colombia, Argentina, and Chile. Numbering in the millions, they are among the largest Indigenous groups in the Americas.

History

Quechua spread widely as the administrative language of the Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu), which absorbed many distinct Andean peoples. After the Spanish conquest in the 16th century, Quechua remained the lingua franca of the highlands and was used by the Catholic Church for evangelization, even as colonial policies and later republican-era discrimination pushed many speakers toward Spanish.

Culture

Quechua culture is characterized by high-altitude agriculture (potatoes, quinoa, maize), camelid herding (llamas and alpacas), reciprocal labour practices such as ayni and minka, vibrant textile traditions, and festivals blending Catholic and pre-Columbian Andean spirituality, including reverence for Pachamama (Mother Earth).

Contemporary issues

Quechua is co-official in Peru and Bolivia, and intercultural bilingual education programs exist across the region, though many varieties are endangered. Quechua political movements have advanced Indigenous rights, land claims, and recognition of Andean cultural heritage.
