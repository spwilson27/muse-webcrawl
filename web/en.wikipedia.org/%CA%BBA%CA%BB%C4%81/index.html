ʻAʻā

ʻAʻā is one of three basic types of flow lava, named from the Hawaiian language. It is basaltic lava characterised by a rough, rubbly surface composed of broken lava blocks called clinkers.

Characteristics

ʻAʻā flows move in open channels; the surface cools and breaks into jagged fragments that are carried along and tumble over the flow front, so the finished flow has a loose, spiny surface that is difficult to walk on. Compared with pāhoehoe, ʻaʻā generally has higher viscosity and flows more turbulently, though the same eruption can produce both types as lava cools and slows.

Etymology

The term was introduced into volcanology from Hawaiian, where ʻaʻā also evokes the stony roughness of the flow and, in folk rendering, the cry of someone walking barefoot across it.
