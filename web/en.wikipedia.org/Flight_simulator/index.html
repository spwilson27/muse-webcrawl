Flight simulator

A flight simulator is a device that artificially re-creates aircraft flight and the environment in which it flies, for pilot training, design, or other purposes. It includes duplicating the equations that govern how an aircraft flies, how it reacts to applications of flight controls, the effects of other aircraft systems, and how the aircraft reacts to external factors such as air density, turbulence, wind shear, cloud, and precipitation.

History

One of the earliest successful devices was the Link Trainer, developed by Edwin Link in 1929, a simple mechanical simulator with a short motion system driven by pumps and valves. The Link Trainer became standard equipment for training pilots during the Second World War, when well over half a million Allied aircrew were trained on it. Modern full flight simulators reproduce a complete cockpit on a moving platform with six degrees of freedom, wide field-of-view visual systems, and sound and vibration cues.

Levels of fidelity

Regulators such as the FAA and EASA qualify simulators at several levels. A full flight simulator (FFS), qualified at Level D (the highest), can be used for zero-flight-time type conversion training, in which a pilot's first flight in a new aircraft type is made with passengers on a revenue service. Simpler devices — flight training devices and basic instrument trainers — are used for procedures and instrument practice. Simulators are essential for rehearsing emergencies, such as engine failure on take-off, fires, and decompression, that would be dangerous to practise in a real aircraft.

Entertainment

Flight simulation is also a longstanding genre of home computing, from the early Flight Simulator programs of the 1980s to Microsoft Flight Simulator (2020), which streams satellite-derived terrain for the whole Earth, and open-source projects such as FlightGear. Enthusiasts build home cockpits with replica controls and multiple displays.
