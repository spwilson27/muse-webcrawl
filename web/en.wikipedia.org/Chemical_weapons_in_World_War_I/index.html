Chemical weapons in World War I

Chemical weapons in World War I were used extensively for the first time in military history, beginning with small-scale tear gas use in 1914 and escalating to lethal agents.

On 22 April 1915, Germany released chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres — the first large-scale lethal gas attack. Phosgene and mustard gas followed; by the war's end, all major belligerents used chemical shells, with roughly 1.3 million casualties and about 90,000–100,000 deaths attributed to gas.

Gas masks and protective equipment evolved rapidly. Fritz Haber directed Germany's program. The horror of gas warfare led to the 1925 Geneva Protocol banning chemical weapons use in war.

Famous responses include John McCrae's poem "In Flanders Fields" context and Wilfred Owen's "Dulce et Decorum Est", which describes a gas attack.
