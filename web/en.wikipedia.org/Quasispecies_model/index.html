Quasispecies model

The quasispecies model is a description of Darwinian evolution of self-replicating entities within physical chemistry, originally proposed by Manfred Eigen and Peter Schuster in the 1970s. A quasispecies is a large group or "cloud" of related genotypes that exist in an environment of high mutation rate, where a large fraction of offspring are mutants. This is in contrast to a species, which from an evolutionary perspective is a stable genotype whose offspring are mostly identical.

The model

The entities — typically RNA molecules or RNA viruses — replicate with frequent copying errors. Selection acts on the whole distribution of sequences ("mutant cloud") rather than on a single fittest sequence, because mutants can back-mutate and support one another collectively. The quasispecies equations describe the time evolution of sequence concentrations under replication, mutation and degradation.

Error threshold

The model predicts an error threshold: if the mutation rate exceeds a critical value that depends on genome length and the fitness advantage of the master sequence, the genetic information can no longer be maintained and the population drifts into a uniform distribution over sequence space — an "error catastrophe". This places an upper limit on the genome size of primitive replicators and on the mutation rate sustainable by RNA viruses, and it motivates "lethal mutagenesis" as an antiviral strategy for pushing viruses such as poliovirus and foot-and-mouth disease virus over their error threshold.

Applications

The quasispecies concept is applied to RNA viruses (influenza, HIV, hepatitis C), prion strain behaviour, and origin-of-life research on prebiotic replicators.
