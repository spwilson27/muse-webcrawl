Chronic myeloid leukaemia

Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), also known as chronic myelogenous leukaemia, is a cancer of the white blood cells characterised by the uncontrolled growth of predominantly myeloid cells in the bone marrow and their accumulation in the blood. It is a clonal disorder of the haematopoietic stem cell and is defined by a characteristic chromosomal abnormality, the Philadelphia chromosome, which creates the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene. The introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors, beginning with imatinib in 2001, transformed CML from a fatal disease into a condition with near-normal life expectancy for most patients.

Signs and symptoms

Many patients are diagnosed incidentally when a routine blood count shows a raised white cell count. Symptoms, when present, include fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, early satiety and discomfort from an enlarged spleen, and a tendency to gout from rapid cell turnover. The disease typically progresses through a chronic phase lasting years, an accelerated phase, and a blast crisis resembling acute leukaemia, which is difficult to treat.

Cause and diagnosis

CML is caused by a reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, first described in Philadelphia in 1960. The resulting BCR::ABL1 fusion protein is a permanently active tyrosine kinase that drives cell proliferation. Diagnosis rests on the blood count and film, bone marrow examination, and demonstration of the translocation by cytogenetics, FISH, or PCR; quantitative PCR for BCR::ABL1 transcripts is also used to monitor treatment response.

Treatment

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) — imatinib, followed by dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib, ponatinib, and asciminib — block the BCR::ABL1 kinase and induce durable remissions in the great majority of patients in chronic phase. Some patients who achieve deep, sustained molecular responses can stop treatment under close monitoring (treatment-free remission). Allogeneic stem cell transplantation, once the mainstay, is now reserved for resistant or advanced disease.
