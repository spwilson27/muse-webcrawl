Weimar Republic

Berlin in the 1920s: the republic's capital was a centre of art, film and nightlife amid political turmoil.

The Weimar Republic, officially named the German Reich, was the government of Germany from 1918 to 1933. It was named after the city of Weimar, where Germany's new constitution was adopted by the National Assembly convened there after the November Revolution that ended the First World War and the monarchy. The Republic's constitution created a semi-presidential system with universal suffrage, a directly elected president, and a parliament, the Reichstag, elected by proportional representation.

Born amid defeat and revolution, the republic faced immediate crises: the harsh Treaty of Versailles reparations, attempted coups (the Kapp Putsch of 1920 and Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch of 1923), armed uprisings, and the hyperinflation of 1923, which destroyed middle-class savings. After stabilisation with the Dawes Plan and foreign loans, the "Golden Twenties" (1924–1929) brought relative prosperity, diplomatic rehabilitation under Foreign Minister Gustav Stresemann, and an extraordinary cultural flowering — Bauhaus design, expressionist cinema such as Metropolis, and Berlin's cabaret scene.

Collapse

The Wall Street Crash of 1929 ended American lending and plunged Germany into mass unemployment — some six million jobless by 1932. Chancellor Heinrich Brüning governed by presidential emergency decree under Article 48 as parliamentary government broke down. The Nazi Party and Communists surged in the fragmented Reichstag. On 30 January 1933, President Paul von Hindenburg appointed Adolf Hitler chancellor; within months the Reichstag Fire Decree and Enabling Act dismantled democracy, and the republic gave way to Nazi Germany, though the Weimar constitution was never formally abolished.

Legacy

Historians debate whether the republic was doomed from birth — the "republic without republicans" thesis citing its hostile judiciary, military, and elites, the burden of Versailles and the stab-in-the-back myth — or whether it had stabilised sufficiently by 1929 that only the Depression destroyed it. The Basic Law of the Federal Republic of Germany in 1949 deliberately corrected Weimar's perceived defects, including a constructive vote of no confidence, a weaker presidency, and a threshold for small parties.
