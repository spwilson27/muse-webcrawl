Ostriches are large flightless birds of the genus Struthio, native to Africa. Two living species are generally recognised: the common ostrich (Struthio camelus) and the Somali ostrich (Struthio molybdophanes). The ostrich is the world's largest and heaviest living bird, lays the largest eggs of any extant animal, and — despite being flightless — is a superb runner, sustaining about 50 km/h and sprinting faster, aided by long muscular legs and two-toed feet unique among birds.

Description

Males sport bold black-and-white plumage; females and immature birds are greyish brown. Ostriches have long bare necks and legs, powerful kicking feet with a large clawed toe, and the largest eyes of any land vertebrate (about 5 cm across), giving them exceptional vision. Lacking a keel on the sternum, they belong to the ratites, kin to emus, cassowaries, rheas and kiwis.

Behaviour and reproduction

Ostriches live in nomadic groups of 5–50 birds, grazing on seeds, shrubs and occasional insects and small animals. Males compete for harems of hens, which lay their eggs in a single communal nest — a scrape in the ground incubated by the dominant hen by day and the male by night. An ostrich egg weighs about 1.4 kg, equivalent to some two dozen chicken eggs. Contrary to the famous myth, ostriches do not bury their heads in sand; threatened birds flatten their necks against the ground or flee, and can deliver dangerous kicks.

Humans and ostriches

Ostriches have been hunted and farmed for feathers (long prized in fashion), meat, leather and eggs; commercial farming, pioneered in South Africa in the 19th century, now occurs worldwide. The Arabian ostrich was hunted to extinction by the mid-20th century, and wild populations elsewhere face habitat loss and hunting, though the common ostrich remains widespread.
