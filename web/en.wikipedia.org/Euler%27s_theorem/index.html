Euler's theorem

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783). His theorem generalises Fermat's little theorem in number theory.

In number theory, Euler's theorem (also known as the Fermat–Euler theorem or Euler's totient theorem) states that, if n and a are coprime positive integers, then a raised to the power of Euler's totient function of n, φ(n), is congruent to 1 modulo n:

aφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n).

Relation to Fermat's little theorem

If n is a prime number p, then φ(p) = p − 1, and the statement reduces to Fermat's little theorem, ap−1 ≡ 1 (mod p). Euler's theorem is thus a genuine generalisation. It was first proved by Leonhard Euler in 1736, using properties of the residues coprime to n; in modern terms it follows from the fact that the multiplicative group of integers modulo n has order φ(n), so by Lagrange's theorem any element raised to the group order gives the identity.

Applications

Euler's theorem is fundamental in public-key cryptography. In the RSA cryptosystem, encryption and decryption exponents are chosen as inverses modulo φ(n) (or the Carmichael function λ(n)), and the theorem guarantees that decryption recovers the original message. It is also used to reduce huge exponents in modular arithmetic computations: the exponent of a modulo n may be reduced modulo φ(n) when a and n are coprime. Carmichael's theorem sharpens the result by providing a smaller universal exponent.

Other theorems of the name

Many results are called "Euler's theorem", including Euler's rotation theorem in geometry, his theorem on homogeneous functions in calculus, and his polyhedron formula; this article concerns the number-theoretic result.
