Tableau économique

The Tableau économique (French pronunciation: [tablo ekonomik], "economic table") is an economic model first described by French economist François Quesnay in 1759, which laid the foundation of the physiocrats' economic theories. It is regarded as one of the first systematic models of an economy, depicting the circulation of goods and money between different sectors of society.

Quesnay's Tableau, showing the zigzag circulation of income between classes

Contents of the model

Quesnay divided society into three classes: the productive class (agricultural laborers and farmers), the proprietary class (landowners, including the king and the clergy), and the sterile class (artisans, merchants, and manufacturers). The Tableau traced how the net product (produit net) generated by agriculture circulated through the economy in a zigzag pattern of expenditures between these classes.

Central to the physiocratic view was the doctrine that land—and agriculture alone—produced a surplus over the cost of production. Manufacturing and commerce merely transformed or moved goods and were therefore "sterile", adding no new wealth. From this analysis the physiocrats derived policy conclusions favoring free trade in grain and a single tax on land, the impôt unique.

Significance

The Tableau is considered a landmark in the history of economic thought. Karl Marx studied and commented on it extensively, and it anticipated later tools such as input–output analysis, developed in the 20th century by Wassily Leontief, and the circular-flow diagrams of modern macroeconomics. Joseph Schumpeter praised Quesnay's model as a pioneering attempt to render the economic process as an interdependent system.
