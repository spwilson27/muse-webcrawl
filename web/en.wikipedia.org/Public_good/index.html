Public good

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, a public good (also referred to as a social good or collective good) is a good that is both non-excludable and non-rivalrous. For such goods, users cannot be barred from accessing or using them for failing to pay for them, and one individual's use does not reduce availability to others. Examples classically cited include national defence, public fireworks displays, street lighting, and lighthouses.

Definition and taxonomy

Goods are classified by two properties: excludability (whether non-payers can be prevented from using the good) and rivalry (whether one person's use diminishes another's). Pure public goods are non-excludable and non-rival. Private goods are both excludable and rival. Goods that are non-rival but excludable are club goods (such as subscription streaming services), and goods that are rival but non-excludable are common-pool resources (such as fisheries), whose overuse can produce the tragedy of the commons.

The free-rider problem

Because no one can be excluded from benefiting from a public good, individuals have an incentive to free-ride — to consume the good without contributing to its cost — which can lead competitive markets to underprovide it. Paul Samuelson's 1954 paper "The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure" formalized the theory and the condition for optimal provision. Mancur Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965) analysed how group size and incentives affect collective provision. Governments commonly address the problem through taxation, though public goods can also be provided privately, through social norms, or by assurance contracts.

Global public goods

Some goods, such as climate stability, disease eradication, and scientific knowledge, have benefits that cross national borders and are called global public goods. Their provision raises additional coordination problems because there is no world government to compel contributions.
