Cord blood (also called umbilical cord blood) is blood that remains in the umbilical cord and placenta after childbirth. It is a rich source of haematopoietic stem cells — the blood-forming stem cells also found in bone marrow and peripheral blood — and can be collected, processed, and cryopreserved in cord blood banks for use in stem cell transplantation.

Cord blood is collected from the umbilical cord after it has been clamped and cut; the procedure poses no risk to mother or baby.

Medical uses

Cord blood transplantation is an established treatment for many of the same conditions treated by bone marrow transplant, including leukaemias, lymphomas, immune system disorders, and inherited metabolic and blood disorders such as Fanconi anaemia and thalassaemia. The first successful cord blood transplant was performed in Paris in 1988 on a child with Fanconi anaemia, using blood from his newborn sister. Cord blood grafts tolerate greater HLA mismatch than adult grafts and carry a lower risk of graft-versus-host disease, though the limited cell dose in a single unit can slow engraftment, especially in adults; double-unit transplants and ex vivo expansion techniques address this.

Collection and banking

After delivery, the cord is clamped and blood is drained from the umbilical vein into a sterile collection bag, typically yielding 40–200 mL. Units are tested for infectious diseases and HLA type, processed to remove excess plasma and red cells, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored. Public banks store donated units listed on registries for any matched patient; private (family) banks store a baby's cord blood for a fee for possible family use — a practice professional bodies regard as controversial because the probability of a child using its own stored unit is low, and autologous units cannot treat the child's own genetic diseases or leukaemia.

Research

Cord blood also contains other cell populations under investigation, including mesenchymal and endothelial progenitors, and is being studied in trials for conditions such as cerebral palsy and type 1 diabetes. Guidelines on the timing of cord clamping balance the newborn's benefit from delayed clamping against the volume available for collection.
