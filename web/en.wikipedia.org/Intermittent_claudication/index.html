Intermittent claudication

Intermittent claudication, also known as vascular claudication, is a symptom that describes muscle pain on mild exertion (ache, cramp, numbness or sense of fatigue), classically in the calf muscle, which occurs during exercise, such as walking, and is relieved by a short period of rest. It is classically associated with early-stage peripheral artery disease, and can progress to critical limb ischemia unless risk factors are attended to.

Signs and symptoms

One of the hallmarks of arterial claudication is that it is intermittent: it appears after a reproducible amount of exertion and resolves within a few minutes of standing still. The pain is most commonly in the calf, but may affect the thigh, buttock or foot depending on the level of arterial narrowing. It must be distinguished from neurogenic claudication (pseudoclaudication) caused by spinal canal stenosis, in which symptoms are posture-dependent.

Causes

Most commonly, intermittent claudication is a symptom of peripheral arterial disease caused by atherosclerosis—hardening and narrowing of the arteries supplying the legs. During exercise, the muscles demand more oxygen than the narrowed arteries can deliver, causing ischemic pain. Smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension and hypercholesterolaemia are major risk factors.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on the history, physical examination of pulses, and the ankle–brachial pressure index (ABPI), which compares blood pressure at the ankle with that in the arm. Imaging such as duplex ultrasound, CT angiography or MR angiography may be used when intervention is planned.

Treatment

Management focuses on cardiovascular risk reduction—smoking cessation, statins, antiplatelet therapy, and control of blood pressure and diabetes—along with supervised exercise therapy, which improves walking distance. The drug cilostazol may help some patients. Endovascular procedures or bypass surgery are reserved for severe or disabling cases.
