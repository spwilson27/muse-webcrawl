Quantitative revolution

In geography, the quantitative revolution (QR) was a paradigm shift that sought to develop a more rigorous and systematic methodology for the discipline. It came as a response to the inadequacy of regional geography to explain general spatial processes and dynamics. The main claim for the quantitative revolution was that it led to a shift from a descriptive (idiographic) geography to an empirical law-making (nomothetic) geography, drawing heavily on statistical and mathematical techniques and on the philosophy of positivism.

Background and development

The revolution took place mainly during the 1950s and 1960s, centred on universities in the United States and Britain. Fred K. Schaefer's posthumous 1953 paper "Exceptionalism in Geography" attacked the prevailing view that geography was a uniquely descriptive discipline, arguing instead for spatial laws. Geographers such as William Bunge, Richard Chorley, Peter Haggett and William Garrison applied regression, factor analysis, gravity models and network analysis to questions of settlement, transport and location, aided by the new availability of computers and census data.

Criticism and legacy

From the late 1960s the quantitative revolution was criticised by humanistic, behavioural and radical (especially Marxist) geographers for ignoring social meaning, reducing people to points in models, and serving state and corporate planning. David Harvey, an early proponent, became a leading critic. Nevertheless, quantitative methods remain embedded in the discipline through spatial analysis, geographic information systems (GIS) and geostatistics, while modern "critical GIS" and geocomputation movements revisit the revolution's assumptions.
