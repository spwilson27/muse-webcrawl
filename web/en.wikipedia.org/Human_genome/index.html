Human genome

The human genome is the complete set of nucleic acid sequences for humans, encoded as DNA within the 23 chromosome pairs in cell nuclei and in a small DNA molecule found within mitochondria. It consists of approximately 3.1 billion base pairs. The haploid human genome contains roughly 19,000–20,000 protein-coding genes — far fewer than early estimates of 100,000 or more — while most of the sequence is non-coding DNA whose functions include regulation, structural roles, and large fractions of repetitive and "junk" DNA.

Sequencing

The Human Genome Project, an international effort launched in 1990, published a draft sequence in 2001 (simultaneously with Celera Genomics' privately funded sequence) and declared the genome essentially complete in 2003. Remaining gaps in highly repetitive regions were resolved in 2022 by the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium, which published the first truly complete human genome, including centromeres. Reference genomes are continually refined (e.g. GRCh38).

Variation and medicine

Any two humans share about 99.9% of their DNA sequence; the differences include single-nucleotide polymorphisms, insertions, deletions, and copy-number variants. Genome-wide association studies link variants to diseases, and clinical genome sequencing is increasingly used to diagnose rare disorders and guide cancer treatment. Databases such as the 1000 Genomes Project and gnomAD catalogue human genetic variation.
