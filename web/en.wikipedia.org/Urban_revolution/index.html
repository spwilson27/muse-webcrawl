Urban revolution

The urban revolution is a term popularised by the Australian archaeologist V. Gordon Childe to describe the process by which small, kin-based agricultural villages transformed into large, socially complex, literate cities. Childe introduced the concept in his 1936 book Man Makes Himself and elaborated it in a famous 1950 article in the Town Planning Review, where he listed ten criteria he saw as characterising the earliest cities.

Childe's ten criteria

Childe's traits included: large and dense settlements; full-time craft specialists; concentration of agricultural surplus by a god or king through taxation or tribute; monumental public buildings; a ruling class exempt from manual labour; systems of recording and writing; exact and predictive sciences such as arithmetic and astronomy; sophisticated art styles; long-distance trade in raw materials; and social organisation based on residence rather than kinship.

Process and causes

Following the earlier Neolithic Revolution in farming, the urban revolution occurred independently in several regions: Mesopotamia in the fourth millennium BC (the Uruk period), Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, Mesoamerica and the Andes. Scholars debate the drivers, proposing combinations of irrigation management, warfare, trade, population pressure and religious centralisation; Robert McC. Adams's comparative work on Mesopotamia and Mesoamerica emphasised social and ecological complexity over any single cause.

Criticism

Modern archaeologists treat Childe's checklist cautiously: some early cities lack certain traits, writing and monumentality appear in different orders in different regions, and the "revolution" unfolded over centuries rather than as a single event. Nevertheless, the concept remains a foundational framework for studying state formation and early urbanism.
