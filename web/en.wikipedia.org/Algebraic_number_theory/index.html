Algebraic number theory

Algebraic number theory is a branch of number theory that uses the techniques of abstract algebra to study the integers, rational numbers, and their generalizations. Number-theoretic questions are expressed in terms of properties of algebraic objects such as algebraic number fields and their rings of integers, finite fields, and function fields. These properties, such as whether a ring admits unique factorization, the behavior of ideals, and the Galois groups of fields, can resolve questions of primary importance in number theory, like the existence of solutions to Diophantine equations.

History

Diophantus

The beginnings of algebraic number theory can be traced to Diophantine equations, named after the 3rd-century Alexandrian mathematician Diophantus, who studied them and developed methods for solving some kinds of Diophantine equations. A typical Diophantine problem is to find two integers x and y such that their sum and the sum of their squares, respectively, equal two given numbers A and B.

Fermat, Euler, and Gauss

The study of Fermat's Last Theorem motivated much of the early development of the subject, including the work of Ernst Kummer on ideal numbers. Carl Friedrich Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) laid foundations through the theory of quadratic forms and cyclotomic fields.

Basic notions

Algebraic number fields

An algebraic number field is a finite extension of the field of rational numbers Q. The degree of the extension is called the degree of the field. The ring of integers of such a field consists of the algebraic integers it contains.

Failure of unique factorization

An important property of the ring of integers is that it is a Dedekind domain, so ideals factor uniquely into prime ideals, even though elements themselves may fail to factor uniquely into irreducible elements. The class group measures this failure and is one of the central objects of the theory.

Major results and applications

Algebraic number theory encompasses the proofs of quadratic reciprocity, the class number formula, and Dirichlet's unit theorem. In the twentieth century it became central to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem by Andrew Wiles, via the modularity theorem for elliptic curves, and it underlies modern approaches in the Langlands program.
