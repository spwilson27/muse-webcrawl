Vagus nerve stimulation

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is a medical treatment that involves delivering electrical impulses to the vagus nerve. It is used as an adjunctive therapy for drug-resistant epilepsy and for treatment-resistant depression, and is investigated for conditions including migraine, tinnitus and inflammatory disorders.

Method

Implanted VNS uses a pulse generator, similar to a pacemaker, placed under the skin of the chest and connected by a lead to electrodes wrapped around the left vagus nerve in the neck. The device cycles on and off — typically 30 seconds of stimulation every five minutes — and patients can trigger an extra dose with a handheld magnet at the onset of a seizure. Non-invasive devices stimulate the auricular or cervical branch through the skin.

Approved uses

The US Food and Drug Administration approved implanted VNS for epilepsy in 1997 and for treatment-resistant depression in 2005. Roughly half of epilepsy patients achieve a reduction in seizure frequency of at least 50 percent, with benefits often increasing over time.

Side effects and mechanism

Common side effects during stimulation include hoarseness, cough, throat tingling and shortness of breath; serious complications are uncommon. The exact mechanism is uncertain but involves afferent fibres projecting to the nucleus tractus solitarius and onward to the locus coeruleus and limbic structures, altering neurotransmitter release and cerebral blood flow.
