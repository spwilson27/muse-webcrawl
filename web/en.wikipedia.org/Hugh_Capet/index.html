Hugh Capet (c. 941 – 24 October 996) was the king of the Franks from 987 to 996 and the founder of the Capetian dynasty, whose direct and cadet lines ruled France for centuries. As count of Paris and duke of the Franks, he was the leading magnate of West Francia before his election.

Accession

When the Carolingian king Louis V died childless in 987, the assembled magnates at Senlis elected Hugh over the Carolingian claimant Charles of Lorraine. He was crowned at Noyon on 3 July 987 and immediately had his son Robert crowned as associate king, a practice that helped make the succession hereditary in effect.

Reign

Hugh's direct domain was small — the Île-de-France and Orléanais — and great lords such as the counts of Blois, Anjou and Flanders were often more powerful. He relied on church support, particularly Archbishop Adalbero of Reims and Gerbert of Aurillac, and spent much of his reign containing rivals and Viking-descended Normandy. His nickname "Capet" is traditionally linked to the cape (cappa) of St Martin that he wore as lay abbot.

Legacy

Though personally unremarkable as a conqueror, Hugh founded the dynasty that, uniquely in Europe, passed the crown father-to-son for over 300 years and made Paris the centre of an expanding French monarchy.
