Antigone (Ἀντιγόνη) is a tragedy by the ancient Greek playwright Sophocles, written in or before 441 BC. It is the third of Sophocles' three Theban plays in terms of the events depicted, though it was written first. The play tells of Antigone, daughter of Oedipus, who defies the decree of King Creon of Thebes forbidding the burial of her brother Polynices, and is condemned to death for her act of pious disobedience. It is one of the most celebrated and influential of all Greek tragedies, a foundational text for debates about natural law, civil disobedience, family duty and state authority.

Background

After Oedipus' exile, his sons Eteocles and Polynices killed each other in battle over the throne of Thebes. Their uncle Creon, the new ruler, honoured Eteocles with burial but ordered that Polynices, who had attacked the city, be left unburied — a profound outrage in Greek religion, denying the dead passage to the underworld.

Plot

Antigone resolves to bury Polynices herself; her sister Ismene, fearing the death penalty, refuses to help. Antigone performs a symbolic burial with dust and libations and is caught. Before Creon she defends herself by appealing to the "unwritten and unfailing" laws of the gods, which override human edicts. Creon, stubbornly asserting the authority of the state, condemns her to be walled up alive in a tomb. His son Haemon, Antigone's betrothed, pleads in vain for her life. The prophet Tiresias warns Creon that the gods reject Thebes' altars because of the unburied corpse and the imprisoned living girl; Creon relents too late — he buries Polynices and goes to free Antigone, but she has hanged herself. Haemon kills himself over her body, and Creon's wife Eurydice, hearing of her son's death, also dies. Creon is left broken, acknowledging his guilt.

Themes and influence

The play's central conflict — Antigone's divine and familial duty against Creon's civic authority — has been read for over two millennia as a drama of conscience versus law, and neither figure is simply a villain: Creon's concerns for public order are real, and Antigone's devotion is inseparable from the doom of her house. Hegel's reading of the play as a collision of two equally justified ethical powers (family and state) shaped modern interpretation. Notable adaptations include versions by Jean Anouilh (1944, performed in occupied Paris), Bertolt Brecht, and Seamus Heaney (The Burial at Thebes, 2004).
