Auditory system

The auditory system is the sensory system for the sense of hearing. It includes both the sensory organs (the ears) and the auditory parts of the sensory system in the brain. Together with the vestibular system, it forms the vestibulocochlear system of the inner ear.

The outer, middle and inner ear convert sound into neural signals.

Outer and middle ear

Sound waves are gathered by the pinna and conducted down the ear canal to the tympanic membrane (eardrum). Vibrations pass through the three ossicles of the middle ear — the malleus, incus and stapes — which act as a lever system and match the impedance between air and the fluid of the inner ear, concentrating pressure at the oval window.

Inner ear

In the cochlea, pressure waves travel along the basilar membrane, whose stiffness gradient means different frequencies peak at different places — high frequencies at the base, low frequencies at the apex. Hair cells in the organ of Corti transduce the motion into electrical signals: inner hair cells are the principal sensory receptors, while outer hair cells act as an active amplifier. Auditory nerve fibres carry the signals to the brainstem.

Central auditory pathways

From the cochlear nuclei, signals ascend through the superior olivary complex (important for localising sounds by timing and intensity differences between the ears), the inferior colliculus and the medial geniculate body of the thalamus, reaching the primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. Processing in the cortex underlies the perception of pitch, timbre, speech and music.

Disorders

Damage to the system produces conductive hearing loss (outer or middle ear) or sensorineural hearing loss (cochlea or nerve), as well as tinnitus and central auditory processing disorders. Treatments include hearing aids and cochlear implants.
