Paris Salon

The Paris Salon was the official art exhibition of the Académie des Beaux-Arts in Paris and, from the mid-18th century to the end of the 19th, the most important annual or biennial art event in the Western world. Held in the Salon Carré of the Louvre (from which it took its name) and later in larger venues, it was the arena in which academic careers were made and the standard of official taste was displayed to an enormous public.

Origins and heyday

The Académie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture, founded in 1648, began exhibiting members' work in 1667; exhibitions became regular from 1737, open to the public and accompanied by printed catalogues (livrets). Denis Diderot's celebrated Salons, written from 1759, effectively invented modern art criticism. Admission was controlled by a jury enforcing the academic hierarchy of genres — history painting highest, then portraiture, genre scenes, landscape, and still life. Canvases were hung one above another to the ceiling ("skyed" works being at a disadvantage), and success at the Salon brought state purchases, medals, and eligibility for the Prix de Rome.

Challenge and decline

By the mid-19th century the Salon's conservatism provoked organised opposition. In 1863, after unusually harsh rejections, Napoleon III authorised the Salon des Refusés, where Manet's Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe scandalised the public. The Impressionists mounted their own independent exhibitions from 1874. In 1881 the state withdrew, handing the Salon to the artist-run Société des Artistes Français; rival salons (the Salon des Indépendants, 1884; the Salon d'Automne, 1903) multiplied, and the official Salon's authority faded, though it continues in successor forms to this day.
