William Henry Beveridge, 1st Baron Beveridge (5 March 1879 – 16 March 1963) was a British economist and Liberal politician who was a progressive and social reformer. His 1942 report, Social Insurance and Allied Services (known as the Beveridge Report), served as the basis for the post-war welfare state put in place by the Labour government elected in 1945, including the establishment of the National Health Service.

Early career

Born in Bengal to a judge of the Indian Civil Service, Beveridge was educated at Charterhouse and Balliol College, Oxford. He worked at Toynbee Hall, wrote leaders for the Morning Post, and joined the Board of Trade under Winston Churchill, designing the labour exchanges system and helping implement the National Insurance Act 1911. His study Unemployment: A Problem of Industry (1909) was an early classic. From 1919 to 1937 he directed the London School of Economics, expanding it greatly, and from 1937 was Master of University College, Oxford.

The Beveridge Report

Commissioned in 1941 to review social insurance, Beveridge proposed a comprehensive system of flat-rate contributions and benefits to abolish the "five giants" of Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness. Published in December 1942, the report sold over 600,000 copies and became a wartime bestseller and a rallying point for post-war reconstruction. Beveridge followed it with Full Employment in a Free Society (1944).

Later life

Elected Liberal MP for Berwick-upon-Tweed in 1944, he lost his seat in 1945 and was raised to the peerage in 1946, leading the Liberals in the House of Lords. He died in Oxford in 1963.
