The Arapaho are a Native American people historically living on the eastern plains of Colorado and Wyoming. They call themselves Hinono'eino, "our people". Speakers of an Algonquian language closely related to that of the Gros Ventre, the Arapaho were closely allied with the Cheyenne and played a central role in the nineteenth-century Plains Indian world of buffalo hunting, trade and warfare.

History

Arapaho ancestors lived in the Red River Valley region of Minnesota and Manitoba before migrating westward onto the Great Plains in the eighteenth century, where they adopted a fully equestrian buffalo-hunting culture. In the 1840s the tribe divided into Northern and Southern bands. The Northern Arapaho eventually settled on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming, established by the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie and shared with the Eastern Shoshone, while the Southern Arapaho were removed to Indian Territory (Oklahoma) with the Southern Cheyenne.

Language and culture

The Arapaho language (Hinóno'eitíít) is a Plains Algonquian language, today spoken fluently mainly by elders; both tribal nations operate language revitalisation programmes, including immersion schools. Traditional religion centres on ceremonies such as the Sun Dance (Offerings Lodge), and Arapaho art is known for quillwork and beadwork with distinctive geometric designs. Arapaho servicemen, like the better-known Navajo code talkers, used their language for secure communications in the Second World War.

Modern tribal nations

Today the Arapaho are organised as two federally recognised tribes: the Northern Arapaho Tribe of the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming, and the Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes of Oklahoma. Both operate tribal governments, cultural programmes and enterprises including casinos.
