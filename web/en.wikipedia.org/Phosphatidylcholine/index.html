Phosphatidylcholine

Phosphatidylcholines (PC) are a class of phospholipids that incorporate choline as a headgroup. They are a major component of biological membranes and can be isolated from sources such as egg yolk (from Greek lekithos, hence the older name lecithin) and soybeans, from which they are extracted mechanically or chemically using hexane. Phosphatidylcholine is also a member of the lecithin group of yellow-brownish fatty substances occurring in animal and plant tissues.

Structure and function

A phosphatidylcholine molecule consists of a glycerol backbone esterified to two fatty acids and to a phosphoric acid bearing a choline group. The molecule is amphipathic: the choline-phosphate head is hydrophilic while the fatty acid tails are hydrophobic, so phosphatidylcholines spontaneously form bilayers in water. In animal cell membranes, phosphatidylcholine is the most abundant phospholipid of the outer leaflet, and it is a major structural component of lung surfactant and of lipoproteins in the blood.

Biosynthesis

Cells synthesize phosphatidylcholine mainly by the CDP-choline (Kennedy) pathway, which attaches choline — an essential nutrient — to diacylglycerol. In the liver, a second route, the PEMT pathway, methylates phosphatidylethanolamine using S-adenosylmethionine.

Health and uses

Phosphatidylcholine is a source of dietary choline and has been investigated for roles in liver health, ulcerative colitis (where delayed-release preparations have shown benefit in some studies), and cognitive function. Commercially, lecithin rich in phosphatidylcholine is widely used as an emulsifier in foods — for example in chocolate and margarine — and in pharmaceuticals and cosmetics. Liposomes made from phosphatidylcholine are used as drug-delivery vehicles.
