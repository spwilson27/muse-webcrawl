Pygmalion (play)

Pygmalion is a play by Irish playwright George Bernard Shaw, named after the Greek mythological figure Pygmalion. It premiered in Vienna in 1913, in German translation, and opened in London in 1914 with Herbert Beerbohm Tree as Henry Higgins and Mrs Patrick Campbell as Eliza Doolittle. The play tells the story of a professor of phonetics who wagers that he can train a Cockney flower girl to pass as a duchess by teaching her upper-class speech and manners, and it is a sharp satire of class, language, and the treatment of women.

Plot

Professor Henry Higgins, a phonetician, boasts to Colonel Pickering that within months he could pass off the flower seller Eliza Doolittle as a lady by changing her speech. Eliza, seeking lessons to better her prospects, comes to his house; Pickering funds the experiment. After months of drills, Eliza triumphs at an embassy ball. But she has gained independence as well as polish, and the play ends in confrontation: treated as a successful experiment rather than a person, Eliza asserts herself and leaves Higgins, who is left oddly bereft. In a prose sequel appended to published editions, Shaw insisted Eliza marries the admiring Freddy Eynsford Hill and opens a flower shop, firmly resisting romantic readings of the Higgins–Eliza relationship.

Themes

Shaw, a phonetics enthusiast and Fabian socialist, used the play to argue that accent and manners — not inherent worth — determine social class, and that education can transform a person's prospects. The play also probes the ethics of creating another person's identity and the independence of the created self, inverting the Pygmalion myth: Shaw's Galatea does not fall in love with her creator.

Adaptations

The play was filmed in 1938 with Leslie Howard and Wendy Hiller; Shaw shared the Academy Award for the adapted screenplay. It was adapted into the Lerner and Loewe stage musical My Fair Lady (1956), filmed in 1964, whose softened, romantic ending Shaw's estate long resisted. Modern retellings include the films Educating Rita (inspired by it), She's All That (1999), and numerous stage revivals that restore Shaw's less sentimental conclusion.
