Nollywood, a portmanteau of "Nigeria" and "Hollywood," is the popular name for the Nigerian film industry, one of the largest film producers in the world by volume. Emerging in the early 1990s with the straight-to-video phenomenon, Nollywood produces thousands of films annually in English and in Nigerian languages including Yoruba, Hausa (whose industry is sometimes called Kannywood), and Igbo. Its films, distributed across Africa and the diaspora, have profoundly shaped popular culture on the continent.

History

Nigerian filmmaking dates to the colonial era and the acclaimed celluloid works of directors such as Ola Balogun and Eddie Ugbomah in the 1970s, but economic decline and the collapse of cinema infrastructure curtailed production. The industry was reborn when electronics dealer Kenneth Nnebue financed Living in Bondage (1992), shot on VHS and sold directly on video cassettes; its success triggered a boom in low-budget, quickly produced video films sold in markets such as Lagos's Idumota and Onitsha.

Characteristics

Classic Nollywood films were shot in days or weeks on minimal budgets, with stories centered on family conflict, romance, religion, witchcraft, wealth, and morality, often told in multi-part melodramas. Distribution relied on informal marketer networks, which also fueled piracy. From the 2000s, rising production values, cinema releases, and streaming deals with platforms such as Netflix and Amazon Prime Video gave rise to a "New Nollywood" of bigger-budget theatrical films, while the prolific video sector continues.

Impact

The industry employs hundreds of thousands directly and indirectly and has made stars of actors such as Genevieve Nnaji, Omotola Jalade Ekeinde, and Ramsey Nouah. Scholars study Nollywood as a unique model of informal, entrepreneurial media production, and its influence extends across African television and popular storytelling.
