Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy
Proposed byAlbert Bandura (1977)
FieldPsychology
Part ofSocial cognitive theory

In psychology, self-efficacy is an individual's belief in their capacity to act in the ways necessary to reach specific goals. The concept was originally proposed by the psychologist Albert Bandura, who defined it as people's beliefs about their capabilities to exercise control over their own functioning and the events that affect their lives. A strong sense of self-efficacy is associated with greater motivation, persistence in the face of difficulty, resilience, and achievement; a weak sense leads to avoidance of challenging tasks and vulnerability to stress and depression.

Sources of self-efficacy

Bandura identified four main sources of efficacy beliefs: mastery experiences — successful performances, the most influential source; vicarious experiences — observing similar others succeed; verbal persuasion — encouragement from others; and physiological and emotional states — interpreting stress responses as signs of incapability undermines efficacy, while calm states support it.

Effects and applications

Self-efficacy influences the goals people choose, the effort they expend, and how long they persevere. It is usually task-specific — a person may have high academic but low athletic self-efficacy. The construct has been applied widely in education, health behaviour (such as smoking cessation and exercise), organizational psychology, and clinical treatment of phobias and other conditions. It is related to, but distinct from, self-esteem (a general sense of self-worth) and locus of control.

Measurement and criticism

Self-efficacy is typically measured with scales tailored to a domain of functioning. Critics have debated whether very general measures retain predictive power, and how efficacy beliefs interact with actual skill and outcome expectations.
