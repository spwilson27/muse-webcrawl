Formula: CaMg(CO3)2
Crystal system: Trigonal
Mohs hardness: 3.5 to 4
Color: White, gray, pinkish

Dolomite (mineral)

Dolomite is an anhydrous carbonate mineral composed of calcium magnesium carbonate, ideally CaMg(CO3)2. The term is also used for a sedimentary carbonate rock composed mostly of the mineral dolomite (see Dolomites for the mountain range, and dolomite rock).

Properties

Dolomite crystals are typically rhombohedral, often with curved faces, and range from colorless to white, pink, or brownish. Unlike calcite, dolomite only reacts weakly with cold dilute hydrochloric acid, a property used to distinguish it in the field.

Formation

Dolomite forms in sedimentary environments, often through the alteration of limestone by magnesium-rich waters, a process called dolomitization. The "dolomite problem" refers to the puzzle that dolomite is abundant in ancient rocks but rare in modern marine sediments, suggesting special conditions are required for its formation.

Uses

Dolomite rock is used as a building stone, in the production of cement and refractory materials, and as a source of magnesium. Crushed dolomite is also applied to agricultural soils to neutralize acidity and supply magnesium.
