Solar prominence

A solar prominence is a large, bright feature extending outward from the Sun's surface. Prominences are anchored to the Sun's surface in the photosphere, and extend outwards into the Sun's hot outer atmosphere, called the corona. A prominence forms over timescales of about a day, and stable prominences may persist in the corona for several months, looping hundreds of thousands of kilometres into space.

Structure

Prominences are composed of relatively cool plasma (around 5,000–8,000 K, compared with the million-degree corona) suspended within magnetic field structures. When viewed against the solar disc, the same structures appear as dark filaments because they absorb light from below. Prominences are classified as quiescent — large, stable sheets found far from active regions — or active, which are smaller, more dynamic and associated with sunspot groups.

Eruptions

Prominences can become unstable and erupt, releasing their material into space. Such eruptions are frequently associated with coronal mass ejections and solar flares, which can affect space weather at Earth. A famous historical eruption, observed in 1859 around the time of the Carrington Event, produced spectacular aurorae and disturbed telegraph systems.

Observation

Prominences are readily observed with hydrogen-alpha filters and by space observatories such as SOHO and the Solar Dynamics Observatory, which monitor them continuously in ultraviolet wavelengths.
