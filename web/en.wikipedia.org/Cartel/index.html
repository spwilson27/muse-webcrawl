Cartel

A cartel is a formal agreement among firms to control prices or exclude competition

A cartel is a group of independent market participants who collude with each other in order to improve their profits and dominate the market. Cartels are usually associations in the same sphere of business, and thus an alliance of rivals. Cartel behaviour typically involves fixing prices, limiting output, allocating markets or customers, or rigging bids.

Overview

Because cartel behaviour is formally an agreement among competitors, it is generally illegal under competition law in most jurisdictions, including in the United States under the Sherman Antitrust Act and in the European Union under Article 101 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. Cartels are harmful to consumers because their activities raise prices above competitive levels and reduce output and choice.

Types

  • Price-fixing cartels agree on the prices at which members will sell.
  • Output cartels restrict the quantity each member may produce.
  • Market-sharing cartels divide customers or territories among members.
  • Bid-rigging cartels coordinate on supposedly competitive bids.

Stability

Cartels face an inherent instability: each member has an incentive to cheat on the agreement by secretly cutting prices or expanding output to gain market share. Game-theoretic analysis, famously using the prisoner's dilemma, explains why cartels often break down without effective monitoring and punishment.

Examples

The most prominent legal and openly tolerated international cartel is OPEC, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, whose members coordinate oil production. Well-known prosecuted cartels include the international lysine and vitamins cartels of the 1990s. The term "cartel" is also commonly applied to criminal drug trafficking organisations, particularly in Latin America, though these are not cartels in the strict economic sense.
