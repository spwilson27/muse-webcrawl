Food safety in China

Food safety in China has been a major public concern since the country's rapid economic development transformed its food system. A series of high-profile scandals — most notoriously the 2008 milk scandal, in which formula adulterated with melamine sickened some 300,000 infants — undermined public trust and prompted sweeping regulatory reform.

Major incidents

Besides melamine in dairy products, widely reported problems have included "gutter oil" recycled from waste cooking oil, pork treated with the banned additive clenbuterol, exploding watermelons associated with growth chemicals, and cadmium-contaminated rice. Such incidents have driven consumer demand for imported food and for premium domestic brands with traceable supply chains.

Regulation

China enacted a comprehensive Food Safety Law in 2009 and substantially strengthened it in 2015, introducing heavier fines and criminal penalties. In 2018, regulatory functions were consolidated in the State Administration for Market Regulation. Challenges persist because production is fragmented among hundreds of millions of small farms and workshops, complicating inspection and enforcement.

Public response

Surveys consistently rank food safety among the top concerns of Chinese consumers. The issue has influenced trade, tourism and the growth of organic and imported food markets, and it remains a sensitive topic in Chinese media and online discussion.
