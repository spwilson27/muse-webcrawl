Publius Vergilius Maro (70–19 BC), usually called Virgil in English, was an ancient Roman poet of the Augustan period. He is regarded as Rome's greatest poet and one of the most influential authors in Western literature. His three major works — the Eclogues (pastoral poems), the Georgics (on farming and rural life), and the epic Aeneid — became foundational school texts in Rome and throughout medieval and Renaissance Europe.

Life

Born near Mantua in Cisalpine Gaul, Virgil was educated at Cremona, Milan, and Rome. After his family's farm was threatened by land confiscations for veterans, he came under the patronage of Maecenas and the future emperor Augustus. He spent his mature years in Naples and Sicily and died at Brundisium in 19 BC, returning from a trip to Greece, reportedly asking on his deathbed that the unfinished Aeneid be burned — a wish Augustus overruled.

Works

The Eclogues (c. 42–39 BC) are ten pastoral poems modelled on Theocritus, rich in coded references to contemporary politics; the Fourth Eclogue was later read by Christians as a prophecy of Christ's birth. The Georgics (c. 37–29 BC) treat crops, trees, livestock, and bees with philosophical depth. The Aeneid (29–19 BC), twelve books in dactylic hexameter, follows the Trojan hero Aeneas from fallen Troy to Italy, where his descendants would found Rome — intertwining Homeric imitation, Roman destiny, and the costs of empire in the famous opening "Arma virumque cano" ("I sing of arms and the man").

Influence

Dante made Virgil his guide through Hell and Purgatory in the Divine Comedy. Virgil's works shaped poets from Ovid to Milton and remain central to the classical canon.
