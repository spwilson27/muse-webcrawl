La Venta is an archaeological site of the pre-Columbian Olmec civilization located in the modern Mexican state of Tabasco, near the Gulf of Mexico coast. Flourishing between about 900 and 400 BCE, it succeeded San Lorenzo as the principal Olmec centre and is considered one of the earliest great ceremonial cities of Mesoamerica.

Site layout

The site occupies a low island in the swampy Tonalá River floodplain. Its ceremonial core was laid out along a precise north–south axis, an early example of formal Mesoamerican city planning. At its centre stands the Great Pyramid (Complex C), a fluted earthen cone about 33 metres high, originally thought to be a stepped pyramid but now understood to be an eroded platform mound, possibly representing a volcano or sacred mountain.

Monuments and offerings

La Venta is famous for four colossal basalt heads, each carved from stone transported from the Tuxtla Mountains dozens of kilometres away, as well as altars, stelae, and basalt column tombs. Excavations revealed elaborate buried offerings, including massive mosaic pavements of serpentine blocks arranged in abstract mask-like designs and caches of jade celts and figurines, deliberately interred and covered — offerings made to the earth rather than displayed.

Investigations and preservation

The site was brought to scholarly attention in the 1920s and excavated by Matthew Stirling and colleagues in the 1940s and 1950s. Petroleum development around the site in the twentieth century damaged parts of it; many of the major monuments were moved for protection to the Parque-Museo La Venta in Villahermosa, where they are displayed in an open-air museum.
