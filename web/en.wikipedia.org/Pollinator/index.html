Pollinator

A pollinator is an animal that transfers pollen from the male anther of a flower to the female stigma, enabling fertilization and seed production. Pollinators are essential to the reproduction of most flowering plants and to global agriculture: roughly one in three bites of food eaten in human diets depends on animal pollination, and about 75 percent of leading food crops benefit from it.

Kinds of pollinators

Insects are the dominant pollinators: bees (some 20,000 species, including honey bees, bumblebees, and solitary bees), butterflies and moths, beetles, flies, and wasps. Vertebrate pollinators include hummingbirds and other nectar-feeding birds, bats, and a few mammals and lizards. Many plants are specialized for particular pollinators — a mutualism called a pollination syndrome — while others are generalists visited by many species.

Importance and decline

The economic value of insect pollination has been estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars annually. Wild pollinators are often more effective per visit than managed honey bees for many crops. Since the late twentieth century, many pollinator populations have declined due to habitat loss, pesticides (including neonicotinoids), parasites such as the Varroa mite, pathogens, and climate change. Colony collapse disorder in honey bees drew wide attention from 2006 onward.

Conservation

Conservation measures include planting pollinator-friendly forage, reducing pesticide use, protecting nesting habitat, and policies such as the European Union's restrictions on neonicotinoids and various national pollinator strategies.
