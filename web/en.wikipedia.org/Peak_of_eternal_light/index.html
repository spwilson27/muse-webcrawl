Peak of eternal light

A peak of eternal light (PEL) is a point on a body within the Solar System that is always, or almost always, in sunlight. Such points exist at the poles of bodies with very small axial tilt. The concept was first proposed in the 19th century, and peaks of eternal light near the lunar poles have been identified as attractive sites for future Moon bases.

Peak of eternal light
Found at: Polar high points of low-tilt bodies
Notable examples: Lunar poles; Mercury's poles

Lunar peaks

Because the Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5° to the ecliptic, crater rims near its poles can receive sunlight for most of the lunar year. High-resolution mapping by orbiters such as the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has identified areas near the north pole illuminated more than 80% of the time.

Applications

Near-constant sunlight would allow solar-powered installations to operate with minimal interruption, and nearby permanently shadowed craters may hold water ice. These combined advantages make PELs prime candidates for crewed lunar outposts.
