Bobbin lace

Bobbin lace is a lace textile made by braiding and twisting lengths of thread, which are wound on bobbins to manage them. As the work progresses, the weaving is held in place with pins set through the pattern holes in a lace pillow, the placement of the pins usually determined by a pattern or pricking pinned on the pillow.

History

Bobbin lace developed in the 16th century in Italy and Flanders, evolving from passementerie (braided trimmings). It spread through Europe as a luxury fabric for collars, cuffs, and ecclesiastical vestments. Famous varieties include Venetian gros point, Brussels lace, Valenciennes, Chantilly, Honiton (England), and the Bedfordshire and Buckinghamshire laces of the East Midlands. Lacemaking was a major cottage industry, employing large numbers of women and children, often in harsh conditions.

Technique

Pairs of threads wound on wooden bobbins are crossed and twisted in sequence, following a pricked pattern pinned to a stuffed pillow. The two fundamental movements are the cross and the twist; combinations produce grounds (meshes), clothwork, and decorative picots. Pins temporarily hold each stitch until the fabric is self-supporting.

Decline and revival

Machine-made nets and laces, beginning in the early 19th century, undercut handmade lace, and the industry collapsed by the early 20th century. Today bobbin lace survives as a skilled hobby and a protected tradition in centres such as Bruges and Idrija.
