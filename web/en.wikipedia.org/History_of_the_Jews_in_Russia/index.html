History of the Jews in Russia

The history of the Jews in Russia spans more than a millennium, from early Jewish communities in the Caucasus and Crimea to the vast population incorporated into the Russian Empire through the partitions of Poland, life in the Pale of Settlement, mass emigration, the Holocaust, and the refusenik movement of the Soviet era.

Early history to the partitions

Jewish communities existed in the Bosporan Kingdom, the Caucasus, and among the Khazars, whose elite converted to Judaism in the early medieval period. In Kievan Rus', a Jewish community existed in Kyiv, though later Muscovite rulers largely excluded Jews. The partitions of Poland (1772–1795) brought nearly a million Jews under Russian rule — the largest Jewish population in the world at the time.

The Pale of Settlement and pogroms

Catherine II confined most Jews to the Pale of Settlement (1791), the western borderlands where they were permitted to live. Under Alexander II some restrictions eased, but after the assassination of Alexander II in 1881 waves of pogroms swept the empire, and the May Laws (1882) tightened discrimination. Between 1881 and 1914 roughly two million Jews emigrated, chiefly to the United States. The Kishinev pogrom (1903) and the Beilis blood libel trial (1911–1913) drew international condemnation.

Soviet era

The 1917 revolution abolished the Pale and legal disabilities; Jews participated prominently in Soviet life, while Yiddish culture briefly flourished before Stalinist repression. Nazi occupation and the Holocaust murdered over a million Soviet Jews; late Stalinism brought the anti-"cosmopolitan" campaign and the Doctors' plot (1953). From the 1970s, "refuseniks" denied exit visas campaigned for emigration to Israel; mass emigration followed the USSR's collapse in 1991, reshaping Jewish communities in Israel, the US, and Germany.
