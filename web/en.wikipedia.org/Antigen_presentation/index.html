Antigen presentation is the process by which cells display fragments of proteins — antigens — on their surface bound to major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, so that they can be inspected by T lymphocytes. It is the essential link between the innate detection of pathogens and the activation of the adaptive immune response: a T cell can only recognise its antigen when it is presented in this form (MHC restriction).

MHC class I pathway

Nearly all nucleated cells present peptides on MHC class I molecules to cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. Proteins from the cytosol — including viral proteins in an infected cell — are degraded by the proteasome into short peptides, transported into the endoplasmic reticulum by the TAP transporter, loaded onto newly synthesised MHC I molecules, and carried to the cell surface. If a CD8+ T cell recognises a foreign peptide, it kills the presenting cell.

MHC class II pathway

MHC class II molecules are expressed mainly by professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) — dendritic cells, macrophages and B cells. These cells internalise material from their surroundings by phagocytosis or endocytosis; the proteins are broken down in endolysosomal compartments, and the resulting peptides are loaded onto MHC II (whose groove is protected until then by the invariant chain/CLIP) and displayed to helper CD4+ T cells, which then orchestrate antibody production, macrophage activation and inflammation.

Cross-presentation and activation

Dendritic cells — the most potent activators of naive T cells — can also route exogenous antigens onto MHC I, a process called cross-presentation, which is crucial for initiating responses against viruses that do not infect dendritic cells themselves and against tumours. Productive T-cell activation requires co-stimulatory signals (such as CD80/CD86 engaging CD28) in addition to antigen recognition; presentation without co-stimulation can induce tolerance. Pathogens and cancers frequently evade immunity by interfering with antigen processing and presentation, for example by down-regulating MHC I.
