Social learning

Social learning is learning that takes place in a social context, through observation of others and the consequences of their actions. It can occur at a merely individual level but is often studied at societal scale. The best-known formulation is Albert Bandura's social learning theory, later expanded into social cognitive theory.

Bandura's theory

Bandura proposed that people can learn new behaviors by watching a model, without direct reinforcement — a claim supported by his Bobo doll experiments (1961–63), in which children imitated aggressive behavior they had seen adults perform. Effective observational learning requires attention, retention, reproduction, and motivation. Observing whether the model is rewarded or punished (vicarious reinforcement) shapes whether the behavior is imitated.

Key concepts

Central concepts include self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to succeed), reciprocal determinism (the mutual influence of person, behavior, and environment), and modeling. The theory bridged behaviorist accounts, which emphasized direct reinforcement, and cognitive accounts of learning.

Applications

Social learning principles underpin modelling-based therapies for phobias, media-effects research on televised and online violence, educational practices using peer models, and criminology, where Edwin Sutherland's differential association theory and Ronald Akers's social learning theory explain criminal behavior as learned through interaction.
