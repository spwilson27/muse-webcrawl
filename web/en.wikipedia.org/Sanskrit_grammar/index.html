Sanskrit grammar

The grammar of Sanskrit is highly systematic and was codified by the ancient grammarian Pāṇini in his Aṣṭādhyāyī ("Eight Chapters"), composed around the 5th–4th century BCE. This work of nearly 4,000 compressed rules (sūtras) describes the language with a precision that has made it a landmark in the history of linguistics.

Phonology and sandhi

Sanskrit has a phoneme inventory of roughly 48 sounds, conventionally arranged in a remarkably scientific order from the back of the mouth forward. Sounds change at word and morpheme boundaries according to rules of sandhi (euphonic combination), which are obligatory in the standard written language.

Morphology

The language is strongly inflectional. Nouns, adjectives and pronouns are declined for three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), three numbers (singular, dual, plural) and eight cases (nominative, accusative, instrumental, dative, ablative, genitive, locative and vocative). Verbs conjugate for three persons, three numbers, two voices (active and middle), and a rich system of tenses and moods built on ten present-stem classes. Sanskrit also possesses an elaborate system of word formation — primary and secondary derivation, and nominal compounding (samāsa) of great complexity, allowing compounds of many members.

Pāṇini's system

Pāṇini's grammar employs a sophisticated metalanguage with marker letters (anubandhas), ordered rule application and the phonological abbreviation device of the Śivasūtras. Commentators such as Kātyāyana and Patañjali (author of the Mahābhāṣya) developed the tradition further. When European scholars encountered Sanskrit grammar in the 18th–19th centuries, it catalysed the birth of comparative linguistics and influenced modern formal language theory.
