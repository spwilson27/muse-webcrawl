Hattusa

Hattusa, also Hattuşa (Hittite: 𒌷𒄩𒀜𒌅𒊭, romanized: Ḫattuša), was the capital of the Hittite Empire in the late Bronze Age. Its ruins lie near modern Boğazkale in Çorum Province, Turkey, in the great loop of the Kızılırmak River, about 145 km east of Ankara. Hattusa was added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 1986.

History

The site was first settled by the indigenous Hattians in the 3rd and early 2nd millennia BCE, and was home to an Assyrian merchant colony (karum) trading with Mesopotamia. The city was destroyed around 1700 BCE; Hittite tradition blamed Anitta of Kusshara, who cursed the site. Hattusili I (c. 1650–1620 BCE) nevertheless refounded Hattusa and made it his capital, taking his throne name from the city. From then until the empire's collapse around 1180 BCE, Hattusa was the royal seat of the Hittite Great Kings, except for a period when Muwatalli II moved the capital to Tarhuntassa in the south before his son moved it back.

At its height in the 13th century BCE, the city covered about 180 hectares, enclosed by massive stone walls with sculpted gates — the Lion Gate, the Sphinx Gate and the King's Gate — and contained the royal citadel of Büyükkale, the Great Temple of the storm god in the Lower City, and archives of cuneiform tablets that remain a primary source for Hittite history, law and diplomacy, including the Egyptian–Hittite peace treaty with Ramesses II. The city was destroyed and abandoned amid the Late Bronze Age collapse; a smaller Phrygian settlement later occupied part of the site.

Excavations

The ruins were identified in the 19th century by travellers such as Charles Texier. Excavations by the German Archaeological Institute began under Hugo Winckler in 1906–1907, recovering thousands of tablets, and resumed under Kurt Bittel and successors through the 20th and 21st centuries. A notable modern addition is a reconstructed section of the mudbrick city wall, built in 2003–2005 using ancient techniques. Excavations have progressively uncovered temples, granaries with huge storage capacity, and the upper city's temple quarter with around thirty temples.
