Sea squirt

Sea squirts, or ascidians, are sessile marine filter-feeding invertebrates of the class Ascidiacea within the subphylum Tunicata. Despite their simple, sac-like adult bodies, they are chordates — members of the same phylum as vertebrates — revealed by their tadpole-like larvae, which possess a notochord and dorsal nerve cord. About 2,300 ascidian species are described, living in seas worldwide, solitary or in colonies.

Anatomy

The adult body is enclosed in a tough outer "tunic" made partly of tunicin, a cellulose-like material unusual in animals. Water is drawn in through an oral siphon, passed through a large perforated pharynx (branchial sac) where food particles are trapped in mucus, and expelled through an atrial siphon, driving both feeding and gas exchange. If disturbed, the animal contracts and squirts water from its siphons — hence the name.

Life cycle

The free-swimming larva resembles a tadpole, with a notochord, nerve cord and tail used to find a suitable surface. On settling it resorbs its tail, brain and notochord and metamorphoses into the sessile adult — a classic demonstration that adult form can conceal chordate affinities. Colonial species reproduce asexually by budding as well, forming mats of connected zooids; some, such as Didemnum vexillum, are invasive fouling pests. The model species Ciona intestinalis is widely used in developmental biology.
