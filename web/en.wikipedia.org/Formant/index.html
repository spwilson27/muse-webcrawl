Formant

Spectrogram of the vowel [i] with formants labelled F1–F3.

In speech science and acoustics, a formant is the broad spectral maximum that results from an acoustic resonance of the human vocal tract. In speech, formants are the distinguishing frequency components of vowels and other sonorants: the positions of the formants, especially the first two, determine which vowel a listener hears. The term was introduced by analogy with acoustics, where a formant is a concentration of acoustic energy around a particular frequency.

Formants and vowels

The first formant (F1) varies inversely with vowel height: high vowels such as [i] and [u] have a low F1 (around 300 Hz), while low vowels such as [a] have a high F1 (around 700–800 Hz). The second formant (F2) reflects frontness and backness: front vowels like [i] have a high F2 (above 2,000 Hz), while back vowels like [u] have a low F2. Lowering of the third formant (F3) characterizes r-coloured vowels in English such as [ɚ]. Plotting F1 against F2 produces the familiar vowel chart of phonetics in acoustic form.

Source–filter theory

According to the source–filter theory of speech production, associated with Gunnar Fant, the glottis produces a harmonic source signal which is then filtered by the resonances of the pharyngeal, oral and nasal cavities; changing the shape of the tract — tongue position, jaw opening, lip rounding — moves the formant frequencies. Lip rounding lengthens the tract and lowers all formants.

Applications

Formant analysis underpins speech recognition, speech synthesis (the classic Klatt formant synthesizer modelled vowels and consonants with cascaded resonators), voice identification and clinical assessment of speech disorders. In singing, the clustering of upper formants produces the "singer's formant" that lets an operatic voice carry over an orchestra.
