Web search engine

A web search engine is a software system designed to search for information on the World Wide Web. The results are presented in a search engine results page (SERP) as a ranked list of links, snippets and increasingly rich features such as knowledge panels, images and direct answers. Search engines are the primary gateway to web content for most users.

How search engines work

Three core stages power a general search engine. Crawling: automated programs ("spiders") traverse the web by following links, downloading pages and respecting site rules such as robots.txt. Indexing: the fetched content is analysed — text, links, media, metadata — and stored in an inverted index mapping terms to documents, together with signals about quality and context. Ranking: when a user submits a query, algorithms retrieve matching pages and order them by predicted relevance, using hundreds of signals including link structure (as in Google's PageRank), content match, freshness, location and personalisation. Results are typically served in well under a second from distributed data centres.

History and market

Early tools such as Archie (1990, for FTP files), WebCrawler and Lycos (1994), and directories like Yahoo! gave way to algorithmic engines including AltaVista (1995) and Google (1998), whose link-based ranking proved decisively better. Google came to dominate global search with a share of around 90 per cent, followed by Microsoft Bing, with regional leaders including Baidu in China and Yandex in Russia. DuckDuckGo and others compete on privacy. Since the early 2020s, engines have integrated generative-AI answers, raising questions for the web's traffic and advertising model, which historically funded free search through paid listings.

Issues

Search engines face persistent challenges: spam and manipulation countered by search engine optimisation (SEO) and counter-SEO, bias and filter bubbles, privacy of query logs, the "right to be forgotten", copyright in snippets, and antitrust scrutiny of default-placement deals and self-preferencing.
