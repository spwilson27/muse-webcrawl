Initial public offering

An initial public offering (IPO) is the process by which a private company first offers its shares to the public on a stock exchange, becoming a publicly traded company. An IPO raises capital, provides liquidity to early investors and employees, and subjects the company to public disclosure requirements.

Process

The company appoints investment banks as underwriters, prepares a prospectus or registration statement such as an S-1 in the United States, undergoes regulatory review, markets shares in a roadshow, and sets an offer price through book building. Shares then begin trading, often with a lock-up period for insiders.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include access to capital, prestige and an acquisition currency. Disadvantages include costs, disclosure, short-term market pressure and loss of privacy.

Alternatives

Alternatives include direct listings, mergers with special-purpose acquisition companies (SPACs), and remaining private.
