Battle rifle

TypeService rifle
Defining featureChambered for full-power rifle cartridges

A battle rifle is a military service rifle chambered for a full-power rifle cartridge, such as 7.62×51mm NATO, and typically fed from a detachable box magazine. The term, largely a retrospective and chiefly American coinage, distinguishes these weapons from assault rifles, which use intermediate-power cartridges and are optimized for controllable automatic fire at shorter ranges. Battle rifles emphasize range, penetration and barrier performance at the cost of heavier weight, heavier ammunition and strong recoil.

History

The first generation of self-loading battle rifles appeared before and during the Second World War, including the American M1 Garand, the Soviet SVT-38 and SVT-40, and the German Gewehr 43. After the war, the debate over cartridge power split the emerging NATO alliance: Britain championed the intermediate .280 British round and the EM-2 rifle, while the United States insisted on the full-power 7.62×51mm NATO. The resulting classic battle rifles of the Cold War were the Belgian FN FAL — adopted by more than 90 countries and nicknamed "the right arm of the Free World" — the German Heckler & Koch G3, and the American M14.

Decline and revival

Combat experience, especially in Vietnam, showed most infantry firefights occurred within 300 metres, and assault rifles in 5.56×45mm NATO and 5.45×39mm became the general standard. Battle rifles survived mainly as designated marksman and sniper-support weapons. In the 2000s, fighting in Afghanistan at longer ranges revived interest in 7.62mm rifles such as the HK417 and the US Mk 17 SCAR-H, and the US Army's Next Generation Squad Weapon program adopted the XM7 rifle in the high-pressure 6.8×51mm cartridge, blurring the category once more.

Characteristics

Typical battle rifles weigh 4–5 kg unloaded, use magazines of 20 rounds, and have effective ranges of 600 metres or more. Their full-power cartridges make fully automatic fire difficult to control, so many designs are used primarily in semi-automatic mode.
