Materialism

Materialism is a form of philosophical monism holding that matter is the fundamental substance of nature and that all things, including mental states and consciousness, result from material interactions. It stands opposed to idealism, dualism, and phenomenalism. In modern philosophy of mind, the closely related view is usually called physicalism.

History

Materialism arose independently in several ancient traditions: the atomism of Leucippus and Democritus in Greece, the Carvaka school in India, and early Chinese thinkers such as Yang Zhu and Wang Chong. Epicurus and Lucretius developed atomism into a complete worldview. In the 17th and 18th centuries, thinkers such as Thomas Hobbes, Pierre Gassendi, and Julien Offray de La Mettrie — whose L'homme machine (1747) treated humans as machines — revived materialism against Cartesian dualism.

Dialectical materialism

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels adapted Hegel's dialectics into a materialist account of history, later codified as dialectical and historical materialism, which became the official philosophy of many communist states and influenced 20th-century social theory.

Contemporary forms

Contemporary physicalists debate how mental properties relate to physical ones: identity theory identifies mental states with brain states; functionalism defines them by causal roles; eliminative materialism, associated with Paul and Patricia Churchland, holds that folk-psychological categories may be discarded as neuroscience matures. Critics raise the hard problem of consciousness and qualia against all such views.
