Glass casting

Glass casting is the process in which glass objects are cast by directing molten glass into a mould where it solidifies. The technique has been used since the period of the Egyptian New Kingdom (c. 1500 BCE) and was practised by the Romans, but it is best known today as a central method of contemporary kiln-formed glass art.

Kiln casting

In modern studio practice, a model is made in clay, wax, or another material, and a refractory mould (often a mixture of plaster and silica) is taken from it. Glass — as billets, chunks (cullet), or granules — is placed in or above the mould and fired in a kiln to temperatures around 800–900 °C, at which the glass becomes viscous enough to flow into the mould's details. The piece must then be annealed: cooled very slowly over hours or days to relieve internal stresses that would otherwise crack thick glass.

Related techniques

  • Lost-wax casting (cire perdue), used by French firms such as Daum and Lalique for pâte de verre;
  • Pâte de verre — a paste of crushed glass fired in a mould, producing a frosted, sugary surface;
  • Sand casting — pouring molten glass into a sand mould, often for large-scale work, as in the sculptures of Stanislav Libenský and Jaroslava Brychtová, the Czech masters renowned for monumental kiln-cast sculpture.

Cast glass is also used industrially, for example in large telescope mirror blanks and architectural glass blocks.
