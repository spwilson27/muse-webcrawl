The African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), also known as the African claw-toed frog or simply xenopus, is a species of African aquatic frog of the family Pipidae. Its name is derived from the short black claws on its hind feet. The word Xenopus means 'strange foot', and laevis means 'smooth'.

The species is found throughout much of sub-Saharan Africa, and in isolated, introduced populations in North America, South America, Europe, and Asia. African clawed frogs are fully aquatic, living in ponds, lakes, and slow-moving streams, and rarely leave the water except to migrate between bodies of water during drought.

Description

Adult African clawed frogs reach a body length of about 12 cm (5 in). They have flattened, smooth bodies with mottled olive-brown to grey dorsal colouring and paler undersides. Unlike most frogs they have no visible external eardrum and no tongue; instead they feed by using their hands to shovel food into their mouths, assisted by a hyobranchial pump. The hind feet are fully webbed and bear three short black claws, while the front toes are unwebbed. Albino varieties are popular in the aquarium trade.

Behaviour and reproduction

These frogs are scavengers and opportunistic predators, eating living and dead invertebrates, small fish, and other organic matter. They are largely nocturnal. Males produce underwater clicking calls to attract females. Females lay between a few hundred and over a thousand small eggs, which are attached individually to submerged vegetation and stones. The tadpoles are filter feeders that hang head-down in the water column.

Relationship with humans

Xenopus laevis is one of the most important model organisms in developmental biology and was the first vertebrate to be successfully cloned, in experiments by John Gurdon in 1958 that later earned him a Nobel Prize. In the 1930s and 1940s the species was used worldwide in the Hogben pregnancy test, in which urine from a woman was injected into a female frog, which would ovulate if the woman was pregnant. Escapes and releases from laboratories and the pet trade established feral populations on several continents. The species has also been implicated, alongside habitat factors, in the global spread of the amphibian chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis.
