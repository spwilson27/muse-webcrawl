Chandrasekhar limit

The Chandrasekhar limit is the maximum mass of a stable white dwarf star. The currently accepted value of the limit is about 1.44 solar masses (M☉). White dwarfs resist gravitational collapse primarily through electron degeneracy pressure, provided their mass is below this limit. White dwarfs composed chiefly of electron-degenerate matter are typical for the progenitors of most stars, which will end their lives as white dwarfs after fusing their usable nuclear fuel.

Physics

In a white dwarf, gravitational contraction is halted by the pressure of a degenerate electron gas, as required by the Pauli exclusion principle. As mass is added, the electrons are forced to higher momenta and become relativistic; in the relativistic limit the pressure no longer increases fast enough to counteract gravity, and at a critical mass no equilibrium is possible. The limit can be written approximately as

Mlimit ≈ 5.76 Ye² (hc/G)3/2 (1/mH)²

where Ye is the number of electrons per nucleon (about 0.5 for carbon–oxygen matter), giving ≈ 1.44 M☉.

History

Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar calculated the limit in 1930–31, on his voyage from India to England to begin graduate study at Cambridge, publishing the result in 1931. His conclusion — that sufficiently massive stars must collapse indefinitely — was publicly disputed by Arthur Eddington, which delayed its acceptance. Chandrasekhar later won the Nobel Prize in Physics (1983) in part for this work. An earlier equivalent calculation had been made by Wilhelm Anderson (1929) and Edmund Stoner (1930).

Consequences

A white dwarf pushed above the limit — for example by accreting from a companion star — cannot remain stable: it either ignites runaway carbon fusion as a Type Ia supernova, or collapses to a neutron star or black hole. The uniformity of the limit makes Type Ia supernovae useful as standardisable candles for measuring cosmic distances.
