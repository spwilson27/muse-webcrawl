Combined arms

Combined arms is a military doctrine of integrating different combat arms — infantry, armour, artillery, engineers, air defence and aviation — so that they complement one another and compensate for each other's weaknesses, presenting an enemy with a dilemma in which countering one arm exposes him to another. The approach is applied from the tactical (battalion battlegroups and combined arms teams) to the operational and joint levels.

Principles

Each arm has characteristic strengths and vulnerabilities: tanks are fast and protected but vulnerable in close terrain without infantry; infantry can hold ground but lacks mobility and firepower; artillery provides heavy fire support but cannot take ground. Combined arms tactics synchronise them in time and space — artillery suppresses anti-tank defences while armour and mechanised infantry manoeuvre, engineers breach obstacles under covering fire, and air defence protects the force from air attack.

History

Pre-modern armies combined infantry, cavalry and archers, but modern combined arms emerged in the First World War, when German stormtrooper infiltration tactics of 1918 integrated infantry, artillery, machine guns, mortars and aircraft. The concept underpinned Second World War mechanised warfare, notably the German Blitzkrieg and Soviet deep battle, and remains the basis of NATO and other modern doctrines, extended today to joint operations integrating land, air, sea, space and cyber capabilities.
