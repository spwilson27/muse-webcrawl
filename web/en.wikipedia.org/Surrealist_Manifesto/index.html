Surrealist Manifesto

The Surrealist Manifesto (Manifeste du surréalisme) was written by the French poet André Breton and published in Paris in 1924. It formally founded the Surrealist movement, defining Surrealism as "pure psychic automatism" and setting out the movement's aims of liberating thought from reason, morality and aesthetic convention. Breton issued a second manifesto in 1929, and a further prolegomena toward a third in 1942.

Contents

Breton defined Surrealism as "Psychic automatism in its pure state, by which one proposes to express — verbally, by means of the written word, or in any other manner — the actual functioning of thought. Dictated by thought, in the absence of any control exercised by reason, exempt from any aesthetic or moral concern." Drawing on Freud's ideas of dreams and the unconscious and on the automatic writing Breton had practised with Philippe Soupault in Les Champs magnétiques (1920), the manifesto named precursors from the Marquis de Sade to Arthur Rimbaud and Guillaume Apollinaire, who had coined the word "surrealism".

Second manifesto and later texts

The Second Manifesto (1929) was more political and polemical, attacking former associates, aligning Surrealism more closely with dialectical materialism, and calling for the "occultation" of Surrealism. A projected third manifesto was prefaced by the Prolegomena to a Third Surrealist Manifesto or Not (1942), written in exile during the Second World War.

Legacy

The 1924 manifesto is regarded as one of the most influential art manifestos of the 20th century. It launched an organised movement that reshaped poetry, painting, photography and cinema, and its techniques — automatism, dream imagery, the juxtaposition of incongruous objects — remain central to the Surrealist legacy.
