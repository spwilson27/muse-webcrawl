History of the Internet

The history of the Internet began with research into packet switching and computer networking in the 1960s, sponsored largely by the US Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA). The ARPANET, first connected in 1969, linked universities and research centres; the TCP/IP protocols standardised by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn in the 1970s allowed separate networks to inter-operate as a single "internet". The invention of the World Wide Web by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989–91 and the commercial opening of the network in the 1990s turned the Internet into a global mass medium used by more than five billion people.

Origins: packet switching

In the early 1960s, J. C. R. Licklider of ARPA promoted a vision of an "Intergalactic Computer Network" of interactive, linked computers. Independently, Paul Baran at RAND designed a distributed communications network that broke messages into blocks, and Donald Davies at Britain's National Physical Laboratory developed the same idea — which he named "packet switching" — to use lines efficiently. Leonard Kleinrock provided queueing-theory foundations. These ideas displaced the circuit-switching model of the telephone system for data communication.

ARPANET

Under Lawrence Roberts, ARPA built the ARPANET, contracting Bolt, Beranek and Newman to construct Interface Message Processors (IMPs). The first four hosts — UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah — were connected in late 1969; the first host-to-host message was sent on 29 October 1969 (the system crashed after "LO" of "LOGIN"). The network grew steadily: email, invented by Ray Tomlinson in 1971 and using the "@" sign, soon dominated traffic; file transfer (FTP) and remote login (Telnet) followed. In 1973 the network went international with connections to Norway and Britain.

Internetworking and TCP/IP

To join dissimilar networks — ARPANET, packet radio, and satellite networks — Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn designed the Transmission Control Protocol, published in 1974 and later split into TCP and the Internet Protocol (IP). On 1 January 1983 ("flag day"), ARPANET switched to TCP/IP, the date often taken as the Internet's operational birth. The Domain Name System (1983–84, Paul Mockapetris) replaced central host tables. CSNET and NSFNET (1985) extended networking across US academia, and NSFNET's backbone became the Internet's core as ARPANET was decommissioned in 1990.

The Web and commercialisation

At CERN in 1989, Tim Berners-Lee proposed a hypertext system for sharing documents; by 1991 he had built the first web server, browser, and site, using URLs, HTTP, and HTML. CERN released the Web royalty-free in 1993, the same year the Mosaic browser (Marc Andreessen's team at NCSA) popularised graphical browsing. Netscape's IPO in 1995 ignited the dot-com boom. The US lifted restrictions on commercial traffic, NSFNET was retired in 1995, and the network passed to commercial providers. Search engines (Google, 1998), e-commerce, and broadband followed.

The social and mobile Internet

In the 2000s, user-generated platforms — Wikipedia (2001), Facebook (2004), YouTube (2005), Twitter (2006) — defined "Web 2.0", while smartphones after 2007 made the Internet a constant companion and carried it to billions in the developing world. Internet governance, net neutrality, cybersecurity, and the balance between openness and regulation remain central public questions. By the mid-2020s more than two-thirds of humanity was online.
