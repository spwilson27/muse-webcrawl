Johnson solid

There are exactly 92 strictly convex Johnson solids.

A Johnson solid is a strictly convex polyhedron whose faces are regular polygons but which is not uniform — that is, it is not a Platonic solid, Archimedean solid, prism or antiprism. Because the faces must be regular but the solid need not be vertex-transitive, the class includes many pyramids, cupolae and rotundae and their elongated and gyrated combinations.

History and enumeration

In 1966 Norman Johnson published a list of 92 such solids and conjectured that the list was complete. Victor Zalgaller proved completeness in 1969. The solids are conventionally numbered J1 to J92 and given descriptive names, largely coined by Johnson, such as the square pyramid (J1), the pentagonal orthobicupola and the parabiaugmented truncated dodecahedron (J69).

Construction and classification

Many Johnson solids are formed by joining simpler pieces — pyramids, cupolae and rotundae — to prisms, antiprisms or Archimedean solids, sometimes with parts rotated (gyrated) or removed (diminished). Others, such as the sphenocorona and the bilunabirotunda, are "elementary": they cannot be dissected into such pieces. Johnson solids arise naturally as the convex deltahedra-adjacent forms in geometry and appear in chemistry and architecture wherever regular-faced but non-uniform polyhedra occur.
