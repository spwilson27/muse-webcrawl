Howard Phillips Lovecraft (20 August 1890 – 15 March 1937) was an American writer of weird, science, fantasy and horror fiction. He is best known for his creation of the Cthulhu Mythos, a shared fictional universe of ancient cosmic entities and forbidden knowledge. During his lifetime Lovecraft was virtually unknown and published only in pulp magazines; his reputation grew enormously after his death, and he is now regarded as one of the most influential horror writers of the 20th century.

Life

Born in Providence, Rhode Island, Lovecraft was a precocious child whose father's death in a psychiatric hospital and the family's loss of fortune shadowed his youth. Largely self-educated after ill health interrupted his schooling, he wrote voluminous letters and amateur journalism before turning to fiction. A brief, unhappy marriage and years in New York (1924–1926) preceded his return to Providence, where he produced most of his major tales in his final decade, dying in poverty of cancer at 46.

Work and themes

Lovecraft's fiction expresses "cosmicism": humanity is insignificant in an indifferent universe inhabited by beings such as Cthulhu, Azathoth and Yog-Sothoth, whose mere apprehension drives mortals mad. Major works include "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928), "The Shadow over Innsmouth", "The Dunwich Horror" and the novella At the Mountains of Madness. His essay Supernatural Horror in Literature remains a classic survey of the genre. Lovecraft's racism, expressed in some letters and early poems, is widely acknowledged and debated in assessment of his legacy.

Influence

Through Arkham House, founded by his correspondents August Derleth and Donald Wandrei to preserve his work, Lovecraft's stories reached a mass audience. His influence pervades modern horror literature, film, games and music; "Lovecraftian horror" is a recognised subgenre.
