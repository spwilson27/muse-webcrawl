Urban legend

An urban legend (or urban myth, contemporary legend) is a genre of modern folklore consisting of stories, often with macabre or humorous elements, circulated as true despite lacking evidence. The tales are typically attributed to a "friend of a friend" and reflect contemporary anxieties about crime, technology, and social change. Well-known examples include the vanishing hitchhiker, alligators in city sewers, poisoned Halloween candy, and the killer in the back seat.

Study

The term was popularised by folklorist Jan Harold Brunvand, whose 1981 book The Vanishing Hitchhiker brought the study of contemporary legends to a wide audience. Folklorists analyse urban legends as narratives that encode cultural fears — about strangers, new products, immigration, or youth culture — and note that their plausibility and local detail help them spread. Many legends are modern reworkings of much older motifs.

Spread and the internet

Before the internet, legends travelled by word of mouth, photocopied faxes, and chain letters; email and social media massively accelerated their circulation. Fact-checking sites such as Snopes (founded in 1994 to investigate urban legends) arose in response. Some legends have caused real harm, prompting product tampering panics and false crime reports, while others have been deliberately planted as hoaxes or marketing.

Characteristics

Typical features include a realistic contemporary setting, a twist or moral, an unnamed but proximate source, and resistance to disproof — retellers often adapt details to local places and circumstances. Scholars distinguish legends (told as true) from rumours and jokes, though the boundaries blur in practice.
