Prisoner's dilemma

The prisoner's dilemma is a game theory thought experiment involving two rational agents, each of whom can either cooperate for mutual benefit or betray the other ("defect") for their individual benefit. It was designed by Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher in 1950 at the RAND Corporation, and framed in its famous prison story by Albert W. Tucker.

The dilemma

Two members of a criminal gang are arrested and interrogated separately. Each can stay silent (cooperate) or betray the other (defect). If both stay silent, each serves one year. If one betrays and the other stays silent, the betrayer goes free and the silent one serves three years. If both betray, each serves two years.

Each prisoner reasons that betraying is better whatever the other does, so both betray — yet both would have done better by cooperating. This tension between individual and collective rationality has made the game a paradigm for studying cooperation, arms races, oligopoly pricing, and the tragedy of the commons.

Iterated prisoner's dilemma

When the game is repeated, strategies such as tit for tat — which won Robert Axelrod's famous computer tournaments — can sustain cooperation, because defection today invites retaliation tomorrow.
