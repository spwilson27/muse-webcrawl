North African campaign

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North African campaign of the Second World War took place in North Africa from 10 June 1940 to 13 May 1943. It was fought in the Libyan and Egyptian deserts (the Western Desert campaign), in Morocco and Algeria (Operation Torch), and in Tunisia. The campaign was fought between the Axis powers — Germany and Italy — and the Allies, principally Britain and the Commonwealth, Free France, and from 1942 the United States.

Western Desert campaign

Italy invaded Egypt in September 1940, but the British Operation Compass counteroffensive destroyed the Italian Tenth Army. Germany dispatched the Afrika Korps under Erwin Rommel, whose offensives drove the front back and forth across Cyrenaica for two years. The siege of Tobruk, the battles of Gazala and the First Battle of El Alamein marked the struggle, which turned decisively at the Second Battle of El Alamein (October–November 1942), where Bernard Montgomery's Eighth Army forced the Axis into retreat across Libya.

Torch and Tunisia

On 8 November 1942, Anglo-American forces landed in Morocco and Algeria in Operation Torch. Axis forces were then squeezed in Tunisia from east and west; after hard fighting around the Mareth Line and Kasserine Pass, the Axis bridgehead collapsed, and some 275,000 German and Italian troops surrendered in May 1943.

Significance

The campaign secured the Suez Canal and Middle Eastern oil, knocked Italy towards surrender, and set up the Allied invasion of Sicily and Italy. War in the desert was dominated by supply: ports such as Tripoli and Tobruk, the long coastal road, and Malta-based interdiction of Axis shipping were decisive.
