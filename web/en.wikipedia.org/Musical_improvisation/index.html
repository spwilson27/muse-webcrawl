Musical improvisation

A jazz ensemble improvising

Musical improvisation (also known as musical extemporization) is the creative activity of immediate musical composition, combining performance with real-time communication of emotions and instrumental technique. Improvisers create melodies, harmonies, or rhythms spontaneously, rather than reproducing a fixed written work.

In Western classical music

Improvisation was central to Baroque and Classical practice: organists improvised preludes and fugues, harpsichordists realised figured bass, and soloists improvised cadenzas in concertos. Composers such as J. S. Bach, Handel, Mozart, and Beethoven were renowned improvisers. The practice declined in the 19th and 20th centuries as fidelity to the written score became dominant, though it survives among organists and in contemporary experimental music.

In jazz and popular music

Improvisation is the defining feature of jazz, where soloists invent lines over a song's chord progression, and of blues, rock guitar solos, and freestyle rap. Entire styles — free jazz, jam bands, and live electronic performance — are built around collective improvisation.

In world traditions

Indian classical music is largely improvised within the framework of raga and tala; Arabic and Turkish music use improvised taqsim; flamenco and many folk traditions likewise depend on spontaneous elaboration of inherited formulas.
