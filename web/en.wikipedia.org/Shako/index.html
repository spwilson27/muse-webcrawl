Shako

A shako is a tall, cylindrical military cap, usually with a visor, formerly a standard part of infantry uniforms.

A shako (/ˈʃækoʊ/, /ˈʃɑːkoʊ/, or /ˈʃeɪkoʊ/) is a tall, cylindrical military cap, usually with a visor, and sometimes tapered at the top. It is usually adorned with an ornamental plate or badge on the front, metallic chin scales, and often a feather hackle or pom-pom. The name derives from the Hungarian word csákó, the peaked cap worn by Hungarian hussars from the eighteenth century.

History

The shako spread from hussar dress to become the standard headgear of infantry throughout Europe and the Americas during the Napoleonic Wars, replacing the tricorne and bicorne hats of the eighteenth century. Successive models varied in silhouette — the "stovepipe" shako of the early 1800s, the flared "bell-top" of the 1820s–30s, and progressively lower patterns later in the century. In most armies the shako had been replaced for field service by the kepi, pith helmet or peaked cap before the First World War, although it survived in full dress.

Modern use

Today shakos survive in ceremonial and performing contexts: they are worn by some guard units in full dress, by military academies, and notably by marching bands and drum corps, where the tall hat with plume is an iconic part of the uniform.
