Democratic National Committee

The Democratic National Committee (DNC) is the principal governing organisation of the United States Democratic Party. Founded in 1848, it coordinates party strategy, fundraising, the national platform and the Democratic National Convention held every four years to nominate the party's presidential candidate.

Role and structure

The DNC is composed of state party chairs and vice chairs, elected members from each state and territory, and representatives of Democratic officeholders and constituency groups — over 400 members in total. It is led by a chair elected by the membership; daily operations are run from its headquarters in Washington, D.C. Although the DNC organises the presidential nominating process and sets debate and delegate rules, state parties administer primaries and caucuses, and the committee historically coordinates with — but does not control — the party's congressional campaign committees.

History

The committee was created at the 1848 Democratic National Convention, making it the oldest continuing party organisation of its kind. Reforms after the chaotic 1968 convention, through the McGovern–Fraser Commission, shifted delegate selection to primaries and reduced the power of party leaders, while the later creation of superdelegates — curtailed by the Unity Reform Commission after 2016 — partially restored it. In 2016 the committee's internal emails were hacked and leaked, an episode attributed by US intelligence agencies to Russian interference in the presidential election.
