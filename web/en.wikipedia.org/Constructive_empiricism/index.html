Constructive empiricism

Constructive empiricism counsels belief only in what a theory says about observable things.

Constructive empiricism is a philosophical view about science developed by Bas van Fraassen in his book The Scientific Image (1980). It holds that the aim of science is not truth about the world as a whole, but empirical adequacy: a theory is empirically adequate if what it says about observable things and events is true. Accordingly, accepting a scientific theory involves believing only that it is empirically adequate, and remaining agnostic about its claims concerning unobservable entities such as electrons or curved spacetime.

Arguments

Van Fraassen motivated the view partly through the pessimistic meta-induction: since past successful theories have repeatedly turned out false in their claims about unobservables, belief in current theories' unobservable content is risky. He also resisted inference to the best explanation as a rule compelling belief, arguing that it is at most a pragmatic virtue. His semantic, or model-theoretic, account of theories presents a theory as a family of models, with empirical adequacy consisting in the observable phenomena being embeddable in one of those models.

Criticism

Scientific realists object that the distinction between observable and unobservable is vague and shifts with instrumentation, that agnosticism cannot explain the predictive success of science (the "no miracles" argument), and that experimenters in practice treat theoretical entities as real. Van Fraassen replied that the observable/unobservable line is drawn by what human beings can detect unaided, which he treats as a matter of scientific fact about us. Constructive empiricism remains one of the most influential antirealist positions in the philosophy of science.
