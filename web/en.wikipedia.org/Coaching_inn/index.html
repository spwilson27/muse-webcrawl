Coaching inn

A coaching inn is a type of inn that served travellers using horse-drawn coaches, providing stabling for horses, fresh teams for hire, food, drink, and lodging. Coaching inns flourished in Britain and Europe from the seventeenth century until the coming of the railways in the mid-nineteenth century.

History

As road networks improved and scheduled stagecoach and mail coach services developed, inns at regular intervals along the main routes — typically every ten to fifteen miles, the distance a team of horses could comfortably work — became essential stopping points. Horses were changed in a matter of minutes at busy inns, while passengers took refreshments. The largest coaching inns, such as those in London's coaching districts, were substantial establishments with yards surrounded by galleries, stabling for hundreds of horses, and accommodation for passengers, coachmen, ostlers, and guards.

Function

Beyond transport, coaching inns served as booking offices, parcel depots, market-day meeting places, venues for auctions, inquests, and political meetings, and centres of local news. Mail coaches were required to deliver and collect mail at designated inns, making them hubs of communication.

Decline

The expansion of the railway network from the 1830s and 1840s destroyed the long-distance coaching trade with remarkable speed. Many inns closed; others survived as hotels or public houses. Numerous notable examples survive today, often retaining their arched carriage entrances and stable yards.
