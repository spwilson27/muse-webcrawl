Flight instruments

Flight instruments are the instruments in the cockpit of an aircraft that provide the pilot with data about the flight situation of that aircraft, such as altitude, airspeed and direction. They improve safety by allowing the pilot to fly the aircraft in level flight, and make turns, without a reference outside the aircraft such as the horizon. Visual flight rules (VFR) require an airspeed indicator, an altimeter, and a compass or other suitable magnetic direction indicator. Instrument flight rules (IFR) additionally require a gyroscopic pitch-bank (artificial horizon), direction (directional gyro) and rate of turn indicator, plus an adjustable altimeter and a clock.

Pitot-static instruments

The pitot-static system supplies air pressure information to three instruments. The airspeed indicator measures the difference between ram air pressure from the pitot tube and static pressure. The altimeter is an aneroid barometer calibrated to read altitude above a pressure datum. The vertical speed indicator shows the rate of climb or descent by sensing the rate of change of static pressure.

Gyroscopic instruments

The attitude indicator (artificial horizon) shows the aircraft's orientation relative to the horizon, giving pitch and bank at a glance. The heading indicator (directional gyro) shows the aircraft's heading against the compass rose. The turn coordinator and its predecessor, the turn and slip indicator, show the rate and quality of a turn, including whether the aircraft is yawing ("slipping" or "skidding").

Compass systems and glass cockpits

A magnetic compass provides heading information independent of aircraft power but suffers from dip and acceleration errors. Modern aircraft increasingly replace the six analogue dials with electronic flight instrument systems (EFIS) — "glass cockpits" — in which primary flight displays present the same information digitally, often with integrated navigation and terrain data.
