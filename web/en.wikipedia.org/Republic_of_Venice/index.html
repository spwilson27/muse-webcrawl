The Republic of Venice, commonly called La Serenissima ("the Most Serene"), was a sovereign state and maritime republic centred on the lagoon city of Venice in northeastern Italy. Founded, by tradition, in 697, it lasted for over a thousand years until abolished by Napoleon Bonaparte in 1797. For centuries Venice dominated trade between Europe and the Levant, built an overseas empire in the Adriatic and eastern Mediterranean, and was famed for its stable oligarchic constitution.

History

Venice grew from settlements of refugees fleeing Germanic and Hun invasions into a Byzantine dependency that gradually asserted independence. Its position between the Frankish, Byzantine and Islamic worlds made it an entrepôt for spices, silk, grain and slaves. Venetian crusading fleets gained trading privileges across the eastern Mediterranean; in 1204 Venice diverted the Fourth Crusade to sack Constantinople and took a share of the Byzantine Empire, including Crete and numerous ports — the basis of the Stato da Màr.

Rivalry with Genoa produced a series of wars, ending with Venice ascendant after the War of Chioggia (1380). In the 15th century Venice also built a mainland empire, the Terraferma, reaching toward Milan. The Portuguese discovery of the sea route to India (1498) and the rise of Ottoman power undermined Venetian commerce; Cyprus fell to the Ottomans in 1571 (the year of the Venetian-led victory at Lepanto) and Crete in 1669. The republic declined into a wealthy but conservative state until Napoleon ended it in 1797, ceding Venice to Austria by the Treaty of Campo-Formio.

Government

The republic was headed by the Doge, elected for life by an elaborate multi-stage process designed to frustrate faction. Sovereignty formally lay with the Great Council of hereditary patricians, closed as a caste in the Serrata of 1297. Power was balanced among the Great Council, the Senate, the Council of Ten (responsible for state security) and other magistracies — a mixed constitution praised by political theorists as a model of stability.

Economy and culture

Venice pioneered finance and commerce: the gold ducat (minted from 1284) became a standard currency, and Venetian merchants developed double-entry bookkeeping and state-convoyed trade (mude). The Arsenal was Europe's largest industrial complex, mass-producing galleys. The city flourished as a centre of printing, glassmaking (Murano), music and painting — home to Bellini, Titian, Tintoretto and Veronese.
