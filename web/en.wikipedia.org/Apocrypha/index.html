Apocrypha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Apocrypha from a 17th-century Bible

The word apocrypha comes from the Koine Greek τὰ ἀπόκρυφα, meaning "things that are hidden", via Medieval Latin. It is a term for writings of uncertain or disputed authorship and authority, especially relating to the Bible.

In the context of the Bible, the term Apocrypha most often denotes the books found in the Septuagint and in Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments but not in the Hebrew Bible or most Protestant canons — works such as Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, together with additions to Esther and Daniel. Catholics call these books deuterocanonical; many Protestants class them as apocryphal while still historically regarding them as edifying reading.

Usage and canon

Jerome, translator of the Latin Vulgate, distinguished the apocrypha from the canon, though the books were copied in the Vulgate and were affirmed by the Council of Trent (1546) as canonical for Catholics. The Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles say the Church reads the apocryphal books "for example of life and instruction of manners" but does not apply them to doctrine. The original 1611 King James Version included the Apocrypha as a separate section, as did many later editions until the 19th century.

The term is also used more broadly: New Testament apocrypha are early Christian writings not included in the New Testament — gospels, acts, epistles and apocalypses attributed to apostles, such as the Gospel of Thomas and the Gospel of Peter. In secular usage, "apocryphal" describes any story of doubtful authenticity, such as an apocryphal anecdote about a famous person.
