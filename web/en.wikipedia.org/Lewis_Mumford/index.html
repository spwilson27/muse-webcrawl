Lewis Mumford (October 19, 1895 – January 26, 1990) was an American historian, sociologist, philosopher of technology, and literary critic. He was particularly noted for his study of cities and urban architecture, and he had a broad career as a writer, including a period as an influential literary critic.

Life and career

Mumford was born in Flushing, Queens, New York City. He graduated from Stuyvesant High School in 1912 and studied at the City College of New York and the New School for Social Research, though illness prevented him from completing a degree. He worked as an editor for The Dial, a modernist literary journal, and later wrote for The New Yorker as its architecture critic for over thirty years.

Major works

His first book, The Story of Utopias (1922), examined utopian literature and thought. Technics and Civilization (1934) traced the development of technology and its effects on society, introducing influential concepts such as the "megamachine." His best-known work, The City in History (1961), won the National Book Award and argued that the city is humanity's greatest invention, while warning against the sprawling, formless metropolis.

Ideas

Mumford distinguished between "authoritarian" and "democratic" technics, and advocated regional planning, garden cities, and limits to growth. He was a vocal critic of nuclear weapons, suburban sprawl, and the automobile-dominated city. His two-volume The Myth of the Machine (1967, 1970) developed his critique of large-scale technological systems.

Legacy

Mumford received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1964 and was made an honorary Knight Commander of the Order of the British Empire. His writings influenced generations of urban planners, architects, and environmental thinkers.
