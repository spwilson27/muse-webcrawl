First transcontinental railroad

The First Transcontinental Railroad (originally the "Pacific Railroad") was a 1,911-mile (3,075 km) continuous railroad line built between 1863 and 1869 that connected the existing eastern U.S. rail network at Council Bluffs, Iowa (via Omaha, Nebraska) with the Pacific coast at the Oakland Long Wharf in San Francisco Bay. Its completion revolutionized the settlement and economy of the American West.

Construction

Authorized by the Pacific Railroad Act of 1862, the line was built by three companies: the Central Pacific Railroad, building eastward from Sacramento through the Sierra Nevada with a workforce composed largely of Chinese immigrants; the Union Pacific Railroad, building westward from Omaha with Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans; and the Western Pacific Railroad, which completed the final segment to the Bay. The companies received government bonds and land grants for each mile completed, producing a famous race to lay the most track.

Completion

The rails were joined at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, on 10 May 1869, when Leland Stanford drove the ceremonial golden spike with a silver hammer. coast-to-coast travel time fell from months to about one week, and freight costs dropped dramatically.

Impact

The railroad accelerated migration to the West, integrated western resources — timber, minerals, cattle and agriculture — into national markets, and hastened the displacement of Native American peoples and the near-extermination of the bison. It was the first of several transcontinental lines in the United States and Canada.
