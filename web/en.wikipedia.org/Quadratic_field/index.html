Quadratic field

In algebraic number theory, a quadratic field is an algebraic number field of degree two over the rational numbers Q. Every quadratic field is of the form Q(√d), where d is a uniquely determined square-free integer different from 0 and 1. If d > 0, the field is called a real quadratic field; if d < 0, an imaginary quadratic field or complex quadratic field. Quadratic fields have been studied in great depth, initially as part of the theory of binary quadratic forms.

Ring of integers and discriminant

The ring of integers of Q(√d) depends on d mod 4: it is Z[√d] when d ≡ 2 or 3 (mod 4), and Z[(1 + √d)/2] when d ≡ 1 (mod 4). The discriminant of the field is d in the latter case and 4d otherwise. Unlike the rational integers, the ring of integers of a quadratic field need not have unique factorisation; the extent of the failure is measured by the class number. Only nine imaginary quadratic fields have class number one (the Heegner numbers), a result connected with the Stark–Heegner theorem.

Units and class groups

Real quadratic fields have an infinite group of units, generated (up to sign) by a single fundamental unit, which is related to solutions of Pell's equation. The class numbers of quadratic fields feature in Gauss's class number problem and in the Cohen–Lenstra heuristics. Quadratic fields are the simplest non-trivial number fields and serve as the testing ground for much of algebraic number theory, including the theory of quadratic reciprocity.
