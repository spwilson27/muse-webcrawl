Driving forcesDifferences in water density from temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline)

Thermohaline circulation (THC) is a part of the large-scale ocean circulation that is driven by global density gradients created by surface heat and freshwater fluxes. The adjective thermohaline derives from thermo-, referring to temperature, and -haline, referring to salt content — both factors that determine the density of seawater.

Mechanism

In the far North Atlantic and around Antarctica, cold, salty surface water becomes dense enough to sink to the ocean floor, forming deep water masses such as North Atlantic Deep Water and Antarctic Bottom Water. These deep waters creep slowly through the ocean basins and eventually rise back to the surface, principally in the Southern Ocean and the North Pacific. Surface currents such as the Gulf Stream complete the loop, carrying warm water poleward. The whole circuit is sometimes called the global ocean conveyor belt.

Climate role

The circulation redistributes heat, carbon and nutrients around the planet and helps moderate regional climates, including the relatively mild winters of northwestern Europe. Because it depends on salinity, heavy freshwater input from melting ice could weaken the Atlantic overturning; past abrupt climate changes, such as the Younger Dryas cooling, have been linked to disruptions of this circulation.
