Quill

A quill pen and inkwell

A quill is a writing tool made from a moulted flight feather of a large bird, most often a goose. Quills were the principal writing instrument in the Western world from roughly the 6th to the 19th century, used to write the great majority of surviving medieval and early modern manuscripts before being displaced by metal dip pens and later the fountain pen.

Manufacture

The best quills came from the primary flight feathers of the left wing of geese, swans, and later turkeys. After cleaning, the feather shaft was hardened by plunging it into hot sand, a process called "dutching", and the tip was cut to a nib with a pen knife, shaped according to the writer's hand and the script required. A quill had to be re-sharpened frequently; a heavily used quill might last only a week.

History

Quills replaced the earlier reed pen in late antiquity because their hollow shaft held a small reservoir of ink and their flexible nib allowed variation in line width, enabling cursive scripts. The United States Declaration of Independence and Constitution were written with quills, and goose quills remained in legal and ceremonial use into the 1800s.

Quills as pennae

The stiff central shaft of a feather is also termed a quill, giving its name to the hollow spines of porcupines and hedgehogs, which are modified hairs composed of keratin.
