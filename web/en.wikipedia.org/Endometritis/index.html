Endometritis is inflammation of the endometrium, the inner lining of the uterus, generally due to infection. It is usually divided into pregnancy-related (obstetric) and non-pregnancy-related forms, and into acute and chronic types. Symptoms may include fever, lower abdominal or pelvic pain, abnormal uterine bleeding, and foul-smelling or abnormal discharge. It should not be confused with endometriosis, a different condition in which endometrial-like tissue grows outside the uterus.

Causes and risk factors

Endometritis is typically polymicrobial, involving organisms of the normal vaginal flora ascending into the uterus, and in some cases sexually transmitted bacteria such as Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae. The most common setting is the postpartum period, particularly after caesarean section, prolonged labour, or prolonged rupture of membranes. Non-obstetric risk factors include pelvic inflammatory disease, intrauterine devices, and uterine instrumentation such as curettage or hysteroscopy.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on fever and uterine tenderness, supported by laboratory findings such as raised white cell count and cultures. Chronic endometritis — associated with infertility and recurrent implantation failure — is diagnosed definitively by endometrial biopsy showing plasma cells (often confirmed by CD138 immunohistochemistry), and hysteroscopy may reveal micropolyps and stromal oedema.

Treatment

Treatment is with antibiotics. Postpartum endometritis is commonly treated with intravenous clindamycin plus gentamicin until the patient is afebrile; chronic endometritis is typically treated with oral doxycycline, sometimes combined with metronidazole and a fluoroquinolone. Prompt treatment reduces the risk of spread to pelvic peritonitis, abscess formation and sepsis.
