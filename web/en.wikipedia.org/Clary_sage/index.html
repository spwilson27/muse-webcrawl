Clary sage

Binomial name: Salvia sclarea L.
Family: Lamiaceae
Native range: northern Mediterranean to central Asia

Clary sage (Salvia sclarea) is a biennial or short-lived perennial herb in the mint family, native to the northern Mediterranean Basin and parts of North Africa and Central Asia. In its first year it forms a rosette of large, wrinkled, hairy leaves; in the second it sends up flowering stems up to about 1.2 metres tall bearing pale lilac to mauve flowers with showy pinkish bracts.

Uses

The plant is grown commercially chiefly for its essential oil, distilled from the flowering tops and leaves. Clary sage oil contains linalyl acetate and linalool and is widely used in perfumery as a fixative and fragrance note. It is also the principal natural source of sclareolide, a starting material for producing ambrox, a synthetic substitute for ambergris. Historically the mucilaginous seeds were used to clear foreign objects from the eye—hence the name "clary" (from Latin clarus, clear, via "clear-eye")—and the leaves were used to flavour wines and vermouths in Germany and England.

Cultivation

Clary sage prefers full sun and well-drained, even poor soils, tolerating drought once established. It self-seeds readily and is grown as an ornamental for its tall, colourful bracts, which attract bees and other pollinators.
