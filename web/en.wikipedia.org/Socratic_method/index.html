Socratic method

The Socratic method (also known as the method of Elenchus or Socratic debate) is a form of cooperative argumentative dialogue between individuals, based on asking and answering questions to stimulate critical thinking and to draw out ideas and underlying presuppositions. It is named after the Classical Greek philosopher Socrates and is introduced by him in Plato's Theaetetus as midwifery (maieutics) because it is employed to bring out definitions implicit in the interlocutors' beliefs, or to help them further their understanding.

The Socratic method is a method of hypothesis elimination, in that better hypotheses are found by steadily identifying and eliminating those that lead to contradictions. It searches for general, commonly held truths that shape beliefs and scrutinizes them to determine their consistency with other beliefs.

The elenchus

In Plato's early dialogues, the elenchus is the technique Socrates uses to investigate, for example, the nature or definition of ethical concepts such as justice or virtue. The method typically proceeds as follows: an interlocutor proposes a thesis, which Socrates considers false; through a series of questions, Socrates secures agreement to further premises that are shown to contradict the original thesis, forcing the interlocutor to abandon or refine it. The result is often aporia — a state of puzzlement that Socrates regarded as the necessary first step toward wisdom.

Modern usage

The method is widely used in law school teaching, where professors question students about the principles underlying court decisions, and in psychotherapy, education, and philosophy, where guided questioning helps expose assumptions and clarify concepts.
