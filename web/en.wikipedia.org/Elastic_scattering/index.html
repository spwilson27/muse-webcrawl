Elastic scattering

Elastic scattering
FieldParticle, nuclear and atomic physics
Key propertyTotal kinetic energy of the system is conserved
ContrastInelastic scattering
ExamplesRutherford scattering, Thomson scattering, Bragg diffraction

Elastic scattering is a form of particle scattering in which the total kinetic energy of the colliding system is conserved and the internal states of the particles are unchanged; only their directions (and hence momentum vectors) change. Elastic scattering is a fundamental probe of the structure of matter, from Ernest Rutherford's gold-foil experiment to modern collider physics.

Physics

In the centre-of-mass frame, elastic scattering leaves the magnitude of each particle's momentum unchanged and merely redistributes the directions. The angular distribution of scattered particles encodes the interaction potential: the differential cross section is proportional to the squared modulus of the scattering amplitude, which in quantum mechanics is computed via partial-wave analysis (for short-range potentials) or the Born approximation.

Examples

Rutherford scattering of alpha particles by atomic nuclei revealed the nuclear atom in 1911. Thomson scattering is the elastic scattering of low-energy photons by free electrons, while Rayleigh scattering of light by molecules explains the blue of the sky. Diffraction of X-rays, electrons and neutrons by crystals — Bragg diffraction — is elastic scattering used to determine atomic structure, and elastic electron scattering mapped the charge distribution of nuclei and the proton.

Applications

Elastic scattering underlies Rutherford backscattering spectrometry in materials analysis, small-angle scattering methods for soft-matter structure, and measurements of form factors and total cross sections in high-energy physics, where elastic proton collisions are studied with forward detectors.
