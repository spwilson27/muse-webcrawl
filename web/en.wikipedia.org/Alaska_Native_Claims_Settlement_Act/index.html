Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act

Enacted: 18 December 1971

Signed by: President Richard Nixon

Land conveyed: approx. 44 million acres (18 million ha)

Payment: $962.5 million

Acronym: ANCSA

The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) is a United States federal law signed on 18 December 1971 that settled aboriginal land claims in Alaska. It was the largest indigenous land claims settlement in American history, extinguishing Alaska Native claims to most of the state in exchange for nearly 44 million acres of land and $962.5 million.

Background

Native land claims became urgent after Alaska statehood in 1959, as the state selected federal lands under its statehood act, and after the discovery of oil at Prudhoe Bay in 1968 made a clear title essential for building the Trans-Alaska Pipeline. The Alaska Federation of Natives, founded in 1966, led the lobbying for a settlement.

Terms

Rather than creating reservations or a trust system like that in the contiguous United States, ANCSA conveyed land and money to for-profit corporations owned by Alaska Native shareholders. Twelve (later thirteen) Alaska Native regional corporations and more than 200 village corporations were created. Alaska Natives born on or before 18 December 1971 were enrolled as shareholders. Major regional corporations include Arctic Slope Native Association affiliates, Sealaska, Cook Inlet Region, Inc., Doyon, Limited, and NANA Regional Corporation.

Effects and later developments

ANCSA transformed Alaska Native economic and political life, making Native corporations major landowners and employers, though outcomes varied widely. Amendments in 1987 (the "1991 amendments") addressed the impending alienability of shares. A separate provision, section 17(d)(2), set aside lands that led to the vast Alaska conservation units established by the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980.
