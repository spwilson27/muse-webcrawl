Immune tolerance, also known as immunological tolerance or immunotolerance, refers to the immune system's state of unresponsiveness to substances or tissue that would otherwise have the capacity to elicit an immune response. It is induced by prior exposure to that specific antigen and contrasts the immune system's conventional response, in which it attacks antigens.

Central and peripheral tolerance

Central tolerance is established during lymphocyte development in the thymus and bone marrow, where strongly self-reactive cells are deleted. Peripheral tolerance operates in the tissues through mechanisms including anergy, suppression by regulatory T cells, and deletion of mature self-reactive lymphocytes.

Clinical significance

Failure of tolerance underlies autoimmune diseases and allergy, whereas inducing tolerance is a goal in transplantation medicine and in treating autoimmune disorders.
