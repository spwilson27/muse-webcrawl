Graphics processing unit

A discrete graphics card built around a GPU

A graphics processing unit (GPU) is a specialized electronic circuit designed to accelerate computer graphics and image processing. GPUs are found in embedded systems, mobile phones, personal computers, workstations, and game consoles, either as discrete cards, as integrated graphics, or as part of a system on a chip.

Architecture

Unlike a CPU, which devotes most of its transistors to caches and control logic for a few powerful cores, a GPU devotes most of its area to thousands of simpler arithmetic units organized for data-parallel throughput. Modern GPUs execute programs (shaders and compute kernels) across thousands of threads simultaneously, with high-bandwidth memory systems feeding them. This design makes GPUs extremely fast at the matrix and vector operations that underpin both 3D rendering and machine learning.

History

Graphics accelerators appeared in the 1970s and 1980s as display controllers and 2D accelerators. The term "GPU" was popularized by Nvidia at the 1999 launch of the GeForce 256, the first card marketed with hardware transform and lighting. Programmable shaders arrived in 2001, and general-purpose computing on GPUs (GPGPU) took off with CUDA in 2007 and OpenCL in 2009.

Applications

Beyond rendering, GPUs now dominate the training and inference of deep neural networks, scientific simulation, cryptocurrency mining, and video encoding. Data-center accelerators from Nvidia, AMD, and others form the hardware backbone of modern artificial intelligence, driving enormous investment in GPU manufacturing and supply chains.
