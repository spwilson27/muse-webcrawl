Mourning

Mourning is the expression of an experience that is the consequence of an event in life involving loss, grief, or sorrow, causing the person to not be in their usual state of mind. More specifically it is the outward expression of grief, often in compliance with social and cultural customs, whereas grief itself is the internal emotional response to loss.

Customs

Mourning practices vary widely across cultures and historical periods. In many Western societies, mourners traditionally wear black clothing; in parts of Asia, white is the colour of mourning. In the Victorian era, codified stages of mourning prescribed dress, jewellery (often of jet), and social withdrawal for widows for periods of up to two years. Jewish tradition prescribes shiva, seven days of mourning, followed by longer periods of reduced observance. In Islam, the mourning period for a widow (iddah) is four months and ten days. Hindus observe mourning periods involving ritual impurity and ceremonies such as shraddha.

States may declare periods of national mourning after the death of a head of state or a mass tragedy, marked by flags at half-mast, closed entertainment venues, and official ceremonies.

Psychology

The psychology of mourning overlaps with that of bereavement. While older models described fixed stages of grief, contemporary research treats mourning as a more individual process, shaped by the mourner's relationship to the deceased, cultural expectations, and the social acknowledgement of the loss — including "disenfranchised grief" for losses that are not socially sanctioned.
