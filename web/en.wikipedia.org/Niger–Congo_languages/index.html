Niger–Congo languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Niger–Congo languages form one of the world's major language families and Africa's largest in terms of geographical area, number of speakers and number of distinct languages, with an estimated 1,500 or more languages. The family is named after the Niger and Congo river basins, across which most of its languages are spread. A characteristic feature of many Niger–Congo languages is an elaborate system of noun classes, marked by prefixes on nouns and agreement throughout the sentence.

Major branches

The largest and best-known branch is the Bantu group, whose roughly 500 languages — including Swahili, Zulu, Shona, Yoruba-adjacent Benue–Congo relatives and Kikuyu — dominate central, eastern and southern Africa following the Bantu expansion of the last several thousand years. Other major branches include Atlantic languages of the West African coast (such as Wolof and Fula), Mande languages (such as Bambara and Mandinka), Gur languages, Kwa languages (such as Akan and Ewe), Kru languages, and the large Benue–Congo grouping that includes Yoruba, Igbo and the Bantu languages. The family's exact internal classification and even its unity remain debated, particularly regarding the Mande, Dogon, Ijoid and Kordofanian groups.

Characteristics

Most Niger–Congo languages are tonal, and many have vowel harmony systems, typically based on the ATR (advanced tongue root) distinction. Noun class systems pair singular and plural classes; in Bantu languages these classes can number ten to twenty and govern concord on verbs, adjectives and pronouns. Word order is predominantly subject–verb–object.

History of classification

The family was largely established by Joseph Greenberg's classification of African languages in the mid-twentieth century, which grouped Bantu with the languages of West Africa into a single phylum. Subsequent scholarship has refined the branches, and Niger–Congo is often considered within a still larger proposed Niger–Kordofanian grouping.
