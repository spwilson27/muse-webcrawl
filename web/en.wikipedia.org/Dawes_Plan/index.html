Dawes Plan

The Dawes Plan (1924) was an arrangement negotiated by an international committee headed by American banker Charles G. Dawes to ease the crisis over the reparations imposed on Germany after the First World War. It rescheduled Germany's payments, reorganized its finances under foreign supervision, and triggered a large flow of American loans into the German economy.

Background

Germany's failure to meet its reparations schedule led France and Belgium to occupy the Ruhr in 1923. Passive resistance, a collapse of the German currency, and hyperinflation followed. In late 1923 the Allied Reparations Commission appointed a committee of experts under Dawes to devise a workable payments scheme.

Terms

The plan, adopted at the London Conference of August 1924, set a rising scale of annual payments — beginning at one billion gold marks and reaching 2.5 billion by 1928–29 — without fixing a final total. The Reichsbank was reorganized as an independent central bank with foreign oversight, the German railway system was placed under a partly foreign administration as security, and an Agent-General for Reparations supervised transfers. An initial international loan, largely raised in the United States, restarted payments.

Effects and successor

The plan stabilized Germany's economy and politics in the mid-1920s and Dawes shared the 1925 Nobel Peace Prize. But it depended on continuous American lending. In 1929 it was replaced by the Young Plan, which fixed a final sum; the Wall Street Crash soon ended reparations transfers altogether.
