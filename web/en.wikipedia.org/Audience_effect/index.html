Audience effect

The audience effect is a phenomenon in psychology and ethology in which an individual's behaviour changes in the presence of a passive spectator audience. It is closely related to the concept of social facilitation, one of the oldest topics in social psychology, first systematically studied by Norman Triplett in 1898, who found that cyclists performed faster when racing against others than when racing alone against a clock.

Mechanisms

Several explanations have been proposed. Drive theory, advanced by Robert Zajonc in 1965, holds that the mere presence of others increases physiological arousal, which strengthens dominant (well-learned) responses — improving performance on simple or practised tasks while impairing performance on novel or complex ones. Evaluation apprehension theory suggests that it is not mere presence but the concern about being judged that drives the effect, while distraction–conflict theory emphasises the attentional conflict between the task and the audience.

In animals

Audience effects have also been documented in non-human animals: chimpanzees, for example, modulate their alarm calls depending on who is listening, and male Siamese fighting fish display more aggressively when a conspecific audience is present. Such findings suggest the effect has deep evolutionary roots in social signalling and reputation management.

Related concepts

The reverse phenomenon — performing worse on simple tasks or better on complex tasks when alone — is sometimes termed social inhibition. Related ideas include the Hawthorne effect, in which behaviour changes because subjects know they are being observed for research purposes.
