Acid dissociation constant

An acid dissociation constant, Ka, (also known as acidity constant, or acid-ionization constant) is a quantitative measure of the strength of an acid in solution. It is the equilibrium constant for the reaction HA ⇌ A⁻ + H⁺, defined as Ka = [A⁻][H⁺] / [HA]. Because the values span many orders of magnitude, they are usually reported as pKa = −log₁₀ Ka; a lower pKa means a stronger acid.

Interpretation

A large Ka value indicates that the equilibrium strongly favors dissociation: the acid readily donates its proton. Strong acids such as hydrochloric acid are essentially fully dissociated in water, while weak acids such as acetic acid (pKa ≈ 4.76) dissociate only partially. The pKa also determines the pH at which a buffer made from the acid and its conjugate base resists pH change most effectively, via the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.

Polyprotic acids

Acids with more than one ionizable proton have successive constants Ka1, Ka2, and so on. Carbonic acid, for example, has pKa1 ≈ 6.35 and pKa2 ≈ 10.33; phosphoric acid has three stepwise values of about 2.15, 7.20 and 12.35.

Factors affecting pKa

Acid strength depends on the stability of the conjugate base, influenced by electronegativity, inductive effects, resonance and solvation. Temperature and ionic strength shift measured values, so tabulated pKa values specify conditions, typically 25 °C in water. In other solvents such as DMSO, pKa scales differ markedly and are important in organic synthesis.
