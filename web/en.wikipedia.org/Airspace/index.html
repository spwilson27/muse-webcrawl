Airspace

Airspace
Governed byICAO standards; national aviation authorities
Classes (ICAO)A, B, C, D, E (controlled); F, G (uncontrolled)
Key conceptFlight information region (FIR)

Airspace is the portion of the atmosphere controlled by a country above its territory, including its territorial waters, and the system by which that volume of sky is classified and managed for aviation. Under the 1944 Chicago Convention, every state has complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory, and the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) sets the standards by which airspace is structured worldwide.

Classification

ICAO defines airspace classes A through G. Classes A–E are controlled airspace, where air traffic control (ATC) provides separation services; classes F and G are uncontrolled. In broad terms: Class A is high-altitude en-route airspace in which only instrument flight rules (IFR) traffic is permitted; Class B surrounds the busiest airports; Classes C and D cover progressively smaller terminal areas; Class E fills most remaining controlled airspace; and Class G, uncontrolled airspace, typically extends from the surface up to the base of Class E, where both IFR and visual flight rules (VFR) traffic may operate without an ATC clearance.

Structures and services

The world is divided into flight information regions (FIRs), each assigned to an authority providing flight information and alerting services. Within FIRs, airspace is further organised into control areas, control zones around aerodromes, airways (corridors typically 10 nautical miles wide), and terminal manoeuvring areas. Special-use airspace includes prohibited, restricted and danger areas set aside for military activity, hazards or security. Aircraft transiting between regions follow published routes and reporting points, increasingly defined by satellite-based area navigation (RNAV) rather than ground beacons.

Legal and strategic issues

States may close their airspace in emergencies or conflicts, and air defence identification zones (ADIZ) require aircraft approaching national airspace to identify themselves. Airspace over international waters is managed by delegated FIRs but remains international in law. The vertical extent of sovereign airspace is not precisely fixed in treaty law; by convention it ends where outer space begins, often associated with the Kármán line at about 100 km altitude. Growing traffic from drones and commercial spaceflight is prompting new frameworks such as U-space in Europe and UTM in the United States.
