Ecological succession

Ecological succession is the process by which the species structure of an ecological community changes over time. After a disturbance, pioneer species colonise an area and progressively modify conditions, allowing other species to establish, until — in classical theory — a relatively stable climax community develops.

Primary and secondary succession

Primary succession begins on surfaces with no soil, such as new lava flows or land exposed by retreating glaciers; lichens and mosses weather the rock and build organic matter. Secondary succession follows a disturbance that leaves soil intact — fire, flood or abandonment of farmland — and is much faster, as at Mount St. Helens after 1980.

Mechanisms

Succession proceeds through facilitation (early species make conditions better for later ones), tolerance (later species outcompete pioneers regardless) and inhibition (established species resist replacement). Seral stages typically progress from annual herbs to shrubs to fast-growing trees to shade-tolerant hardwoods in temperate forests.

Modern views

Gleason's individualistic concept challenged Clements's idea of the community as a super-organism; modern ecology emphasises multiple possible pathways and stable states, disturbance regimes, and the role of succession in conservation and restoration.
