Dyslexia

Dyslexia
FieldNeuropsychology, pediatrics, education
SymptomsDifficulty reading, decoding, and spelling despite normal intelligence
OnsetEarly childhood; lifelong
PrevalenceEstimated 5–10% (up to 17% by some criteria)

Dyslexia is a learning disability of neurobiological origin characterized by difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition and by poor spelling and decoding abilities, despite adequate instruction, intelligence, and sensory function. It is the most common learning disability and occurs across languages, though its manifestation depends on the writing system: it is more visibly a problem of accuracy in deep orthographies like English and more a problem of fluency in transparent orthographies like Italian or Finnish.

Causes

The most widely supported account is the phonological deficit hypothesis: people with dyslexia have difficulty representing and manipulating the speech sounds (phonemes) of their language, which impairs mapping letters to sounds. Dyslexia is highly heritable, runs in families, and neuroimaging shows atypical activation in left-hemisphere reading networks, including the temporoparietal and occipitotemporal ("visual word form") regions. Several candidate genes, including DCDC2, KIAA0319, and ROBO1, have been associated with it.

Diagnosis and support

Diagnosis involves assessment of reading, spelling, phonological awareness, and rapid naming, ruling out inadequate instruction or sensory problems. Evidence-based intervention focuses on structured, explicit phonics-based instruction, often delivered early; accommodations such as extra time and text-to-speech technology help older students and adults. Dyslexia is not related to intelligence, and many people with dyslexia succeed in demanding fields, though unsupported it is associated with lower educational attainment and self-esteem.
