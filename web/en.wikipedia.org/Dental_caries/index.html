Dental caries

Dental caries, also known as tooth decay or cavities, is the breakdown of teeth due to acids produced by bacteria. The cavities may be a number of different colours, from yellow to black. Symptoms may include pain and difficulty with eating. Complications may include inflammation of the tissue around the tooth, tooth loss, and infection or abscess formation.

Advanced caries destroying the crown of a molar

Cause

Caries result from the interaction of bacteria in dental plaque — especially Streptococcus mutans — with fermentable sugars in food. The bacteria produce lactic acid, which demineralizes enamel and dentine. Saliva normally counteracts this by remineralizing early lesions, so decay reflects a balance between attack and repair; frequent sugar intake, dry mouth, and poor oral hygiene tip the balance toward destruction.

Signs and progression

Early caries appear as chalky white spots and may be reversible. Once a cavity forms in enamel it advances into the softer dentine and can reach the pulp, causing pulpitis, pain, and eventually necrosis and abscess. Untreated caries is the most common chronic disease of childhood worldwide, and affects billions of people.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention centres on fluoride (in toothpaste, water, or varnish), reducing sugar frequency, brushing and flossing, and fissure sealants. Treatment involves removing decayed tissue and restoring the tooth with fillings, inlays, or crowns; teeth with infected pulps need root canal therapy or extraction.
