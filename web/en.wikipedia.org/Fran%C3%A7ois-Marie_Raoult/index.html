François-Marie Raoult (1830–1901) was a French chemist best known for formulating Raoult's law, which describes the vapour pressure of ideal solutions, and for his pioneering work on the colligative properties of solutions.

Career

Raoult studied at the University of Paris and worked first as a schoolteacher before obtaining academic posts. From 1870 he held the chair of chemistry at the University of Grenoble, where he spent the rest of his career conducting meticulous measurements of freezing points and vapour pressures of solutions.

Scientific contributions

In the 1880s Raoult showed that the depression of the freezing point of a solvent is proportional to the concentration of dissolved substance, providing a practical method (cryoscopy) for determining molecular weights. His law of vapour pressure — that the partial vapour pressure of each component of an ideal mixture equals the vapour pressure of the pure component multiplied by its mole fraction — became a cornerstone of solution thermodynamics and helped lay the groundwork for the physical chemistry of van 't Hoff and Arrhenius.

Honours

Raoult was elected a corresponding member of the French Academy of Sciences and received honours from scientific societies across Europe, including the Davy Medal of the Royal Society in 1892.
