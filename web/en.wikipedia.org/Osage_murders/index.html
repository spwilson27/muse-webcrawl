Osage murders

Date1918–1931 (peak 1921–1926)
LocationOsage County, Oklahoma, United States
VictimsMembers of the Osage Nation; at least 60, likely many more

The Osage Indian murders were a series of murders of Osage Native Americans in Osage County, Oklahoma, during the 1910s–1930s, often called the "Reign of Terror". Members of the Osage Nation had become extraordinarily wealthy from oil discovered beneath their reservation land, and they were systematically targeted by killers seeking to inherit their headrights — shares of the tribe's mineral income.

Background

Under the Osage Allotment Act of 1906, oil revenues were distributed to tribal members through headrights, which could only be inherited, not sold. Many Osage were assigned white "guardians" who controlled their finances. As oil money flooded in, Osage people began dying in suspicious circumstances — shootings, poisonings, bombings, and staged accidents.

Investigation

Local law enforcement failed to solve the crimes, and the Bureau of Investigation (the predecessor of the FBI), under J. Edgar Hoover, took the case. Undercover agents, led on the ground by former Texas Ranger Tom White, implicated cattle baron William King Hale, who had arranged murders including those of Anna Brown and of the family of Mollie Burkhart — Hale's own niece by marriage — through killers such as Ernest Burkhart, Mollie's husband, and John Ramsey.

Trials and legacy

Hale and Ramsey were convicted in 1926 and Burkhart pleaded guilty, receiving life sentences. The case was an early, reputation-making success for the young FBI. Congress responded by restricting headright inheritance to those of at least half Osage ancestry (later amended). The murders were popularised for modern audiences by David Grann's 2017 book Killers of the Flower Moon and Martin Scorsese's 2023 film adaptation.
