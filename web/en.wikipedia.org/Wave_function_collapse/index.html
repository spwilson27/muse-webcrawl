Wave function collapse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In quantum mechanics, wave function collapse, also called reduction of the state vector, occurs when a wave function — initially in a superposition of several eigenstates — reduces to a single eigenstate due to interaction with the external world. This interaction is called an observation, and is the essence of a measurement in quantum mechanics, which connects the wave function with classical concepts such as position and momentum.

Mathematical description

Before collapse, the state of a quantum system is described by a wave function that is a linear combination of the eigenstates of an observable. When a measurement is performed, the probability of obtaining a particular eigenvalue is given by the Born rule, proportional to the squared modulus of the corresponding coefficient. After the measurement, the wave function is said to have collapsed into the eigenstate associated with the measured value, a discontinuous and non-unitary change that stands in contrast to the smooth, deterministic evolution described by the Schrödinger equation.

History and interpretations

The idea of collapse was formalised by John von Neumann in his 1932 treatise Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics. In the Copenhagen interpretation, collapse is a real physical process triggered by measurement. Other interpretations treat it differently: the many-worlds interpretation denies that collapse ever occurs, holding instead that all outcomes are realised in branching universes, while objective collapse theories such as the GRW model modify the Schrödinger equation so that collapses happen spontaneously. The related phenomenon of quantum decoherence explains the appearance of collapse through entanglement with the environment, though it does not by itself select a single outcome.

The measurement problem

Collapse is central to the measurement problem: quantum theory does not specify precisely what constitutes a measurement, where the boundary between quantum system and classical apparatus lies, or why a definite outcome occurs at all. Thought experiments such as Schrödinger's cat and Wigner's friend dramatise the tension. Despite a century of debate, collapse remains one of the most discussed conceptual features of quantum theory.
