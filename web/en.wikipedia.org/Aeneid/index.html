The Aeneid is a Latin epic poem by Virgil, written between 29 and 19 BC, that tells the legendary story of Aeneas, a Trojan who fled the fall of Troy and travelled to Italy, where he became the ancestor of the Romans. It is widely regarded as the national epic of ancient Rome and one of the greatest works of Latin literature.

Summary

The first six books, modelled on the Odyssey, recount Aeneas's wanderings: his escape from Troy, his affair with Dido, queen of Carthage — whom he abandons to fulfil his destiny, causing her suicide — and his descent into the underworld, where his father Anchises reveals the future glory of Rome. The last six books, modelled on the Iliad, describe the war in Latium against Turnus and the Rutuli, ending with Aeneas killing Turnus in single combat.

Composition and themes

Virgil wrote the poem under the patronage of the emperor Augustus, and it links Rome's ruling family to the gods and to the heroic past. Its opening words, Arma virumque cano ("I sing of arms and the man"), announce its twin halves of war and journey. Central themes include fate, duty (pietas), the cost of empire, and the tension between personal desire and historical destiny. Virgil died in 19 BC leaving the poem unfinished; according to tradition he asked for it to be burned, but Augustus ordered its publication.

Influence

The Aeneid was a school text throughout the Roman Empire and the Middle Ages, profoundly influencing Dante (who made Virgil his guide in the Inferno), Milton, and Western epic poetry generally.
