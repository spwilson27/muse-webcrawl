Proof of work

Proof of work (PoW) is a form of cryptographic proof in which one party (the prover) proves to others (the verifiers) that a certain amount of a specified computational effort has been expended. Verifiers can subsequently confirm this expenditure with minimal effort on their part. The concept was first proposed in 1993 by Cynthia Dwork and Moni Naor as a method to deter denial-of-service attacks and spam: a service requester would have to perform a modest computation before being served, making abuse expensive while keeping legitimate use cheap.

Hashcash and Bitcoin

Adam Back's Hashcash (1997) implemented the idea for email: senders computed a partial hash collision — a nonce making the SHA-1 hash of a header begin with a required number of zero bits. Satoshi Nakamoto adapted Hashcash-style proof of work as the consensus mechanism of Bitcoin (2009). Miners compete to find a nonce such that the double-SHA-256 hash of a candidate block header falls below a target; difficulty adjusts every 2,016 blocks so that blocks arrive about every ten minutes. The longest (heaviest) chain of such blocks is accepted as the ledger, making history revision computationally prohibitive and solving the double-spending problem without a central authority. Miners are rewarded with new bitcoin and transaction fees.

Properties and variants

A proof-of-work function must be moderately hard to compute, trivial to verify, and non-amortisable in parallelisable ways where fairness is desired. Variants include memory-hard functions such as scrypt (used by Litecoin) and Ethash, designed to resist specialised ASIC hardware, and "useful" proofs of work that redirect computation to scientific problems — none has displaced hash-based puzzles in major networks.

Criticism

Proof of work consumes enormous electricity — Bitcoin mining has been estimated to use energy comparable to a medium-sized country — drawing environmental criticism and policy responses, including China's 2021 mining ban. This energy cost, and scalability limits, motivated alternatives such as proof of stake; Ethereum, the second-largest blockchain, abandoned proof of work for proof of stake in "the Merge" of September 2022. Defenders argue the expenditure is the source of Bitcoin's security and censorship resistance, and that mining can monetise stranded or renewable energy.
