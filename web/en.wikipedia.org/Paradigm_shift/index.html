Paradigm shift

A paradigm shift is a fundamental change in the basic concepts and experimental practices of a scientific discipline. The term was introduced by the American philosopher of science Thomas S. Kuhn in his landmark 1962 book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, and it has since passed into general use for any radical transformation of outlook.

In Kuhn's account, science normally proceeds as "normal science": researchers work within a shared paradigm, solving puzzles it defines. Anomalies — results the paradigm cannot explain — accumulate until the field enters a crisis. A scientific revolution follows, in which a rival paradigm replaces the old one. Classic examples include the Copernican displacement of geocentric astronomy, the chemical revolution associated with Lavoisier, Darwinian evolution, and the transition from Newtonian mechanics to relativity and quantum mechanics.

Incommensurability

Kuhn argued that competing paradigms are partly "incommensurable": their proponents use key terms differently, observe the world differently, and may lack neutral standards by which to compare the frameworks. This claim, and the suggestion that paradigm choice is not purely a matter of logic and evidence, provoked lasting controversy, with critics such as Karl Popper and Imre Lakatos defending more rationalist accounts of scientific change.

Beyond science

The phrase is now widely applied to major changes in technology, business, politics, and culture — sometimes so loosely that it has become a cliché — though Kuhn himself restricted the concept largely to the mature natural sciences.
