A blazar is an active galactic nucleus (AGN) with a relativistic jet — composed of ionised matter travelling at nearly the speed of light — directed very nearly towards an observer. Relativistic beaming of electromagnetic radiation from the jet makes blazars appear many times brighter, and far more variable, than they would if the jet were pointed in a direction away from Earth.

Properties

Blazars are among the most luminous and energetic persistent phenomena in the universe. They emit radiation across the entire electromagnetic spectrum, from radio waves to gamma rays, and are characterised by rapid and large-amplitude variability on timescales as short as minutes, high polarisation of their optical emission, and apparently superluminal motion in their parsec-scale jets observed with very-long-baseline interferometry.

Classification

Blazars are divided into two main classes: BL Lacertae objects (BL Lacs), which show weak or absent emission lines in their spectra, and optically violently variable quasars, now generally classed as flat-spectrum radio quasars, which show strong broad emission lines. In the unified model of AGN, blazars are understood to be radio galaxies — typically giant ellipticals hosting supermassive black holes — whose jets happen to be aimed almost directly at Earth.

Emission processes

The spectral energy distribution of a blazar shows two broad humps: a low-energy component attributed to synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons in the jet, and a high-energy component produced by inverse Compton scattering and, in some sources, possibly by hadronic processes. Blazars such as Markarian 421 and Markarian 501 are among the brightest known sources of TeV gamma rays. In 2018 the blazar TXS 0506+056 was identified as the first likely source of a high-energy astrophysical neutrino, opening a new window in multi-messenger astronomy.
