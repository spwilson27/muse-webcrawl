Infinite regress

FieldPhilosophy, logic, epistemology
TypeProblem in the structure of justification and explanation
Classic responsesFoundationalism, coherentism, infinitism

An infinite regress is an infinite series of entities governed by a recursive principle that generates each member of the series from the preceding one. In philosophy, regress arguments arise when a proposed solution to a problem itself raises the same problem again, so that the explanation or justification is never completed and recedes endlessly.

The epistemic regress problem

The best-known form concerns justified belief: if a belief is justified by another belief, and that one by a further belief, the chain either goes on forever, circles back on itself, or stops at a belief that is justified in some other way or not in need of justification. These three options form the Münchhausen trilemma (or Agrippa's trilemma). Foundationalists accept a stopping point of basic beliefs; coherentists accept a kind of circularity within a web of beliefs; infinitists accept the infinite chain as non-vicious.

Vicious and benign regresses

Not every infinite regress is a problem. In mathematics, recursive definitions and infinite descent arguments are legitimate, and some regresses in metaphysics or semantics are considered harmless. A regress is vicious when each step depends on the completion of the next, so that nothing is ever fully explained or grounded; it is benign when each member is adequately accounted for independently of the whole series.

Famous examples

Examples include the cosmological "turtles all the way down" anecdote about what supports the world, homunculus arguments in the philosophy of mind, where an inner observer itself needs an inner observer, and Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles", which dramatises a regress in logical inference.
