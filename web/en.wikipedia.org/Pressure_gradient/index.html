Pressure gradient

In atmospheric science, the pressure gradient (typically of air, but more generally of any fluid) is a physical quantity that describes in which direction and at what rate the pressure increases the most rapidly around a particular location. The pressure gradient is a vector quantity, pointing from regions of lower pressure toward regions of higher pressure, with units of pressure per unit length (for example, pascals per metre).

Pressure-gradient force

In fluid dynamics, air (or any fluid) accelerates in response to the pressure gradient: the pressure-gradient force acts from high pressure toward low pressure, opposite to the gradient vector itself. In the atmosphere, this force is one of the main drivers of wind. On a weather map, closely spaced isobars indicate a steep pressure gradient and hence strong winds, while widely spaced isobars indicate light winds.

Horizontal and vertical gradients

The vertical pressure gradient is much larger than the horizontal one — pressure falls by roughly one hectopascal every eight metres near sea level — but it is nearly balanced by gravity in hydrostatic equilibrium. The much smaller horizontal gradients are what drive most weather systems, modified by the Coriolis effect, friction and centrifugal effects to produce the geostrophic and gradient winds.

Applications

Pressure gradients are fundamental to meteorology (wind forecasting and storm analysis), oceanography (driving ocean currents in combination with wind stress), acoustics (sound propagation), and engineering disciplines such as aerodynamics and hydraulics. In the body, pressure gradients drive blood flow and breathing.
