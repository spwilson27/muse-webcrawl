In neuroscience, synaptic plasticity is the ability of synapses to strengthen or weaken over time in response to increases or decreases in their activity. Plastic changes in synaptic strength are widely considered the cellular mechanism underlying learning and memory: as Donald Hebb proposed in 1949, "cells that fire together wire together".

Forms of plasticity

  • Long-term potentiation (LTP): a persistent strengthening of synapses following high-frequency stimulation, first characterised in the hippocampus by Bliss and Lømo (1973).
  • Long-term depression (LTD): a lasting weakening of synapses following prolonged low-frequency stimulation.
  • Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP): the precise timing of pre- and postsynaptic spikes determines whether a synapse is strengthened (pre before post) or weakened (post before pre).
  • Short-term plasticity: facilitation, augmentation, and depression lasting milliseconds to minutes, governed by residual presynaptic calcium.
  • Homeostatic plasticity: slower scaling mechanisms that keep overall network activity stable.

Mechanisms

At most excitatory synapses, plasticity is gated by NMDA receptors, which act as coincidence detectors: they pass calcium only when presynaptic glutamate release coincides with postsynaptic depolarisation. Calcium influx activates kinases such as CaMKII, driving AMPA receptors into the postsynaptic membrane (LTP), or phosphatases that remove them (LTD). Late-phase plasticity requires new protein synthesis and gene transcription (via CREB), consolidating changes into lasting structural remodelling of dendritic spines.
