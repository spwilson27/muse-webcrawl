The Highlands (Scots: the Hielans; Scottish Gaelic: A' Ghàidhealtachd, pronounced [ə ˈɣɛːəl̪ˠt̪əxk], 'the place of the Gaels') is a historical region of Scotland. Culturally, the Highlands and the Lowlands diverged from the Late Middle Ages into the modern period, when Lowland Scots replaced Scottish Gaelic throughout most of the Lowlands. The term is also used for the area north and west of the Highland Boundary Fault, although the exact boundaries are not clearly defined, particularly to the east. The Great Glen divides the Grampian Mountains to the southeast from the Northwest Highlands. The Scottish Gaelic name of A' Ghàidhealtachd literally means "the place of the Gaels" and traditionally, from a Gaelic-speaking point of view, includes both the Western Isles and the Highlands.

The area is very sparsely populated, with many mountain ranges dominating the region, and includes the highest mountain in the British Isles, Ben Nevis. During the 18th and early 19th centuries the population of the Highlands rose to around 300,000, but from c. 1841 and for the next 160 years, the natural increase in population was exceeded by emigration (mostly to Canada, the United States, Australia and New Zealand, as well as migration to Scotland's industrial cities) and the population fell steadily. The area is now one of the most sparsely populated in Europe. At 9.1 per km2 in 2012, the population density in the Highlands and Islands is less than one seventh of Scotland's as a whole, comparable with that of Bolivia, Chad and Russia.

History

The Highland clan system — organised around chiefs, kinship and the dùthchas or hereditary right — shaped the social and military order of the region for centuries. The failure of the Jacobite rising of 1745, the subsequent disarming acts, and the abolition of heritable jurisdictions in 1747 weakened the power of the chiefs. During the late 18th and 19th centuries, the Highland Clearances saw the forced eviction of much of the population to make way for sheep farming and, later, deer forests and grouse moors; the clearances remain an emotive and contested part of Highland history.

Geography and geology

The Highlands lie upon some of the oldest rocks in Europe, including Lewisian gneiss in the far northwest, among the most ancient exposed rock in the world. The region is cut by the Great Glen fault, which contains the chain of lochs — Loch Ness, Loch Lochy and Loch Linnhe — followed by the Caledonian Canal. Peaks over 914 m (3,000 ft) are classified as Munros; there are 282 of them, all in the Highlands.

Culture

The Highlands are strongly associated with Gaelic language and culture, clan tartans and the great Highland bagpipe, as well as the Highland games held each summer. Whisky distilling is a major industry; the Scotch whisky regions of Highland and Speyside lie largely within the Highlands. Tourism — hillwalking, mountaineering, winter sports at resorts such as Aviemore — is now economically dominant.
