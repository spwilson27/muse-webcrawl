The Lockheed F-117 Nighthawk is a retired American single-seat, twin-engine stealth attack aircraft developed by Lockheed's Skunk Works and operated by the United States Air Force. It was the first operational aircraft designed around stealth technology: its faceted surfaces were shaped to deflect radar waves away from their source, giving it a radar cross-section estimated to be extremely small. Despite the "F" (fighter) designation, it was a pure ground-attack aircraft with no radar and no air-to-air capability.

Development

The F-117 grew out of the secret Have Blue demonstrators of the late 1970s, which applied Soviet physicist Pyotr Ufimtsev's theories of electromagnetic-wave diffraction, via Lockheed engineer Denys Overholser's insight that a shape made of flat facets could be computed with the computers of the day. The program was developed in deep secrecy at Groom Lake (Area 51) and Tonopah Test Range Airport. The first F-117 flew on 18 June 1981, and the type reached initial operating capability in 1983, but its existence was not officially acknowledged until November 1988, after years of night-only operations and public speculation.

Operational history

The Nighthawk's combat debut came in the 1989 invasion of Panama (Operation Just Cause). In the 1991 Gulf War, F-117s flew some of the first strikes on Baghdad and, while comprising a small fraction of coalition attack aircraft, struck a large share of strategic targets with laser-guided bombs, with no losses. They also served over Yugoslavia in 1999—where one, serial 82-0806, was shot down on 27 March 1999 by a Yugoslav S-125 Neva-M (SA-3) battery, the only F-117 lost in combat—and in the 2003 invasion of Iraq. The Air Force retired the type in April 2008 as the F-22 and F-35 assumed its roles, though a number of airframes have since been observed flying in research and aggressor roles.

Design features

Stealth dictated every aspect of the design: faceted external surfaces, radar-absorbent materials and coatings, shielded engine intakes with fine grids, a V-tail, internal weapons carriage, and the elimination of a radar set (relying instead on infrared sensors and laser designation). The aerodynamically unstable shape required quadruplex fly-by-wire controls adapted from the F-16 programme. The aircraft was subsonic, with a combat radius of roughly 1,000 km and aerial-refuelling capability.
