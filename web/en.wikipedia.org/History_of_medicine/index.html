The history of medicine is both a study of medicine throughout history and a multidisciplinary field of study that seeks to explore the diverse healing practices of human societies. Medicine has existed since prehistory, when herbal remedies, trepanation and ritual healing were practised. Organised medical traditions arose in ancient Egypt (the Edwin Smith and Ebers papyri), Mesopotamia, India (Ayurveda; the Sushruta Samhita on surgery), and China (acupuncture and herbal medicine systematised in the Huangdi Neijing).

Greek, Roman and Islamic medicine

Greek medicine, associated with Hippocrates (5th–4th century BC) and the Hippocratic Corpus, emphasised observation, prognosis and natural rather than supernatural causes of disease, and the theory of the four humours. Galen of Pergamum (2nd century AD) systematised anatomy and physiology and dominated European medicine for over a millennium. In the Islamic Golden Age, physicians such as al-Razi and Ibn Sina (Avicenna), whose Canon of Medicine remained a standard textbook in Europe into the 17th century, advanced hospitals, pharmacology and clinical method.

Renaissance to germ theory

The Renaissance brought anatomical revival through Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica (1543); William Harvey described the circulation of the blood in 1628. Edward Jenner introduced smallpox vaccination in 1796. In the 19th century anaesthesia, antisepsis (Joseph Lister), and the germ theory of disease (Louis Pasteur, Robert Koch) transformed surgery and public health, alongside advances in sanitation driven by figures such as John Snow and Florence Nightingale.

Modern medicine

The 20th century saw antibiotics (penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928), vaccines against polio and other diseases, insulin, imaging (X-rays, MRI), randomised controlled trials, organ transplantation, and the molecular biology revolution culminating in genomics and personalised medicine. Medicine also professionalised, with licensing, hospitals and public health systems, though its history includes episodes of unethical experimentation that led to codes such as the Nuremberg Code and the Declaration of Helsinki.
