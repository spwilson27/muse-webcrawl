Radiation burn

Local radiation injuries progress through erythema, blistering and, at high doses, necrosis.

A radiation burn is a damage to the skin or other biological tissue, caused by exposure to ionizing radiation. The damage can be localised, as in cutaneous radiation injury, or part of the systemic acute radiation syndrome. The most common cause of localised injuries is accidental exposure to industrial or medical radiation sources, and prolonged fluoroscopy procedures; historically, early radiologists and patients of poorly calibrated radiotherapy suffered severe burns.

Beta radiation penetrates only shallowly, so beta burns are typically skin-deep; the fallout victims of nuclear tests and the firefighters of Chernobyl suffered extensive beta burns. X-rays and gamma rays penetrate more deeply and can injure internal tissues, blood vessels and bone.

Progression

Radiation burns characteristically evolve in waves over weeks. After exposure there may be a brief prodromal phase of redness and itching, followed by a latent period in which the skin looks normal, and then a manifest phase: erythema, swelling, blistering (dry or moist desquamation) as the basal cells of the epidermis, killed by the dose, fail to be replaced. At higher local doses (above roughly 15–25 Gy) the lesion ulcerates and may become necrotic, with damage to the underlying vasculature causing poor healing that can recur for years. Unlike thermal burns, the full extent of a radiation burn is not apparent at first.

Treatment

Care is mainly supportive: wound care, infection control, analgesia and, for deep injuries, surgical excision and skin grafting, sometimes aided by growth factors or stem-cell therapy. Because damage may extend deeper than it appears, surgical planning uses dosimetry and imaging to estimate the affected volume. Severe local injuries have occurred in radiotherapy accidents, lost-source incidents such as the Goiânia accident, and among interventional radiology patients after very long procedures, prompting modern dose-monitoring requirements.
