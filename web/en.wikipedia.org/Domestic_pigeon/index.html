The domestic pigeon (Columba livia domestica) is a pigeon subspecies derived from the rock dove. It is one of the first birds domesticated by humans — possibly more than 5,000 years ago in Mesopotamia — and has been kept for food, sport, message-carrying, and ornament. All domestic pigeons share the rock dove's remarkable homing ability, which can return a bird to its loft over hundreds of kilometres.

Uses

Homing pigeons carried messages in war and peace into the twentieth century; decorated military pigeons such as Cher Ami saved soldiers' lives in the First World War. Racing pigeons compete over distances up to 1,000 km, with champion birds selling for over a million euros. Fancy breeds — fantails, pouters, tumblers, frillbacks — are bred for show, while utility breeds are raised for squab, a prized meat.

Scientific importance

Charles Darwin bred pigeons extensively and used their dramatic variation under domestication — all derived from a single species — as the opening argument of On the Origin of Species (1859) for the power of selection. Pigeons remain important in studies of navigation, magnetoreception, cognition, and genetics of colour and crest variation.

Feral pigeons

Escaped domestic birds founded the feral pigeon populations of cities worldwide. Though often regarded as pests, city pigeons descend from the same stock as prized racers and show birds.
