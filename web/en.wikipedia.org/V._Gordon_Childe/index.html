Vere Gordon Childe (14 April 1892 – 19 October 1957) was an Australian archaeologist who specialized in the study of European prehistory and became one of the most influential archaeologists of the 20th century. He coined the terms Neolithic Revolution and Urban Revolution to describe the transformations brought by farming and by the rise of cities.

Career

Educated at the University of Sydney and at Oxford, Childe worked in politics and journalism in Australia before returning to Britain. His The Dawn of European Civilization (1925) synthesized the prehistory of a continent. From 1927 to 1946 he was Abercromby Professor of Archaeology at the University of Edinburgh, where he excavated the remarkably preserved Neolithic village of Skara Brae in Orkney. In 1946 he became the first director of the Institute of Archaeology in London, a post he held until his retirement in 1956.

Ideas

Influenced by Marxism, Childe framed prehistory as a sequence of economic and technological revolutions punctuated by long periods of conservatism. Man Makes Himself (1936) and What Happened in History (1942) brought his synthesis to a mass readership; his 1950 paper "The Urban Revolution" listed ten criteria for early cities. Although later archaeology revised many of his diffusionist explanations, his questions and concepts remain foundational.

Death

Childe returned to Australia in 1957 and died on 19 October of that year after falling from Govetts Leap in the Blue Mountains, an apparent suicide documented in a letter he left behind.
