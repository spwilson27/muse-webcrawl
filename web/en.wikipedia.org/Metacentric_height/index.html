Metacentric height

The metacentric height (GM) is a measurement of the initial static stability of a floating body. It is calculated as the distance between the centre of gravity (G) of a ship and its metacentre (M) — the point about which the hull effectively rotates when heeled at small angles, where the vertical line through the shifted centre of buoyancy intersects the ship's centreline.

Stability

If the metacentre lies above the centre of gravity, the metacentric height is positive and the vessel is stable: the buoyancy force creates a righting moment that returns it upright. A larger GM makes a ship "stiffer", with a short, uncomfortable roll period; a small GM makes it "tender", rolling slowly but risking capsize, and a negative GM means the vessel is unstable and will overturn. Naval architects therefore choose a GM within regulatory limits for the ship's type and loading condition.

Measurement

The metacentric height can be determined experimentally by an inclining test, in which known weights are moved across the deck and the resulting angle of heel is measured, or calculated from the hull geometry using the relation GM = KB + BM − KG, where BM is the distance from the centre of buoyancy to the metacentre.

History

The concept was introduced by the French scientist Pierre Bouguer in the eighteenth century, in his Traité du navire (1746), one of the founding works of naval architecture.
