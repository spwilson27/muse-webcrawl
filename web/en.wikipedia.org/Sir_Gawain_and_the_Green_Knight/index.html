Sir Gawain and the Green Knight is a late 14th-century Middle English chivalric romance written in alliterative verse. It is considered one of the finest works of Middle English literature and one of the best-known Arthurian stories. The anonymous author is generally identified with the poet of Pearl, Patience, and Cleanness, with which Gawain shares its unique surviving manuscript, Cotton Nero A.x.

Plot

At a New Year's feast in Camelot, a gigantic green knight interrupts and proposes a beheading game: any knight may strike him with his axe, provided the knight agrees to receive a return blow in a year and a day. Sir Gawain accepts and decapitates the knight, who calmly picks up his head, reminds Gawain of the bargain, and rides away. The following autumn Gawain journeys north to find the Green Chapel, lodging at the castle of Sir Bertilak, where he resists the seductions of the host's wife but accepts a protective green girdle, concealing it from Bertilak in breach of their exchange-of-winnings agreement. At the Green Chapel the knight — revealed as Bertilak, transformed by Morgan le Fay — nicks Gawain's neck for his small dishonesty but praises him as the most faultless knight alive. Gawain returns to Camelot wearing the girdle as a badge of shame; the court adopts it as a badge of honour.

Themes and style

The poem explores the tension between the chivalric code of honour and courtly love, Christian virtue, and human fallibility. It is structured in 101 stanzas of varying length, each ending in a "bob and wheel" refrain. Notable translations and adaptations include those by J. R. R. Tolkien and Simon Armitage, and the 2021 film The Green Knight.
