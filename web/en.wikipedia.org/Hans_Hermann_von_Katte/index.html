Hans Hermann von Katte (28 February 1704 – 6 November 1730) was a Lieutenant of the Prussian armed forces and a close friend of the future Frederick the Great, then Crown Prince Frederick. He was executed by beheading for his role in Frederick's attempt to flee Prussia in 1730.

Background

Katte came from a noble Brandenburg family and served as an officer in the elite Gens d'Armes cavalry regiment in Potsdam. Cultured and musical, he became an intimate companion of Crown Prince Frederick, who chafed under the harsh discipline of his father, King Frederick William I, and shared with Katte his interests in literature, philosophy and the flute.

The flight attempt

In August 1730, during a royal progress through southern Germany, Frederick planned with Katte's help to escape to France and ultimately to England. The plot was betrayed — a letter was intercepted — and at Steinsfurt the King had both men arrested. A court-martial at Köpenick sentenced Katte to life imprisonment, but Frederick William, enraged, overruled the court and ordered Katte's execution.

Execution

Katte was beheaded on 6 November 1730 at the fortress of Küstrin, in front of the window of the room where Frederick was imprisoned, forcing the prince to witness his friend's death. Frederick reportedly fainted as the sentence was carried out. The episode became one of the most famous in Prussian history and is often seen as a formative trauma in Frederick's life; after becoming king he rehabilitated Katte's memory and honoured his family.
