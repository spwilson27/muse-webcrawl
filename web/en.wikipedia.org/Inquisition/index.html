The Inquisition was a system of official tribunals developed by the Catholic Church to identify, try, and punish heresy. The term derives from the Latin inquisitio, referring to the inquisitorial legal procedure in which the judge actively investigated a case rather than waiting for an accuser. Beginning in the 12th century in response to movements such as Catharism in southern France, inquisitions took several historically distinct forms over roughly six centuries.

Medieval Inquisition

The Episcopal Inquisition, established by bishops under the decree Ad abolendam (1184) of Pope Lucius III, was followed by the Papal Inquisition of the 1230s, when Pope Gregory IX entrusted trained inquisitors — frequently Dominican and Franciscan friars — with combating heresy. Inquisitors travelled through towns, announced a period of grace for voluntary confession, and conducted secret trials in which the accused could be subjected to imprisonment and, under restrictions introduced in 1252 by the bull Ad extirpanda, torture. Those found guilty and unrepentant were "relaxed" to the secular arm for punishment, which could include burning at the stake. Targets included Cathars, Waldensians, and later alleged witches and dissident Franciscans.

Spanish Inquisition

The Spanish Inquisition, established in 1478 by Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile with papal approval, was unique in being effectively controlled by the Spanish Crown rather than the papacy. It initially targeted conversos — Jews who had converted to Christianity, often under pressure — suspected of secretly practising Judaism. Its authority later extended to Moriscos (converted Muslims), Protestants, and a wide range of moral and intellectual offences. Sentences were proclaimed at ceremonies called autos-da-fé. Modern historians generally estimate that some 3,000–5,000 people were executed over its 356-year history, though its records document roughly 150,000 trials. The Spanish Inquisition was formally abolished in 1834.

Portuguese and Roman Inquisitions

The Portuguese Inquisition, established in 1536, operated in Portugal and its empire, including a tribunal at Goa in India, and was abolished in 1821. The Roman Inquisition, founded in 1542 as the Congregation of the Holy Office, combated Protestantism and later took up cases involving science and philosophy, most famously the 1633 trial of Galileo Galilei for his support of heliocentrism. Its successor body is today's Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith.

Procedures and punishments

Inquisitorial procedure typically featured secret denunciations, witnesses whose names were concealed from the accused, trials without defence counsel in the modern sense, and sentences read publicly. Penalties ranged from penances, pilgrimages, fines, and the wearing of penitential garments (the sanbenito) to imprisonment and confiscation of property — a major source of institutional revenue and of conflicts of interest.

Historiography and legacy

The Inquisition became a central element of the "Black Legend" of Spanish cruelty, popularised by Protestant polemic and Enlightenment writers. Modern archival scholarship has revised some older exaggerations downward while documenting the system's real coercive reach, its role in censorship (the Index Librorum Prohibitorum), and its long suppression of religious minorities in Spain and its colonies.
