Assyriology

Assyriology is the archaeological, historical, and linguistic study of Assyria and the wider ancient Mesopotamia (and, broadly, the ancient Near East) that used cuneiform writing. Assyriologists study the languages written in cuneiform — principally Sumerian and Akkadian (with its Babylonian and Assyrian dialects) — as well as the art, architecture, and material culture preserved in hundreds of thousands of clay tablets and excavated sites.

History

The discipline began in the 19th century with the decipherment of cuneiform. Henry Rawlinson's copying and translation of the trilingual Behistun Inscription in Persia (1835–1847) provided the key to Old Persian and then Babylonian cuneiform. Excavations by Austen Henry Layard at Nimrud and Nineveh, and Hormuzd Rassam's discovery of Ashurbanipal's library of some 30,000 tablets — including the Epic of Gilgamesh, translated by George Smith in 1872 — revealed a civilisation largely independent of biblical sources.

Scope

Assyriologists edit and translate texts of every genre: royal inscriptions, laws (such as the Code of Hammurabi), letters, contracts, omens, medical texts, mathematics, astronomy and literature. The field also encompasses the archaeology of Mesopotamian cities such as Ur, Babylon, Nineveh and Mari, and the study of neighbouring cultures that adopted cuneiform, including the Hittites, Elamites and Urartians. Despite its name, the field covers all of Mesopotamia, not only Assyria, and spans roughly three millennia from the invention of writing around 3400–3300 BCE to the last cuneiform texts of the 1st century CE.

Modern practice

University departments in Europe, North America, and the Middle East train specialists in Sumerian and Akkadian grammar. Digital projects such as the Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (CDLI) photograph and catalogue tablets from museum collections worldwide, making the primary sources openly available.
