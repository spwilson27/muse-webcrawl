Abductive reasoning

Abductive reasoning (also called abduction, abductive inference, or retroduction) is a form of logical inference that seeks the simplest and most likely conclusion from a set of observations. Unlike deduction, which guarantees its conclusion, abduction yields a plausible conclusion that does not guarantee its truth: observing that the grass is wet, one abduces that it rained, though a sprinkler could also explain it.

Peirce's account

The term was introduced into modern philosophy by Charles Sanders Peirce, who argued that abduction is the only kind of reasoning that generates new ideas: deduction explicates, induction evaluates, but abduction originates hypotheses. Peirce gave it the form: "The surprising fact C is observed; but if A were true, C would be a matter of course; hence, there is reason to suspect that A is true."

Inference to the best explanation

In contemporary philosophy, abduction is commonly identified with inference to the best explanation (IBE), a phrase associated with Gilbert Harman and Peter Lipton. Explanatory considerations — simplicity, scope, coherence with existing knowledge, and fruitfulness — guide the choice among competing hypotheses. Critics note that the best available explanation may still be false, and that abduction provides justification for pursuing a hypothesis rather than accepting it outright.

Applications

Abductive reasoning is central to medical diagnosis, detective work, fault diagnosis in engineering, and scientific discovery. In artificial intelligence and logic, formal models of abduction treat it as the problem of finding hypotheses that, combined with background knowledge, entail the observations, and it underlies abductive logic programming.
