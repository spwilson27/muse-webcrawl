Myocardium

The myocardium is the muscular middle layer of the heart wall, lying between the outer epicardium and the inner endocardium. Composed of cardiac muscle tissue, its rhythmic contractions pump blood through the circulatory system.

Structure

The myocardium consists of individual heart muscle cells, cardiomyocytes, joined end-to-end by intercalated discs that contain gap junctions, allowing electrical signals to spread rapidly so the tissue contracts as a coordinated syncytium. The layer is thickest in the wall of the left ventricle, which must pump blood around the whole body, and thinnest in the atria. Muscle fibres are arranged in complex spiral and circumferential sheets that wring blood out of the chambers during systole.

Blood supply and electrical activity

Despite being bathed in oxygenated blood, the myocardium is fed by the coronary arteries, because its wall is too thick for diffusion alone. Its contractions are initiated and coordinated by the heart's conduction system — the sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node, bundle of His, and Purkinje fibres — which are themselves specialized myocardial cells.

Clinical significance

The defining disease of the myocardium is myocardial infarction (heart attack), in which blockage of a coronary artery kills a region of heart muscle. Other disorders include myocarditis (inflammation, often viral), cardiomyopathies (diseases of the muscle itself, whether dilated, hypertrophic, or restrictive), and hypertrophy in response to chronic pressure overload.
