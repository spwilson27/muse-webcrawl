Sinoatrial node

The sinoatrial node (1) initiates the impulse that travels through the heart's conduction system.

The sinoatrial node (also known as the sinuatrial node, SA node or sinus node) is an oval shaped region of special cardiac muscle in the upper back wall of the right atrium made up of cells known as pacemaker cells. The sinus node is approximately 15 mm long, 3 mm wide, and 1 mm thick, located directly below and a little lateral to the superior vena cava. These cells can spontaneously produce an electrical impulse — the cardiac action potential — that travels through the heart via the electrical conduction system, causing it to contract, and because it sets the heart's rhythm it is called the natural pacemaker of the heart.

Function

Pacemaker cells in the SA node generate impulses at an intrinsic rate of about 60–100 per minute at rest, faster than any other part of the conduction system, so they normally entrain the whole heart. The impulse spreads across both atria, causing atrial contraction, and reaches the atrioventricular node, where a short delay allows the ventricles to fill before ventricular contraction begins.

Regulation and clinical significance

The node's rate is modulated by the autonomic nervous system: sympathetic stimulation speeds it, while vagal (parasympathetic) input slows it, adjusting heart rate to activity, emotion and blood pressure. The node is supplied by the sinoatrial nodal artery, arising from the right coronary artery in most people. Dysfunction of the node produces sick sinus syndrome, with bradycardia, pauses or tachy-brady alternation, which may require an artificial pacemaker. The SA node was first described by Arthur Keith and Martin Flack in 1906–07.
