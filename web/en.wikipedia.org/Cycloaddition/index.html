Cycloaddition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cycloaddition is a chemical reaction, usually in organic chemistry, in which "two or more unsaturated molecules (or parts of the same molecule) combine with the formation of a cyclic adduct in which there is a net reduction of the bond multiplicity". The resulting reaction is a cyclisation. Many but not all cycloadditions are concerted and pericyclic. As a consequence of the principle of microscopic reversibility, the reverse reaction — a cycloelimination or cycloreversion — is conceptually the same process run backwards.

Cycloadditions are often described by the size of each reacting fragment: a [π4s + π2s] cycloaddition, for example, combines a 4-π-electron component (such as a diene) with a 2-π-electron component (a dienophile). The classic example is the Diels–Alder reaction between butadiene and ethylene to give cyclohexene, reported by Otto Diels and Kurt Alder in 1928 and recognised by the 1950 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Types

  • Diels–Alder reaction — [4+2] cycloaddition of a conjugated diene and a substituted alkene, valued for building six-membered rings with predictable stereochemistry.
  • 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition — addition of a 1,3-dipole (such as an azide or nitrone) to a dipolarophile; the copper-catalysed azide–alkyne version is the flagship of "click chemistry".
  • [2+2] cycloaddition — thermally forbidden for simple alkenes by orbital symmetry, but accessible photochemically; the ketene [2+2] cycloaddition is synthetically important.
  • Cheletropic reactions — cycloadditions in which two sigma bonds are made or broken at the same atom, such as the addition of sulfur dioxide to a diene.

Mechanism and rules

The Woodward–Hoffmann rules, developed in the 1960s, predict which cycloadditions are thermally allowed by analysing conservation of orbital symmetry: a pericyclic reaction is symmetry-allowed if the total number of (4q+2)-electron suprafacial components is odd. Frontier molecular orbital theory explains the Diels–Alder reaction's acceleration by electron-withdrawing groups on the dienophile and electron-donating groups on the diene. Some apparent cycloadditions instead proceed by stepwise ionic or radical pathways and are known as formal cycloadditions.

Applications

Cycloadditions are central to the synthesis of complex natural products and pharmaceuticals because they form two bonds and up to four stereocentres in a single step. Intramolecular variants build bridged and polycyclic skeletons found in many natural products.
