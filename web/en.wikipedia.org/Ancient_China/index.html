Ancient China

Ancient China refers to the early periods of Chinese civilisation, from Neolithic cultures along the Yellow and Yangtze rivers through the Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties to the establishment of the Qin empire in 221 BC. It was one of the world's earliest civilisations, developing agriculture, writing, bronze metallurgy, and distinctive philosophies largely independently.

Neolithic and the Xia

Farming communities such as the Yangshao (c. 5000–3000 BC) and Longshan cultures cultivated millet in the north and rice in the south, produced painted pottery, and built walled settlements. Traditional histories begin with the Xia dynasty (c. 2070–1600 BC), long considered legendary; the Bronze Age site of Erlitou is often associated with it.

Shang and Zhou

The Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BC) is the first with contemporary written records: oracle bone inscriptions used for divination, ancestral worship, and finely cast ritual bronzes. The Zhou overthrew the Shang, justifying rule by the Mandate of Heaven. During the long Zhou (1046–256 BC) the state fragmented — the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods saw iron technology, coinage, large infantry armies, and the Hundred Schools of Thought, including Confucianism, Daoism, Mohism, and Legalism.

Unification

In 221 BC the state of Qin under Qin Shi Huang conquered the rival kingdoms and unified China, standardising script, weights, measures, and axle gauges, and laying the imperial framework that endured, with interruptions, for two millennia.
