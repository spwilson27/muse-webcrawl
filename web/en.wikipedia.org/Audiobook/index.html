Audiobook

An audiobook (or talking book) is a recording of a book or other work being read out loud. Full-length recordings are typically narrations of the complete text, while abridged versions shorten it. Audiobooks are widely used for entertainment, education, and accessibility for people with visual impairments or reading disabilities.

History

Spoken-word recordings date to the earliest phonograph cylinders; Thomas Edison recited "Mary Had a Little Lamb" in 1877. In the 1930s, programs such as the American Foundation for the Blind's Talking Books and the UK's RNIB service produced recordings for blind readers on long-playing records. Commercial audiobooks grew with cassette tapes in the 1970s–80s, CDs in the 1990s, and digital downloads and streaming in the 2000s, led by services such as Audible (founded 1995, acquired by Amazon in 2008).

Production and narration

Audiobooks are narrated by professional voice actors, by the author, or increasingly by celebrity readers; some productions use full casts and sound effects. Preparation involves marking the text, pronunciation research, and studio recording and editing, often requiring many hours of production per finished hour.

Market

Digital audiobooks are among the fastest-growing segments of publishing, listened to chiefly on smartphones while commuting, exercising, or doing household tasks. Libraries lend audiobooks through apps such as Libby, and projects like LibriVox provide free recordings of public-domain works read by volunteers.
