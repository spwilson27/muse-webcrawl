Conscription in the United States, commonly known as the draft, has been employed by the U.S. federal government in various forms, beginning during the American Civil War. Conscription, in the form of the Selective Service System, remains codified in law as an inactive program that could be activated in a national emergency, and registration with the Selective Service remains a legal requirement for young men.

History

Colonial and Civil War era

Colonial militias required service from able-bodied men, and states drafted soldiers during the Revolutionary War. The first national conscription came with the Enrollment Act of 1863 during the Civil War; its provision allowing commutation and substitutes sparked the New York City draft riots. The Confederacy had enacted its own draft a year earlier, in 1862.

World wars

The Selective Service Act of 1917 raised an army of millions for the First World War and was upheld by the Supreme Court in the Selective Draft Law Cases. The Selective Training and Service Act of 1940 created the first peacetime draft in American history; during the Second World War more than 10 million men were inducted.

Cold War and Vietnam

The draft continued through the Korean War and the Cold War. During the Vietnam War, inductions became deeply controversial; a lottery system was introduced in 1969. The last induction took place in 1973, when the United States moved to an all-volunteer force under the Gates Commission's recommendations.

Selective Service today

Although inductions ended in 1973 and registration was suspended in 1975, President Jimmy Carter reinstated registration in 1980 following the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. Men must register within 30 days of their eighteenth birthday. Failure to register can affect eligibility for federal student aid, government employment, and, for immigrants, naturalization. Proposals to extend registration to women have been debated in Congress but not enacted.
