Fluid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, a fluid is a liquid, gas, or other material that may continuously move and deform (flow) under an applied shear stress, or external force. Unlike a solid, a fluid cannot resist a sustained shear: it deforms continuously until the stress is removed. Fluids include liquids, gases, plasmas, and — to some degree — plastic solids.

Properties

The behaviour of fluids is characterised by properties such as density, pressure, temperature, and viscosity — the measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. Newtonian fluids (water, air, most common liquids) have viscosity independent of shear rate, whereas non-Newtonian fluids (blood, ketchup, cornflour suspensions) do not. Fluids are described as compressible or incompressible depending on how much their density changes with pressure; liquids are usually treated as incompressible, gases are not.

Fluid mechanics

The study of fluids is fluid mechanics, divided into fluid statics (fluids at rest), kinematics, and fluid dynamics. Its governing equations are the Navier–Stokes equations, expressing conservation of mass, momentum, and energy. Simplifying models include Bernoulli's principle for steady incompressible flow and the concept of an ideal (inviscid) fluid. Key phenomena include laminar and turbulent flow, boundary layers, buoyancy, and surface tension.

Applications

Fluid mechanics underlies hydraulics, aerodynamics, weather forecasting, oceanography, blood-flow physiology, and much of chemical and civil engineering.
