Publius Decius Mus (died 279 BC) was a Roman consul who served with Publius Sulpicius Saverrio in 279 BC. He is best known for his legendary self-sacrifice — the devotio — at the Battle of Asculum during the Pyrrhic War against King Pyrrhus of Epirus.

Family and the devotio tradition

Decius belonged to the plebeian gens Decia. His grandfather, Publius Decius Mus, was said to have devoted himself at the Battle of Veseris in 340 BC, and his father to have done the same at the Battle of Sentinum in 295 BC. In the Roman tradition, a commander performing devotio dedicated himself and the enemy army to the gods of the underworld, then charged into the enemy ranks to die, securing victory for Rome.

Battle of Asculum and death

At Asculum in 279 BC the Roman army met Pyrrhus in heavy fighting over two days. According to the later Roman tradition preserved by authors such as Cicero and Florus, Decius followed the example of his father and grandfather and devoted himself, riding into the enemy to his death. Modern historians are more cautious: the battle was in fact a costly victory for Pyrrhus — the origin of the phrase "Pyrrhic victory" — and the death of Decius in the fighting may have been embellished into a formal devotio by family tradition and annalistic writers.

Legacy

The three Decii became stock exemplars of self-sacrificing patriotism in Roman moralising literature, cited by Cicero and others as models of putting the republic above one's life. Their story was revived in Renaissance and early modern art and political thought.
