Swiss cheese model

The Swiss cheese model is a model of accident causation used in risk analysis and risk management in fields including aviation, healthcare, engineering and emergency services. It compares an organisation's defences against failure to a stack of slices of Swiss cheese: each layer of defence has "holes" (weaknesses), and an accident occurs only when the holes in successive layers momentarily align, letting a hazard pass through all defences.

Origin

The model was developed by James Reason of the University of Manchester and described in his 1990 book Human Error. Reason distinguished between active failures — unsafe acts by operators at the "sharp end" — and latent conditions — pre-existing weaknesses created by designers, managers and organisational processes, which lie dormant until they combine with active failures and local circumstances.

Application

The model shifted accident investigation away from blaming individuals toward examining systemic and organisational causes. It is widely used in patient safety, aviation safety reporting and industrial safety management, and informs the design of multiple, independent safeguards. Criticisms include that the metaphor is oversimplified, that the "holes" are hard to define and measure, and that it may understate the dynamic interaction of failures.

Legacy

Despite its limitations, the Swiss cheese model remains one of the most influential frameworks in safety science and a staple of training in high-reliability industries.
