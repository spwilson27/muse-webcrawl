Great Canadian flag debate

The Great Canadian flag debate was a national controversy in 1964–1965 over the design of a new official national flag for Canada. Until then Canada had used the Canadian Red Ensign, a British ensign bearing the Canadian shield, which many felt did not reflect Canada's independence or its French heritage.

Background

Calls for a distinctly Canadian flag went back decades, and committees had examined the question in 1925 and 1946 without a decision. Prime Minister Lester B. Pearson, who had experienced the awkwardness of Canada lacking its own flag during the Suez Crisis of 1956, committed his Liberal government to adopting a new flag in time for Canada's centennial in 1967.

The debate

Pearson's preferred design — three red maple leaves between two blue bars, quickly dubbed the "Pearson Pennant" — met strong opposition. Former prime minister John Diefenbaker led the defence of the Red Ensign, and the parliamentary debate dragged on through a filibuster in 1964. The matter was handed to an all-party committee, which after reviewing thousands of submissions recommended a design derived from George Stanley's proposal, inspired by the flag of the Royal Military College of Canada: a single red maple leaf on a white square between two red bars. The historian of the final design's heraldic execution was Jacques Saint-Cyr.

Adoption

After the government invoked closure, the House of Commons approved the new flag in December 1964, and the Senate followed. Queen Elizabeth II proclaimed the National Flag of Canada, and it was first raised on Parliament Hill on 15 February 1965 — a date now celebrated as National Flag of Canada Day.
