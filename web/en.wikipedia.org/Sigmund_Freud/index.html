Sigmund Freud (born Sigismund Schlomo Freud; 6 May 1856 – 23 September 1939) was an Austrian neurologist and the founder of psychoanalysis, a clinical method for evaluating and treating pathologies seen as originating from conflicts in the psyche, through dialogue between a patient and a psychoanalyst, and the distinctive theory of mind and human agency derived from it.

Life

Freud was born to Galician Jewish parents in the Moravian town of Freiberg, in the Austrian Empire. He qualified as a doctor of medicine at the University of Vienna in 1881 and, after work at the Vienna General Hospital and a formative period in Paris under Jean-Martin Charcot, established a clinical practice in Vienna in 1886, specialising in nervous disorders. With Josef Breuer he published Studies on Hysteria (1895); his The Interpretation of Dreams (1899) laid out the theory of the unconscious and dream interpretation. In 1938, after the Anschluss, Freud emigrated to London, where he died the following year.

Ideas

Freud developed therapeutic techniques such as free association and transference, and elaborated the structural model of the psyche — id, ego and superego — in The Ego and the Id (1923). His concepts of the Oedipus complex, infantile sexuality, repression, the death drive, and the "Freudian slip" permeated twentieth-century culture, literature, and the humanities, even as the scientific standing of psychoanalysis became — and remains — vigorously contested.

Legacy

Freud's work has been enormously influential and controversial in equal measure: foundational for psychotherapy and literary and cultural theory, yet criticised for unfalsifiability and now largely superseded within scientific psychology. His London home at 20 Maresfield Gardens is now the Freud Museum; his Vienna apartment at Berggasse 19 is also a museum.
