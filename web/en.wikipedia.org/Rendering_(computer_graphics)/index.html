Rendering (computer graphics)

Rendering or image synthesis is the process of generating a photorealistic or non-photorealistic image from a 2D or 3D model by means of a computer program. The resulting image is referred to as a render. Multiple models can be defined in a scene file containing objects in a strictly defined language or data structure; the scene file contains the geometry, viewpoint, texture, lighting, and shading information describing the virtual scene.

Techniques

Two broad families of algorithms dominate. Rasterization projects 3D geometry onto the image plane and shades the resulting fragments; it is fast and underlies real-time rendering in video games, usually implemented through graphics APIs such as OpenGL, Direct3D and Vulkan running on GPUs. Ray tracing simulates the transport of light by tracing paths from the camera into the scene, naturally producing reflections, refractions and soft shadows; extensions such as path tracing use Monte Carlo integration to achieve global illumination. Other approaches include radiosity, scanline rendering and Reyes.

Shading and appearance

Shading models — from flat and Gouraud shading to the Phong model and modern physically based rendering (PBR) based on the rendering equation formulated by James Kajiya in 1986 — determine how light interacts with surfaces. Textures, bump mapping and displacement mapping add surface detail, while tone mapping and colour management prepare the high-dynamic-range result for display.

Applications

Rendering is used in film and television visual effects (with renderers such as RenderMan, Arnold and V-Ray), architectural visualisation, product design, video games and simulation. Offline rendering for feature films may take hours per frame on large render farms, whereas real-time rendering must sustain tens of frames per second.
