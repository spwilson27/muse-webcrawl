Cyberbullying

Cyberbullying often takes place through social media, messaging apps and online games.

Cyberbullying (or cyberharassment) is a form of bullying or harassment using electronic means, most commonly on social media, messaging platforms, gaming platforms and mobile phones. It is defined by aggressive, intentional and repeated behaviour directed at a victim who cannot easily defend themselves. Common tactics include posting rumours or embarrassing photos, sending threatening or abusive messages, exclusion from online groups, impersonation, and doxing — publishing private personal information.

Characteristics

Researchers distinguish cyberbullying from traditional bullying by several features: it can occur at any hour and reach the victim at home; content can spread to a very large audience almost instantly and persist indefinitely; and perpetrators may feel shielded by anonymity or physical distance, a form of the online disinhibition effect. At the same time, surveys show extensive overlap: most victims of cyberbullying are also bullied in person, and most perpetrators are known to their victims.

Prevalence and effects

Prevalence estimates vary widely by definition and method; studies of adolescents commonly report that between roughly 10 and 40 percent have experienced cyberbullying in some form. Victimisation is associated with anxiety, depression, lowered self-esteem, school avoidance and, in severe cases, self-harm. High-profile cases, such as those of Megan Meier in the United States and Amanda Todd in Canada, drew international attention to the issue in the 2000s and 2010s.

Prevention and response

Responses operate at several levels. Schools adopt anti-bullying policies that explicitly cover online conduct and teach digital citizenship. Platforms provide reporting, blocking and content-moderation tools, though their effectiveness is debated. Parents and clinicians are advised to watch for behavioural changes and to preserve evidence rather than deleting messages. Bystander intervention — refusing to forward harmful content and supporting the victim — is a recurring theme of prevention campaigns.

Legal responses

Many jurisdictions have legislated against cyberbullying and related conduct such as non-consensual image sharing. In the United States, most states require school policies on harassment including electronic forms, and Megan's Law–era federal legislation (the Megan Meier Cyberbullying Prevention Act) was proposed but not enacted. Other countries, including Canada, New Zealand and members of the European Union, have adopted specific offences or regulatory duties for platforms.
