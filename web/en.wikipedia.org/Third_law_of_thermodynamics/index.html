Third law of thermodynamics

The third law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of a closed system at thermodynamic equilibrium approaches a constant value when its temperature approaches absolute zero. This constant value cannot depend on any other parameters characterizing the system, such as pressure or applied magnetic field. At absolute zero (zero kelvin) the system must be in the state with the minimum possible energy.

The law was developed by the German chemist and physicist Walther Nernst during the years 1906–12, and is sometimes known as Nernst's theorem or Nernst's postulate. In its common form, the law states that the entropy of a perfect crystal at absolute zero is exactly zero, because a perfect crystal has only one possible microstate. Statistical mechanics explains this: entropy S = kB ln Ω, and for a single microstate (Ω = 1) the entropy is zero.

Consequences

A corollary of the third law is the unattainability of absolute zero: no finite sequence of processes can cool a system to exactly 0 K, providing a theoretical basis for the limits of cryogenics even as laboratory temperatures have reached mere billionths of a kelvin. The law also allows absolute entropies to be tabulated, because it fixes the zero of entropy, enabling the calculation of chemical equilibrium constants from calorimetric data.

Formulations

Alternative formulations exist, including statements by Gilbert N. Lewis and Merle Randall, and the "unattainability principle". The law holds for most systems but is subject to subtleties in systems with degenerate ground states or residual entropy, as in glasses and ordinary ice.
