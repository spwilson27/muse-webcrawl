Ophelia is a painting by the British artist Sir John Everett Millais, completed in 1851–52 and held in Tate Britain in London. It depicts Ophelia, a character from William Shakespeare's play Hamlet, singing while floating in a river just before she drowns. The work is regarded as one of the masterpieces of the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood, celebrated for its intense botanical accuracy and luminous colour.

Composition and making

Millais painted the landscape background outdoors on the Hogsmill River at Ewell in Surrey, working for months in the open air in keeping with Pre-Raphaelite fidelity to nature. Each flower — poppies, daisies, pansies and the violets Ophelia wears — carries symbolic meaning drawn from the language of flowers. The figure was painted in the studio: Elizabeth Siddal, later the wife of Dante Gabriel Rossetti, posed lying in a bathtub of water warmed by lamps underneath; she became seriously ill when the lamps went out and the water chilled.

Reception

Exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1852, the painting was initially criticised for its stark naturalism but came to be one of the most popular and frequently reproduced of all Pre-Raphaelite works. It has influenced generations of artists, illustrators and filmmakers, and remains among Tate Britain's best-known pictures.
