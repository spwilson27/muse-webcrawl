The Navajo Nation (Navajo: Naabeehó Bináhásdzo) is a Native American reservation in the southwestern United States, occupying portions of northeastern Arizona, northwestern New Mexico and southeastern Utah. At roughly 71,000 square kilometres it is the largest land area held by a Native American tribe in the United States, larger than ten US states. The Navajo (Diné, "the People") form the largest federally recognised tribe, with about 400,000 enrolled members.

History

The Navajo are an Athabaskan-speaking people who migrated into the Southwest centuries ago. Conflict with the United States culminated in the campaign of Kit Carson and the forced "Long Walk" of 1864, in which thousands of Navajo were marched to internment at Bosque Redondo (Hwéeldi) in New Mexico. The Treaty of 1868 allowed their return to a portion of their homeland and established the reservation, which was expanded repeatedly into the twentieth century.

Government

The Navajo Nation is governed from Window Rock, Arizona, by an elected president and vice president and the Navajo Nation Council. Local affairs are handled through 110 chapter houses. The Nation operates its own police force, courts and social services.

Culture and language

The Navajo language (Diné bizaad) is the most widely spoken Native American language north of Mexico; during the Second World War, Navajo "code talkers" used it as the basis of an unbroken battlefield code for the US Marine Corps. Traditional life centres on kinship through clans, weaving, silversmithing and sandpainting ceremonies aimed at restoring hózhó — harmony and balance. Landmarks within the Nation include Monument Valley, Canyon de Chelly and Antelope Canyon.
