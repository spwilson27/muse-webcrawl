Garrett James Hardin (April 21, 1915 – September 14, 2003) was an American ecologist and microbiologist principally known for his 1968 essay "The Tragedy of the Commons", published in the journal Science, which described how individuals acting in their own self-interest can deplete shared resources held in common. He was a professor of human ecology at the University of California, Santa Barbara.

The Tragedy of the Commons

In the famous essay, Hardin imagined herdsmen grazing cattle on a common pasture: each gains the full benefit of adding an animal while sharing the cost of overgrazing with all, so rational self-interest leads to ruin of the commons. He argued that such problems — including population growth and pollution — have "no technical solution" and require "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon," through regulation, privatisation, or other constraints on freedom in the commons. The essay became one of the most cited in social science; it was later developed and debated by scholars including Elinor Ostrom, whose work showed that communities can govern commons sustainably without privatisation or central control.

Other work and views

Hardin wrote extensively on population, resources, and ethics, including Nature and Man's Fate (1959), Exploring New Ethics for Survival (1972), and Living Within Limits (1993). He proposed the "lifeboat ethics" metaphor opposing food aid and open immigration, positions that were and remain controversial; his views on immigration and his associations drew criticism, and the Southern Poverty Law Center has characterised him as a white nationalist, a description disputed by some of his defenders. Hardin suffered from polio in childhood, which left him with a lifelong interest in the consequences of human frailty and limits.
