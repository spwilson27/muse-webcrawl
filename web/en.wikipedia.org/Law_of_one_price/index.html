Law of one price

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Trading identical goods enforces a single price]

In economics, the law of one price states that in competitive markets free of transportation costs and trade barriers, identical goods sold in different locations must sell for the same price when prices are expressed in a common currency. If the same good traded at different prices in two places, arbitrageurs would buy it where it is cheap and sell it where it is dear until the prices converged.

Statement and conditions

The law rests on the assumptions of efficient markets: no transaction costs, no tariffs or other barriers, and full information. In financial markets, the related no-arbitrage principle requires that assets or portfolios with identical future cash flows have the same price, a foundation of modern asset pricing and of the derivation of risk-neutral valuation.

Applications

Applied internationally, the law underlies the theory of purchasing power parity: if the law held for every good, exchange rates would equalize the price of a common basket across countries. Journalistic measures such as The Economist's Big Mac index exploit this idea by comparing the price of an identical product worldwide.

Deviations

Empirically, persistent deviations are common. Transport costs, non-traded components of retail prices (such as local rent and wages), tariffs, differing taxes, and pricing-to-market strategies all drive wedges between prices. Studies of prices across borders and even within countries find large and durable violations, a topic of continuing research in international economics; in financial markets, apparent violations (such as closed-end fund discounts and twin-share price gaps) have likewise attracted attention.
