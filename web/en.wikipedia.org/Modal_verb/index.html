Modal verb

The core modal verbs of English

A modal verb is a type of verb that is used to indicate modality — that is, likelihood, ability, permission, request, capacity, suggestions, order, obligation, or advice. Modal verbs always accompany the base (infinitive) form of another verb having semantic content. In English, the modal verbs commonly used are can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, and ought to. Certain expressions that are closely related to these, such as have to and be going to, are sometimes called semi-modals, quasi-modals, or pseudo-modals.

English modals

The modal auxiliaries of English are a small class with distinctive syntactic properties. They do not take an -s ending in the third person singular, they have no infinitive or participle forms, they invert with the subject in questions without the use of do, and they are followed by the bare infinitive of the main verb. Negation is formed by adding not directly after the modal: cannot, will not.

Semantics

Modals are typically divided into epistemic modals, which concern what is possible or necessary given what is known ("It must be raining"), and deontic modals, which concern obligation and permission ("You must stop"). Many modal verbs are ambiguous between the two readings, and context determines which is intended. A third category, dynamic modality, covers ability and volition. Formal semanticists following Angelika Kratzer have analysed modals as quantifiers over possible worlds restricted by a conversational background.

History

Most English modals descend from Old English "preterite-present" verbs — verbs whose present tense had the form of a past tense. Can comes from cunnan ("to know"), may from magan ("to be able"), and must from mōtan. Over the Middle English period these verbs lost their non-finite forms and became a closed auxiliary class. Will, originally a full verb meaning "want", grammaticalised into a future marker.
