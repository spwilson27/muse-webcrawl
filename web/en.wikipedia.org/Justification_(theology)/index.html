Justification (theology)

In Christian theology, justification is the act by which God declares or makes a sinner righteous, forgiving sins and restoring the person to a right relationship with God. It is a central doctrine of the Protestant Reformation and remains a major point of discussion — and historically of division — between Protestant, Roman Catholic, and Eastern Orthodox traditions.

Biblical basis

The doctrine rests chiefly on the letters of Paul, especially Romans and Galatians, which speak of a person being "justified by faith" apart from works of the law (e.g. Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16). The Epistle of James, by contrast, states that "a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (James 2:24), a tension that the traditions have resolved in different ways.

Protestant view

Classical Protestant theology, following Martin Luther and John Calvin, teaches justification by faith alone (sola fide): a forensic or legal declaration in which the righteousness of Christ is imputed to the believer, received through faith alone. Luther called justification the article by which the church stands or falls. Sanctification — growth in holiness — is distinguished from justification, which is instantaneous and complete.

Catholic view

The Council of Trent (1545–1563) taught that justification includes both the forgiveness of sins and an inner renewal by grace: the justified person is not merely declared righteous but made righteous, with faith working through love. Justification is initiated by grace, received in baptism, and can increase through a life of faith and good works.

Ecumenical developments

In 1999 the Lutheran World Federation and the Roman Catholic Church signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, affirming a shared understanding that by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work, believers are accepted by God.
