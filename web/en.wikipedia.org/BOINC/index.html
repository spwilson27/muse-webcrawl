The Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing (BOINC) is an open-source middleware system for volunteer computing, a type of distributed computing in which members of the public donate idle processing time on their personal computers to scientific research projects. Developed at the University of California, Berkeley, originally to support the SETI@home project, BOINC was generalised into a platform used by dozens of projects in mathematics, astrophysics, climate science, molecular biology, and medicine.

History

BOINC development began in 2002 under the leadership of David Anderson, building on the success of SETI@home (launched in 1999), which had attracted millions of participants. Funding came from the National Science Foundation. The first projects on the platform included Predictor@home, ClimatePrediction.net, and Einstein@Home, which searches for gravitational waves using data from the LIGO observatories.

Design

A BOINC project consists of a server complex that distributes units of work, called tasks, to client programs running on volunteers' computers ("hosts"). The client downloads application executables and input files, computes results — often using otherwise-idle CPU and GPU capacity — and uploads output for validation and assimilation into the project's database. A credit system rewards participants and is used to compare contributions, which, together with team competitions and message boards, sustains a large volunteer community. The software supports checkpointing, screensaver graphics, and scheduling so that computing does not interfere with the owner's use of the machine.

Projects

Notable BOINC projects have included Rosetta@home (protein structure prediction), World Community Grid (launched by IBM in 2004, later hosted by the Krembil Research Institute), Folding@home-like grid efforts, MilkyWay@home, PrimeGrid, and the LHC@home projects distributing work for CERN. During the COVID-19 pandemic several BOINC projects redirected computation toward coronavirus research.
