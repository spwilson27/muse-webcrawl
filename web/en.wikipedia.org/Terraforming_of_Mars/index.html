Terraforming of Mars

The terraforming of Mars is the hypothetical process of planetary engineering that would modify the Martian atmosphere, temperature, surface topography, and ecology to make large regions of the planet hospitable to human life. The concept appears in science fiction of the 20th century and has been studied scientifically since the 1970s, notably by Carl Sagan and, in detail, by Christopher McKay, Robert Zubrin, and others. No such process is feasible with present technology, and major obstacles — above all the total inventory of accessible volatiles — remain debated.

Motivations and prerequisites

Mars is the most Earth-like planet in the Solar System: it has water ice, a day of similar length, seasons, and terrain suggesting abundant past water. Terraformers usually envision first warming the planet by several tens of degrees so that frozen carbon dioxide in the south polar cap and regolith sublimates, thickening the atmosphere in a runaway greenhouse effect; this would raise pressure above the Armstrong limit so humans could walk outdoors with only an oxygen mask, though not yet breathe the air. Converting the resulting CO₂ atmosphere into an oxygen-rich one would require photosynthesis at planetary scale over tens or hundreds of thousands of years, unless advanced technology intervened.

Proposed methods

Proposals include importing ammonia- or methane-rich asteroids, manufacturing super-greenhouse gases such as perfluorocarbons on Mars from local fluorine, darkening the polar caps to increase absorption, placing orbital mirrors to focus sunlight, and seeding engineered microbes. Sublimating the poles could roughly double the present thin atmosphere (today averaging about 0.6% of Earth's sea-level pressure). A 2018 study concluded that Mars's accessible CO₂ reservoirs are insufficient to create an Earth-like atmosphere, though imported volatiles could in principle overcome this. Mars also lacks a global magnetic field to shield a thickened atmosphere from solar-wind sputtering, a serious long-term problem some proposals address with an artificial magnetosphere at the Mars–Sun L1 point.

Ethics and debate

Objections include the cost and timescale (centuries to millennia), the risk to possible indigenous Martian life — making terraforming a planetary-protection dilemma — and the argument that resources would be better spent on Earth or on enclosed habitats. Under the Outer Space Treaty, the legality of radically altering another world is unsettled.
