Online harassment

Online harassment is the use of digital communications — including social media, messaging, email, forums, and online games — to intimidate, threaten, humiliate, or otherwise harm another person. It encompasses a wide range of behaviours, from a single insulting message to sustained campaigns of abuse. Surveys by organisations such as the Pew Research Center have found that a substantial share of internet users, particularly younger adults, women, and public figures, report having experienced some form of online harassment.

Forms

  • Cyberbullying — repeated harassment, especially among young people, through social platforms and messaging.
  • Doxing — publishing a person's private information, such as a home address or phone number, to encourage further abuse.
  • Swatting — making a hoax emergency call to send armed police to a victim's address.
  • Cyberstalking — persistent unwanted monitoring and contact that causes fear.
  • Threats — including threats of violence or sexual assault directed at individuals.
  • Non-consensual intimate imagery — sharing private sexual images without consent, sometimes called "revenge porn".
  • Coordinated harassment campaigns — organised "raids" or brigading in which many accounts target one person, as seen in several controversies in gaming and media culture.

Effects

Targets of online harassment report anxiety, depression, withdrawal from public life, professional damage, and in severe cases self-harm. Journalists, politicians, activists, and women in public roles are disproportionately targeted, which critics argue can silence marginalised voices and distort public discourse.

Responses

Responses include platform moderation and reporting tools, blocking and filtering features, civil lawsuits, and criminal laws addressing threats, stalking, and non-consensual imagery in many jurisdictions. Debates continue over the balance between combating abuse and protecting free expression, the effectiveness and transparency of platform enforcement, and the role of anonymity online. Researchers and advocacy groups also promote digital literacy and bystander intervention.
