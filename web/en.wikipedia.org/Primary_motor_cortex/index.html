Primary motor cortex

The primary motor cortex (Brodmann area 4) is a region of the cerebral cortex in the frontal lobe that is the main source of neural signals driving voluntary movement. It is located in the posterior portion of the frontal lobe, on the precentral gyrus immediately anterior to the central sulcus. Neurons of the primary motor cortex project via the corticospinal tract to motor neurons in the spinal cord, and its giant pyramidal Betz cells are among the largest neurons in the brain.

The motor homunculus maps body parts onto the primary motor cortex.

Organisation

The primary motor cortex is organised somatotopically: neighbouring neurons control neighbouring body parts, forming the distorted body map known as the motor homunculus, famously charted by Wilder Penfield through electrical stimulation during neurosurgery. Areas requiring fine control — the hands, face, and tongue — occupy disproportionately large cortical territories, while the trunk and legs have relatively small representations. The leg area lies on the medial surface of the hemisphere, and the face laterally near the Sylvian fissure.

Function

Individual neurons encode parameters of movement such as force and direction; populations of neurons together specify movement vectors. The primary motor cortex receives input from the premotor and supplementary motor areas, the somatosensory cortex, and the thalamus, and works with the basal ganglia and cerebellum in planning and refining movement. Damage causes weakness or paralysis on the opposite side of the body, with fine finger movements especially affected.

History

The motor function of the region was demonstrated in 1870 by Gustav Fritsch and Eduard Hitzig, who evoked movements by electrically stimulating the cortex of dogs. John Hughlings Jackson inferred a somatotopic organisation from the "march" of epileptic seizures across the body.
