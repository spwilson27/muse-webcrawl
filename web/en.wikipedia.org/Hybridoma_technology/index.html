Hybridoma technology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hybridoma technology is a method for producing large numbers of identical antibodies (also called monoclonal antibodies). This process starts by injecting a mouse (or other mammal) with an antigen that provokes an immune response. A type of white blood cell, the B cell, produces antibodies that bind to the injected antigen. These antibody-producing B cells are then harvested from the mouse and, in turn, fused with immortal myeloma (B cancer) cells to produce a hybrid cell line called a hybridoma, which has both the antibody-producing ability of the B cell and the longevity and reproductivity of the myeloma.

History

The technique was developed by Georges Köhler and César Milstein at the Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, England, and published in Nature in 1975. Köhler and Milstein shared the 1984 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Niels Kaj Jerne for the discovery. The invention was famously not patented, allowing its rapid spread throughout research and industry.

Method

A laboratory animal, typically a mouse, is immunised with the target antigen, often with repeated injections over several weeks. Antibody-producing B lymphocytes are isolated from the spleen and fused with myeloma cells using a fusogen such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) or inactivated Sendai virus. The myeloma cell line used lacks the enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT).

The fused culture is grown in HAT medium (containing hypoxanthine, aminopterin, and thymidine), which blocks the main pathway of nucleotide synthesis: unfused myeloma cells die because they cannot use the salvage pathway, unfused B cells die naturally after a short lifespan, and only fused hybridomas survive. The culture supernatants are screened — commonly by ELISA — for the desired antibody, and positive hybridomas are cloned by limiting dilution to ensure monoclonality.

Applications

Hybridomas can be grown indefinitely in culture or injected into animals to form antibody-producing tumours (ascites). Monoclonal antibodies produced by hybridoma technology are used in diagnostics (for example pregnancy tests and immunoassays), in research, and as therapeutics for cancer, autoimmune disease, and infectious disease, including drugs such as rituximab and trastuzumab.
