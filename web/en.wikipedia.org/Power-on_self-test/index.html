Power-on self-test

A power-on self-test (POST) is a process performed by firmware or software routines immediately after a computer or other digital electronic device is powered on. The results of the POST may be displayed on a panel that is part of the device, output to an external device, or stored for future retrieval by a test or diagnostic tool. Since a self-test might detect that the system's usual human-readable display is non-functional, an indicator lamp or a speaker may be provided to show error codes as a sequence of flashes or beeps.

Process

The principal duties of the POST are handled by the BIOS (or UEFI firmware) and occur in a defined sequence: verifying the CPU registers, checking the integrity of the BIOS code itself, verifying basic components such as the DMA controller and timer, testing and sizing system memory, initializing the video controller, and identifying and configuring other devices such as keyboards, disk drives, and expansion cards. Any fatal errors encountered during these early stages may cause the system to halt and signal the error via beep codes, LED patterns, or, in some systems, POST code displays on diagnostic cards.

History

Early computers relied on front-panel lights and manual checks, but as microcomputers spread in the late 1970s and 1980s, automatic self-testing became standard in the IBM PC and its compatibles. Apple's Macintosh computers became known for the startup chime indicating a passed hardware test and the "Chimes of Death" for hardware failures on some models. In embedded systems and networking equipment, POST routines similarly check processors, memory, and interfaces before loading the operating system.

Related concepts

The POST is one part of a device's bootstrapping sequence. More elaborate diagnostics, called built-in self-tests (BIST), may be performed by individual components such as memory and processors. Modern systems may also log POST results to non-volatile memory for later analysis by technicians.
