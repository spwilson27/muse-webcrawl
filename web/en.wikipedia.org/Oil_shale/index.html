Oil shale

Oil shale is an organic-rich fine-grained sedimentary rock containing kerogen, a solid mixture of organic chemical compounds, from which liquid hydrocarbons (shale oil) can be produced by heating (retorting), hydrogenation, or thermal dissolution. Oil shale deposits occur worldwide; the largest are in the United States (Green River Formation), with significant industries historically in Estonia, China, and Brazil.

Geology and reserves

Oil shales formed from the accumulation of algal and planktonic organic matter in lakes, shallow seas, and lagoons, buried before complete decay. Global in-place shale oil resources are estimated in the trillions of barrels, but most are not economically recoverable. Oil shale should not be confused with "shale oil" or tight oil produced by hydraulic fracturing of petroleum-bearing shales, nor with oil sands.

Extraction and use

Rock may be mined and crushed, then retorted at about 500 °C to pyrolyse kerogen into shale oil, gas, and spent shale; or heated in situ underground. Estonia has burned oil shale directly for electricity for a century, supplying most of its power. The industry is water-intensive and produces large volumes of waste ash and significant CO2 emissions, which have limited its expansion elsewhere.
