Sook Ching massacre

LocationSingapore (Syonan-to)
Date18 February – 4 March 1942
PerpetratorsImperial Japanese Army (Kempeitai)
VictimsEthnic Chinese residents; estimates from c. 5,000 to 50,000
The Civilian War Memorial in Singapore

The Sook Ching massacre was a systematic purge of the ethnic Chinese population of Singapore by the Imperial Japanese Army during the Japanese occupation, beginning shortly after the fall of Singapore in February 1942. Sook ching (肅清) means "purge" or "cleansing". The operation, carried out principally by the Kempeitai military police, targeted Chinese men suspected of anti-Japanese sympathies — including communists, volunteers who had fought in the defence of Singapore, donors to China's war relief fund, and men with tattoos, who were presumed to be triad members.

The purge

Between 18 February and 4 March 1942, Chinese men aged roughly 18 to 50 were ordered to report to screening centres. Those who failed arbitrary checks were loaded onto lorries, taken to remote beaches and sites such as Changi, Punggol, and Siglap, and shot, bayoneted, or drowned. The death toll remains disputed: Japanese records suggested around 5,000, while the Singapore Chinese community estimated up to 50,000.

Aftermath

After the war, several Japanese officers were tried by a British military court in 1947; two were sentenced to death and others to life imprisonment. The commanding general, Tomoyuki Yamashita, had already been executed for war crimes in the Philippines. In 1962 mass graves of victims were discovered, prompting renewed pressure on Japan; in 1966 Japan agreed to pay Singapore S$50 million in compensation and investment, though it declined to issue a formal apology at the time. The victims are commemorated by the Civilian War Memorial, unveiled in 1967.
