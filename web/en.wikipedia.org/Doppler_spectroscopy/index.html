Doppler spectroscopy


The radial-velocity method detects exoplanets by the Doppler shift of starlight.

Doppler spectroscopy, also known as the radial-velocity method, is a technique for detecting extrasolar planets and binary stars by measuring the Doppler shift of spectral lines in a star's light. As a planet orbits its star, its gravity causes the star to wobble slightly; this motion shifts the star's light periodically toward blue as it approaches Earth and toward red as it recedes.

Method

Spectrographs record the wavelength shifts of absorption lines in the stellar spectrum over months or years. The amplitude and period of the resulting radial-velocity curve yield the planet's minimum mass and orbital period, while the shape of the curve constrains orbital eccentricity. The technique is most sensitive to massive planets in close orbits, though precision now reaches metres per second.

History and discoveries

The method produced the first planet known around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b, announced by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz in 1995, a discovery recognised with the 2019 Nobel Prize in Physics. Radial velocity remains a workhorse of exoplanet science, having confirmed and characterised hundreds of planets, often in combination with the transit method.
