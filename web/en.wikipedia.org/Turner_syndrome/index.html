Turner syndrome (TS), also known as 45,X or 45,X0, is a genetic disorder in which a female is partially or completely missing an X chromosome. Signs and symptoms vary among those affected, but commonly include short stature, a webbed neck, low hairline, swelling of the hands and feet at birth (lymphedema), heart defects such as a bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation of the aorta, and ovarian insufficiency leading to delayed puberty and infertility. Intelligence is usually normal, though specific difficulties with spatial reasoning and mathematics may occur.

Cause

Turner syndrome results from a nondisjunction event during formation of the reproductive cells in one of the parents, or an error in early fetal development. About half of cases are complete monosomy X (45,X); others have mosaicism (e.g. 45,X/46,XX) or structural abnormalities of the second X chromosome. It is not usually inherited from the parents.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis may occur before birth via prenatal testing, or after birth when features are noticed; it is confirmed by karyotyping. There is no cure, but growth hormone therapy in childhood can increase final adult height, and estrogen replacement induces and maintains puberty and protects bone and cardiovascular health. Lifelong surveillance covers the heart and aorta, kidneys, thyroid, hearing, and metabolic risk. Most individuals with Turner syndrome live full lives, though life expectancy is modestly reduced, mainly from cardiovascular disease.

The condition is named after American endocrinologist Henry Turner, who described it in 1938; its chromosomal basis was identified by Charles Ford and colleagues in 1959.
