Antitrust law in the United States

US antitrust law rests on statutes enacted from 1890 onwards

Antitrust law in the United States is a collection of federal and state government laws that regulate the conduct and organization of businesses to promote competition and prevent unjust monopolies. The main statutes are the Sherman Act of 1890, the Clayton Act of 1914, and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914.

Principal statutes

The Sherman Act prohibits, in Section 1, contracts, combinations, and conspiracies in restraint of trade, and in Section 2, monopolization and attempts to monopolize. The Clayton Act supplements it by targeting specific practices—price discrimination, tying arrangements, exclusive dealing, and mergers likely to substantially lessen competition—while also exempting labour unions. The FTC Act created the Federal Trade Commission and bans "unfair methods of competition."

Enforcement

Public enforcement is carried out by the Antitrust Division of the US Department of Justice (which alone may bring criminal prosecutions, typically against cartels) and the Federal Trade Commission. State attorneys general also enforce antitrust law, and private parties injured by violations may sue for treble damages.

Landmark cases

Major decisions include Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States (1911), which dissolved the Standard Oil trust under a "rule of reason"; United States v. AT&T (1982 consent decree), which broke up the Bell System; and United States v. Microsoft Corp. (2001), concerning monopolization of the personal computer operating system market. Modern debates concern the consumer welfare standard, big technology platforms, and labour-market monopsony.
