The designated hitter (DH) is a baseball position that allows a team to have a player bat in place of the pitcher. Unlike a pinch hitter, the designated hitter is named in the starting lineup, bats throughout the game for the pitcher (and any relief pitchers), and never takes the field defensively. The rule was created to increase offence, since pitchers are traditionally weak hitters, and to extend the careers of ageing sluggers.

History

Proposals for a designated hitter date to the early twentieth century (Connie Mack advocated an early version in 1906). The American League adopted the DH on a trial basis in 1973 — Ron Blomberg of the New York Yankees was the first DH to bat in a regular-season game, on 6 April 1973 — and the experiment became permanent. The National League resisted for nearly fifty years, preserving the strategic element of pitchers batting, until the DH became universal in Major League Baseball in 2022 under the new collective bargaining agreement. Most other professional and amateur leagues worldwide also use the rule in some form.

Rules

The DH is optional: a team may elect to have its pitcher bat. The DH must be selected before the game and is locked into the pitcher's spot in the batting order. If the DH enters the game defensively, or the pitcher bats for the DH, the DH role is terminated for the remainder of the game. Under the "Ohtani rule" (2022), a starting pitcher who also serves as DH may remain in the game as DH after being removed as pitcher.

Strategy and criticism

Supporters argue the DH produces more offence and lets fans watch specialist hitters such as Edgar Martínez and David Ortiz — both primarily DHs elected to the Hall of Fame. Critics contend it removes managerial strategy associated with the pitcher's spot (double switches, sacrifice bunts, pinch-hitting decisions) and creates an asymmetric, less complete player.

Award

The American League (now MLB) presents the Edgar Martínez Award annually to the outstanding designated hitter.
