William B. Hartsfield

William Berry Hartsfield
Born: March 1, 1890, Atlanta, Georgia
Died: February 22, 1971
Office: Mayor of Atlanta (1937–1941, 1942–1962)

William Berry Hartsfield (March 1, 1890 – February 22, 1971) was an American politician who served as mayor of Atlanta, Georgia, for a record total of more than twenty years. He is widely credited with shaping modern Atlanta, overseeing its growth into a major commercial centre and promoting its municipal airport, which became one of the busiest in the world and now bears his name as Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport.

Early life and career

Hartsfield was born in Atlanta and studied law without completing a formal university education, working in law firms and entering politics in the 1920s. He served on the Atlanta City Council, where he championed aviation, helping establish the city's first municipal airfield on the site of the former Candler Field racetrack.

Mayor of Atlanta

First elected mayor in 1936, Hartsfield took office in 1937 and (after a brief interruption) served from 1942 until 1962, the longest mayoral tenure in the city's history. He guided Atlanta through the Depression-era fiscal crisis, annexed wealthy suburbs such as Buckhead in 1952, and expanded the airport. On race relations, he steered a pragmatic course, famously calling Atlanta "the city too busy to hate," and in 1948 the city hired its first Black police officers during his administration, though segregation remained in force through most of his tenure.

Legacy

Atlanta's airport was renamed Hartsfield Atlanta International Airport in his honour in 1971, shortly before his death; in 2003 "Jackson" was added for Maynard Jackson. A bronze statue of Hartsfield stands at the airport.
