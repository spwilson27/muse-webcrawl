Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory

Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory is a framework for cross-cultural psychology, developed by Geert Hofstede. It describes the effects of a society's culture on the values of its members, and how these values relate to behaviour, using a structure derived from factor analysis. It originated in the 1970s from a large survey of IBM employees conducted in more than seventy countries, and has become one of the most widely used frameworks in cross-cultural communication and international business.

The original four dimensions

Hofstede's original model, published in Culture's Consequences (1980), identified four dimensions along which national cultures could be positioned:

  • Power distance index (PDI) – the extent to which less powerful members of a society accept and expect that power is distributed unequally.
  • Individualism versus collectivism (IDV) – the degree to which people are integrated into groups; individualist societies have loose ties, while collectivist societies integrate people into strong, cohesive in-groups.
  • Uncertainty avoidance index (UAI) – a society's tolerance for uncertainty and ambiguity; high-scoring cultures minimise the possibility of unstructured situations by strict codes of conduct and belief in absolute truths.
  • Masculinity versus femininity (MAS) – the distribution of emotional roles between the genders; masculine societies value achievement, heroism, assertiveness and material rewards for success, while feminine societies value cooperation, modesty, caring for the weak and quality of life.

Later dimensions

Research by Michael Harris Bond and colleagues added a fifth dimension, long-term versus short-term orientation (LTO), drawn from a study of students in 23 countries using the Chinese Culture Connection survey. In 2010, a sixth dimension, indulgence versus restraint (IVR), was added based on analysis by Michael Minkov of data from the World Values Survey. Indulgence stands for a society that allows relatively free gratification of basic human drives related to enjoying life and having fun; restraint suppresses gratification of needs through strict social norms.

Applications

The dimensions model is used extensively in international business, marketing, cross-cultural communication training, and organisational studies. Practitioners use country scores to anticipate differences in management style, negotiation behaviour, advertising appeals and consumer behaviour.

Criticism

The theory has been criticised on several grounds: that surveys are an inappropriate instrument for measuring cultural differences; that national cultures are assumed to be homogeneous although many countries contain diverse populations; that the IBM sample was unrepresentative, being drawn from the sales and service staff of a single company; and that the dimensions may reflect the period in which the data was gathered rather than enduring national traits. Hofstede and his defenders replied that the model was intended as a paradigm for research rather than a complete description of any individual culture.
