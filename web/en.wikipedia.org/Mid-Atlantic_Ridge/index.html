Mid-Atlantic Ridge

The Mid-Atlantic Ridge is a mid-ocean ridge — an underwater mountain system formed by plate tectonics — that runs along the floor of the Atlantic Ocean and forms part of the longest mountain range in the world. It separates the Eurasian Plate and North American Plate in the North Atlantic, and the African Plate and South American Plate in the South Atlantic. The ridge extends from a junction with the Gakkel Ridge northeast of Greenland southward to the Bouvet triple junction in the South Atlantic, a length of some 16,000 kilometres (10,000 mi).

Discovery

The existence of a ridge under the Atlantic was first inferred from soundings taken during the laying of transatlantic telegraph cables in the nineteenth century. It was mapped in detail by the German Meteor expedition of 1925–1927, and oceanographic surveys in the 1950s, notably by Bruce Heezen and Marie Tharp, revealed its central rift valley. The ridge became a key piece of evidence for seafloor spreading and the theory of plate tectonics.

Geology

The ridge marks a divergent boundary where tectonic plates move apart at an average rate of about 2.5 cm per year. As the plates separate, magma rises from the mantle to fill the gap, solidifying into new oceanic crust of basalt and gabbro. The crest of the ridge typically lies about 2,500 metres below sea level and is cut by a rift valley 25–50 km wide. Numerous transform faults offset the ridge axis, and the surrounding fracture zones record the history of spreading.

Along the ridge, hydrothermal vents discharge mineral-rich water heated by magma; some, called black smokers, support chemosynthetic ecosystems independent of sunlight.

Islands

In a few places the ridge rises above the sea. Iceland, the largest such island, straddles the ridge in the North Atlantic and is being slowly pulled apart by it. Other islands formed on or near the ridge include the Azores, Ascension Island, Saint Helena, Tristan da Cunha and Bouvet Island.

History of the Atlantic

The ridge began to form during the breakup of the supercontinent Pangaea in the Triassic period, roughly 200 million years ago. The Atlantic Ocean continues to widen along the ridge today, while the Pacific Ocean correspondingly shrinks.
