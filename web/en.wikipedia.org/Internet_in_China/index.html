The Internet in China serves the largest online population of any country, with more than one billion users. China connected fully to the global Internet in April 1994, sent its first e-mail in 1987 ("Across the Great Wall we can reach every corner in the world"), and by 2008 had the world's largest number of users. Its internet developed largely on mobile platforms and domestic services, and is characterised by extensive state regulation and censorship, widely known through the "Great Firewall".

History and infrastructure

Early academic networks (CSTNET, CERNET) and the commercial ChinaNET brought public access in the mid-1990s. Broadband and then mobile internet expanded explosively in the 2000s–2010s; most users now access the internet by smartphone. Domestic platforms dominate: Baidu (search), Alibaba and JD.com (e-commerce), Tencent's WeChat (messaging, payments and "super-app" services), ByteDance's Douyin (short video; TikTok abroad), Sina Weibo (microblogging) and Bilibili (video). China operates the largest 5G and fibre networks in the world.

Censorship and governance

The state regulates online content through the "Great Firewall" — a system of IP blocking, DNS filtering, keyword filtering and deep-packet inspection that blocks many foreign services (including Google, Facebook, X/Twitter, YouTube and Wikipedia in all languages since 2019) — together with real-name registration requirements, platform self-censorship obligations and laws such as the 2017 Cybersecurity Law, the Data Security Law and the Personal Information Protection Law (both 2021). Many users circumvent restrictions with VPNs, whose unauthorised use is itself restricted. The Cyberspace Administration of China (founded 2014) is the principal regulator.

Economy and society

China's digital economy spans e-commerce (with events such as Singles' Day exceeding Western counterparts in sales), ubiquitous mobile payments (Alipay, WeChat Pay), food delivery, ride-hailing and livestream commerce. Government policy promotes "Internet Plus", digital infrastructure and indigenous technology champions, while periodic regulatory campaigns have targeted gaming, private tutoring, fintech and celebrity fan culture.
