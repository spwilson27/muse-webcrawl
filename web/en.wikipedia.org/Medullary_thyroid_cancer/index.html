Medullary thyroid cancer

Medullary thyroid cancer (MTC) is a form of thyroid carcinoma which originates in the parafollicular cells (C cells), which produce the hormone calcitonin. Medullary tumors are the third most common of all thyroid cancers, comprising about 3% of all thyroid cancer cases. MTC was first characterized in 1959.

Signs and symptoms

The principal symptom of metastatic medullary thyroid cancer is diarrhoea, sometimes accompanied by flushing. Both can occur especially with liver metastases. Bone pain may occur with skeletal spread. A thyroid lump, often with enlarged cervical lymph nodes, is the usual presenting feature.

Genetics

Approximately 25% of medullary thyroid cancer cases are genetic in nature, caused by a mutation in the RET proto-oncogene. This form is classified as familial MTC. When MTC occurs by itself it is termed sporadic MTC. When it occurs alongside tumours of the parathyroid and medulla of the adrenal glands it is called multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2 (MEN2). Measuring calcitonin in blood or stimulated testing can aid early detection in at-risk relatives.

Markers and treatment

Medullary tumours secrete calcitonin and often carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA); both serve as tumour markers. Unlike differentiated thyroid cancers, MTC does not take up radioactive iodine, so treatment is primarily surgical — total thyroidectomy with central neck dissection. Targeted therapies such as vandetanib, cabozantinib, and the selective RET inhibitors selpercatinib and pralsetinib are used for advanced disease.

Prognosis

Prognosis depends largely on stage at diagnosis and the completeness of surgery; patients with disease confined to the thyroid generally do well, while distant metastases carry a poorer outlook.
