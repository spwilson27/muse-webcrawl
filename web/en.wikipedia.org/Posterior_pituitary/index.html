The posterior pituitary (or neurohypophysis) is the posterior lobe of the pituitary gland, forming the rear part of the body's master endocrine gland at the base of the brain. Unlike the anterior pituitary, it is not a true gland that synthesises its own hormones; it is an extension of the nervous system, consisting of axons and nerve terminals projecting from the hypothalamus, together with supporting glial cells called pituicytes.

Structure and development

The posterior pituitary develops as a downward evagination of the floor of the diencephalon, and remains connected to the hypothalamus by the infundibulum (pituitary stalk). It comprises the pars nervosa, the infundibular stem, and the median eminence. Cell bodies of the relevant neurons lie in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus.

Hormones

Two peptide hormones, synthesised in hypothalamic neurons, are transported down their axons, stored in swellings called Herring bodies, and released into the circulation from the posterior pituitary:

  • Oxytocin — stimulates uterine contractions during labour and milk ejection during breastfeeding; it also has roles in social bonding and behaviour.
  • Vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone, ADH) — regulates water reabsorption in the kidneys and constricts blood vessels, controlling blood osmolarity and pressure.

Clinical significance

Damage to the posterior pituitary or its stalk, from tumours, surgery, trauma, or infiltrative disease, can cause central diabetes insipidus — failure of ADH release leading to large volumes of dilute urine and thirst. Conversely, inappropriate ADH secretion (SIADH), often from ectopic sources, causes water retention and hyponatremia. Tumours of the posterior pituitary itself, such as pituicytomas, are rare.
