The Galata Bridge (Turkish: Galata Köprüsü) is a bridge that spans the Golden Horn in Istanbul, Turkey. From the end of the 19th century in particular, the bridge has featured in Turkish literature, theatre, poetry and novels. It links Eminönü in the Fatih district — the historic old city — with Karaköy (ancient Galata) in Beyoğlu, and is lined with fishermen and restaurants, making it one of the city's best-known landmarks.

History

Crossings of the Golden Horn date to Byzantine and early Ottoman times; Leonardo da Vinci proposed an unbuilt bridge design for Sultan Bayezid II in 1502–1503, and Michelangelo was also invited to design one. The first modern Galata Bridge, built by the French company in 1845 at the order of Valide Sultan, was a wooden structure. Successive bridges followed in 1863, 1875 (an iron pontoon bridge by a British firm) and 1912 (a German-built floating bridge). The 1912 bridge survived until 1992, when it was badly damaged by fire and towed away to be replaced by the present structure.

The current bridge

The fifth bridge, completed in 1994, is a double-leaf bascule bridge 490 metres long with a deck 42 metres wide. It has two vehicle lanes and a walkway in each direction, a central reservation carrying the T1 tram line (extended across in 2005), and a lower deck of restaurants and cafés. The central span lifts to allow shipping into the Golden Horn.

Culture

Generations of anglers fishing from its railings have become a cliché of Istanbul life, and the bridge — with views to the Galata Tower on one side and the mosques of the old city on the other — is a staple of Turkish visual culture.
