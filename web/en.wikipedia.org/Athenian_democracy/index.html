Athenian democracy

Athenian democracy developed around the 6th century BC in the Greek city-state (polis) of Athens. It is the earliest known system of direct democracy, in which eligible citizens voted directly on legislation and executive bills, rather than electing representatives.

Development

The reforms of Solon (594 BC) laid early foundations, but democracy is generally credited to Cleisthenes (508/507 BC), who reorganized the citizen body into ten tribes and demes. Ephialtes and Pericles extended it in the 5th century BC, introducing pay for jury service so poorer citizens could participate.

Institutions

The sovereign body was the Ekklesia (assembly), open to all adult male citizens, which met on the Pnyx about forty times a year. The Boule of 500, chosen by lot, prepared business. Popular courts (dikasteria) with large juries selected by lot decided legal cases. Generals (strategoi) were elected and could be re-elected. Ostracism allowed the assembly to exile a citizen for ten years by popular vote.

Limitations

Only adult male citizens — perhaps 10–20% of the population — could participate; women, slaves, and resident aliens (metics) were excluded. Athenian democracy was interrupted by oligarchic coups in 411 and 404 BC and was finally suppressed by Macedon in 322 BC.
