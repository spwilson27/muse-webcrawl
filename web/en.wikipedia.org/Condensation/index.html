Condensation

Water vapour condensing on a cold surface.

Condensation is the change of the physical state of matter from the gas phase into the liquid phase, and is the reverse of vaporization. The word most often refers to the water cycle: water vapour in the air condenses into liquid water when it comes into contact with a cooler surface or is cooled to its dew point. Condensation releases latent heat, which is the energy absorbed during evaporation, and this release plays a major role in weather and in the operation of heat engines and refrigeration systems.

Initiation

Condensation begins when vapour is cooled or compressed, or when it encounters condensation nuclei—microscopic particles such as dust, salt, and smoke—around which droplets can form. In clouds, water vapour condenses on such cloud condensation nuclei to form droplets that may grow into rain. On surfaces, condensation appears as dew when objects cool overnight, or as frost when temperatures fall below freezing and vapour deposits directly as ice.

Applications

Condensation is exploited in distillation, where vapour is condensed to purify liquids; in steam turbines and power plants, where exhaust steam is condensed for reuse; and in air conditioning and refrigeration, where heat is moved by cycling a refrigerant through evaporation and condensation. Condensers are key components in chemical engineering, liquefaction of gases, and water harvesting devices such as fog collectors and atmospheric water generators.

In buildings

Unwanted condensation on windows and within wall cavities is a common building problem, contributing to mould growth and deterioration of materials. It is controlled by ventilation, insulation, heating, and vapour barriers that keep moist indoor air from reaching cold surfaces.
