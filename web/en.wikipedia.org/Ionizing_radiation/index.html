Ionizing radiation

Ionizing radiation
TypesAlpha, beta, gamma, X-rays, neutrons, high-energy particles
UnitsGray (absorbed dose), sievert (equivalent dose), becquerel (activity)
SourcesRadioactive decay, cosmic rays, medical and industrial devices

Ionizing radiation is radiation, travelling as a wave or as subatomic particles, that carries sufficient energy to detach electrons from atoms or molecules, thereby ionizing them. The threshold lies at roughly 10 electronvolts, corresponding to ultraviolet light at the high-frequency end of the spectrum; visible light and lower-frequency radiation are non-ionizing.

Types

Directly ionizing radiation includes charged particles such as alpha particles (helium nuclei), beta particles (electrons or positrons) and protons; indirectly ionizing radiation includes photons (gamma rays and X-rays) and neutrons, which ionize through secondary particles. Penetrating power varies greatly: alpha particles are stopped by paper or skin, beta particles by a few millimetres of metal, while gamma rays require dense shielding such as lead or concrete.

Sources and exposure

People are continuously exposed to background radiation from cosmic rays, terrestrial radioactivity in rocks and soil, radon gas and internal isotopes such as potassium-40, averaging about 2–3 millisieverts per year worldwide. Artificial sources include medical imaging and radiotherapy, nuclear power and weapons fallout, and occupational exposures.

Health effects

Ionization damages molecules including DNA. High doses cause acute radiation syndrome; lower doses carry a stochastic risk of cancer, the basis of the linear no-threshold model used in radiological protection. Protection follows the principles of justification, optimization (ALARA — as low as reasonably achievable) and dose limits, implemented through time, distance and shielding. Ionizing radiation is also put to beneficial use in cancer radiotherapy, medical imaging, sterilisation of equipment and smoke detectors.
