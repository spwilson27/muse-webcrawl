Diesel locomotive

A modern diesel-electric freight locomotive.

A diesel locomotive is a type of railway locomotive powered by an onboard diesel engine. Diesel locomotives largely replaced steam locomotives on the world's non-electrified railways during the mid-twentieth century, a transition known as dieselisation.

Transmission types

Because a diesel engine cannot start under load and works efficiently only over a limited speed range, the engine's power must reach the wheels through a transmission. In the dominant diesel-electric design, the engine drives a generator or alternator whose electricity powers traction motors geared to the axles; this provides excellent low-speed tractive effort and allows dynamic braking. Diesel-hydraulic locomotives use torque converters and were favoured in Germany, while diesel-mechanical designs, with a gearbox, are used in small shunters and railcars.

History

Experimental diesel locomotives appeared before the First World War, and practical switchers entered service in the 1920s. The breakthrough for mainline service came with streamlined diesel-electric passenger trains in the 1930s — such as the German Flying Hamburger and American streamliners — and with EMD's FT freight locomotive of 1939. By the 1960s steam had essentially vanished from North American and most other railways. Builders such as EMD (Electro-Motive Diesel) and GE Transportation became the dominant suppliers of freight locomotives.

Advantages and use today

Compared with steam, diesels are far more efficient, need no water stops or coaling infrastructure, start instantly, can be operated in multiple by a single crew, and require much less maintenance. Diesel-electric locomotives remain the standard for freight haulage across North America, Australia, and much of the world wherever lines are not electrified, while passenger services in densely trafficked regions often use electric traction or diesel multiple units.
