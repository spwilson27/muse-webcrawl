The Directory (also called the French Directory; French: Directoire) was the governing five-member committee in the French First Republic from 2 November 1795 until 9 November 1799, when it was overthrown by Napoleon Bonaparte in the Coup of 18 Brumaire and replaced by the French Consulate. It was the penultimate stage of the French Revolution.

Constitution and government

The Constitution of the Year III (1795) — drawn up after the Thermidorian Reaction against Robespierre — established a bicameral legislature: the Council of Five Hundred, which initiated legislation, and the Council of Ancients, which approved or rejected it. Executive power lay with five Directors chosen by the Ancients from a list drawn up by the Five Hundred; one Director stood down each year by lot. Property qualifications restricted the vote, and the regime deliberately sought a middle course between royalist restoration and Jacobin radicalism. Paul Barras was the only Director to serve throughout the regime's four years.

Instability and war

The Directory was plagued by inflation and the collapse of the assignat currency, corruption, royalist and Jacobin revivals, and chronic electoral defeats which it repeatedly answered with coups against its own legislature: the Coup of 18 Fructidor (1797) annulled royalist electoral gains and deported deputies; the Coup of 22 Floréal (1798) purged Jacobin winners; and the Coup of 30 Prairial (1799) forced out Directors themselves. Abroad, the War of the First Coalition wound down with French victories in Italy, where General Napoleon Bonaparte's dazzling campaigns of 1796–97 produced the Treaty of Campo-Formio; France annexed Belgium and the left bank of the Rhine and created "sister republics". Bonaparte's Egyptian expedition of 1798, though scientifically famous, ended in military failure, and the War of the Second Coalition in 1799 brought new French reverses.

Overthrow

On 9 November 1799 (18 Brumaire of the Year VIII), Napoleon, newly returned from Egypt, conspired with the Director Emmanuel Joseph Sieyès and others to overthrow the regime; the councils were dispersed by troops the following day at Saint-Cloud, and a Consulate of three consuls — with Napoleon as First Consul — took power, effectively ending the French Revolution.

Legacy

The Directory is often remembered for corruption, instability, and the stylish social world satirised in the Incroyables et Merveilleuses, but historians also credit it with consolidating some revolutionary gains, stabilising finances under able administrators, and extending French power in Europe to a degree that made Napoleon's later empire possible.
