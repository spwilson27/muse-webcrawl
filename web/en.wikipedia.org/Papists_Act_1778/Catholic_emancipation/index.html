Catholic emancipation was the process by which, between 1778 and the early 20th century, the restrictions imposed by the penal laws on Roman Catholics in Great Britain and Ireland were gradually removed. At its height the penal code barred Catholics from voting, sitting in Parliament, holding most public offices, owning land freely and educating their children in their faith.

Relief acts

The Papists Act 1778 was the first relief measure: it allowed Catholics who swore an oath of allegiance to inherit and purchase land and join the army, and repealed penalties against priests and schoolmasters. Its passage provoked the anti-Catholic Gordon Riots of 1780 in London. Further relief acts followed in 1791 and, in Ireland, 1793, which restored the franchise (though not seats in Parliament).

The 1829 settlement

Full emancipation came only after Daniel O'Connell's Catholic Association mobilised mass support in Ireland and O'Connell himself won the County Clare by-election of 1828 despite being barred from taking his seat. Fearing civil unrest, the Duke of Wellington's government passed the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, admitting Catholics to Parliament and almost all public offices.
