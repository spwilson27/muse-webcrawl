The Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829 (10 Geo. IV c. 7) was the culminating statute of Catholic emancipation in the United Kingdom. It repealed the Test and Corporation Acts' sacramental tests as they applied to Catholics and permitted Catholics to sit in Parliament and hold nearly all public offices, on taking a new oath denying the Pope's temporal authority and refusing allegiance to any foreign prince's civil jurisdiction.

Background

The Act followed Daniel O'Connell's victory in the County Clare by-election of July 1828, which exposed the contradiction of a Catholic being lawfully elected yet unable to sit. Prime Minister the Duke of Wellington and Home Secretary Robert Peel, both long opposed to emancipation, concluded that refusing it risked civil war in Ireland. King George IV gave his assent reluctantly.

Terms and consequences

A handful of offices remained closed to Catholics, including Lord Chancellor of England and Lord Lieutenant of Ireland. As a counterweight, the accompanying Parliamentary Elections (Ireland) Act raised the Irish county franchise qualification from 40 shillings to £10, disenfranchising many of the very freeholders whose votes had powered O'Connell's campaign. The 1829 Act removed the last major statutory disabilities of the penal era, closing a process begun, amid the backlash of the Gordon Riots, by the Papists Act 1778.
