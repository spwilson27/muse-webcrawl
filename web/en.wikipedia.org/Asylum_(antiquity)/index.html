Asylum (antiquity)


Temples and sanctuaries offered inviolable refuge in the ancient world.

In the ancient world, an asylum (from Greek asylon, "inviolable") was a place of refuge whose occupants could not lawfully be seized. Sanctuaries, temples and altars across the Greek and Roman world, and cities of refuge in ancient Israel, offered protection to fugitives, including runaway slaves and those accused of crimes, pending judgement or purification.

Greek practice

Greek temples enjoyed asylia, a recognised inviolability that certain sanctuaries and whole cities obtained formally from other states. Suppliants who grasped an altar or cult statue could not be removed by force without sacrilege, though they might be starved out or persuaded to leave. Famous sanctuaries such as Delphi and Delos held widely recognised rights of asylum.

Roman and biblical traditions

Rome's foundation legend depicts Romulus establishing an asylum on the Capitoline to attract settlers. The Hebrew Bible designates cities of refuge where those guilty of accidental manslaughter could flee the avenger of blood. In late antiquity the right of sanctuary passed to Christian churches, and the ancient institution is an ancestor of both ecclesiastical sanctuary and modern political asylum.
