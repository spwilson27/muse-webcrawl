Yoruba religion

The Yoruba religion (Yoruba: Ìṣẹ̀ṣe) comprises the traditional religious and spiritual concepts and practices of the Yoruba people of southwestern Nigeria and neighbouring Benin and Togo. It centres on a supreme creator, Olodumare (Olorun), and a pantheon of divinities called orishas, and it has profoundly influenced Afro-diasporic religions in the Americas.

Cosmology and practice

Olodumare, remote and transcendent, delegates the world's governance to the orishas — among them Orunmila (wisdom and divination), Shango (thunder and kingship), Ogun (iron and war), Yemoja (motherhood and waters), Oshun (love and rivers), Eshu/Elegba (the trickster messenger who carries sacrifices), and Obatala (creation and purity). Humans possess a chosen destiny (ayanmo) and a spiritual double (ori, "inner head") whose veneration is central.

Divination through Ifa — a UNESCO-recognised heritage practice — uses palm nuts or a divining chain (opele) interpreted by priests (babalawo) against a vast oral corpus of verses (odu). Ritual includes prayer (adura), offerings and sacrifice (ebo), festivals, drumming, and spirit possession dance.

Diaspora

Carried by enslaved Yoruba people, the religion seeded Cuban Santería (Regla de Ocha/Lucumí), Brazilian Candomblé, Trinidad Orisha, and related traditions, in which orishas were syncretised with Catholic saints. In the late 20th century, movements such as the Oyotunji African Village in South Carolina and a global "Orisha revival" reconnected diaspora practice with West African sources — a living faith with tens of millions of adherents.
