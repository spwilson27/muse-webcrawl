The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) is a legally non-binding resolution passed by the United Nations in 2007. It delineates and defines the individual and collective rights of Indigenous peoples, including their ownership rights to cultural and ceremonial expression, identity, language, employment, health, education, and natural resources.

Contents

The declaration's 46 articles affirm that Indigenous peoples have the right to self-determination, to maintain their distinct institutions and cultures, and to pursue their own economic, social, and cultural development. It emphasizes the right to free, prior and informed consent before states adopt measures or approve projects affecting Indigenous peoples or their territories, and prohibits forced assimilation and discrimination.

Adoption

The declaration was adopted by the General Assembly on 13 September 2007 by 144 votes to 4, with Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States voting against; all four later reversed their positions and endorsed it. The document was the product of more than twenty years of negotiation involving Indigenous representatives.

Status

Although a declaration rather than a treaty and therefore not legally binding, UNDRIP is influential: it has shaped national legislation (such as British Columbia's 2019 DRIPA and Canada's federal UNDRIP Act of 2021), court decisions, and the policies of international agencies.
