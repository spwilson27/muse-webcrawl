Dutch East Indies campaign

The Dutch East Indies campaign of 1941–42 was the conquest of the Dutch East Indies (present-day Indonesia) by forces from the Empire of Japan in the early days of the Pacific War. Forces from the Allies attempted unsuccessfully to defend the islands. The East Indies were targeted by the Japanese for their rich oil resources, which would become a vital asset during the war. The campaign and subsequent three-and-a-half years of Japanese occupation were a major factor in the end of Dutch colonial rule in the region.

Background

The East Indies were one of Japan's primary objectives in going to war because the colony possessed abundant supplies of precious natural resources, the most important of which were its rubber plantations and oil fields; the colony was the eighth largest exporter of oil in the world. Japan had been heavily dependent on imports for oil, largely from the United States; the American oil embargo of 1941 made securing the East Indies' oil essential to the Japanese war effort.

The campaign

Japan declared war on the Netherlands on 11 January 1942 and invaded Tarakan the same day. Japanese forces advanced rapidly through Borneo, Celebes, Sumatra and the Moluccas. The American-British-Dutch-Australian Command (ABDA) under General Sir Archibald Wavell attempted to coordinate resistance, but its naval forces were defeated in the Battle of the Java Sea on 27 February 1942.

Japanese troops landed on Java on 1 March 1942. On 8 March, the Dutch commander, Lieutenant-General Hein ter Poorten, surrendered along with some 58,000 troops at Bandung. The campaign ended the following day, leaving the Japanese in control of the entire archipelago.

Aftermath

The Japanese occupation lasted until the end of the war in 1945. The occupation sowed the seeds of the Indonesian National Revolution, and Indonesia proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945, two days after Japan's surrender.
