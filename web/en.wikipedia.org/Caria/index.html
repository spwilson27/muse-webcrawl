Caria

Caria (from Greek Karia) was a region of western Anatolia extending along the coast from mid-Ionia south to Lycia and east to Phrygia, in what is now southwestern Turkey. Its inhabitants, the Carians, spoke the Carian language, written in an alphabetic script, and were renowned in the ancient world as mercenary soldiers and seafarers.

History

Carians served as mercenaries in Egypt from the 7th century BC — Carian inscriptions survive as graffiti at Abu Simbel — and Herodotus, himself a native of the Carian city of Halicarnassus, reported that the Carians were credited with inventing helmet crests and shield devices. The region came under Persian rule as a satrapy; its most famous ruler was Mausolus (r. 377–353 BC), who ruled semi-independently and moved the capital to Halicarnassus. His monumental tomb, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, built by his widow Artemisia II, was one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World and gave us the word "mausoleum".

Cities and legacy

Important Carian cities included Halicarnassus, Miletus (on its northern edge), Cnidus, Caunus, Mylasa, Aphrodisias and Labraunda, many heavily Hellenised while retaining Carian cults, notably that of Zeus Karios. Under Rome, Caria was joined with Phrygia as a province. The Carian language, a member of the Anatolian branch of Indo-European, was deciphered in the late 20th century using Carian–Egyptian bilingual inscriptions.
