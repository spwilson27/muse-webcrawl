Masoretic Text

The Masoretic Text (MT or 𝕸; Hebrew: נֻסָּח הַמָּסוֹרָה, romanised: Nūssāḥ hamMāsōrā, lit. 'Text of the Tradition') is the authoritative Hebrew and Aramaic text of the 24 books of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) in Rabbinic Judaism. The Masoretic Text defines the Jewish canon and the precise letter-text of these biblical books, with their vocalisation (nikkud) and accentuation known as the Masorah.

History

The Masoretes were Jewish scribe-scholars active between the 6th and 10th centuries, chiefly in Tiberias and Jerusalem, who standardised the pronunciation, paragraph divisions and cantillation of the biblical text. The Tiberian school of Aaron ben Moses ben Asher produced the most authoritative tradition; its exemplar, the Aleppo Codex (c. 920 CE), is the oldest complete manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, though partly lost. The Leningrad Codex (1008 CE), the oldest complete surviving Masoretic manuscript, underlies modern critical editions such as Biblia Hebraica.

The Masorah

The Masoretes added marginal notes (the Masorah) counting verses, words and letters, and recording variant readings (qere and ketiv) and unusual spellings, in order to transmit the consonantal text with extraordinary fidelity. Comparison with the much older Dead Sea Scrolls shows the Masoretic Text preserves a textual tradition reaching back to the Second Temple period, though the Scrolls and the Septuagint sometimes preserve differing readings.
