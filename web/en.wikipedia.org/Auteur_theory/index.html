Auteur theory

Auteur theory holds that a film reflects the creative vision of its director, its "author".

Auteur theory, in film criticism, holds that a film's director is its primary creative author (French: auteur), and that a director's work bears a recognisable personal stamp across a body of films, in the same way as a novelist's or painter's does. The theory emerged from French film criticism of the 1950s and became one of the most influential — and contested — ideas in cinema studies.

Origins

The critic Alexandre Astruc proposed the idea of the caméra-stylo ("camera-pen") in 1948, arguing that filmmakers should use the camera as a writer uses a pen. In 1954 François Truffaut published "A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema" in Cahiers du cinéma, attacking the literary "Tradition of Quality" in French film and championing directors who imposed their personality on their material. Critics at Cahiers, including Jean-Luc Godard, Éric Rohmer, Jacques Rivette and Claude Chabrol, praised Hollywood directors such as Alfred Hitchcock, Howard Hawks, John Ford and Nicholas Ray — figures previously treated as studio craftsmen — as auteurs.

Development

The American critic Andrew Sarris imported the idea in his 1962 essay "Notes on the Auteur Theory" and applied it comprehensively in The American Cinema (1968), ranking directors in categories from the "Pantheon" down. Sarris proposed three criteria: technical competence, a distinguishable personality, and "interior meaning" arising from the tension between the director and the material.

Criticism

Pauline Kael's 1963 rebuttal "Circles and Squares" attacked the theory for elevating directors over screenwriters and for valuing consistency of style over quality. Later scholars noted that film is an industrial, collaborative art involving writers, cinematographers, editors, actors and studios, and structuralist and post-structuralist critics questioned the romantic notion of the solitary author. Variants such as "auteur-structuralism" shifted attention from the director's biography to recurring thematic and visual structures. Despite the criticisms, auteurism remains the default framework of much popular film criticism, festival programming and marketing.
