An odeon (Greek: ᾠδεῖον, ōideion, literally "singing place") was a building in ancient Greece and Rome used for musical performances, poetry recitations, singing competitions and sometimes council meetings. Odeons resembled small theatres but, unlike the open-air Greek theatre, they were usually roofed, giving them better acoustics for music and speech and making them suitable in all weather.

Characteristics

An odeon typically had a semicircular or rectangular auditorium with tiered seating, an orchestra, and a raised stage building (skene). Capacity was modest — often a few hundred to a few thousand spectators — compared with the great open theatres. The roof was commonly carried on columns or timber trusses spanning the seating.

Notable examples

  • The Odeon of Pericles in Athens (c. 442 BC), said to have been modelled on the tent of Xerxes, used for musical contests of the Panathenaea;
  • The Odeon of Herodes Atticus (AD 161) on the south slope of the Acropolis;
  • The Odeon of Agrippa in the Athenian Agora;
  • Odeons at Pompeii, Ephesus, Aphrodisias and Corinth, several doubling as council chambers (bouleuteria).

Later usage

The name survived into modern times: "Odeon" was widely adopted in the twentieth century for cinemas — most famously the British Odeon chain — and for concert halls and theatres in several countries.
