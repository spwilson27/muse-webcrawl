Douglas Sirk


Sirk's lush 1950s melodramas were later celebrated as subtle social critique.

Douglas Sirk (born Hans Detlef Sierck; April 26, 1897 – January 14, 1987) was a German film director best known for his Hollywood melodramas of the 1950s. Once dismissed as glossy soap operas, his films are now regarded as masterpieces of mise-en-scène and ironic social commentary.

Career

Sierck began his career in Weimar German theatre and directed films for UFA in the 1930s. Fleeing the Nazis, he emigrated to the United States in 1937, anglicised his name, and after work in various genres found his signature style at Universal-International. His celebrated run includes Magnificent Obsession (1954), All That Heaven Allows (1955), Written on the Wind (1956), The Tarnished Angels (1957), and Imitation of Life (1959).

Legacy

Sirk retired to Switzerland in 1959. French and later American critics, and directors such as Rainer Werner Fassbinder and Todd Haynes — whose Far from Heaven (2002) is an explicit homage — championed his work as a critique of Eisenhower-era conformity, materialism, and racial and sexual hypocrisy, concealed beneath a polished surface.
