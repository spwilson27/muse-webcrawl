Quiddity

In metaphysics, quiddity (Latin: quidditas) was another name for the essence of an object, literally its "whatness" — that is, the answer to the question "what is it?" (quid est?). The term derives from the Latin word quidditas, used by medieval scholastic philosophers, and ultimately from quid, "what". It was introduced into Latin philosophy through translations of Avicenna (Ibn Sina), whose distinction between essence and existence was highly influential.

Scholastic usage

For scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas, the quiddity of a material thing is composed of matter and form, and knowing the quiddity of a thing is knowing its definition. Duns Scotus distinguished quiddity from haecceity (Latin: haecceitas, "thisness"), the property that makes a thing this particular individual rather than another.

Later usage

In ordinary English, "quiddity" later came to mean a trifling nicety or quibble, an evasive distinction in argument — a sense used by Shakespeare in Hamlet, where Hamlet mocks "quiddities and quillities" of lawyers. In modern analytic philosophy the term is occasionally revived in discussions of essence and identity, where "quiddity" denotes a primitive, non-qualitative property that a thing has in virtue of being the kind of thing it is.
