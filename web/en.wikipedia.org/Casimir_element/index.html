Casimir element

In mathematics, a Casimir element is a distinguished central element of the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra. A prototypical example is the squared angular momentum operator used in three-dimensional rotations, which plays a central role in the representation theory of the rotation group.

Definition

Let g be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field, equipped with a nondegenerate invariant bilinear form. The Casimir element is the element of the universal enveloping algebra U(g) obtained by summing products of dual bases with respect to that form. It commutes with every element of U(g), hence acts as a scalar on any irreducible representation by Schur's lemma.

History

Casimir elements are named after the Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir, who identified them in his 1931 study of the rigid rotator. The concept was later generalized by Israel Gelfand and others to arbitrary semisimple Lie algebras.

Applications

Casimir elements label irreducible representations: for the Lie algebra sl(2), the Casimir eigenvalue distinguishes spin representations. In physics, Casimir invariants such as mass and spin classify elementary particles via the representations of the Poincaré group. Higher-order Casimir elements exist for higher-rank algebras and are related to the center of the enveloping algebra, described by the Harish-Chandra isomorphism.
