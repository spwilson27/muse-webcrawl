Pulmonary circulation

The pulmonary circulation is the portion of the cardiovascular system in which oxygen-poor blood is pumped from the heart, via the pulmonary arteries, to the lungs and oxygenated blood returns to the heart via the pulmonary veins. It is one of the two circuits of the double circulation in mammals and birds, the other being the systemic circulation.

Pathway

Deoxygenated blood leaves the right ventricle of the heart through the pulmonary trunk, which divides into right and left pulmonary arteries supplying each lung. After gas exchange in the pulmonary capillaries surrounding the alveoli — carbon dioxide is released and oxygen absorbed — oxygenated blood returns through the four pulmonary veins to the left atrium, then passes to the left ventricle for distribution to the body.

Characteristics

The pulmonary circuit is a low-pressure, low-resistance system: pulmonary arterial pressure averages about 15 mmHg, far lower than systemic pressures, allowing thin-walled vessels and protecting the delicate alveolar capillaries. The entire cardiac output passes through the lungs with each circuit. In the fetus, most blood bypasses the lungs through the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus, since gas exchange occurs at the placenta.

History

The pulmonary circulation was described by the Syrian physician Ibn al-Nafis in 1242, who correctly rejected Galen's theory of pores in the heart septum. Michael Servetus described it in 1553, and William Harvey's De Motu Cordis (1628) placed it within the complete theory of circulation.

Disorders

Diseases of the pulmonary circulation include pulmonary embolism (obstruction by a clot), pulmonary hypertension (elevated pressures), and pulmonary edema, which may arise from left-heart failure.
