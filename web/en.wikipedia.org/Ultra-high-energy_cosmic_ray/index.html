Ultra-high-energy cosmic ray

In astroparticle physics, an ultra-high-energy cosmic ray (UHECR) is a cosmic ray with an energy greater than 1 EeV (1018 electronvolts, roughly the energy of a tennis ball served at about 80 km/h packed into a single subatomic particle), far beyond both the mass and energy typical of other cosmic ray particles.

Extreme-energy particles

The most energetic particle ever observed was detected on 15 October 1991 by the Fly's Eye detector in Utah and dubbed the "Oh-My-God particle", with an estimated energy of about 3×1020 eV. A comparably extreme event, the "Amaterasu particle", was reported by the Telescope Array in 2023. Such particles arrive at a rate of roughly one per square kilometre per century, so enormous detectors are needed to study them.

The GZK limit

Above about 5×1019 eV, protons should interact with photons of the cosmic microwave background and lose energy — the Greisen–Zatsepin–Kuzmin (GZK) limit — so extreme particles must originate within roughly 100–160 million light-years. Observations by the Pierre Auger Observatory and Telescope Array show a suppression of the flux at the highest energies consistent with this effect, though the interpretation remains debated.

Sources

Candidate accelerators include active galactic nuclei, gamma-ray bursts, starburst galaxies and magnetars, but magnetic deflection makes it difficult to trace particles back to their sources. Arrival-direction studies have found correlations with nearby starburst galaxies and the supergalactic plane.
