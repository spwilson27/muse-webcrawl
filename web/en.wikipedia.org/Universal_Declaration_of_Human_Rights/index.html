The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) is an international document adopted by the United Nations General Assembly on 10 December 1948 at the Palais de Chaillot in Paris. Drafted in the aftermath of the Second World War and the Holocaust, it proclaims the inalienable rights to which all human beings are entitled, regardless of race, sex, nationality, religion, or any other status. The General Assembly adopted it by 48 votes to none, with eight abstentions.

Structure and content

The Declaration consists of a preamble and 30 articles. Articles 1–2 establish that "all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights" and are entitled to the Declaration's rights without distinction. Articles 3–21 set out civil and political rights: life, liberty, and security of person; freedom from slavery and torture; equality before the law; freedom of movement, thought, conscience, religion, opinion, and expression; and the right to participate in government. Articles 22–27 set out economic, social, and cultural rights, including social security, work, rest, an adequate standard of living, education, and participation in cultural life. Articles 28–30 address the conditions for realizing these rights and prohibit their abuse to destroy the rights of others.

Drafting and legacy

The drafting committee was chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt; the first draft was prepared by Canadian legal scholar John Peters Humphrey, with major contributions from René Cassin, Charles Malik, P. C. Chang, and Hansa Mehta (who secured the phrase "all human beings" in place of "all men"). Although not legally binding as a treaty, the Declaration is considered a foundational text of international human rights law; it inspired the International Covenants of 1966 and constitutions worldwide, and it is the most translated document in the world, available in over 500 languages.
