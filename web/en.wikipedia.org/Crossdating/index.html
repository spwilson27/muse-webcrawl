Crossdating

Crossdating is the principle of matching patterns of variation from one sample or context to another in order to establish dates. It is the foundational technique of dendrochronology, in which the sequence of wide and narrow tree rings in a piece of wood is matched against a master chronology built from overlapping samples, allowing the exact calendar year in which each ring grew to be identified.

In dendrochronology

Because trees in a region respond to the same climate, a dry year produces a narrow ring in all of them, creating a distinctive barcode-like pattern. By overlapping patterns from living trees, old buildings and archaeological timbers, chronologies can extend back thousands of years. The method, systematised by A. E. Douglass in the early twentieth century, dates timbers in archaeology and historic buildings and also underpins the calibration of radiocarbon dating.

Wider use

The term is also used in archaeology for dating a site or artefact by its occurrence in a securely dated context elsewhere — for example, an imported pottery type found in two places — and for comparable pattern-matching in ice cores and sediments, where distinctive marker layers such as volcanic ash are matched between sequences.
