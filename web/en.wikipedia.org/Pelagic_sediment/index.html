Pelagic sediment


Deep-sea sediment cores reveal ocean history

Pelagic sediments are fine-grained sediments that accumulate slowly on the deep ocean floor, far from land, by the settling of particles through the water column. Accumulating at rates of only millimetres to a few centimetres per thousand years, they blanket much of the abyssal plains and record the history of ocean life, chemistry, and climate in their layers.

Types

Pelagic sediment is classed by composition. Oozes contain at least 30% skeletal remains of microscopic organisms: calcareous oozes are made of the shells of foraminifera, coccolithophores, and pteropods, and occur above the calcite compensation depth; siliceous oozes are made of diatom and radiolarian tests and dominate beneath highly productive waters such as the Southern Ocean and the equatorial Pacific. Where biological remains are scarcer, the sediment is red clay (abyssal clay) — wind-blown dust, volcanic ash, and authigenic minerals accumulating extremely slowly in the deepest, least productive waters of the central ocean basins. Pelagic deposits also include manganese nodules scattered across some abyssal plains.

Significance

Because they accumulate continuously with little disturbance, pelagic sediment cores — recovered by ocean drilling programmes since 1968 — are a principal archive of past climate: the oxygen-isotope ratios and species assemblages of foraminifera shells document ice ages and ocean temperatures over tens of millions of years.
