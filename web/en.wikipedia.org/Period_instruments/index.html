Period instruments

Period instruments are musical instruments built or restored to the design and construction characteristic of the era in which the music performed on them was composed. The term is closely associated with the early music and historically informed performance movements, in which period-instrument orchestras and ensembles play Baroque, Classical and sometimes Romantic repertoire on instruments resembling those the composers would have known.

Characteristics

Period string instruments generally differ from their modern counterparts in having lower string tension, gut (or gut-core) strings, shorter and differently shaped necks and fingerboards, and bows of earlier design, such as the convex Baroque bow. Wind instruments include natural trumpets and horns without valves, one-keyed wooden flutes, and Baroque oboes and bassoons with simpler keywork. Keyboard instruments include the harpsichord, clavichord and fortepiano rather than the modern grand piano. Pitch standards are typically lower than the modern A = 440 Hz, often A = 415 Hz for Baroque repertoire, and historical temperaments may be used in tuning.

History

Interest in old instruments grew from the work of pioneers such as Arnold Dolmetsch in the early twentieth century and the instrument maker and scholar Thurston Dart. From the 1960s, specialist ensembles such as Concentus Musicus Wien, the Academy of Ancient Music, the English Concert and Les Arts Florissants made period instruments mainstream for earlier repertoire, and builders met demand with faithful reproductions of surviving museum instruments.
