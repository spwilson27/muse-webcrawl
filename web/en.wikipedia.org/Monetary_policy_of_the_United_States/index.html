Monetary policy of the United States

Monetary policy of the United States concerns those measures taken by the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, or "Fed," to influence the availability and cost of money and credit in the economy. It is the process by which the monetary authority of the United States controls the supply of money, often targeting an inflation rate or interest rate to ensure price stability and general trust in the currency.

United States monetary policy is formulated by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which consists of the seven members of the Board of Governors and five of the twelve Reserve Bank presidents. Under the Federal Reserve Act, the Fed pursues a "dual mandate": maximum employment and stable prices, together with moderate long-term interest rates.

History

The Federal Reserve was created by the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 in response to a series of financial panics, most notably the Panic of 1907. Earlier American experiments with central banking included the First and Second Banks of the United States (1791–1811 and 1816–1836). The Employment Act of 1946 and the Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977 codified the goals of monetary policy.

Important episodes include the Fed's role in the Great Depression, the Treasury–Fed Accord of 1951 that restored the Fed's independence, the Great Inflation of the 1970s, and Chairman Paul Volcker's sharp tightening in 1979–1982 that brought inflation down at the cost of a severe recession. Under Alan Greenspan and his successors, policy became more transparent, culminating in the adoption of an explicit 2 percent inflation target in 2012.

Tools

  • Federal funds rate target — the FOMC sets a target range for the overnight interbank lending rate, implemented largely through interest on reserve balances.
  • Open market operations — purchases and sales of Treasury and agency securities to adjust the supply of reserves.
  • Quantitative easing — large-scale asset purchases used at the effective lower bound on interest rates, notably in 2008–2014 and 2020–2022.
  • Discount window lending — loans to depository institutions at the primary credit rate.
  • Forward guidance — communication about the expected future path of policy.
  • Reserve requirements — historically used; set to zero in March 2020.

Transmission and effects

Changes in the federal funds rate affect other interest rates, asset prices, exchange rates, and ultimately spending, employment, and inflation, often with long and variable lags. Monetary policy operates as a key instrument of macroeconomic stabilization alongside fiscal policy, countering excessive inflation by tightening financial conditions and cushioning downturns by easing them.
