Treaty of Brétigny

Signed8 May 1360
LocationBrétigny, France
PartiesEngland; France

The Treaty of Brétigny was a treaty, drafted on 8 May 1360 and ratified on 24 October 1360 as the Treaty of Calais, between King Edward III of England and King John II of France. It brought the first phase of the Hundred Years' War to a close.

Background

John II had been captured at the Battle of Poitiers in 1356 and was held in England. The earlier Treaties of London, which imposed harsher terms, had been rejected by the French Estates General, and a renewed English campaign in 1359–60 persuaded both sides to compromise at Brétigny, a village near Chartres.

Terms

Edward III renounced his claim to the French throne in return for full sovereignty over an enlarged Aquitaine, together with Calais, Ponthieu, and other territories, amounting to roughly a third of France. John II was to be released for a ransom of three million gold crowns, paid in instalments, with hostages held as security. Because the formal renunciations were never fully exchanged as stipulated, the legal basis for renewed war remained; fighting resumed in 1369.
