Pictish stones

Pictish stones are monumental carved stones found across eastern and northern Scotland, created by the Picts, a confederation of peoples who lived in what is now Scotland during Late Antiquity and the Early Middle Ages. Dating roughly from the 6th to the 9th centuries AD, the stones bear a distinctive repertoire of symbols — including the crescent and V-rod, double disc and Z-rod, and the enigmatic "Pictish beast" — whose meanings remain debated.

Classification

Scholars classify the stones into three groups. Class I stones are unshaped boulders incised with symbols alone. Class II stones are shaped slabs bearing symbols in relief together with Christian crosses and figurative scenes, reflecting the Christianisation of the Picts. Class III stones lack the distinctive symbols and include cross-slabs and grave markers.

The symbols

Around thirty core symbols recur across the stones. They are often arranged in pairs, leading to hypotheses that they record names, lineages, marriages, or memorials to the dead. Animals — salmon, wolf, eagle, and boar — appear alongside abstract geometric designs and everyday objects such as mirrors and combs.

Notable stones

Famous examples include the Aberlemno stones in Angus, the Hilton of Cadboll Stone (now in the Museum of Scotland), the Drosten Stone at St Vigeans, and the Sueno's Stone near Forres, a towering cross-slab carved with a battle scene. Many stones remain in their original landscapes; others are preserved in local museums and the Meigle Sculptured Stone Museum.
