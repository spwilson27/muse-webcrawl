Opossums are marsupials of the order Didelphimorphia, native to the Americas. With more than 100 known species, they are the largest order of marsupials in the Western Hemisphere. Most are opportunistic omnivores with long snouts, prominent whiskers, and—in many species—a naked, prehensile tail used for grasping branches and carrying nesting material.

The word "opossum" derives from the Powhatan (Virginia Algonquian) word aposoum, meaning "white dog" or "white beast." Opossums are sometimes called "possums," though that name properly belongs to the unrelated Australasian marsupials of the suborder Phalangeriformes.

Characteristics

Opossums range in size from the tiny pygmy opossums to the Virginia opossum (Didelphis virginiana), which is about the size of a house cat. Females bear underdeveloped young after a very short gestation—under two weeks in the Virginia opossum—which crawl to a pouch (marsupium) and nurse for several months. Opossums have more teeth (50) than any other North American land mammal, an opposable, clawless hallux ("thumb") on the hind foot, and a remarkably low body temperature that makes them poor hosts for rabies.

"Playing possum"

When threatened, the Virginia opossum may fall into an involuntary catatonic state—"playing possum"—lying limp with mouth open and tongue lolling, emitting a foul-smelling fluid from its anal glands. The response, which can last from minutes to hours, is thought to discourage predators that prefer live prey or are deterred by the appearance of carrion.

Ecology

Opossums consume snails, slugs, insects, carrion, fruit, and garbage, and are noted for eating large numbers of ticks. In South America, species such as the water opossum or yapok are semi-aquatic. Opossums have expanded northward in North America over the past century, aided by human food sources and milder winters.
