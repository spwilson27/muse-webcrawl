Radio navigation

Radio navigation or radionavigation is the application of radio frequencies to determine a position on the surface of the Earth, as distinct from radar, which uses radio signals reflected from the target. Radio navigation systems use direction-finding, distance measurement, or hyperbolic techniques to calculate position, and historically were vital for ships and aircraft before and alongside satellite navigation.

Direction finding

The earliest radio navigation technique was radio direction finding (RDF), in which a receiver with a directional (loop) antenna determines the bearing to a transmitting station. By taking bearings to two or more stations, a navigator could triangulate a position. Non-directional beacons (NDBs) became standard aids for aviation, used with automatic direction finders (ADF).

Distance and hyperbolic systems

Later systems measured distance or time differences. The British Gee system of the Second World War and its successors used pulsed signals from ground chains; LORAN (Long Range Navigation) and its improved version LORAN-C used hyperbolic lines of position derived from time differences between signals from master and secondary stations, providing coverage over ocean areas. The Decca Navigator System used continuous-wave phase comparison and was widely used by shipping. Omega, a very-low-frequency global system, operated until 1997.

VOR and DME

For aviation, the VHF omnidirectional range (VOR), introduced after the Second World War, provides bearing information relative to a ground station, while distance measuring equipment (DME) provides slant-range distance. The combination allows precise position fixing along airways. TACAN provides similar functions for military users.

Satellite navigation

Satellite systems — GPS (United States), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (European Union) and BeiDou (China) — have largely superseded terrestrial radio navigation for most purposes, offering global, highly accurate positioning. Nevertheless, terrestrial systems remain as backups because satellite signals are weak and vulnerable to jamming and spoofing.
