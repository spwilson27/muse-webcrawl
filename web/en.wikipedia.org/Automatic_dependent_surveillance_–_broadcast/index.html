Automatic dependent surveillance – broadcast (ADS-B) is an aviation surveillance technology in which an aircraft determines its position by satellite navigation (GPS) and periodically broadcasts it, enabling it to be tracked. The information can be received by air traffic control ground stations and by other aircraft to provide situational awareness.

How it works

It is "automatic" because no external stimulus is required, and "dependent" because it relies on the aircraft's own navigation systems. The aircraft broadcasts its identity, position, altitude, velocity and other data roughly every second. Unlike radar, which measures position from the ground, ADS-B reports the position the aircraft itself computes, typically with high accuracy.

Links and receivers

Most of the world uses the 1090 MHz Mode S Extended Squitter link; the United States also permits a 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver for aircraft operating below 18,000 feet. Ground stations rebroadcast traffic information, and space-based receivers on satellite constellations now provide coverage over oceans and remote regions. Inexpensive software-defined radio receivers allow hobbyists to track aircraft, feeding public flight-tracking websites.

Adoption

ADS-B Out equipage became mandatory in much of US controlled airspace on 1 January 2020, with phased mandates in Europe, Australia and elsewhere. ADS-B is a cornerstone of next-generation air traffic management programmes such as NextGen and SESAR.

Criticism

The broadcasts are unencrypted and unauthenticated, raising concerns about spoofing and about the privacy of aircraft operators, although regulators regard the safety benefits as outweighing these risks.
