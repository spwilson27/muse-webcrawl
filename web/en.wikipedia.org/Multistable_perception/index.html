Multistable perception


Field: Perception, psychology, neuroscience
Examples: Necker cube, duck–rabbit, binocular rivalry

Multistable perception (or bistable perception when two interpretations compete) is a perceptual phenomenon in which an unchanging sensory stimulus gives rise to spontaneous alternations between two or more distinct interpretations. The stimulus stays the same, but the observer's experience flips.

Examples

Classic examples include the Necker cube, which reverses in depth; Rubin's vase, which alternates between vase and faces; the duck–rabbit and other ambiguous images; and structure-from-motion displays such as a spinning dancer silhouette. In binocular rivalry, different images presented to the two eyes compete for awareness. Auditory analogues include streaming of tone sequences.

Characteristics

Reversals occur involuntarily after a few seconds, though attention and intention can bias or hold an interpretation. Levelt's propositions describe how rivalry dynamics change with stimulus strength. Individuals differ in reversal rates, which have been linked to mood disorders and altered in some clinical conditions, making multistability a tool for studying consciousness and perceptual decision-making.

Neural mechanisms

Theories model alternations as competition between neuronal populations subject to adaptation and noise, with contributions from early sensory areas and frontoparietal networks. Multistable perception is widely used to isolate neural correlates of conscious perception, since the physical input is constant while awareness changes.
