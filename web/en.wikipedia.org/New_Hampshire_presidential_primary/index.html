The New Hampshire presidential primary is the first in a series of nationwide party primary elections held in the United States every four years as part of the process of choosing the Democratic and Republican nominees for president. Held in the small state of New Hampshire, it traditionally follows the Iowa caucuses by about a week and receives enormous media attention relative to the state's small delegate count, because it is the first primary election—and the second contest overall—of the nominating season.

History

New Hampshire first held a presidential primary in 1916. Its significance grew after 1952, when voters could vote directly for presidential candidates rather than only for delegates, enabling Estes Kefauver's upset of President Harry Truman on the Democratic side—an outcome that contributed to Truman's decision not to seek re-election. A 1975 state law requires the Secretary of State to set the primary date at least seven days ahead of any "similar election" in another state, a provision that has repeatedly pushed the primary into January as other states moved their contests earlier.

Significance

Because of its early position, the primary winnows the field: candidates who perform poorly usually drop out, while strong showings can generate momentum, donations, and media coverage. Retail politics dominates—candidates hold town hall meetings and visit diners and living rooms across the state. The midnight voting at Dixville Notch, where a handful of voters cast ballots just after midnight, became a celebrated tradition. Since 1952 no candidate has won the presidency without finishing in the top two in New Hampshire's primary of their party, a streak often cited in debates over the state's privileged role.

Criticism

Critics argue that New Hampshire's population is unrepresentative of the United States—whiter, older, and more rural—and that its outsized influence is undemocratic. Party rule changes in the 2020s, particularly the Democratic Party's decision to elevate South Carolina in its calendar, challenged New Hampshire's position, though the state retained its first-in-the-nation primary by law.
