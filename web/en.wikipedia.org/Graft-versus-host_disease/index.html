Graft-versus-host disease

SpecialtyHaematology, immunology
SymptomsSkin rash, diarrhoea, jaundice; chronic: dry eyes and mouth, skin tightening, lung disease
CauseDonor immune cells attacking recipient tissues after allogeneic stem cell transplant
PreventionHLA matching, calcineurin inhibitors, methotrexate, post-transplant cyclophosphamide, T-cell depletion
TreatmentCorticosteroids; ruxolitinib and other agents for steroid-refractory disease

Graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) is a medical complication of allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplantation in which mature donor immune cells — principally T cells contained in the graft — recognise the recipient's tissues as foreign and attack them. It is the mirror image of graft rejection, in which the recipient's immune system attacks the donor organ.

Acute GvHD

Acute GvHD was traditionally defined as occurring within the first 100 days after transplant, though modern classifications rely on clinical features rather than timing alone. It chiefly affects the skin (a maculopapular rash that may blister in severe cases), the gastrointestinal tract (profuse secretory diarrhoea, abdominal pain, bleeding) and the liver (cholestatic jaundice). Severity is graded I–IV; severe grade IV disease carries high mortality. Risk rises with the degree of HLA mismatch, older donor or recipient age, and the intensity of conditioning.

Chronic GvHD

Chronic GvHD resembles autoimmune disease and may involve the skin (lichenoid or sclerotic changes), mouth and eyes (dryness), lungs (bronchiolitis obliterans), joints, liver and gut. It is a leading cause of late non-relapse mortality and impaired quality of life after transplantation, and often requires years of immunosuppression.

Pathophysiology

The classic Billingham criteria state that GvHD requires immunocompetent cells in the graft, histoincompatibility between donor and recipient, and a recipient unable to eliminate the donor cells. Conditioning regimens damage host tissues, releasing inflammatory cytokines that activate antigen-presenting cells; donor T cells then proliferate against host minor histocompatibility antigens and are recruited to target organs, where cytotoxicity and cytokines cause injury. The same donor immunity can be beneficial as a graft-versus-leukaemia effect, reducing relapse.

Prevention and treatment

Prophylaxis typically combines a calcineurin inhibitor (ciclosporin or tacrolimus) with methotrexate, increasingly supplemented or replaced by post-transplant cyclophosphamide, which preferentially depletes alloreactive T cells. First-line treatment of significant acute GvHD is high-dose corticosteroids; steroid-refractory disease may respond to the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib, extracorporeal photopheresis, or other immunosuppressants. A transfusion-associated form of GvHD, caused by viable lymphocytes in blood products given to immunocompromised recipients, is prevented by irradiating cellular blood components.
