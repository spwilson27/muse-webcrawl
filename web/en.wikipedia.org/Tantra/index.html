Tantra (Sanskrit: तन्त्र) is an esoteric tradition of ritual, meditation, and doctrine that developed in India from about the middle of the first millennium CE, within Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. The word derives from a Sanskrit root meaning "to weave" or "extend", and a tantra is also a class of texts presenting these teachings, often as a dialogue between a god and goddess (e.g. Shiva and Shakti).

Characteristics

Tantric practice is characteristically initiatory and guru-centred: teachings are transmitted from teacher to disciple after initiation (diksha). Its toolkit includes mantra (sacred sound formulas), yantra and mandala (geometric diagrams contemplated or entered ritually), mudra (gestures), visualization of deities, and elaborate ritual worship (puja). Many tantric systems aim at liberation (moksha) together with worldly power and enjoyment (bhukti), holding the body and the senses to be instruments — rather than obstacles — to awakening. In Shaiva and Shakta traditions, divine energy (shakti) and the subtle body with its channels (nadis) and centres (chakras) are central themes.

Development and spread

From early medieval India tantra spread with Buddhism to Tibet (where its form is called Vajrayana, the "Diamond Vehicle"), Nepal, China, Japan (Shingon), and Southeast Asia, while Hindu tantra shaped temple ritual, yoga, and devotional practice across South Asia. Some late tantric schools employed deliberately transgressive rites (the "five M's") within tightly controlled ritual settings; these remain a small and often misunderstood part of the tradition as a whole.

In the modern West

Since the 20th century, "tantra" in Western popular culture has become heavily associated with sexuality and with modern "neotantra" movements, which draw selectively on translated texts while departing substantially from historical Indian practice — a distortion frequently noted by scholars.
