John Smeaton

Born: 8 June 1724, Austhorpe, Yorkshire, England
Died: 28 October 1792 (aged 68), Austhorpe
Occupation: Civil engineer, physicist
Known for: Eddystone Lighthouse (1759); coining the profession "civil engineer"; Smeatonian Society of Civil Engineers (1771)

John Smeaton FRS (8 June 1724 – 28 October 1792) was a British civil engineer responsible for the design of bridges, canals, harbours and lighthouses. He was also a capable mechanical engineer and an eminent physicist. Smeaton was the first person to describe himself as a "civil engineer", and is often regarded as the "father of civil engineering".

Early life and instruments

Smeaton was born at Austhorpe near Leeds. Intended for the law, he left his father's office to become a mathematical instrument maker in London, developing precision instruments including a pyrometer and a whirling arm apparatus for aerodynamic experiments. Elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1753, he won its Copley Medal in 1759 for his experimental research into the power of water and wind to turn mills, work that established the concept of maximum efficiency in waterwheels and introduced the distinction between overshot and undershot wheels.

Eddystone Lighthouse

Smeaton's most famous work is the third Eddystone Lighthouse, built 1756–1759 on the dangerous Eddystone Rocks off Plymouth. Drawing on oak-tree geometry, he constructed a tapering tower of dovetailed granite blocks pinned together and set in hydraulic lime of his own devising — a rediscovery of the Roman practice of adding pozzolana to make cement set under water. The lighthouse stood for over 120 years and became the model for lighthouse design worldwide; when it was eventually replaced, its upper portion was re-erected on Plymouth Hoe as a memorial.

Other works and legacy

Smeaton designed numerous bridges (including Coldstream and Perth bridges), canals such as the Forth and Clyde Canal, harbour works at Ramsgate and Aberdeen, and drainage schemes in the Fens. In 1771 he founded the Society of Civil Engineers, later renamed the Smeatonian Society, the first engineering society in the world. The Institution of Civil Engineers' predecessor tradition traces directly to his professional model of consultancy grounded in experiment.
