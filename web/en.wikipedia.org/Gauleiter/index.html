Gauleiter

A Gauleiter (German pronunciation: [ˈɡaʊ̯ˌlaɪ̯tɐ]) was the leader of a regional branch of the Nazi Party (NSDAP) in Nazi Germany, in charge of a Gau, or district. The Gauleiter were among the most powerful figures in the regime, answering directly to Adolf Hitler, and they exercised wide authority over party affairs and, increasingly, over the civil administration of their regions.

Role and history

The office was created in the mid-1920s when the refounded Nazi Party organised Germany into districts for propaganda and electoral purposes. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933 the Gauleiter — many of them "old fighters" from the party's early years — became rivals to the state governors (Reichsstatthalter), an office several of them also held. During the Second World War their powers grew further: most were appointed Reich Defence Commissioners for their districts, responsible for civil defence, mobilisation of labour, and, in 1944–45, command of the Volkssturm militia.

Aftermath

Because they were appointed by and reported personally to Hitler, the Gauleiter formed a key element of the polycratic Nazi state, and several played leading roles in the persecution of Jews and other crimes in their districts. After the war a number were tried and executed or imprisoned, including Julius Streicher (hanged at Nuremberg), Arthur Greiser, and Amon Göth's superior officials in occupied Poland; others committed suicide in 1945. The rank of Gauleiter was the second-highest in the Nazi Party, below only the rarely conferred Reichsleiter.
