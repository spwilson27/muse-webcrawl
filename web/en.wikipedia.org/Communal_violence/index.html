Communal violence

Communal violence is a form of violence perpetrated across ethnic, religious, or other communal lines, in which the perpetrators feel solidarity for their respective groups and victims are chosen on the basis of group membership. The term includes conflicts between ethnic, religious, tribal, or caste groups, as well as violence between majority and minority communities within a state.

Causes

Communal violence is often driven by competition over resources, political power, land, or status, and can be inflamed by rumours, hate speech, and historical grievances. Political entrepreneurs may mobilise communal identity to gain electoral advantage, a process sometimes called the instrumentalisation of ethnic or religious conflict. Weak state institutions, impunity for past violence, and segregated settlement patterns are widely cited risk factors.

Forms

Communal violence ranges from targeted assassinations and pogroms to riots, massacres, and full-scale civil war. Riots are a recurrent form: in South Asia, Hindu–Muslim riots have been a persistent feature of urban life since the colonial period, notably during the Partition of India in 1947, the anti-Sikh riots of 1984, and the Gujarat riots of 2002. In Northern Ireland, sectarian violence between Protestant unionists and Catholic nationalists characterised the Troubles.

Prevention

Scholars and policymakers emphasise the role of intercommunal civic networks, impartial policing, rapid official response to rumours, and prosecution of offenders in preventing escalation. Peace committees, integrated schooling, and mixed residential patterns have been associated with lower incidence of riots in otherwise similar cities.
