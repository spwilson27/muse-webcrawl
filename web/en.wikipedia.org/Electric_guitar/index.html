Electric guitar

An electric guitar is a guitar that requires external amplification in order to be heard at typical performance volumes. It uses one or more electromagnetic pickups to convert the vibration of its metal strings into electrical signals, which are sent to an amplifier and loudspeaker. The electric guitar was invented in the early 1930s and became central to jazz, blues, country, rock, and popular music generally.

A solid-body electric guitar, the type popularised by Fender and Gibson in the 1950s.

History

The first commercially successful electric guitar was the lap steel "Frying Pan," developed by George Beauchamp and Adolph Rickenbacker around 1931. Hollow-body archtop electrics such as the Gibson ES-150 followed for jazz guitarists. In 1950–51 Leo Fender launched the Telecaster and the first mass-produced solid-body guitar, and Gibson answered with the Les Paul (1952) and Fender with the Stratocaster (1954). These designs remain iconic.

Construction and sound

Electric guitars may be solid-body, semi-hollow, or hollow-body. Single-coil pickups give a bright, clear tone; humbucking pickups give a thicker sound with less hum. Amplifiers and effects—overdrive, distortion, reverb, delay—shape the instrument's voice, and feedback and distortion became expressive tools in the hands of players such as Jimi Hendrix, Eric Clapton, and Jimmy Page.

Cultural impact

The electric guitar became the emblematic instrument of rock and roll and youth culture in the second half of the 20th century, driving genres from rockabilly and surf to heavy metal, punk, and indie rock.
