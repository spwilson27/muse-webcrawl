Burlesque

Burlesque is a literary, dramatic, or musical work intended to cause laughter by caricaturing the manner or spirit of serious works, or by ludicrous treatment of their subjects. In American theatre, the term came to denote a variety entertainment combining parody, satire, comedy sketches, and — from the early twentieth century — the striptease acts for which it became famous.

Literary burlesque

Burlesque literature ranges from "high burlesque", in which an elevated style is applied to trivial subjects (as in Alexander Pope's mock-epic The Rape of the Lock), to "low burlesque", where a serious subject is treated frivolously. The form overlaps with parody, travesty, and the mock-heroic.

Victorian and American burlesque

In Victorian Britain, burlesque was a popular stage genre of comic parodies of opera and classical plays, full of puns and topical songs. Exported to the United States, burlesque evolved into a touring variety format of comedians, chorus lines, and striptease. Performers such as Gypsy Rose Lee became celebrities, and comedians including Abbott and Costello and Phil Silvers honed their craft in burlesque houses. Campaigns against indecency, notably in New York in the 1930s and 1940s, drove the classic format into decline.

Neo-burlesque

Since the 1990s a revival known as neo-burlesque has combined classic striptease glamour with performance art, satire, and body-positive and feminist themes, with festivals and troupes in cities worldwide.
