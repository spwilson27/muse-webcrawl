Animal welfare

Animal welfare concerns how animals are coping with the conditions in which they live.

Animal welfare is the well-being of nonhuman animals. Formal standards of animal welfare vary between contexts and are debated, but a widely accepted definition is that an animal is in a good state of welfare if it is healthy, comfortable, well-nourished, safe, able to express innate behaviour, and not suffering from unpleasant states such as pain, fear, and distress. Animal welfare science uses measures such as longevity, disease, immunosuppression, behaviour, and physiology to assess the state of an animal.

Animal welfare differs from animal rights: welfare frameworks generally accept human use of animals provided suffering is minimised, whereas rights frameworks typically reject the property status of animals altogether.

History

Systematic concern for the welfare of farm animals began in Britain with Martin's Act of 1822, the world's first parliamentary legislation protecting animals, and the founding of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (later the RSPCA) in 1824. Ruth Harrison's Animal Machines (1964) exposed the conditions of intensive farming and prompted the United Kingdom's Brambell Committee, whose 1965 report laid the foundations of modern welfare science and led to the development of the Five Freedoms.

Approaches

Five Freedoms

The Five Freedoms, formalised by the UK Farm Animal Welfare Council, hold that animals should have: (1) freedom from hunger and thirst; (2) freedom from discomfort; (3) freedom from pain, injury, or disease; (4) freedom to express normal behaviour; and (5) freedom from fear and distress.

Five Domains

The Five Domains model, developed by David Mellor and colleagues, extends the framework across nutrition, environment, health, behavioural interactions, and mental state, shifting emphasis from merely avoiding negatives to promoting positive experiences.

Issues

Major welfare issues include intensive confinement systems for poultry and pigs (battery cages, gestation crates), long-distance transport, slaughter methods, painful procedures performed without anaesthesia such as castration and dehorning, the welfare of laboratory animals (addressed through the Three Rs: replacement, reduction, refinement), and welfare in zoos, aquaria, and the pet trade.

Legislation

The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) includes animal welfare in its Terrestrial Animal Health Code. The European Union recognises animals as sentient beings (Treaty of Lisbon, Article 13 TFEU) and has banned battery cages for laying hens (2012) and cosmetics testing on animals. In the United States, the federal Animal Welfare Act of 1966 covers some animals in research, exhibition, and transport but excludes farm animals used for food.
