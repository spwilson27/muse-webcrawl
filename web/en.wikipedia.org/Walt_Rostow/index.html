Walt Whitman Rostow (October 7, 1916 – February 13, 2003) was an American economist, professor, and political theorist who served as national security advisor to U.S. president Lyndon B. Johnson from 1966 to 1969. He is best known for his book The Stages of Economic Growth: A Non-Communist Manifesto (1960), which proposed the Rostowian take-off model of economic development, dividing growth into five stages: traditional society, preconditions for take-off, take-off, drive to maturity, and the age of high mass consumption.

Career

Rostow studied at Yale and, as a Rhodes Scholar, at Balliol College, Oxford. During World War II he served in the Office of Strategic Services. After the war he taught at Oxford, Cambridge, and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where he was professor of economic history. He advised John F. Kennedy during the 1960 presidential campaign and served in the Kennedy and Johnson administrations, first as deputy national security advisor under McGeorge Bundy, then at the State Department, and finally as national security advisor.

Vietnam War

Rostow was a prominent advocate of American military involvement in Southeast Asia and a key adviser during the escalation of the Vietnam War, supporting the bombing of North Vietnam. His role made him a controversial figure; after leaving government in 1969 he taught at the University of Texas at Austin, where he remained for the rest of his life.
