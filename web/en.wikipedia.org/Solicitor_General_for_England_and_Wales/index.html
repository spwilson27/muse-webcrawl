Solicitor General for England and Wales

His Majesty's Solicitor General for England and Wales, known informally as the Solicitor General, is one of the law officers of the Crown in the government of the United Kingdom. The Solicitor General is the deputy of the Attorney General, whose duty is to advise the Crown and Cabinet on matters of law. The Solicitor General can exercise the powers of the Attorney General when that office is vacant or its holder is incapacitated.

Role and functions

Along with the Attorney General, the Solicitor General is a minister in the Attorney General's Office. The post-holder supports the Attorney General in providing legal advice to government departments, in superintending the Crown Prosecution Service and the Serious Fraud Office, and in representing the government in important court cases. The Solicitor General also performs functions such as referring unduly lenient sentences to the Court of Appeal and intervening in litigation in the public interest.

By convention, the Solicitor General is a Member of Parliament (or occasionally a peer) and is almost always a King's Counsel (KC). Although historically the "solicitor" title suggests the solicitors' branch of the profession, holders are in practice barristers.

History

The office developed in the 15th and 16th centuries as the Crown's legal business grew. By the 19th century the Solicitor General was established as the second law officer, and the post has often served as a stepping stone to the office of Attorney General or to senior judicial appointment. Famous holders include Sir Edward Carson, F. E. Smith (later Earl of Birkenhead), and, in the modern era, figures who have gone on to lead their political parties.

Appointment

The Solicitor General is appointed by the monarch on the advice of the Prime Minister and holds office at the pleasure of the Crown, typically changing with governments. Upon appointment, the holder is usually knighted or appointed a dame if not already so honoured, and is sworn of the Privy Council.

Other jurisdictions

Scotland has its own Solicitor General for Scotland, deputy to the Lord Advocate. Many Commonwealth countries and U.S. states also have solicitors general, though their roles differ; the Solicitor General of the United States, for example, represents the federal government before the Supreme Court.
