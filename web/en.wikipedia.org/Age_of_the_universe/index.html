Age of the universe
Value: 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years (Planck, 2018)

The age of the universe is the time elapsed since the Big Bang. Current observations suggest a value of 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years within the concordance Lambda-CDM model, derived most precisely from measurements of the cosmic microwave background by the Planck satellite, published in 2018.

Measurement

Within the standard cosmological model, the age is computed from the Hubble constant and the densities of matter and dark energy, which determine the expansion history of the universe. Independent lower-bound checks come from the ages of the oldest stars and globular clusters (via nucleocosmochronology and cooling white dwarfs) and the ages of the chemical elements from radioactive decay; these consistently give ages somewhat below but compatible with the CMB-derived figure. Earlier measurements by WMAP gave 13.772 ± 0.059 billion years. Discrepancies in local measurements of the Hubble constant (the "Hubble tension") could in principle affect the derived age, but have not overturned the standard value.

History

Estimates have changed dramatically over time: from thousands of years in traditional chronologies, to millions based on geology, to a few billion after Edwin Hubble's discovery of cosmic expansion in 1929, and finally to the modern ~13.8 billion years as cosmological parameters were pinned down.
