Satellite constellation

Satellite constellation
TypeGroup of artificial satellites in coordinated orbits
ApplicationsNavigation, communications, Earth observation
ExamplesGPS, Starlink, Iridium, Galileo

A satellite constellation is a group of artificial satellites working together as a system. Unlike a single satellite, a constellation can provide permanent global or near-global coverage, such that at any time everywhere on Earth at least one satellite is in view. Satellites in constellations are typically placed in sets of complementary orbital planes and connect to geographically distributed ground stations.

Design

Constellation design involves trade-offs among the number of satellites, orbital altitude, inclination, and the number of orbital planes. A common geometry is the Walker constellation, described by a notation giving the total number of satellites, the number of planes, and the relative phasing between satellites in adjacent planes. Medium Earth orbit (MEO) constellations such as GPS use a few dozen satellites, while low Earth orbit (LEO) systems may require hundreds or thousands because each satellite's footprint is smaller and its orbital period shorter.

Applications

Navigation

Global navigation satellite systems (GNSS) are the classic example: the United States' GPS, Russia's GLONASS, the European Union's Galileo, and China's BeiDou each use constellations of roughly 24–30 MEO satellites so that a receiver can see at least four satellites anywhere on Earth.

Communications

The Iridium constellation (66 operational satellites in LEO) provides global voice and data service, including at the poles. Broadband "megaconstellations" such as SpaceX's Starlink and Amazon's Project Kuiper plan thousands of LEO satellites to deliver low-latency internet access.

Earth observation

Constellations of small satellites, such as those operated by Planet Labs, image the entire land surface of the Earth daily, enabling applications in agriculture, disaster response, and environmental monitoring.

Concerns

Large constellations have raised concerns about orbital debris and collision risk (see Kessler syndrome), interference with astronomical observations, and the long-term sustainability of the orbital environment. Operators increasingly build in autonomous collision avoidance and end-of-life deorbiting.
