The Walls of Constantinople are a series of defensive stone walls that surrounded and protected the city of Constantinople (today Istanbul) from its founding as the new capital of the Roman Empire by Constantine the Great in the fourth century. With successive additions and modifications, they formed one of the most complex and sophisticated defensive systems of the ancient and medieval world.

History

The first walls were built by Constantine I around 328–330. As the city grew, the emperor Theodosius II commissioned a new, stronger circuit in the fifth century under the praetorian prefect Anthemius. These Theodosian Walls — a triple line of defense with a moat, an outer wall, and a massive inner wall studded with towers — protected the city's western, landward side.

Sieges

The walls repelled numerous sieges, including those by the Avars and Persians in 626, the Arabs in 674–678 and 717–718, the Bulgars, and the Rus'. They were overcome only twice before the age of gunpowder: by the Crusaders of the Fourth Crusade in 1204, and in part by treachery and scaling rather than by breach. In 1453 the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II's great bombards finally battered the walls, contributing to the fall of Constantinople on May 29, 1453.

The Theodosian Walls today

Substantial stretches of the land walls survive along their roughly 6.5-kilometre course, including the Golden Gate and numerous towers, and sections have been restored. The sea walls along the Golden Horn and the Sea of Marmara survive only in fragments.
