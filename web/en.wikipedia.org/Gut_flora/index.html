Gut flora

Gut flora, or gut microbiota, refers to the community of microorganisms — bacteria, archaea, fungi and viruses — that inhabit the digestive tracts of humans and other animals. The terms "gut flora" and "gut microbiome" are often used interchangeably, although technically the microbiome denotes the collective genomes of the microorganisms. The human gut harbours tens of trillions of microorganisms, with the greatest density in the colon.

Composition

The gut microbiota is dominated by two bacterial phyla, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, together with Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria and others. Common genera include Bacteroides, Faecalibacterium, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. Each person's microbial community is distinctive, shaped by birth mode, diet, antibiotics, geography and age. Colonisation begins at birth and stabilises into an adult-like configuration in early childhood.

Functions

Gut microbes ferment otherwise indigestible dietary fibre into short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, which nourish the colon lining; synthesise vitamins B and K; metabolise bile acids and drugs; train and modulate the immune system; and resist colonisation by pathogens. Disruption of the community (dysbiosis) has been associated with inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, allergies and even neurological conditions, though many associations remain under investigation.

Research

Large-scale projects such as the Human Microbiome Project have catalogued the microbes of healthy adults. Therapies manipulating gut flora include probiotics, prebiotics and faecal microbiota transplantation, the last of which is highly effective against recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection.
