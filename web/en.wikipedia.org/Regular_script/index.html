Regular script

The regular script (Chinese: 楷書; pinyin: kǎishū), also called kaishu or "standard script," is the youngest major style of Chinese calligraphy and the standard form in which Chinese characters are printed and taught. It emerged during the late Han dynasty, maturing in the Wei and Jin periods, and remained the orthodox hand for over a millennium.

Characteristics

In regular script every stroke is written separately and in a prescribed order, with characters occupying a uniform square space. Compared with the flowing semi-cursive and cursive scripts, kaishu is slow, upright and highly legible; its structure forms the basis of the glyphs used in modern printed typefaces (the corresponding printed style in Chinese typography is often called Song or Ming type).

History

Calligrapher Zhong Yao is traditionally credited with developing the script, and it was perfected by masters such as Wang Xizhi. In the Tang dynasty the "Four Great Masters of Kaishu" — Ouyang Xun, Yan Zhenqing, Liu Gongquan and (in the Yuan) Zhao Mengfu — set canonical models still copied by students. Regular script is the first style taught to children and learners of Chinese, Japanese (kaisho) and Korean handwriting.
