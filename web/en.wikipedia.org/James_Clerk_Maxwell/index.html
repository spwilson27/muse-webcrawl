James Clerk Maxwell (June 13, 1831 – November 5, 1879) was a Scottish physicist and mathematician. His most notable achievement was formulating the classical theory of electromagnetic radiation, bringing together electricity, magnetism, and light as manifestations of the same phenomenon.

Electromagnetism

In papers published from 1861 and in his Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism (1873), Maxwell presented the set of equations — now known as Maxwell's equations — that describe how electric and magnetic fields are generated and altered by each other and by charges and currents. The equations predicted electromagnetic waves traveling at the speed of light, leading Maxwell to conclude that light itself is an electromagnetic wave — a unification ranked among the greatest in physics.

Other work

Maxwell developed the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution describing molecular speeds in gases, foundational to statistical mechanics. He analyzed the stability of Saturn's rings, produced the first durable color photograph in 1861, and contributed to control theory and the thought experiment known as Maxwell's demon. As the first Cavendish Professor of Physics at Cambridge, he oversaw the establishment of the Cavendish Laboratory. He died of abdominal cancer at 48.
