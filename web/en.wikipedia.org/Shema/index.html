Shema

Shema Yisrael (Hebrew: שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, "Hear, O Israel") is a Jewish prayer and declaration of faith, centred on the verse from Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: the LORD is our God, the LORD is one." Recited morning and evening, it is the best-known affirmation of Jewish monotheism and of acceptance of God's sovereignty, and stands at the heart of the morning and evening prayer services. The full Shema comprises three Torah passages: Deuteronomy 6:4–9, Deuteronomy 11:13–21, and Numbers 15:37–41.

Content and meaning

The first verse proclaims the unity and uniqueness of God. In the Torah scroll and in prayer books, its first and last words are written with enlarged letters — the final letter of Shema (ayin) and of echad ("one", dalet) — which together spell ed, "witness". The first paragraph (the Ve'ahavta, "and you shall love") commands love of God "with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your might", teaching the words to one's children, and inscribing them on doorposts — the basis of the mezuzah — and binding them as a sign on hand and forehead — the basis of tefillin. The second paragraph links obedience to the commandments with rain and agricultural blessing. The third paragraph commands the wearing of tzitzit (ritual fringes) and recalls the Exodus from Egypt.

Recitation and customs

The Shema is recited aloud, traditionally with the right hand covering the eyes during the first verse to concentrate devotion. It is surrounded in the liturgy by blessings (two before and one after in the morning; two before and two after in the evening). The bedtime Shema is recited before sleep, and the prayer is traditionally said by the dying and by martyrs with their last breath — most famously by Rabbi Akiva, who, according to the Talmud, prolonged the word echad as he was executed.

In other traditions

The Shema is quoted in the New Testament: when asked for the greatest commandment, Jesus cites Deuteronomy 6:4–5 together with Leviticus 19:18 ("love your neighbour as yourself"). The affirmation of God's unity is a point of contact and of theological discussion between Judaism and the other Abrahamic religions.
