Religious affiliation

Predominant religious affiliations by country.

Religious affiliation refers to a person's self-identified association with a religion, denomination or faith community, whether or not the person actively practises it. It is a standard demographic variable in censuses and social surveys and is used by bodies such as the Pew Research Center to estimate the size and distribution of the world's religious populations.

Measurement

Affiliation is usually measured by self-report: respondents choose from a list of religions, or answer an open question. Results depend heavily on question wording and context. In much of Western Europe, many people report a nominal affiliation (for example to a national church) while rarely attending worship — a pattern summed up by sociologist Grace Davie's phrase "believing without belonging", and its inverse, belonging without believing. In East Asia, affiliation categories fit poorly, since many people combine Buddhist, Taoist and folk practices without exclusive membership.

Trends

Pew's global estimates (2020) put Christians at about 31% of world population, Muslims at about 25%, the religiously unaffiliated at about 16% (concentrated in China and high-income countries), Hindus at about 15%, Buddhists at about 7%, and Jews at about 0.2%. The religiously unaffiliated — atheists, agnostics and "nothing in particular" — are the fastest-growing category in many Western countries; in the United States the "nones" reached roughly 28–29% of adults in surveys of the early 2020s. Globally, however, higher fertility among religious populations means the unaffiliated share is projected to shrink slightly by mid-century even as it grows in the West.

Legal significance

Several states formally register affiliation: German residents may be liable for church tax (Kirchensteuer) according to registered affiliation, and identity documents in some countries record religion, with consequences for personal-status law.
