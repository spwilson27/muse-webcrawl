The City of God


A page from a medieval illuminated manuscript of De civitate Dei.

The City of God Against the Pagans (Latin: De civitate Dei contra paganos), often known as The City of God, is a book of Christian philosophy written in Latin by Augustine of Hippo, begun in 413 and completed around 426 AD. Augustine wrote it in response to pagan claims that the sack of Rome by the Visigoths in 410 was punishment for Rome's abandonment of its traditional gods. It is considered one of the most influential works of Western thought, a cornerstone of medieval political philosophy and theology.

Structure and themes

The work comprises 22 books. The first ten refute the pagan charges and attack Roman religion and philosophy; the remaining twelve develop Augustine's positive vision of two symbolic cities. The "City of God" is the community of the redeemed, marked by love of God extending to contempt of self, living by faith as pilgrims in the world; the "Earthly City" is formed by love of self extending to contempt of God. The two cities are intermixed in earthly history and will be separated only at the Last Judgment. Augustine rejects the classical equation of political fortune with divine favor and develops a linear, providential account of history in place of cyclical pagan models.

Influence

The City of God shaped medieval ideas about the relationship between the Church and secular authority, the nature of history, and the justification of war, providing early material for what became just war theory. It also contains Augustine's critiques of Roman virtue, his reflections on suicide in the case of Lucretia, and his discussions of peace, theodicy, and original sin. The phrase "City of God" became a lasting symbol in Christian thought, later borrowed, among others, by the founders of Salt Lake City and by Bishop John Ireland for Saint Paul, Minnesota.
