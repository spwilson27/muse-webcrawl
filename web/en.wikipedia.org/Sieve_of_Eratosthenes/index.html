Sieve of Eratosthenes

In mathematics, the sieve of Eratosthenes is an ancient algorithm for finding all prime numbers up to any given limit. It does so by iteratively marking as composite (i.e., not prime) the multiples of each prime, starting with the first prime number, 2. The multiples of a given prime are generated as a sequence of numbers starting from the square of that prime, with constant difference between them equal to that prime. This is the sieve's key distinction from using trial division to sequentially test each candidate number for divisibility by each prime.

Overview

The algorithm proceeds as follows: create a list of consecutive integers from 2 to n. Initially, let p equal 2, the smallest prime. Enumerate the multiples of p by counting in increments of p from 2p to n, and mark them as composite. Then find the smallest number greater than p that is not marked; if there is none, stop — otherwise it is the next prime, and the process repeats. The numbers remaining unmarked at the end are all the primes below n.

History

The sieve is attributed to Eratosthenes of Cyrene (3rd century BC), the Greek mathematician and chief librarian at the Library of Alexandria, who also calculated the circumference of the Earth. The method is described in Nicomachus's Introduction to Arithmetic.

Complexity and variants

The sieve runs in time O(n log log n) and is among the most efficient ways to find small primes. Later sieves include the sieve of Sundaram, the sieve of Atkin, and wheel factorisation optimisations; the concept underlies modern sieve theory in number theory, including the large sieve and the Green–Tao theorem's methods.
