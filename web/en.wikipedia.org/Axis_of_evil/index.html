Axis of evil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

"Axis of evil" is a term coined by United States President George W. Bush in his State of the Union address on January 29, 2002, to describe three governments that he accused of seeking weapons of mass destruction and supporting terrorism: Iran, Iraq, and North Korea. The phrase deliberately echoed the Axis powers of World War II and the "evil empire" label Ronald Reagan had applied to the Soviet Union.

Background

The phrase appeared in the first State of the Union address after the September 11 attacks, as the administration argued that the danger came not only from terrorist networks but from states that might arm them. Speechwriter David Frum later recounted in his book The Right Man that he had drafted the phrase "axis of hatred," which was revised to "axis of evil" during the editing process.

Reactions and later use

The three named states denied the accusations, and many allied governments and commentators criticized the grouping, noting that Iran and Iraq had fought a bitter war against each other in the 1980s and had little in common. The phrase became a shorthand in debates over the 2003 invasion of Iraq. In May 2002, Under Secretary of State John Bolton expanded the list in a speech titled "Beyond the Axis of Evil," adding Cuba, Libya, and Syria. France's foreign minister Hubert Védrine had earlier used the phrase "axis of good" ironically in response, and the term inspired numerous variants in political rhetoric.

Legacy

"Axis of evil" remains one of the most quoted phrases of the post-Cold War era and is frequently cited in discussions of American foreign policy rhetoric, threat framing, and the road to the Iraq War. Iran and North Korea remained central concerns of U.S. policy in the decades that followed, while Iraq's inclusion rested on weapons programs that postwar investigations found had been largely dismantled years earlier.
