Fixed-point theorem

In mathematics, a fixed-point theorem is a result stating that a function F will have at least one fixed point — a point x for which F(x) = x — under some conditions on F that can be stated in general terms. Fixed-point theorems arise throughout analysis, topology, algebra and computer science, and are often proved by non-constructive methods that guarantee existence without giving an explicit way to find the point.

Principal theorems

Brouwer fixed-point theorem. Every continuous function from a closed unit ball in Euclidean space to itself has a fixed point. It is one of the foundational results of algebraic topology and has famous consequences, including in game theory and economics.

Banach fixed-point theorem (also called the contraction mapping theorem). A contraction mapping on a complete metric space has exactly one fixed point, and the theorem provides an iterative method to find it. It is proved constructively and is widely used to establish the existence of solutions to differential and integral equations.

Schauder fixed-point theorem extends Brouwer's result to infinite-dimensional spaces, guaranteeing a fixed point for a continuous compact mapping of a convex compact subset of a Banach space to itself.

Lefschetz fixed-point theorem counts the fixed points of a map from a compact triangulable space to itself in terms of traces of induced maps on homology groups.

Kakutani fixed-point theorem generalises Brouwer's theorem to set-valued functions and is a key tool in proving the existence of Nash equilibria.

Applications

Fixed-point results underpin the proof of existence of equilibria in economics (via the work of John Nash and of Arrow and Debreu), the analysis of iterative numerical algorithms, and semantics in computer science — for example in the theory of recursive programs and in Stephen Kleene's recursion theorems. In logic, diagonal arguments related to fixed points appear in Gödel's incompleteness theorems.
