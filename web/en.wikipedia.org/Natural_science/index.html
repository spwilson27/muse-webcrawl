Natural science is one of the branches of science concerned with the description, understanding and prediction of natural phenomena, based on empirical evidence from observation and experimentation. Mechanisms such as peer review and repeatability of findings are used to try to ensure the validity of scientific advances.

Natural science can be divided into two main branches: life science (or biological science) and physical science. Physical science is subdivided into branches, including physics, chemistry, Earth science and astronomy. These branches may be further divided into more specialised branches (also known as "fields"). As empirical sciences, natural sciences use tools from the formal sciences, such as mathematics and logic, converting information about nature into measurements which can be explained as clear statements of the "laws of nature".

History

The natural sciences were originally part of natural philosophy, the philosophical study of nature. During the Scientific Revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries, figures such as Nicolaus Copernicus, Galileo Galilei, Johannes Kepler and Isaac Newton transformed the study of nature by emphasising observation, experiment and mathematical description. The term "scientist" itself was coined by William Whewell in 1833, and the professionalisation of the natural sciences proceeded through the 19th century with the founding of journals, societies and university departments.

Branches

Physical sciences

The physical sciences study the inanimate natural world. Physics seeks the fundamental laws governing matter, energy, space and time; chemistry studies the composition, structure and properties of substances and the changes they undergo; astronomy studies celestial objects and phenomena beyond Earth; and Earth science (geoscience) studies the structure and processes of the Earth itself.

Life sciences

The life sciences, or biological sciences, study living organisms — from molecular biology and genetics at the smallest scales to ecology and evolutionary biology at the scale of ecosystems and the history of life. Important subfields include botany, zoology, microbiology, biochemistry and physiology.

Interdisciplinary fields

Many modern fields cross the traditional boundaries: biochemistry and geophysics combine branches of the physical and life sciences; environmental science integrates natural science with social concerns; and neuroscience links biology, psychology and computation.
