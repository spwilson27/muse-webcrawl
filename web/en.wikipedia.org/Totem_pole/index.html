Totem pole

Totem poles in Alaska

Totem poles (Haida: gya'ahlaay) are monumental carvings found in western Canada and the northwestern United States. They consist of poles, posts or pillars carved with symbols or figures, made from large trees, mostly western red cedar, by Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast including the Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw, Nuu-chah-nulth, Nisga'a, and Tahltan cultures.

Meaning and function

Each figure carved on a pole — animals such as ravens, eagles, bears, wolves, killer whales, and frogs, as well as supernatural beings and ancestors — represents a crest (at.oow in Tlingit) owned by a kin group, recalling its history, privileges, and encounters with spiritual beings. Poles serve as memorials to the deceased, records of lineage and history, house posts, grave markers, and public declarations of wealth and status, often raised with a potlatch ceremony. Contrary to a common misconception, the figures were generally not objects of worship.

Decline and revival

Traditional pole raising declined sharply in the 19th century under missionary and colonial pressure, including Canada's potlatch ban (1884–1951). Many poles were removed to museums and private collections abroad. From the mid-20th century, Indigenous carvers such as Mungo Martin led a revival, and communities have pursued repatriation of poles and renewed the carving tradition.

Notable poles

The world's tallest totem pole is commonly cited as the 173-foot (52.7 m) pole in Alert Bay, British Columbia, raised in 1973. Significant collections stand at 'Ksan, Gitanyow, Sitka National Historical Park, and the Royal British Columbia Museum.
