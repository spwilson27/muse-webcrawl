Saprotroph

A saprotroph is an organism that obtains its nutrients from dead or decaying organic matter. Saprotrophs — chiefly fungi and bacteria, together with some protists — are the principal decomposers in most ecosystems, breaking down the remains of plants and animals and returning carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and other elements to circulation. The term comes from the Greek sapros ("rotten") and trophē ("nourishment"). Such organisms were formerly called saprophytes, a term now discouraged because it implied they were plants.

Mechanism

Saprotrophic fungi feed by growing hyphae through their substrate and secreting extracellular enzymes — cellulases, ligninases, proteases and lipases — that digest complex polymers outside the cell; the soluble products are then absorbed. White-rot fungi can degrade lignin, the most resistant major component of wood, while brown-rot fungi mainly attack cellulose. Bacteria perform similar chemistry in soils, sediments and water, and the two groups compete and cooperate in the decomposition sequence.

Ecological role

Without saprotrophs, dead biomass would accumulate and nutrients would remain locked away; decomposition by saprotrophs underpins soil formation, the carbon cycle and the productivity of forests and grasslands. Their activity also produces humus, releases carbon dioxide, and in waterlogged conditions contributes to peat formation when decay is incomplete.

Importance to humans

Saprotrophs are a mixed blessing. They power composting, sewage treatment, and the production of foods such as mushrooms, soy sauce and many cheeses, and they yield antibiotics and industrial enzymes. Conversely, they cause food spoilage, rot timber and building materials, and include plant pathogens and opportunists such as Aspergillus species. The study of saprotrophic fungi, mycology, underlies much of biotechnology's use of decomposer enzymes.
