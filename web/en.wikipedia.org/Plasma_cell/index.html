Plasma cell

A plasma cell with characteristic "clock-face" chromatin.

Plasma cells, also called plasma B cells, are white blood cells that originate in the bone marrow as B lymphocytes and secrete large quantities of antibodies in response to being presented specific substances called antigens. These antibodies are transported from the plasma cells by the blood plasma and the lymphatic system to the site of the target antigens, where they initiate their neutralisation or destruction.

Development

B cells differentiate into plasma cells after activation by an antigen, usually with help from T helper cells in germinal centers of lymphoid tissue. Upon activation, B cells undergo clonal expansion and class switching, producing antibody-secreting plasmablasts and, ultimately, plasma cells. Some plasma cells migrate to the bone marrow and become long-lived plasma cells, which continue to secrete antibodies for years or even a lifetime, providing durable humoral immunity and the basis of immunological memory after infection or vaccination.

Structure

Plasma cells are large lymphocytes, roughly 14–20 micrometres in diameter, with abundant basophilic cytoplasm and an eccentric nucleus containing heterochromatin arranged in a characteristic cartwheel or clock-face pattern. The cytoplasm is rich in rough endoplasmic reticulum, reflecting the cell's role as an antibody factory, and a pale perinuclear zone (the hof) corresponds to a prominent Golgi apparatus. A single plasma cell can secrete several thousand antibody molecules per second.

Clinical significance

Uncontrolled proliferation of a single clone of plasma cells causes multiple myeloma, a cancer of the bone marrow characterised by production of a monoclonal paraprotein. Related disorders include monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), Waldenström macroglobulinaemia, and plasma cell leukaemia. Conversely, failure of plasma cell development contributes to humoral immunodeficiencies such as common variable immunodeficiency.
