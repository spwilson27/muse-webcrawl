Astrophel and Stella (also spelled Astrophil and Stella) is a sonnet sequence by the English poet Sir Philip Sidney, composed in the early 1580s and published posthumously in 1591. It comprises 108 sonnets and 11 songs, and is widely considered the finest Elizabethan sonnet sequence after Shakespeare's.

Content

The speaker, Astrophel ("star-lover"), addresses his beloved Stella ("star"), traditionally identified with Lady Penelope Devereux, later Lady Rich, whom Sidney had once been expected to marry. The sequence traces his unfulfilled love with wit, psychological complexity and formal virtuosity, opening with the famous declaration of poetic intent: "Loving in truth, and fain in verse my love to show". Sonnet 1 closes with the line "'Fool,' said my Muse to me, 'look in thy heart, and write.'"

Influence

The 1591 publication launched a vogue for sonnet sequences in England, imitated by Samuel Daniel, Michael Drayton, Edmund Spenser and others, paving the way for Shakespeare's sonnets. The name "Astrophel" was later used by Spenser for his elegy on Sidney's death.
