Montevideo Convention

The Montevideo Convention on the Rights and Duties of States is a treaty signed at Montevideo, Uruguay, on 26 December 1933, during the Seventh International Conference of American States. It is best known for Article 1, which sets out the classical criteria for statehood in international law.

Article 1: criteria of statehood

Article 1 provides that a state, as a person of international law, should possess the following qualifications:

  1. a permanent population;
  2. a defined territory;
  3. a government; and
  4. the capacity to enter into relations with other states.

These four criteria are widely cited as reflecting customary international law, and they underpin the declarative theory of statehood — the view that statehood depends on meeting objective criteria rather than on recognition by other states. Article 3 states expressly that the political existence of a state is independent of recognition by other states.

Other provisions

The convention also declares that states are juridically equal, that no state may intervene in the internal or external affairs of another, and (in Article 11) that states shall not recognise territorial acquisitions obtained by force — a clause associated with the Stimson Doctrine. The United States signed under President Franklin D. Roosevelt, whose Secretary of State Cordell Hull articulated the Good Neighbor policy toward Latin America at the conference.

Legacy

Though only a limited number of states ratified the convention, its definition of statehood is invoked in debates over entities such as Palestine, Kosovo, Taiwan, and Somaliland, and remains the standard starting point in international law textbooks.
