Jensen's inequality

In mathematics, Jensen's inequality, named after the Danish mathematician Johan Jensen, relates the value of a convex function of an integral to the integral of the convex function. Jensen proved the inequality in 1906, building on an earlier proof by Otto Hölder. In its simplest finite form it states that for a convex function f and numbers x1, …, xn in its domain, the function value at the average is no greater than the average of the function values:

f((x1 + ⋯ + xn)/n) ≤ (f(x1) + ⋯ + f(xn))/n.

Probabilistic form

In probability theory the inequality states that for a random variable X and a convex function f, f(E[X]) ≤ E[f(X)], provided the expectations exist. Intuitively, a convex function lies below the segments joining points on its graph, so averaging inputs before applying the function gives a smaller result than averaging the outputs.

Applications

Jensen's inequality is a workhorse of analysis and its applications. It yields quick proofs of the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality, is used in information theory in the non-negativity of Kullback–Leibler divergence, underlies the expectation–maximisation (EM) algorithm in statistics via the related evidence lower bound, and in economics relates risk aversion and expected utility. The inequality reverses for concave functions.

History

Jensen presented the inequality for convex functions of a real variable in 1906; the general measure-theoretic versions followed in the twentieth century.
