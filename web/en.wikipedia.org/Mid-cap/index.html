Mid-cap (short for "middle capitalisation") describes companies with a market capitalisation in the middle range of the public stock market — in the United States, roughly between $2 billion and $10 billion, though exact boundaries vary by index provider and change over time. Mid-cap sits between small-cap and large-cap in the standard size classification of stocks.

Characteristics

Mid-cap companies are generally more established than small-caps, with proven business models and more analyst coverage, but are considered to have greater growth potential than mature large-cap firms. Their shares tend to be less volatile than small-caps and somewhat more volatile than large-caps, and the segment has historically delivered competitive long-run returns, a pattern sometimes attributed to mid-caps being comparatively neglected by investors.

Indices and funds

The best-known benchmark is the S&P MidCap 400 Index, launched in 1991, which tracks 400 mid-sized US companies and is the basis for widely held index funds and ETFs. Other providers, including Russell (Russell Midcap Index) and MSCI, maintain their own mid-cap indices with differing ranges and constituents.

Investing considerations

Investors use mid-caps to diversify size exposure within equity portfolios, balanced between the stability of large companies and the growth of smaller ones; as companies grow or shrink, they migrate between cap categories at index reconstitutions.
