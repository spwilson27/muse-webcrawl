Cricothyrotomy

A cricothyrotomy (also called cricothyroidotomy) is an emergency incision made through the skin and cricothyroid membrane to establish a patent airway during life-threatening situations, such as airway obstruction by a foreign body, angioedema, or massive facial trauma. The procedure is a last resort in "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" scenarios when endotracheal intubation and other airway manoeuvres have failed.

Indications

Cricothyrotomy is indicated when a patient cannot be oxygenated or ventilated by other means and cannot be intubated — for example, in severe upper airway obstruction, failed intubation attempts with deteriorating oxygen saturation, or major maxillofacial injury. It is generally preferred over an emergency tracheostomy because the cricothyroid membrane is superficial, relatively avascular, and easy to identify, making the procedure faster and associated with fewer complications.

Procedure

In a surgical cricothyrotomy, the clinician stabilises the larynx, identifies the cricothyroid membrane between the thyroid and cricoid cartilages, and makes a transverse or vertical incision through the skin and membrane. The opening is held open with a hook or dilator, and a small cuffed endotracheal or tracheostomy tube is inserted and secured; placement is confirmed by capnography and auscultation. Needle cricothyrotomy, in which a large-bore cannula is passed through the membrane for jet ventilation, is an alternative, particularly in children, in whom surgical cricothyrotomy is relatively contraindicated.

Complications

Complications include bleeding, tube misplacement or displacement, subcutaneous emphysema, injury to the larynx or oesophagus, and later subglottic stenosis. Because of the risk of long-term airway injury, an emergency cricothyrotomy is usually converted to a formal tracheostomy once the patient is stabilised.

History

Procedures for opening the airway below an obstruction have been described since antiquity, and the technique was systematised in the 20th century for battlefield and emergency medicine. Modern airway guidelines, such as those of the Difficult Airway Society, place scalpel cricothyrotomy as the final step of the failed-intubation algorithm for adults.
