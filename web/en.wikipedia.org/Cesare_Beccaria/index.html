Cesare Beccaria

Cesare Bonesana di Beccaria, Marquis of Gualdrasco and Villareggio (15 March 1738 – 28 November 1794), was an Italian criminologist, jurist, philosopher, economist, and politician who is widely considered one of the greatest thinkers of the Age of Enlightenment. He is well remembered for his treatise On Crimes and Punishments (1764), which condemned torture and the death penalty, and was a founding work in the field of penology and the classical school of criminology. Beccaria is also recognised for proposing the formula for the measurement of temperature that bears his name.

Early life

Beccaria was born in Milan, then part of the Habsburg monarchy, into an aristocratic family. He was educated at the Jesuit college in Parma and studied law at the University of Pavia, graduating in 1758. Influenced by the Milanese circle of the Verri brothers and the journal Il Caffè, he turned to questions of economic and legal reform. His first published work, in 1762, concerned the disorder of the currency in the Milanese states.

On Crimes and Punishments

Published anonymously in Livorno in 1764 when Beccaria was 26, Dei delitti e delle pene argued that laws should be clear and public, that punishment should be proportionate to the crime, and that the certainty and swiftness of punishment deter crime more effectively than its severity. He condemned judicial torture as cruel and unreliable, attacked secret accusations, and opposed capital punishment on both utilitarian and social-contract grounds, arguing that the state has no right to take the life of a citizen. The book was translated throughout Europe, praised by Voltaire, and influenced legal reforms in France, Russia, Austria, and the United States, where its ideas informed the Bill of Rights and the thinking of John Adams and Thomas Jefferson.

Later career and economics

In 1768 Beccaria accepted a chair in public economy in Milan, publishing his lectures as Elementi di economia pubblica. He developed an early statement of the division of labour and population theory, and proposed the first mathematical formulation of criminal intent applied to economics. From 1771 he served on the supreme economic council of Milan and later on a commission for criminal-law reform, though he was by temperament reluctant to take public stands after the controversies surrounding his famous book.

Legacy

Beccaria's principles—that punishment must be public, prompt, necessary, the least possible in the circumstances, proportionate, and dictated by the laws—became foundational to modern criminal justice and to the classical school of criminology he inspired through writers such as Jeremy Bentham.
