Sea ice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arctic sea ice floes

Sea ice arises as seawater freezes. Because ice is less dense than water, it floats on the ocean surface. Sea ice covers about 7% of the Earth's surface and about 12% of the world's oceans at any time, mainly in the Arctic Ocean and around Antarctica, growing in winter and shrinking in summer. Unlike icebergs and glaciers, it forms and melts in the sea itself.

Formation and structure

Freezing begins with frazil crystals and grease ice, consolidating into nilas and pancake ice, then into first-year floes typically 0.3–2 m thick; ice surviving a summer becomes thicker, fresher multi-year ice. Growing ice rejects brine, leaving salty channels that host algae and krill communities crucial to polar food webs. Ice attached to shore is fast ice; drifting ice fields are pack ice, broken by leads and ridged into pressure ridges and keels.

Climate role

Sea ice has a high albedo, reflecting sunlight, and insulates the ocean from the atmosphere; its loss amplifies polar warming (the ice–albedo feedback). Arctic September extent has declined sharply since satellite records began in 1979 — roughly 13% per decade relative to late-20th-century averages — with implications for weather, ecosystems (polar bears, seals, walrus), Indigenous communities, and shipping routes such as the Northern Sea Route. Antarctic sea ice grew slightly for decades but fell to record lows in the late 2010s–2020s.
