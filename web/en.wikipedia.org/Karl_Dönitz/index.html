Karl Dönitz (16 September 1891 – 24 December 1980) was a German naval officer who commanded the U-boat fleet and later the entire Kriegsmarine during the Second World War, and who, as Adolf Hitler's designated successor, briefly served as head of state of Nazi Germany in May 1945.

Naval career

Dönitz served as a U-boat commander in the First World War and, between the wars, built the new submarine arm, developing the "wolfpack" (Rudeltaktik) tactics used against Allied convoys in the Battle of the Atlantic. In January 1943 he succeeded Erich Raeder as Commander-in-Chief of the Navy. Despite heavy losses inflicted on Allied shipping, the U-boat campaign was decisively defeated by mid-1943 through convoy escorts, air cover, radar, and codebreaking.

Succession and surrender

Named in Hitler's political testament, Dönitz became President of the Reich on 30 April 1945 and formed the short-lived Flensburg Government. He authorised the unconditional surrender of German forces, signed at Reims and Berlin on 7–8 May 1945, while attempting to let as many soldiers and refugees as possible escape westward from the Red Army.

Trial and later life

At the Nuremberg trials Dönitz was convicted of crimes against peace and war crimes and sentenced to ten years' imprisonment in Spandau Prison. Released in 1956, he published his memoirs, Ten Years and Twenty Days, and lived in retirement near Hamburg until his death in 1980.
