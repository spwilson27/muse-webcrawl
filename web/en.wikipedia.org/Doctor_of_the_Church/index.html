Doctor of the Church

Doctor of the Church (Latin: doctor, "teacher") is a title given by the Catholic Church to saints recognised as having made a significant contribution to theology or doctrine through their research, study, or writing. The title is conferred rarely and only posthumously.

Criteria and history

Tradition, especially as summarised by Prosper of Aquitaine, requires three marks: eminent learning (eminens doctrina), a high degree of sanctity (insignis vitae sanctitas), and a proclamation by the Church (Ecclesiae declaratio). The four great Western Doctors — Ambrose, Augustine, Jerome, and Gregory the Great — were recognised by Boniface VIII in 1298. The Eastern tradition honours figures such as Athanasius, Basil the Great, Gregory of Nazianzus, and John Chrysostom.

List and notable additions

The Catholic Church recognises 37 Doctors. Later additions include Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventure, Catherine of Siena, and John of the Cross. In 1970 Paul VI named Teresa of Ávila and Catherine of Siena the first women Doctors; Thérèse of Lisieux and Hildegard of Bingen followed. In 2015 Francis named Gregory of Narek, the first Doctor from the Oriental tradition outside full communion at the time of his life; John Henry Newman was declared a Doctor in 2025.

Significance

The title does not imply that a Doctor's every opinion is authoritative; rather, it commends their writings as a sure guide for theology. Their feast days are celebrated throughout the Church, and their works remain standard references in seminaries and universities.
