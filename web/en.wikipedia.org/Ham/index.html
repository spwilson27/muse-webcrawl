Ham

Ham
CourseMain course
Main ingredientsPork leg (hind leg cut)
PreparationCuring (wet or dry), often smoking and/or ageing
VariationsProsciutto, jamón, country ham, city ham, gammon

Ham is pork from a leg cut that has been preserved by wet or dry curing, with or without smoking. As a processed meat, the term "ham" includes both whole cuts of meat and ones that have been mechanically formed. Ham is made around the world, including a number of regional specialties. In addition, numerous ham products have specific geographical indication protection, such as prosciutto di Parma in Italy and jamón Ibérico in Spain.

History

The preservation of pork leg as ham has a long history. Cato the Elder wrote about the salting of hams in his De agri cultura around 160 BC, and Chinese sources describe cured hams — the famous Jinhua ham of Zhejiang — with a history said to extend back many centuries. Curing preserved meat before refrigeration, and hams became prized trade goods across Europe and later the Americas.

Production

Traditional ham is made from the hind leg of a pig. In dry curing, the meat is rubbed with salt and curing salts (often including nitrites or nitrates), sometimes with sugar and spices, then hung to dry and age for months or years. In wet curing, the pork is submerged in or injected with brine, yielding a moister product that is often smoked and fully cooked before sale. Smoking over hardwood imparts flavour and colour and aids preservation.

Varieties

Famous dry-cured hams include Italy's prosciutto (notably Prosciutto di Parma and di San Daniele), Spain's jamón serrano and jamón Ibérico, France's jambon de Bayonne, Germany's Black Forest ham, and China's Jinhua ham. In the United States, "country ham" is dry-cured and aged, famously in Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee, while mass-market "city ham" is wet-cured. In Britain, a raw cured leg is called gammon when sold uncooked. Fresh, uncured leg of pork is sometimes marketed as "fresh ham" in the US.

Uses

Ham is eaten sliced as a cold cut in sandwiches, diced into soups, beans, eggs and salads, and served whole as a centrepiece — often glazed with honey, brown sugar or mustard — at celebrations such as Christmas and Easter in many countries. The bone is valued for flavouring soups and broths. Because it is a processed meat, health authorities note its high sodium content, and the World Health Organization's IARC classifies processed meats as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) based on evidence concerning colorectal cancer.
