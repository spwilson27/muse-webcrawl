Bats are mammals of the order Chiroptera. With their forelimbs adapted as wings, they are the only mammals capable of true and sustained flight. Bats are more agile in flight than most birds, thanks to their very long spread-out digits covered with a thin membrane called the patagium. The smallest bat, Kitti's hog-nosed bat, is roughly 29–34 mm in length; the largest are the flying foxes, with wingspans of up to 1.7 metres.

With over 1,400 species, bats account for about 20% of all classified mammal species worldwide and are divided traditionally into the megabats (fruit bats) and the echolocating microbats. About 70% of species are insectivores; most of the rest are frugivores, and a few specialise on fish, blood, or other vertebrates. Many tropical plants depend entirely on bats for pollination or seed dispersal.

Echolocation

Most bats emit ultrasonic calls through the mouth or nose and listen to the returning echoes to build a sonic picture of their surroundings, enabling them to navigate and hunt insects in complete darkness. Echolocating bats can determine the size, shape, distance and even texture of objects from echoes alone.

Ecology and conservation

Bats roost in caves, trees, and human structures, often in large colonies. They provide major ecosystem services as pollinators and controllers of insect pests, but many populations are threatened by habitat loss, the fungal disease white-nose syndrome, and wind turbines.
