Afar Depression

LocationAfar Region, Ethiopia; also Eritrea and Djibouti
Lowest pointLake Assal, about 155 m below sea level (lowest point in Africa)
GeologyTriple junction of the Red Sea Rift, Gulf of Aden Rift and East African Rift
Notable featuresDanakil Depression, Erta Ale volcano, Dallol hydrothermal field

The Afar Depression (also called the Afar Triangle) is a geological depression in the Horn of Africa, spanning the Afar Region of Ethiopia and parts of Eritrea and Djibouti, caused by the Afar triple junction, where the ridges of the Red Sea, the Gulf of Aden and the East African Rift System meet and pull apart. It is one of the few places on land where a mid-ocean ridge system can be studied directly: the crust here is thinning and subsiding as Africa splits, and geologists have observed episodes of rifting — such as the 2005 Dabbahu episode, when a 60-kilometre fissure opened — that resemble those at submarine spreading centres.

The northern part, the Danakil Depression, lies up to about 120 metres below sea level and contains salt flats, potash and salt mining, the colourful sulphur springs of Dallol and the persistently active lava-lake volcano Erta Ale. It is one of the hottest inhabited places on Earth, with average annual temperatures around 34 °C. The Awash River drains into a chain of lakes within the depression rather than reaching the sea.

Palaeontology

The region's sedimentary deposits are world-famous for hominin fossils: the Middle Awash and Hadar localities yielded Australopithecus afarensis, including the 3.2-million-year-old skeleton "Lucy" (discovered in 1974), and the still older Ardipithecus, making the Afar central to the study of human evolution.
