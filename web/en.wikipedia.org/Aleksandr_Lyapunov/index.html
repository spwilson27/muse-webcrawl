Aleksandr Lyapunov

Aleksandr Mikhailovich Lyapunov (Russian: Алекса́ндр Миха́йлович Ляпуно́в; 6 June [O.S. 25 May] 1857 – 3 November 1918) was a Russian mathematician and physicist. He is known for his development of the stability theory of a dynamical system, as well as for his many contributions to mathematical physics and probability theory.

Life

Lyapunov was born in Yaroslavl, in the Russian Empire. His father, Mikhail Vasilyevich Lyapunov, was an astronomer, and his brother Sergei was a composer. He studied mathematics at Saint Petersburg University, where he was a student of Pafnuty Chebyshev and graduated in 1880. He later taught at Kharkov University and was elected to the Russian Academy of Sciences in 1901.

Work

Lyapunov's 1892 doctoral thesis, The General Problem of the Stability of Motion, founded the modern theory of stability. His direct method uses a scalar function — now called a Lyapunov function — whose properties establish whether an equilibrium of a dynamical system is stable without solving the equations of motion. This approach became fundamental in control theory, nonlinear dynamics, and engineering.

He also made important contributions to probability theory, proving a version of the central limit theorem under general conditions (the Lyapunov condition), and worked on the equilibrium shapes of rotating fluids, a problem with applications to the figure of the Earth and celestial mechanics.

Death

Lyapunov died in Odessa in November 1918, shooting himself shortly after the death of his wife from tuberculosis; he died of his wounds on 3 November 1918.
