Melting point

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The melting point of a substance is the temperature at which it changes state from solid to liquid. At the melting point the solid and liquid phases exist in equilibrium. The melting point of water at standard pressure is 0 °C (273.15 K, 32 °F). For most substances the melting and freezing points are approximately equal.

Characteristics

Pure crystalline substances have sharp, characteristic melting points, which makes melting point determination a standard laboratory test of purity: impurities generally lower and broaden the melting range. Amorphous materials such as glass have no true melting point and instead pass through a glass transition as they soften.

Dependence on pressure

For most substances, increasing pressure raises the melting point because the liquid is denser than the solid. Water is a notable exception — ice is less dense than liquid water — so pressure lowers its melting point, an effect described by the Clausius–Clapeyron relation.

Examples

Tungsten has the highest melting point of all metals at 3,422 °C, while mercury has one of the lowest at −38.83 °C, making it a liquid at room temperature. Helium does not solidify at all at ordinary pressures.
