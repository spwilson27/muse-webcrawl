The V-2 (German: Vergeltungswaffe 2, "Retribution Weapon 2"), with the technical designation Aggregat 4 (A-4), was the world's first long-range guided ballistic missile and the first human-made object to travel into space. Developed at Peenemünde under Wernher von Braun, it was used by Nazi Germany against Allied targets, principally London and Antwerp, in the final phase of the Second World War.

Development

The German Army's rocket programme, led by Walter Dornberger, built the A-4 at the Peenemünde research centre on the Baltic coast. After early failures, the first successful flight on 3 October 1942 reached the edge of space; a test flight on 20 June 1944 reached an altitude of about 176 km, making the V-2 the first object in space. The rocket burned liquid oxygen and ethanol, using turbopumps driven by hydrogen peroxide, and was guided by gyroscopes and an inertial control system with jet vanes and external rudders.

Production and slave labour

Following the RAF's bombing of Peenemünde (Operation Hydra, August 1943), production moved underground to the Mittelwerk tunnels near Nordhausen, where prisoners of the Mittelbau-Dora concentration camp assembled the missiles under murderous conditions. More people — an estimated 12,000–20,000 prisoners — died building the V-2 than were killed by its use as a weapon. Over 3,000 V-2s were launched operationally.

Operational use

The first operational launches came on 8 September 1944 against Paris and London. Mobile launch batteries (Meillerwagen trailers) fired from camouflaged sites, chiefly in the Netherlands. About 1,400 fell on London and over 1,600 on Antwerp, Belgium's liberated port; in total V-2 attacks killed roughly 9,000 people. On 17 March 1945 the last V-2 in anger struck London; the final combat launch targeted Antwerp days later. Travelling supersonically, the missile arrived without warning — the explosion was heard before the sound of its descent.

Legacy

At the war's end, the United States (Operation Paperclip) and the Soviet Union seized rockets, hardware and specialists. Von Braun's team formed the nucleus of the American rocket programme, and V-2 derivatives (the American V-2 sounding rockets, the Soviet R-1) seeded both superpowers' ballistic missiles and space programmes; a V-2 carried the first animals (fruit flies, 1947) and the first mammal photography of Earth from space.
