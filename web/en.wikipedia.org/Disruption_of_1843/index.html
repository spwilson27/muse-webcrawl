Disruption of 1843

The Disruption of 1843, also simply known as the Disruption, was a schism in the Church of Scotland in which 474 evangelical ministers — about a third of the ministry — left the established church to form the Free Church of Scotland.

Causes

The split followed a decade of conflict known as the Ten Years' Conflict over lay patronage: the right of landowners to appoint parish ministers against the wishes of congregations. The evangelical majority in the church's General Assembly passed a Veto Act in 1834 and asserted the church's spiritual independence from the civil courts, but the courts and Parliament upheld patronage, most famously in the Auchterarder case. The seceders held that Christ alone was head of the church and that the state must not intrude in spiritual matters.

The Disruption

On 18 May 1843, at the General Assembly in St Andrew's Church, Edinburgh, the outgoing moderator David Welsh read a protest and led a procession of ministers to Tanfield Hall, where the first assembly of the Free Church met, with Thomas Chalmers as its first moderator. The departing ministers gave up their churches, manses and stipends, and the new church had to build hundreds of churches, schools and a theological college within a few years, funded by voluntary giving.

Aftermath

Most of the Free Church reunited with the United Presbyterian Church in 1900 to form the United Free Church, most of which in turn rejoined the Church of Scotland in 1929; a continuing Free Church of Scotland still exists. The Disruption is regarded as one of the defining events of 19th-century Scottish history, commemorated in the famous painting by David Octavius Hill, itself a pioneering work of photography.
