Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff equation

The Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff (TOV) equation describes the structure of a spherically symmetric body of isotropic material in static gravitational equilibrium, as modelled by general relativity. It is the relativistic generalisation of the Newtonian equation of hydrostatic equilibrium used in stellar structure theory. Derived independently from the work of Richard C. Tolman (1939) and of J. Robert Oppenheimer and George Volkoff (1939, in their paper "On Massive Neutron Cores"), it governs the pressure gradient inside a star and is the key equation in the physics of neutron stars.

The equation

In Schwarzschild coordinates, with G the gravitational constant, c the speed of light, ρ(r) the mass-energy density, P(r) the pressure and m(r) the enclosed mass, the equation reads:

dP/dr = − (G/r2) [ ρ + P/c2 ] [ m + 4πr3P/c2 ] [ 1 − 2Gm/(rc2) ]−1

Compared with the Newtonian version, three relativistic correction factors appear: the pressure itself contributes to the gravitating mass density; the pressure also adds to the enclosed mass; and the Schwarzschild geometry factor steepens the gradient. All three strengthen gravity, so in relativity a star needs an ever steeper pressure gradient to support itself — and beyond a certain mass no pressure can suffice.

The TOV limit

Solving the TOV equation with a suitable equation of state gives the mass–radius relation of neutron stars and a maximum possible mass, known as the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit. Oppenheimer and Volkoff, using an idealised gas of free neutrons, found only about 0.7 solar masses; modern equations of state for dense nuclear matter raise the limit to roughly 2.0–2.3 solar masses, consistent with the heaviest pulsars observed (near 2.1 solar masses) and with constraints from the gravitational-wave event GW170817. A compact remnant heavier than the limit cannot stabilise as a neutron star and must collapse into a black hole.

Solving

Equations are integrated outward from the centre (with m(0) = 0 and a chosen central density) together with the mass continuity equation dm/dr = 4πr²ρ, until the pressure falls to zero, which defines the star's surface. The principal unknown in practice is the equation of state of matter at supranuclear density, so measured neutron-star masses and radii are used to test nuclear physics.
