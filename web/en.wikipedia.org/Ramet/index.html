Ramet

A ramet is a member of a clone, or of a clonal colony, that is physiologically capable of independent life. In clonal colonies of plants, a ramet is the individual unit — often a single shoot or stem with its own roots — that belongs to a larger genetic individual called a genet. All the ramets of a genet are genetically identical (except for somatic mutations), because they arise by vegetative reproduction rather than from seed.

Examples

Classic examples include a grove of quaking aspen trees in which each trunk is a ramet of a single genet, a patch of bracken fern spreading by rhizomes, and the offshoots ("pups") produced by many succulents and bromeliads. In grasses and strawberries, runners produce new ramets at their nodes, which root and may eventually become detached from the parent.

Ecological significance

Because ramets may remain physically connected to the parent plant, clonal colonies can share water, carbohydrates and nutrients among their members, allowing the genet to persist for very long periods and to forage across heterogeneous environments. Some genets are estimated to be many thousands of years old, even though each individual ramet lives only a few years. The distinction between ramet and genet is central to plant demography, since counting ramets and counting genets give very different pictures of population size and longevity.
