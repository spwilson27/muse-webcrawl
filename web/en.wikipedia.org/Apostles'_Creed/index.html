The Apostles' Creed (Latin: Symbolum Apostolorum) is an early statement of Christian belief, a creed or "symbol". It is widely used by Western Christian churches for both liturgical and catechetical purposes, and is the most popular creed used in worship services in the Western Church. According to medieval legend each of the twelve apostles contributed one of its twelve articles, though historically it developed from the baptismal interrogations and the Old Roman Creed used in Rome.

Text

The creed professes belief in God the Father, creator of heaven and earth; in Jesus Christ, his only Son, who was conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary, suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died, and was buried, descended into hell, rose again on the third day, ascended into heaven, and will come to judge the living and the dead; and in the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic Church, the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting.

History

The core of the creed (the Old Roman Creed) dates to the second century; the present received form, including phrases such as "creator of heaven and earth" and "descended into hell", emerged in Gaul by the fifth century and was standardised under Charlemagne. It is used in the Roman Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian, and many other traditions; Eastern Orthodox churches rarely use it liturgically. Its clause "descended into hell" (descendit ad inferos) and the word "catholic" remain frequent points of discussion, the latter usually explained as meaning "universal".
