Irish Civil War

The Irish Civil War (Irish: Cogadh Cathartha na hÉireann; 28 June 1922 – 24 May 1923) was a conflict that followed the Irish War of Independence and accompanied the establishment of the Irish Free State, an entity independent from the United Kingdom but within the British Empire. It pitted supporters of the Anglo-Irish Treaty against opponents who rejected the compromise with Britain that had ended their earlier struggle.

Causes

The Anglo-Irish Treaty of December 1921 created a dominion, the Irish Free State, but required an oath to the British Crown and confirmed the partition of Ireland, with six north-eastern counties remaining in the United Kingdom as Northern Ireland. The Irish Republican Army split over whether to accept the settlement. In April 1922 anti-Treaty forces under Rory O'Connor occupied the Four Courts in Dublin.

Course of the war

On 28 June 1922, Free State troops under Michael Collins, under British pressure and after the assassination of Sir Henry Wilson in London, bombarded the Four Courts, opening the war. The pro-Treaty National Army, supplied with British artillery and armoured cars, quickly won the conventional phase, taking the major towns by August 1922. Collins himself was killed in an ambush at Béal na Bláth, County Cork, on 22 August 1922.

The anti-Treaty side, led politically by Éamon de Valera and militarily by Liam Lynch, resorted to guerrilla warfare. The Free State government responded with internment and a policy of executions, carrying out 77 official executions of anti-Treaty prisoners, more than the British had executed during the War of Independence. Lynch was killed in April 1923, and his successor Frank Aiken ordered a ceasefire; anti-Treaty fighters dumped arms on 24 May 1923.

Aftermath

The war left deep bitterness in Irish politics for generations; the two main parties of the Free State and later Republic, Fine Gael and Fianna Fáil, trace their origins to the pro- and anti-Treaty sides respectively. Estimates of the dead vary, with total fatalities generally put between roughly 1,500 and 4,000. The conflict destroyed property including many of Ireland's great houses and the Public Record Office in the Four Courts, whose loss destroyed centuries of archival material.
