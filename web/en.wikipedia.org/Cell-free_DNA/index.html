Cell-free DNA

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is degraded DNA fragments released into the blood plasma or other body fluids, originating mainly from apoptotic and necrotic cells. Isolated from a blood sample, cfDNA can be analyzed to detect genetic material from a fetus, a tumor, or a transplanted organ, forming the basis of "liquid biopsy" tests.

History

Free circulating nucleic acids in blood were first described by Mandel and Métais in 1948. In 1997, Dennis Lo and colleagues demonstrated that fetal DNA circulates in maternal plasma, opening the way to non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) for chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome from a simple maternal blood draw.

Applications

In oncology, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) can reveal cancer-associated mutations, guide targeted therapy, and monitor minimal residual disease. In transplantation medicine, donor-derived cfDNA in the recipient's blood can signal organ rejection. cfDNA fragment sizes cluster around ~166 base pairs, reflecting nucleosome protection during apoptosis, and its half-life in circulation is short, on the order of minutes to a few hours.

Limitations

Tumor-derived DNA may be a tiny fraction of total cfDNA, requiring sensitive sequencing methods; biological confounders such as clonal hematopoiesis can complicate interpretation, and the clinical utility of many screening applications is still being evaluated.
