Performance art

Performance art is an artwork or art exhibition created through actions executed by the artist or other participants. It may be witnessed live or through documentation, spontaneously developed or written, and is traditionally presented to a public in a fine-art context, sometimes blurring the line between art and theatre. The work often involves the artist's own body as its medium and may be ephemeral, existing only in the moment of its enactment and in recordings or memory.

History

Precursors include the provocations of Dada, the Futurist evenings of the 1910s, and the Happenings staged by Allan Kaprow and others in the late 1950s. The term "performance art" came into general use in the 1970s, encompassing the body art of Chris Burden and Marina Abramović, the actions of the Viennese Actionists, and the work of Joseph Beuys, Yoko Ono and Vito Acconci. Feminist artists such as Carolee Schneemann used performance to challenge the objectification of the female body.

Notable works

  • Shoot (1971), Chris Burden — the artist had himself shot in the arm.
  • Rhythm 0 (1974), Marina Abramović — the artist stood passively beside 72 objects the audience could use on her.
  • Seedbed (1972), Vito Acconci.
  • Cut Piece (1964), Yoko Ono.
  • The Artist Is Present (2010), Marina Abramović — a 736-hour silent sitting at MoMA.

Characteristics

Performance art typically emphasises process over product, presence over representation, and the live encounter between performer and audience. Because it resists commodification, it acquired a reputation as a dematerialised and anti-institutional form, though major museums now routinely programme and collect it.
