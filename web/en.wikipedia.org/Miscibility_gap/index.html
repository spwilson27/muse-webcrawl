Miscibility gap

Miscibility gap
FieldThermodynamics, materials science
Related conceptsPhase diagram, spinodal, binodal

A miscibility gap is a region in a phase diagram for a mixture of components where the mixture exists as two or more distinct phases rather than as a single homogeneous mixture. The term is mainly used in materials science and thermodynamics for liquid and solid solutions, such as metal alloys, glasses, and polymer blends, though it also applies to liquid-liquid systems.

Behaviour

Within a miscibility gap, a mixture separates into two phases with different compositions, for example an oil-rich phase and a water-rich phase in an emulsion, or two coexisting solid solutions in an alloy. The boundary of the gap is given by the binodal curve; inside it lies the spinodal region, where the homogeneous phase is unstable and separates spontaneously by spinodal decomposition.

Temperature dependence

Many systems exhibit an upper critical solution temperature (UCST), above which the components become fully miscible, or a lower critical solution temperature (LCST), below which they are miscible. The classic textbook example of a liquid system with a miscibility gap is the phenol-water system.

Applications

Miscibility gaps are exploited in the production of phase-separated glasses such as Vycor, in age-hardening alloys where controlled precipitation strengthens the material, and in the design of polymer blends and extraction processes.
