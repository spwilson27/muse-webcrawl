Populism

Populism refers to a range of political stances that emphasise the idea of "the people" and often position this group in opposition to a real or perceived "elite". The term developed in the late 19th century and has been applied to various democracies and ideological movements. In the widely used definition of political scientist Cas Mudde, populism is a "thin-centred ideology" that considers society to be separated into two homogeneous and antagonistic groups, "the pure people" and "the corrupt elite", and argues that politics should be an expression of the volonté générale (general will) of the people.

Because populism is a thin ideology, it is almost always combined with other, "thick" ideologies such as socialism, nationalism or anti-globalisation, producing left-wing and right-wing variants that differ sharply in policy while sharing a rhetorical structure.

History

The word derives from the Populares faction of the late Roman Republic and was revived in the 1890s by the American People's Party, which mobilised indebted farmers against banks and railroads. The Russian Narodniks developed a related agrarian populism. In the twentieth century, Latin American leaders such as Juan Perón in Argentina and Getúlio Vargas in Brazil built durable movements combining populist rhetoric with labour and welfare policies.

Characteristics

Populist movements typically claim that their leader or party uniquely embodies the authentic people, depict politics as a moral struggle between good and evil rather than a disagreement over policy, and favour direct links between leader and followers — mass rallies, plebiscites and, more recently, social media — over intermediary institutions. Populists in power may challenge judicial independence, press freedom and electoral bodies they portray as elite-controlled.

Debate

Scholars disagree about populism's relationship to democracy. Some see it as a threat that erodes checks and balances and stigmatises opponents and minorities; others, following Ernesto Laclau, treat populism as an emancipatory force that can bring excluded groups into politics and revitalise democratic participation. Empirical research links populist surges to economic insecurity, cultural change and declining trust in established parties.
