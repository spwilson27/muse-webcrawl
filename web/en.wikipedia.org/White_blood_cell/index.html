White blood cells (scientific name leukocytes), also called immune cells or immunocytes, are cells of the immune system that are involved in protecting the body against both infectious disease and foreign invaders. They are produced in the bone marrow and circulate in the blood and lymphatic system.

Types

The five main types are neutrophils, the most abundant, which engulf bacteria; lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells), which mediate specific immunity and antibody production; monocytes, which mature into macrophages; and eosinophils and basophils, involved in parasite defence and allergic responses. Antibodies and lymphocytes are the components most directly engaged by vaccines.

Clinical significance

An abnormally high white cell count (leukocytosis) often indicates infection or inflammation, while a low count (leukopenia) can result from bone-marrow disorders, chemotherapy or some viral infections. Cancers of white blood cells are the leukaemias. Formation of blood cells depends on nutrients including vitamin B12 and folate. Disorders of blood-forming tissues can also affect long bones such as the ulna only indirectly, through general marrow failure.
