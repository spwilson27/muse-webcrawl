Negative liberty

Negative liberty is freedom from interference by other people. It is concerned with the area within which a person can act unobstructed by others, in contrast to positive liberty, which concerns the control of one's own life and self-realisation.

Isaiah Berlin

The distinction was made famous by philosopher Isaiah Berlin in his 1958 lecture "Two Concepts of Liberty". Berlin defined negative liberty as the absence of obstacles, barriers, or constraints imposed by others: "I am normally said to be free to the degree to which no man or body of men interferes with my activity."

History

The idea is associated with thinkers such as Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and John Stuart Mill, whose harm principle in On Liberty (1859) held that power may be exercised over a person against their will only to prevent harm to others.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that a purely negative account ignores internal obstacles and material conditions, such as poverty, that can limit real freedom. The distinction remains central to debates in political philosophy and liberal theory.
