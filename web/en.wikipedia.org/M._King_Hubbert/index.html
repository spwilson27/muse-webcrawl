Marion King Hubbert (5 October 1903 – 11 October 1989) was an American geologist and geophysicist who worked most of his career at the Shell research laboratory in Houston, Texas, and later with the United States Geological Survey. He is best known for the Hubbert peak theory, presented in 1956, which predicted that oil production in the contiguous United States would peak between 1965 and 1971 — a forecast vindicated when US production peaked in 1970 — and which underlies the modern concept of "peak oil."

Life and career

Hubbert was born in San Saba, Texas, and studied geology, mathematics and physics at the University of Chicago, where he earned his PhD in 1937 with a dissertation applying mathematics to geological problems. He taught geophysics at Columbia University in the early 1940s, served on wartime planning boards in Washington, and in 1943 joined Shell Oil, where he directed research in geology and geophysics until retiring in 1964. He then joined the US Geological Survey as a senior research geophysicist, remaining active until 1976, and held a professorship at Stanford.

Hubbert was a prominent member of the Technocracy movement in the 1930s, co-founding Technocracy Incorporated and contributing to its energy-accounting analysis of industrial society, an outlook that shaped his later thinking about resource limits.

Scientific contributions

Peak oil theory

In his 1956 paper "Nuclear Energy and the Fossil Fuels," presented to the American Petroleum Institute, Hubbert argued that the production of a finite resource follows a roughly bell-shaped curve (the Hubbert curve): cumulative production follows a logistic curve, so the production rate peaks when about half the ultimately recoverable resource has been extracted. Applying estimated ultimate US reserves of 150–200 billion barrels, he predicted a US peak between 1965 and 1971. US crude production peaked in November 1970 at about 10 million barrels per day, within his window. Hubbert also projected a world oil peak around the year 2000; global conventional production plateaued in the 2000s, though unconventional oil later raised total output.

Other work

Hubbert made fundamental contributions to the mechanics of rock deformation and fluid flow: his 1940 theory of groundwater flow corrected Darcy's law applications; his work with Willis R. Rubey on fluid pressure and overthrust faulting (1959) explained how enormous rock sheets can slide on near-frictionless, fluid-pressured surfaces, a landmark in structural geology; and he developed theories of hydraulic fracturing and the scaling of physical models in geology (Hubbert's "theory of scale models," 1937). The Hubbert linearization method is still used to estimate ultimately recoverable reserves from production data.

Honours

Hubbert was elected to the National Academy of Sciences (1955) and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, served as president of the Geological Society of America (1962), and received the GSA's Arthur L. Day Medal (1954) and Penrose Medal (1973), as well as the Vetlesen Prize (1981), often called the Nobel Prize of the earth sciences.
