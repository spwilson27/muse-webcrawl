Vkhutemas

Vkhutemas (Russian: Вхутемас, acronym for Vysshie khudozhestvenno-tekhnicheskie masterskie, "Higher Art and Technical Workshops") was a state art and technical school in Moscow, founded in 1920 by decree of the Soviet government. Often compared to Germany's Bauhaus — with which it exchanged ideas and exhibitions — it was the principal school of the Russian avant-garde, uniting fine art with industrial design education until its closure in 1930.

History and structure

Vkhutemas was formed by merging the Moscow School of Painting, Sculpture and Architecture with the Stroganov School of Applied Arts. Students first completed a Basic Course in the analysis of colour, form, space and volume — taught by figures such as Wassily Kandinsky and Lyubov Popova — before specializing in faculties of painting, sculpture, architecture, textiles, ceramics, woodwork, metalwork and printing.

Role in the avant-garde

The school was the institutional home of Constructivism and Rationalism. Alexander Rodchenko taught design and headed the metalwork faculty; Vladimir Tatlin taught a "culture of materials" course; the Vesnin brothers and Nikolai Ladovsky led competing schools of architecture. Its students produced landmark works, from Rodchenko's hanging spatial constructions to Gustav Klutsis's photomontage posters. Vkhutemas exhibited at the 1925 Paris Exposition of Decorative Arts and maintained contacts with the Bauhaus; in 1928 it was renamed Vkhutein, and in 1930, under pressure toward academic realism, it was dissolved and its faculties dispersed to other institutes.

Legacy

With the Bauhaus, Vkhutemas is considered a founding institution of modern design education: its foundation-course pedagogy, integration of art and technology, and rational analysis of form influenced art schools worldwide.
