A mast cell (also known as a mastocyte) is a resident cell of connective tissue that contains many granules rich in histamine and heparin. Mast cells are best known for their role in allergy and anaphylaxis, but they are also important in innate immunity, wound healing, angiogenesis and defence against pathogens.

Development and types

Mast cells derive from haematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow and mature in peripheral tissues under the influence of stem cell factor acting on the c-Kit receptor. Two main types are recognised in humans: mucosal mast cells, found in the gut and lung lining, and connective-tissue mast cells, found in skin and serosal cavities, which differ in their protease content.

Activation and mediators

The classic activation pathway involves immunoglobulin E (IgE): allergen cross-links IgE bound to the high-affinity FcεRI receptor on the mast-cell surface, triggering degranulation — the rapid release of histamine, tryptase and heparin — followed by synthesis of lipid mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes) and cytokines. Mast cells can also be activated by complement, neuropeptides and the MRGPRX2 receptor.

Role in disease

Excessive mast-cell activation causes allergic reactions ranging from urticaria to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Abnormal proliferation occurs in mastocytosis. Mast-cell stabilisers such as cromolyn, antihistamines, anti-IgE therapy (omalizumab) and, for anaphylaxis, adrenaline are used to treat mast-cell–driven disease. Closely related to basophils, mast cells also contribute to defence against parasites and venoms and modulate inflammation in conditions such as asthma and arthritis.
