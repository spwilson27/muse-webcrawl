Undead

In mythology, legend and fiction, the undead are beings that are deceased yet behave as if alive. The term is most often applied to corporeal forms such as vampires, zombies, mummies, skeletons and ghouls, and to incorporeal forms such as ghosts and spectres. Undead creatures feature in the folklore of cultures worldwide and are staples of horror fiction, fantasy and role-playing games.

Etymology and origins

Belief in revenants — the dead returning to trouble the living — is ancient and nearly universal, appearing in Norse draugr tales, the Chinese jiangshi, the vetala of Indian tradition and the restless dead of Egyptian and Mesopotamian religion. Folkloric undead were typically dispatched by staking, decapitation, burning or ritual burial. The English noun "undead" was popularised by Bram Stoker, whose novel Dracula (1897) was originally to be titled The Un-Dead.

Types in fiction

Fiction systematised the undead into recognisable types. Vampires are blood-drinking revenants codified by Stoker and later authors. Zombies derive from Haitian Vodou (zonbi) and were reinterpreted by George A. Romero's films as mindless, flesh-eating ghouls. Mummies arose from Egyptian tomb legends and Universal Pictures' 1932 film. Fantasy games, notably Dungeons & Dragons, created elaborate hierarchies including liches, wights, wraiths and death knights, and often define undead as animated by negative energy and vulnerable to holy power ("turning undead").

Themes

Scholars read the undead as expressions of anxieties about death, disease, decomposition and the boundary between body and person. Epidemics fed vampire panics in 18th-century Eastern Europe, and modern zombie fiction is frequently interpreted as allegory for contagion, consumerism or social collapse. The undead remain among the most commercially successful figures in popular culture, from The Walking Dead to countless video games.
