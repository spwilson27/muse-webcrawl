Zadar (Italian: Zara) is the oldest continuously inhabited city in Croatia, located on the Adriatic Sea in northern Dalmatia. It is the seat of Zadar County and has a population of about 70,000, making it the fifth-largest city in Croatia and the second-largest in Dalmatia after Split.

History

Settled by the Liburnians and later a Roman colony (Iader), Zadar became a Byzantine theme capital and was contested for centuries between Venice, Croatia and Hungary. In 1202, during the Fourth Crusade, Crusader forces indebted to Venice sacked the city — a notorious episode condemned by the Pope. The Treaty of Zadar (1358) briefly returned it to the Hungarian-Croatian crown before Venice bought Dalmatia in 1409 and ruled for nearly four centuries, fortifying the city; its Venetian defensive works are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

After Napoleonic and Austrian rule, Zadar was assigned to Italy by the Treaty of Rapallo (1920) as an enclave surrounded by Yugoslav territory. Heavily bombed by the Allies in 1943–44, it became part of Yugoslavia in 1947 and of independent Croatia in 1991, enduring siege during the Croatian War of Independence.

Sights

The old town occupies a peninsula and preserves a Roman forum, the 9th-century pre-Romanesque Church of St Donatus, the Cathedral of St Anastasia and five historic wells. Modern installations on the waterfront — the Sea Organ (2005), whose steps sound tones as waves move air through pipes, and the Greeting to the Sun solar disc — have made Zadar a major tourist destination. The University of Zadar traces its origins to a Dominican studium founded in 1396.
