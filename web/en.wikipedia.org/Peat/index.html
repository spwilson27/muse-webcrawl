Peat

Peat is an accumulation of partially decayed vegetation or organic matter. It is formed in waterlogged, acidic, oxygen-poor conditions—typically in bogs, mires and other peatlands—where plant material, especially sphagnum moss, does not fully decompose. Peatlands cover about 3% of the Earth's land surface but store roughly twice as much carbon as all the world's forests.

Formation

Peat forms when the rate of plant production exceeds the rate of decomposition. In waterlogged soils, lack of oxygen slows microbial activity, so dead plant material accumulates at a rate of roughly one millimetre per year. Over geological time, buried peat can be transformed by heat and pressure into lignite and eventually coal.

Uses

Peat has traditionally been cut and dried as a fuel for heating and cooking in countries such as Ireland, Scotland and Finland, and burned in some power stations. In horticulture it is widely used in composts and soil conditioners, though environmental concerns have led many countries to restrict peat extraction and promote peat-free alternatives. Peat smoke also flavours some Scotch whiskies.

Environmental significance

Intact peatlands are major carbon stores and habitats for specialised wildlife. Drainage and extraction release carbon dioxide, contributing to climate change, which has driven peatland restoration programmes in many countries. Peat bogs also preserve archaeological material, including "bog bodies," because of their acidic, anaerobic conditions.
