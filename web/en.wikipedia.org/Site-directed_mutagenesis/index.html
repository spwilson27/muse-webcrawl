Site-directed mutagenesis

Site-directed mutagenesis is a molecular biology method used to make specific and intentional mutations in the DNA sequence of a gene and its gene products. Also called site-specific mutagenesis or oligonucleotide-directed mutagenesis, it is a basic tool for studying protein structure and function and for protein engineering.

Methods

The original approach, developed in the 1970s by Michael Smith and co-workers, used a synthetic oligonucleotide carrying the desired mutation as a primer for DNA synthesis on a single-stranded template. Modern methods are largely PCR-based: primers encoding the mutation amplify the whole plasmid, followed by digestion of the parental template (often with the methylation-sensitive enzyme DpnI) and transformation into bacteria. Cassette mutagenesis and whole-gene synthesis are alternatives.

Applications

Site-directed mutagenesis allows researchers to test the role of individual amino acids, introduce or remove features such as restriction sites and affinity tags, and optimise enzymes for industrial use. Smith shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the method. Related techniques include site-saturation mutagenesis, which varies a position across all possible amino acids, and the creation of large-scale mutant libraries for directed evolution.
