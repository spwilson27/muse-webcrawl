Incandescent light bulb

An incandescent light bulb, incandescent lamp or incandescent light globe is an electric light that produces light by heating a filament until it glows. The filament is enclosed in a glass bulb containing a vacuum or an inert gas to protect it from oxidation.

History

Many inventors experimented with incandescent lamps in the nineteenth century, including Humphry Davy, Warren de la Rue, and Joseph Swan. Thomas Edison developed the first commercially practical lamp in 1879, using a high-resistance carbon filament in a high vacuum, and built a complete system of generation and distribution around it. Later improvements included carbonized bamboo filaments, tungsten filaments (from about 1906–1911), and gas-filled bulbs.

Efficiency and phase-out

Incandescent bulbs convert only about 5–10 percent of their energy into visible light, the rest being emitted as heat, with efficacies around 12–17 lumens per watt. Because of this inefficiency, many jurisdictions — including the European Union, the United States, and Canada — have phased out general-purpose incandescent bulbs in favour of compact fluorescents and LEDs. The Centennial Light in Livermore, California, burning since 1901, is cited as the longest-lasting bulb.
