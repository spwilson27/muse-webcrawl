Paideia

Education in ancient Greece aimed at forming the whole citizen, body and mind.

Paideia (also spelled paedeia; Ancient Greek: παιδεία, from pais, "child") refers to the rearing and education of the ideal member of the ancient Greek polis. It incorporated the full cultural formation of a person — not merely instruction in subjects, but the shaping of character, taste and civic virtue through athletics, grammar, rhetoric, music, mathematics, geography, natural history and philosophy.

Meaning and scope

In the culture of ancient Greece and of the Hellenistic world, paideia was "the shaping of Greek men" into a distinctive and valued form of humanity — the process by which a society transmits its culture and identity across generations. The ideal it served is often summed up in the concept of kalokagathia, the union of beauty and goodness. The orator and educator Isocrates and the philosopher Plato each advanced rival conceptions of paideia, centred respectively on rhetoric and on philosophy.

In Athens and Sparta

In Athens, boys of citizen families attended schools teaching reading and writing, music and poetry (above all Homer), and physical training in the gymnasium; from the late fifth century BC, sophists and rhetoricians offered advanced education for public life. Sparta's contrasting system, the agoge, was a state-run regime of austere communal training emphasising endurance, obedience and military skill.

Later influence

Paideia shaped the Roman ideal of humanitas and, through it, the Western liberal-arts tradition. The German classicist Werner Jaeger made the concept famous in modern scholarship with his three-volume Paideia: The Ideals of Greek Culture (1933–1947). The term survives in words such as "encyclopedia" (from enkuklios paideia, "general education") and in the names of modern educational movements and journals.
