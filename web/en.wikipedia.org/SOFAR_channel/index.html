SOFAR channel

The SOFAR channel (short for sound fixing and ranging channel), or deep sound channel, is a horizontal layer of water in the ocean at which the speed of sound is at its minimum. Sound waves become trapped in the channel by refraction and can travel enormous distances — thousands of kilometres — with little loss of energy, much as light is guided through an optical fibre.

Sound speed profile in the ocean; the minimum defines the SOFAR channel.

Physical basis

The speed of sound in seawater increases with both temperature and pressure. In the upper ocean, falling temperature with depth lowers the sound speed, while in the deep ocean, rising pressure with depth increases it. The crossover produces a sound-speed minimum, typically at depths of about 600–1,200 metres in mid-latitudes (shallower at high latitudes). Sound emitted near this axis is refracted back toward it from above and below, so it propagates within the layer without reflecting off the surface or the seabed.

Uses

The channel was identified independently during the Second World War (by Maurice Ewing and others). Applications included the SOFAR bomb, an explosive rescue signal used by downed airmen whose detonation could be located by shore hydrophones, and the Cold War SOSUS hydrophone network for tracking submarines. Fin and blue whales exploit the channel to communicate across ocean basins, and ocean acoustic tomography uses travel times in the channel to measure ocean temperatures — demonstrated by the Heard Island Feasibility Test of 1991, whose signals were detected worldwide.
