Bark (botany)

Bark
Plant part: Stem tissues outside the vascular cambium
Includes: Inner bark (phloem) and outer bark (rhytidome)

Bark is the outermost layer of stems and roots of woody plants. In common usage it comprises all tissues outside the vascular cambium: the living inner bark (secondary phloem) and the dead outer bark (rhytidome), which gives tree trunks their characteristic textures.

Structure

Inner bark transports sugars from the leaves and contains fibres such as bast. The outer bark develops from the cork cambium, producing cork cells filled with suberin that resist water loss. As a trunk expands, the outer bark cracks, peels or sheds in species-specific patterns — plates in pines, strips in eucalyptus, smooth sheets in birch.

Functions

Bark protects the tree from mechanical injury, fire, insects, fungi and temperature extremes, and limits desiccation. Thick bark is a key fire adaptation in species such as giant sequoia and cork oak.

Uses

Humans use bark for cork (from the cork oak), cinnamon and other spices, tannins for leather, medicines such as quinine (cinchona bark) and aspirin precursors (willow bark), mulch, fibre and traditional canoes and cladding. Stripping a complete ring of bark — girdling — kills a tree by severing the phloem.
