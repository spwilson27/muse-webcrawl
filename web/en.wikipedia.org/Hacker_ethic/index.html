Hacker ethic


Early hacker culture at MIT

The hacker ethic is a set of values and beliefs that arose from the early hacker culture of the 1950s–1970s, particularly around the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, and was described by journalist Steven Levy in his 1984 book Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution. It emphasises open access to information, mistrust of authority, decentralisation, and judging people by their work rather than credentials.

The principles

Levy summarised the ethic in six tenets:

  • Access to computers—and anything which might teach you something about the way the world works—should be unlimited and total. Always yield to the Hands-On Imperative!
  • All information should be free.
  • Mistrust authority—promote decentralization.
  • Hackers should be judged by their hacking, not bogus criteria such as degrees, age, race, or position.
  • You can create art and beauty on a computer.
  • Computers can change your life for the better.

Interpretations

The Finnish philosopher Pekka Himanen, in The Hacker Ethic and the Spirit of the Information Age (2001), contrasted a hacker work ethic—passion, freedom, and creativity—with the Protestant work ethic described by Max Weber. Richard Stallman and the free software movement drew on similar values in framing software freedom. The term is also distinguished from the "hacker ethics" of the German Chaos Computer Club, whose principles include respecting others' privacy and not damaging public data utilities. Popular media often conflates the hacker ethic with computer crime, a usage hackers themselves generally reject, applying terms like "cracker" to malicious actors.
