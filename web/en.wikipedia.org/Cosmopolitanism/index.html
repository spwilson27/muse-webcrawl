Cosmopolitanism

Cosmopolitanism
Key ideaAll human beings are citizens of a single moral community
OriginAncient Greece; Diogenes the Cynic
TermFrom Greek kosmopolitēs, "citizen of the world"

Cosmopolitanism is the idea that all human beings are, or could or should be, members of a single moral community. This contrasts with the idea that citizens should be divided among social entities such as nations, which are of variable political and ethical importance. The term derives from the Greek kosmopolitēs (κοσμοπολίτης), from kosmos (κόσμος), "world", and politēs (πολίτης), "citizen".

History

The notion is associated with Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323 BCE), a Cynic philosopher who, when asked where he came from, replied "I am a citizen of the world (kosmopolitēs)". The Stoics developed the idea further, arguing that the universe is governed by a rational law and that all rational beings share in it. Immanuel Kant's 1795 essay Perpetual Peace proposed a cosmopolitan right of universal hospitality and a league of free states.

Modern cosmopolitanism

Contemporary philosophers such as Kwame Anthony Appiah defend a "rooted cosmopolitanism" that combines universal moral concern with loyalty to local cultures and communities. Martha Nussbaum has argued for cosmopolitan education centred on world citizenship. Cosmopolitanism is often contrasted with nationalism and communitarianism, and is invoked in debates about globalization, migration, and global justice.
