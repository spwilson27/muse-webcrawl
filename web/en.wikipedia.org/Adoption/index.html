Adoption

Adoption is a process whereby a person assumes the parenting of another, usually a child, from that person's biological or legal parent or parents. Legal adoptions permanently transfer all rights and responsibilities, along with filiation, from the biological parents to the adoptive parents.

Type: Legal / social process
Effect: Creates parent–child relationship
Related: Foster care, guardianship

History

Adoption has been practiced throughout history, with formal procedures recorded in ancient Roman law, where adoption was often used to secure heirs and political alliances. In many societies, informal kinship care arrangements preceded modern legal frameworks. The first modern adoption law is generally considered to be the Massachusetts Adoption of Children Act of 1851, which emphasized the welfare of the child.

Types of adoption

Adoptions may be domestic or intercountry, open or closed, and may involve infants, older children, stepchildren, or adults. Foster care adoption involves adopting a child from the public child welfare system. Private adoption is arranged through agencies or attorneys.

Legal aspects

Adoption law varies by jurisdiction but typically requires a court order terminating the parental rights of the biological parents and creating a new legal parent–child relationship. Adopted children generally gain the same inheritance and citizenship rights as biological children.

Effects and research

Research generally indicates that adopted children fare comparably to their non-adopted peers on many measures, though questions of identity, attachment, and access to birth records remain subjects of discussion. Open adoption, in which contact with birth families is maintained, has become more common in recent decades.
