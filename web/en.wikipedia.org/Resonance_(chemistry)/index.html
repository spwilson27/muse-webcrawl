Resonance (chemistry)

In chemistry, resonance is a way of describing bonding in certain molecules or polyatomic ions whose electronic structure cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure. The true structure is a hybrid (weighted average) of several contributing structures, called resonance structures or canonical forms, which differ only in the distribution of electrons, not in the positions of the nuclei.

Overview

Resonance is a key feature of aromatic compounds such as benzene, whose six carbon–carbon bonds are all equivalent — a fact inexplicable with alternating single and double bonds. Other classic examples include the carbonate and nitrate ions, carboxylate groups, and conjugated dienes. Resonance generally lowers the energy of a molecule (resonance stabilisation) and equalises bond lengths and charges.

Major and minor contributors

Contributing structures are not equally important: those with full octets, minimal charge separation, and negative charge on more electronegative atoms contribute most to the hybrid. Resonance structures are connected by a double-headed arrow (↔) and must not be confused with an equilibrium between distinct species.
