Exchequer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Exchequer was a part of the English government responsible for collecting and managing royal revenues, and later the name of a court of law that heard cases relating to those revenues. The term derives from the chequered cloth or table (resembling a chessboard, scaccarium in Latin) on which accounts were reckoned with counters in the medieval period.

History

The Exchequer was established during the reign of Henry I in the early 12th century. Sheriffs and other royal debtors accounted at the Exchequer twice a year, at Easter and Michaelmas. Receipts were recorded by notches cut into wooden tally sticks, and the detailed accounts were written up in the Pipe Rolls, an almost continuous series of records running from the 12th century to 1833. The Dialogus de Scaccario ("Dialogue concerning the Exchequer"), written in the 1170s and attributed to Richard FitzNeal, is the classic contemporary account of its procedures.

Over time the Exchequer divided into a receipt side, which handled money, and an audit and judicial side. The Court of Exchequer developed into one of the central common-law courts, and its judges were known as Barons of the Exchequer. In the 19th century the court was absorbed into the new High Court of Justice: the Judicature Acts of 1873–75 created an Exchequer Division, which was merged into the Queen's Bench Division in 1880.

Modern usage

In the United Kingdom, "the Exchequer" survives as an informal name for the government treasury generally, and the Chancellor of the Exchequer is the cabinet minister in charge of finance, heading HM Treasury. The Chancellor's official London residence is 11 Downing Street, next door to the Prime Minister's at Number 10.
