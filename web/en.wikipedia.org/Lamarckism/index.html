Lamarckism

Lamarckism, also known as Lamarckian inheritance, is the idea that an organism can pass on to its offspring physical characteristics that the parent organism acquired through use or disuse during its lifetime. It is named after French naturalist Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1744–1829), who incorporated the idea into his theory of evolution set out in Philosophie zoologique (1809).

The theory

Lamarck proposed two laws: first, that organs used more extensively grow and strengthen while those used less deteriorate ("use and disuse"); second, that such modifications are inherited by offspring. The stock example — which predates Lamarck and which he used illustratively — is the giraffe stretching its neck to reach high leaves, producing progressively longer-necked descendants. Lamarck's broader system also included a tendency toward increasing complexity and the spontaneous generation of simple life.

History and decline

The inheritance of acquired characteristics was widely accepted in the 19th century — Charles Darwin himself incorporated a version of it ("pangenesis") — but it fell from favour after August Weismann's germ-plasm theory and experiments in the 1890s, and especially with the rise of Mendelian genetics and the modern synthesis, which holds that hereditary information flows from genes to the body, not the reverse. In the Soviet Union, the doctrine was disastrously revived by Trofim Lysenko, whose rejection of genetics damaged Soviet agriculture and biology for decades. Modern research in epigenetics has found limited mechanisms by which environmental effects can be transmitted across a few generations, prompting debate about whether these should be called "Lamarckian," though they do not vindicate the classical theory.
