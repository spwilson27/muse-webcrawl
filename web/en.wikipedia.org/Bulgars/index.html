Bulgars

The Bulgars (also Bulghars, Bulgari, Bolgars) were semi-nomadic Turkic warrior tribes that flourished in the Pontic–Caspian steppe and the Volga region between the 5th and 7th centuries CE. They spoke a form of Oghuric Turkic. Under pressure from the Khazars, Bulgar groups dispersed: some remained on the Volga, founding Volga Bulgaria, which endured until the Mongol invasion of the 13th century; others migrated westward into the Balkans.

Danubian Bulgars and Bulgaria

In 681, a Bulgar khanate led by Asparuh was recognised by the Byzantine Empire after defeating Emperor Constantine IV, founding the state that became the First Bulgarian Empire on the lower Danube. Over subsequent centuries the Danubian Bulgars merged with the numerous Slavic population and with Thracian remnants, adopted the Slavic language and (from the 9th century) Orthodox Christianity, and gave their name to the modern nation of Bulgaria and its people, the Bulgarians.

Volga Bulgars

The Volga Bulgars built a prosperous trading state at the confluence of the Volga and Kama rivers, controlling commerce between the Rus', the Khazars and the Islamic world. They adopted Islam in 922, a conversion recorded by the traveller Ahmad ibn Fadlan. Their descendants are often associated with today's Volga Tatars and Chuvash, whose language is the sole living survivor of the Oghuric branch of Turkic.
