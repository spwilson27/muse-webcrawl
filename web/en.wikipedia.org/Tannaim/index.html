Tannaim

The Tannaim (Hebrew: תַּנָּאִים, singular Tanna, "teachers" or "repeaters") were the rabbinic sages whose views are recorded in the Mishnah, from approximately 10–220 CE. They were the inheritors of the Pharisaic tradition and the creators of rabbinic Judaism's foundational texts following the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE.

Period and generations

Scholarship conventionally divides the Tannaim into generations spanning the Yavneh period (after 70 CE), the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE), and the Galilean period that followed. Leading figures include Hillel and Shammai (just before the period proper), Rabban Gamaliel, Rabbi Akiva, Rabbi Meir, and Judah ha-Nasi (Rabbi Judah the Prince), who redacted the Mishnah around 200 CE and is regarded as the last of the Tannaim.

Works

The central Tannaitic work is the Mishnah, the first written redaction of Judaism's Oral Torah, organized into six orders (sedarim) covering agriculture, festivals, family law, civil and criminal law, sacrifices, and ritual purity. Other Tannaitic works include the Tosefta, which supplements the Mishnah, and the halakhic midrashim — Mekhilta, Sifra, and Sifre — which derive laws from biblical verses.

Legacy

The Tannaim succeeded the Zugot and were followed by the Amoraim, the sages of the Talmuds who interpreted the Mishnah. Tannaitic teachings quoted in the Talmud but not included in the Mishnah are called baraitot ("outside" teachings).
