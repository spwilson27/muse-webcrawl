Principles of Geology

AuthorCharles Lyell
Published1830–1833 (three volumes)
SubjectGeology; uniformitarianism
Full titlePrinciples of Geology, being an attempt to explain the former changes of the Earth's surface, by reference to causes now in operation

Principles of Geology is a three-volume work by the Scottish geologist Charles Lyell, published between 1830 and 1833. It became one of the most influential scientific books of the nineteenth century, establishing the doctrine of uniformitarianism: the idea that the Earth's features were shaped by slow, gradual processes — erosion, sedimentation, volcanic activity, and uplift — operating at roughly constant rates over immense spans of time, rather than by sudden catastrophes.

Content and argument

Building on the earlier work of James Hutton, Lyell marshalled field evidence, including his observations of Mount Etna and of the Temple of Serapis at Pozzuoli (whose columns, bored by marine molluscs, appeared as the frontispiece), to argue that past geological change could be explained entirely by causes observable in the present. The book went through twelve editions in Lyell's lifetime.

Influence

Charles Darwin took the first volume aboard HMS Beagle and applied Lyell's gradualism and deep timescale to the transmutation of species, making the book a foundation stone of evolutionary biology. Its insistence on an ancient Earth reshaped both geology and the wider Victorian worldview.
