The Pan American Union was the central organisation of the inter-American system in the first half of the twentieth century — the secretariat and permanent organ of the association of the republics of the Americas — and the direct predecessor of the Organization of American States (OAS).

History

The First International Conference of American States, meeting in Washington in 1889–90, created the International Union of American Republics, served by a Commercial Bureau of the American Republics. Renamed the International Bureau of the American Republics and then, in 1910, the Pan American Union, the organisation gathered and published information on trade, law, agriculture, health and education, convened specialised conferences, and prepared the periodic International Conferences of American States that debated hemispheric policy. In 1910 it moved into a purpose-built marble headquarters in Washington, funded partly by the philanthropist Andrew Carnegie.

Activities

Under directors John Barrett and, for a quarter-century, Leo Stanton Rowe, the Union ran divisions dealing with subjects from public health — cooperating with the Pan American Sanitary Bureau, founded in 1902 — to intellectual cooperation and women's rights, lobbying inter-American conferences that produced pioneering conventions on the nationality of women. Its imposing building, with its Hall of the Americas, symbolised the Pan-American ideal of hemispheric solidarity, although Latin American critics sometimes saw the Union as an instrument of United States influence.

Succession

At the Ninth International Conference of American States at Bogotá in 1948, the Organization of American States was created, and the Pan American Union became its General Secretariat, retaining the same Washington headquarters building.
