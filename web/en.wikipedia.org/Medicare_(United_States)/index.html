Medicare is a federal health insurance program in the United States for people age 65 or older, younger people with certain disabilities, and people with end-stage renal disease. It was created in 1965 under Title XVIII of the Social Security Act, signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson, and is administered by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services within the Department of Health and Human Services. Medicare is funded mainly through payroll taxes, premiums paid by enrollees, and general federal revenue.

Parts of Medicare

  • Part A — hospital insurance: inpatient hospital stays, skilled nursing facility care, hospice, and some home health care.
  • Part B — medical insurance: doctors' services, outpatient care, preventive services, and durable medical equipment.
  • Part C (Medicare Advantage) — private plans approved by Medicare that bundle Parts A and B (and usually D).
  • Part D — prescription drug coverage, created by the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003 and launched in 2006.

History

Proposals for national health insurance for the elderly date to the Truman administration, but Medicare was only enacted in 1965 alongside Medicaid. Benefits began on July 1, 1966, with over 19 million enrolled in the first year; former president Harry Truman received the first Medicare card. Eligibility later expanded to people with disabilities (1972) and to permanent kidney failure. Cost control measures such as prospective hospital payment (DRGs, 1983), the shift to Medicare Advantage, and reforms under the Affordable Care Act have repeatedly reshaped the program.
