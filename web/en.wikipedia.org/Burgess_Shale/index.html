Burgess Shale

The Burgess Shale is a fossil-bearing deposit exposed in the Canadian Rockies of British Columbia, Canada. Famous for the exceptional preservation of the soft parts of its fossils, it records one of the earliest complex marine ecosystems, dating to the middle Cambrian, about 508 million years ago.

Discovery

Palaeontologist Charles Doolittle Walcott of the Smithsonian Institution discovered the deposit in 1909 on Fossil Ridge near Field, British Columbia, and quarried it intensively until 1924, collecting more than 65,000 specimens. Walcott interpreted most of the organisms as primitive versions of modern groups.

The Cambrian explosion

Re-examination beginning in the 1960s, led by Harry Whittington and his students Derek Briggs and Simon Conway Morris, revealed a menagerie of bizarre forms: Anomalocaris, a large apex predator; Opabinia, with five eyes and a frontal nozzle; Hallucigenia, walking on paired spines; and Pikaia, an early chordate. Stephen Jay Gould's book Wonderful Life (1989) made the fauna famous and argued for the contingency of evolution, a view later softened as many "weird wonders" were assigned to stem groups of living phyla.

Preservation

The animals were rapidly buried by mud slumps at the foot of an underwater escarpment, in low-oxygen water that suppressed scavenging and decay, preserving gills, guts and limbs as carbon films. The Burgess Shale is the archetype of such deposits (Burgess Shale-type preservation), comparable to China's Chengjiang biota.

Protection

The sites lie within Yoho National Park and form part of the Canadian Rocky Mountain Parks UNESCO World Heritage Site. Collecting is restricted and access to the Walcott Quarry and Mount Stephen trilobite beds is by guided hike only.
