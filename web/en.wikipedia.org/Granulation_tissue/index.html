Granulation Tissue

Granulation tissue is new connective tissue and microscopic blood vessels that form on the surfaces of a wound during the healing process. It typically grows from the base of a wound and is able to fill wounds of almost any size. Examples include the tissue that forms over a scabbed skinned knee and in surgical incisions healing by secondary intention.

Appearance

During the migratory phase of wound healing, granulation tissue is light red or dark pink in colour, moist, soft to the touch and bumpy (granular) in texture — the granularity arising from loops of new capillaries budding into the tissue. It bleeds easily because of its dense network of thin-walled vessels.

Structure and formation

Granulation tissue forms during the proliferative phase of healing, following inflammation. It consists of:

  • New capillaries formed by angiogenesis, driven by factors such as VEGF released by macrophages and injured tissue;
  • Fibroblasts, which synthesize a provisional extracellular matrix of collagen (initially type III), hyaluronic acid and fibronectin;
  • Inflammatory and immune cells, including macrophages and lymphocytes, which clear debris and defend against infection;
  • Myofibroblasts, which later contract the wound edges.

Role in healing

Granulation tissue provides a scaffold over which epithelial cells migrate to resurface the wound, and it resists infection. As healing proceeds it is remodelled: vessels regress, type III collagen is replaced by stronger type I collagen, and the tissue matures into a scar. Excessive granulation tissue ("proud flesh") rising above the wound surface can impede epithelialization and may require treatment.
