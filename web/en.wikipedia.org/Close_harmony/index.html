Close harmony

Close harmony is an arrangement of the notes of chords within a narrow range, usually no more than an octave apart. In music, it is contrasted with open harmony, in which the notes of a chord are spread over a wider range. Close harmony voicings place the chord tones close together, producing a compact, blended sound.

Vocal close harmony

The term is most often associated with vocal groups, particularly barbershop quartets, doo-wop groups, and country and gospel ensembles. In a typical four-part close harmony arrangement, the melody is usually sung by the second-highest voice (the lead in barbershop terminology), with the other voices harmonizing above and below within a tight span. The resulting chords often feature close intervals such as thirds and sixths, and the style prizes smooth voice leading and a ringing, unified blend.

History

Close harmony singing grew out of nineteenth-century parlor and minstrel traditions and was codified in the barbershop style that flourished in the United States in the early twentieth century. It later influenced jazz vocal groups such as the Mills Brothers, country acts like the Andrews Sisters, and rock and roll doo-wop groups of the 1950s. In instrumental music, close voicing is a standard arranging technique for horn sections and keyboard comping.

Characteristics

Because the voices move within a narrow compass, close harmony arrangements tend to use inversions and doubled chord tones to keep every part singable. The style is closely associated with consonant, triadic harmony and with dominant seventh chords resolved in stepwise fashion.
