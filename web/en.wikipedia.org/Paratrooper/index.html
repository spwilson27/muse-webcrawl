Paratrooper

Paratrooper Role: Airborne infantry
Delivery: Static-line or free-fall parachute from aircraft
First combat jumps: 1940 (Germany, Norway/Denmark)
Famous units: US 82nd and 101st Airborne, British Parachute Regiment, German Fallschirmjäger, Russian VDV

A paratrooper or military parachutist is a soldier trained to parachute into a military objective and to operate there, usually as part of an airborne force. Paratroopers' tactical advantage is that they can be dropped into locations not accessible by land or sea, allowing surprise attacks behind enemy lines, the seizure of airfields and bridges, and the disruption of enemy communications and reserves.

History

The concept was pioneered in the 1920s and 1930s, notably by Italy and the Soviet Union, which held mass airborne exercises in the 1930s; the United States military observed the 1935 Kiev manoeuvres and eventually established its own units. Germany's Fallschirmjäger conducted the first combat parachute operations in 1940, capturing fortifications in Belgium and the Netherlands — most famously Fort Eben-Emael — and spearheading the costly airborne invasion of Crete in 1941, after which Hitler curtailed large German drops.

The Allies undertook the largest airborne operations of the Second World War: the American and British drops on D-Day in Normandy (6 June 1944), Operation Market Garden in the Netherlands (September 1944), and Operation Varsity across the Rhine (March 1945). In the Pacific, Japanese and American paratroopers both saw action, including the U.S. 503rd Parachute Infantry's jump at Corregidor in 1945.

Training and technique

Most paratroopers jump by static line from transport aircraft (such as the C-130 Hercules or C-17 Globemaster) at low altitude, typically around 300–400 metres, so as to minimise time exposed to ground fire; round canopies limit steering but allow dense mass drops. Specialist units train in military free fall (HALO and HAHO) from high altitude. Basic parachute training in most armies includes tower jumps, instruction in parachute-landing falls to reduce injury, and a set of qualifying jumps, after which soldiers earn their parachute wings. Jump injuries, especially ankle and spinal trauma, are a persistent occupational hazard.

Modern role

Since 1945, large-scale opposed parachute assaults have been rare — notable examples include the French and British drops at Suez in 1956 and the American jump into Panama in 1989 — because modern air defences threaten slow transport aircraft. Airborne forces nevertheless remain valued as rapidly deployable elite light infantry, and paratrooper units are maintained by dozens of countries.
