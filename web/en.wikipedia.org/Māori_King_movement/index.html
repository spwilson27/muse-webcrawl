Māori King movement


Labelled image: Tūrongo House, Ngāruawāhia

The Māori King movement, called the Kīngitanga in Māori, is a movement that arose among some Māori iwi (tribes) in the central North Island of New Zealand in the 1850s. It was established to halt the alienation of Māori land, promote unity among iwi, and provide a Māori monarch who could stand as a counterpart to Queen Victoria. The position of Māori monarch has been held continuously since 1858 and carries great mana (prestige), though it has no constitutional role in New Zealand law.

Origins and the first kings

In 1858 the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero was installed as the first Māori King at Ngāruawāhia, which remains the seat of the Kīngitanga at Tūrangawaewae Marae. He was succeeded by his son Tāwhiao (1860–1894), who led the movement through the New Zealand Wars: in 1863–64 government forces invaded the Waikato, and the King Country became a refuge after confiscation (raupatu) of over a million acres of Waikato land. Tāwhiao's successors Mahuta (1894–1912) and Te Rata (1912–1933) engaged, cautiously, with the settler parliament.

Twentieth century to today

Princess Te Puea Hērangi rebuilt Tūrangawaewae and revived the movement's fortunes in the early twentieth century. King Korokī reigned 1933–1966, followed by his daughter Te Atairangikaahu, who reigned for forty years (1966–2006) — the longest-serving Māori monarch — and was succeeded by her son Tūheitia Paki (2006–2024). On Tūheitia's death in August 2024, his daughter Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō was anointed as the eighth Māori monarch. The Kīngitanga remains especially strong among the Tainui iwi, while other iwi have never joined it. Annual Koroneihana (coronation) celebrations draw thousands to Ngāruawāhia.
