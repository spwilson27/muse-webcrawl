Pentagon Papers

The Pentagon Papers, officially titled Report of the Office of the Secretary of Defense Vietnam Task Force, is a United States Department of Defense history of the United States' political and military involvement in Vietnam from 1945 to 1968. Released by Daniel Ellsberg, who had worked on the study, the papers were first brought to the attention of the public on the front page of The New York Times on June 13, 1971.

Contents

The study detailed how successive administrations — Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Johnson — had expanded American involvement in Vietnam while privately doubting the prospects of success and, in several instances, misleading Congress and the public. It showed that the Johnson administration had systematically lied about the scope of the war, including bombing campaigns in Cambodia and Laos not reported in the mainstream media.

Legal case

The Nixon administration sought an injunction to stop publication, resulting in the landmark Supreme Court case New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), in which the Court ruled 6–3 that the government had not met the heavy burden required to justify prior restraint of the press. Ellsberg was charged under the Espionage Act, but charges were dismissed in 1973 due to governmental misconduct, including the break-in at the office of Ellsberg's psychiatrist by the White House "Plumbers".

Impact

The Papers deepened public distrust of the government (the "credibility gap"), influenced debate over the War Powers Resolution, and remain a touchstone for debates over whistleblowing and press freedom. The complete study was declassified and released by the National Archives in June 2011.
