Tiantai (Chinese: 天台; pinyin: Tiāntāi; Japanese: Tendai; Korean: Cheontae) is an important school of Mahayana Buddhism that developed in China during the 6th century CE. Based on the Lotus Sutra, it is named after Mount Tiantai in Zhejiang, where its founder, the monk Zhiyi (538–597), lived and taught. Tiantai influenced other schools including Chan (Zen), Pure Land, and Nichiren Buddhism, and its Japanese form, Tendai, became one of the most influential traditions in Japanese Buddhism.

History

Zhiyi systematized the Buddhist teachings transmitted to him through his teacher Huisi, drawing on the translations of Kumārajīva. Under Sui dynasty patronage — he was honoured by Emperor Yang with the title Zhizhe, “the Wise One” — Tiantai became a dominant school. The tradition later revived in the Song dynasty through figures such as Siming Zhili, and was transmitted to Japan by Saichō in the early ninth century and to Korea.

Doctrines

Tiantai is known for its comprehensive classification of Buddhist teachings (panjiao), which ranks the sutras and doctrines as progressive expressions of a single truth culminating in the Lotus Sutra. Its central philosophical concepts are drawn from Zhiyi's works, including the Mohe Zhiguan: the Three Truths (emptiness, provisional existence, and the middle), the teaching that a single moment of thought contains the Three Thousand Realms (ichinen sanzen), and the doctrine of universal Buddha-nature, extending even to insentient beings and inherent evil (ichchantika included).

Practice

Tiantai meditation combines calm-abiding (śamatha) and insight (vipaśyanā) — the “cessation and contemplation” (zhiguan) of Zhiyi's great meditation manual. Practice also includes Lotus Sutra devotion, repentance rituals, and esoteric and Pure Land elements absorbed over the school's history.
