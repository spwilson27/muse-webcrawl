Epic film

Epic films are a style of filmmaking with large-scale production, sweeping scope and spectacle. The term has shifted meanings over time, sometimes designating a film genre and at other times simply a big-budget production. Like the literary and theatrical epic, the film genre is characterised by heroic subjects, historical or mythological settings, lavish costumes and sets, an orchestral score, extended running times and casts of thousands.

History

The genre began in silent cinema with Italian spectacles such as Quo Vadis? (1913) and Cabiria (1914), which pioneered camera movement and mass scenes. D. W. Griffith's Intolerance (1916) and Cecil B. DeMille's biblical films carried the form into Hollywood. The genre's golden age came in the 1950s and 1960s, when widescreen formats (CinemaScope, VistaVision, 70mm) and "sword-and-sandal" and biblical subjects were used to lure audiences from television: The Ten Commandments (1956), Ben-Hur (1959) — winner of eleven Academy Awards — Spartacus (1960), Lawrence of Arabia (1962) and The Fall of the Roman Empire (1964).

Decline and revival

Escalating costs and the failure of films such as Cleopatra (1963) and The Fall of the Roman Empire ended the classical epic cycle, though industry spectacle continued in other genres. The epic revived in the digital era: Braveheart (1995), Gladiator (2000) and Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings trilogy (2001–2003) used computer-generated imagery to recreate mass battles at manageable cost, while non-Western cinemas produced epics such as India's Baahubali films.

Characteristics

Epic films commonly run over two hours (often with intermission in roadshow releases), were presented as prestige events with souvenir programmes, and centre on turning points of history interwoven with personal, often romantic, subplots. Critics distinguish the historical epic from the war film, the western and the fantasy film, with which it overlaps.
