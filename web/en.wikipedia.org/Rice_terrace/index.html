Rice terraces

Rice terraces are terraced paddy fields built into hillsides and mountains for wet-rice cultivation, allowing farming on steep slopes by creating level, flooded plots separated by earthen or stone retaining walls. They are found across Asia — notably in the Philippines, China, Vietnam, Indonesia, Nepal and Japan — and represent some of the most spectacular human-made agricultural landscapes in the world.

How they work

Terraces convert slopes into a staircase of level paddies. Water is diverted from streams or springs at the top and flows by gravity from terrace to terrace through notches in the bunds, giving each field a shallow, controllable flood. The standing water suppresses weeds, fixes nitrogen via blue-green algae, moderates soil temperature and supports fish and ducks in some systems. Stone-faced terraces in the Philippine Cordilleras were built largely by hand by Ifugao and neighbouring peoples over centuries — popular accounts say two thousand years, though scholars debate the antiquity of the high terraces.

Notable systems

The Banaue and Cordillera terraces of Luzon, including Batad and Bangaan, were inscribed by UNESCO in 1995 as a living cultural landscape. China's Honghe Hani Rice Terraces in Yunnan cover some 16,600 hectares beneath Yuanyang. In Bali, the subak cooperative irrigation system, coordinated through water temples, has managed terraced paddies for a millennium. Vietnam's Sa Pa and Mu Cang Chai terraces and Japan's tanada are also celebrated.

Challenges

Terrace systems are labour-intensive and increasingly threatened by rural outmigration, ageing farmers, tourism pressure, conversion of paddies and climate change. Abandoned terraces erode rapidly, so conservation programmes in the Philippines and China pay farmers to maintain them; the Philippine Cordillera terraces were removed from UNESCO's Danger List in 2012 after restoration efforts.
