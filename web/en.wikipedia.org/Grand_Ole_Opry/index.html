Grand Ole Opry

The Grand Ole Opry is a weekly American country music stage concert in Nashville, Tennessee, founded on November 28, 1925, by George D. Hay as a one-hour radio "barn dance" on WSM radio station. It is the longest-running radio broadcast in US history and one of the most famous stages in country music, often called "the show that made country music famous."

History

The program began as the WSM Barn Dance in 1925. According to tradition, announcer George D. Hay renamed it the "Grand Ole Opry" in 1927 after following an NBC broadcast of classical music, quipping that listeners would now hear "grand ole opry". The show's popularity grew rapidly, and in 1943 it moved to the Ryman Auditorium, which it called home for more than three decades.

Grand Ole Opry House

In 1974 the Opry moved to its current home, the Grand Ole Opry House at Opryland, northeast of downtown Nashville. A circle of oak cut from the stage of the Ryman Auditorium was inlaid in the new stage, so that performers literally stand on a piece of the Opry's history.

Membership

Invitation to become a member of the Opry is considered one of country music's highest honors. Famous members have included Hank Williams, Patsy Cline, Johnny Cash, Dolly Parton, Loretta Lynn and Garth Brooks. Members are expected to perform on the show regularly, and the Opry continues to broadcast several times a week.

Cultural significance

The Grand Ole Opry helped establish Nashville as the center of the American country music industry and has been credited with popularizing the genre across the United States. It was named an official landmark and the Opry House is listed on the National Register of Historic Places.
