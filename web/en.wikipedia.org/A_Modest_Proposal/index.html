A Modest Proposal is a Juvenalian satirical essay written and published by the Irish writer and clergyman Jonathan Swift in 1729. The essay suggests that rather than depending on charity or working, impoverished Irish might ease their economic troubles by selling their children as food to rich gentlemen and ladies. This satirical hyperbole mocked heartless attitudes towards the poor and British policy toward the Irish in general.

Summary

Writing in the voice of a rational, calculating "projector", Swift laments the crowds of begging mothers and children in Ireland and computes, with mock statistical precision, that of 120,000 children born to poor parents each year, 100,000 might be sold at the age of one year as a food source. He describes their culinary merits in detail, estimates prices and profits, and argues the scheme would reduce the number of Catholics, give tenants a valuable commodity, improve marriages, and benefit taverns and the economy. Toward the end he lists — only to dismiss — a series of genuine reforms (taxing absentee landlords, using Irish manufactures, rejecting foreign luxury), which are the essay's actual programme; the persona protests his own sincerity, noting that his youngest child is nine and his wife past child-bearing.

Context and rhetoric

The pamphlet responded to famine, poverty and English trade restrictions that devastated Ireland in the 1720s, and to a flourishing genre of earnest "projector" pamphlets proposing economic schemes. Swift's use of cold economic language — the language of political arithmetic — to discuss human beings is the core of the satire: it imitates and exposes how the poor were treated as mere numbers. The phrase "modest proposal" has since entered English as a stock allusion to a shocking, ostensibly reasonable solution.

Legacy

The essay is regarded as one of the greatest examples of sustained irony in English literature and is widely taught in schools and universities as a classic of satire and rhetorical argument.
