Color depth

Color depth or colour depth (also known as bit depth) is the number of bits used to indicate the colour of a single pixel in a bitmapped image or video frame buffer, or the number of bits used for each colour component of a single pixel. Higher color depth gives a wider range of distinct colours and a more faithful representation of the image.

Common depths

Early systems used 1-bit monochrome (two colours) or 8-bit colour drawn from a palette of 256 colours. A depth of 15 or 16 bits per pixel is known as "high color", allocating 5 bits to each of the red, green and blue components (with 16-bit modes often giving green an extra bit). Images with 24 bits per pixel — 8 bits per component — are called "true color" and can represent about 16.7 million distinct colours, roughly the limit of human perception. Modern professional and high-dynamic-range workflows use 30-bit or deeper "deep color", with 10 or more bits per component.

Indexed color

With indexed colour, pixels do not store colour values directly but indices into a palette or lookup table. This reduced memory use in early computers, at the cost of limiting an image to a small set of colours chosen from a broader range, as in the GIF image format.
