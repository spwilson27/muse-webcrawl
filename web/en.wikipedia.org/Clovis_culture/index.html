Clovis culture

Clovis points are characterized by a distinctive fluted base

The Clovis culture is a prehistoric Paleoamerican culture, named for distinct stone and bone tools found in close association with Pleistocene fauna near Clovis, New Mexico, in the 1920s and 1930s. The culture emerged around 11,100–10,800 BCE (13,100–12,800 years ago) at the end of the last glacial period, and is characterized by the manufacture of large, fluted projectile points known as Clovis points.

Characteristics

The Clovis culture ranges over much of North America. Its tool kit includes bifacially worked spear points with a concave flute running up each face from the base, blade cores, end scrapers, and tools of bone and ivory. Clovis sites often occur as kill sites associated with the remains of megafauna such as mammoths and mastodons, suggesting highly mobile hunters who exploited large game.

Clovis first theory

For much of the twentieth century, the dominant "Clovis first" model held that the Clovis people were the first inhabitants of the Americas, crossing the Bering land bridge and spreading southward through an ice-free corridor around 13,000 years ago. Discoveries of earlier sites — including Monte Verde in Chile, Cooper's Ferry in Idaho, and the White Sands footprints in New Mexico — have since shown that people reached the Americas several thousand years before Clovis, and the Clovis-first model has been abandoned by most archaeologists.

Decline

The Clovis culture disappeared abruptly around 12,800 years ago, at the onset of the Younger Dryas cooling episode and near the extinction of many North American megafauna genera. Later cultures such as the Folsom culture succeeded Clovis in parts of the continent. Genetic studies show that most Native American populations descend in part from populations related to the Clovis-era Anzick child burial in Montana.
