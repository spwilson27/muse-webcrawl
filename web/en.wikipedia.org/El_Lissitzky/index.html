El Lissitzky

Lazar Markovich Lissitzky (Russian: Ла́зарь Ма́ркович Лиси́цкий; 23 November 1890 – 30 December 1941), better known as El Lissitzky (Russian: Эль Лиси́цкий; Yiddish: על ליסיצקי), was a Russian artist, designer, photographer, typographer, polemicist and architect. He was an important figure of the Russian avant-garde, helping develop Suprematism with his teacher Kazimir Malevich, and designing numerous exhibition displays and propaganda works for the Soviet Union.

Career

Lissitzky studied engineering in Darmstadt, Germany, and after the Russian Revolution joined the faculty of the art school in Vitebsk, where Malevich led the UNOVIS group. There he developed his Proun works — abstract compositions he described as "the station where one changes from painting to architecture" — which fused Suprematist geometry with architectural space. His propaganda poster Beat the Whites with the Red Wedge (1919) became an icon of revolutionary art.

In the 1920s Lissitzky spent much time in Germany, where he linked the Soviet avant-garde with the Bauhaus and De Stijl movements, co-editing the trilingual journal Veshch/Gegenstand/Objet and collaborating with Kurt Schwitters and László Moholy-Nagy. He pioneered modern exhibition design with the Abstract Cabinet in Hanover (1927) and the Soviet pavilion at the Cologne Pressa exhibition (1928), and his typographic and photomontage work influenced graphic design worldwide.

Later work and death

Returning to the Soviet Union, Lissitzky designed propaganda exhibitions and the magazine USSR in Construction. His late work was increasingly constrained by the demands of socialist realism. He died of tuberculosis in Moscow in 1941.
