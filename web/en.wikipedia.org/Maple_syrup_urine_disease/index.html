Maple syrup urine disease

Type: Inborn error of metabolism
Inheritance: Autosomal recessive
Cause: Deficiency of branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase
Management: Lifelong dietary restriction of branched-chain amino acids

Maple syrup urine disease (MSUD) is an inherited metabolic disorder in which the body cannot properly break down the branched-chain amino acids leucine, isoleucine and valine. Their accumulation, especially of leucine and its keto acid, is toxic to the brain. The condition takes its name from the sweet, burnt-sugar or maple-syrup odour of affected infants' urine and sweat.

Cause

MSUD results from deficiency of the branched-chain α-keto acid dehydrogenase complex, caused by mutations in genes including BCKDHA, BCKDHB and DBT. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. The classic form is notably more common in Old Order Mennonite communities owing to a founder effect.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Newborns with classic MSUD appear well at birth but within days develop poor feeding, lethargy, irritability, abnormal movements and a characteristic odour; without treatment, metabolic crises can lead to coma, brain injury or death. Many countries include MSUD in newborn blood-spot screening, allowing treatment to start before symptoms.

Treatment

Management is a lifelong, carefully monitored diet restricting branched-chain amino acids, with special medical formulas and emergency regimens during illness to prevent decompensation. Liver transplantation can substantially correct the metabolic defect in severe cases.
