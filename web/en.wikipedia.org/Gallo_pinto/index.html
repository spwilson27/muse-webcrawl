Gallo pinto (Spanish for "spotted rooster") is a traditional rice-and-beans dish from Central America, regarded as a national dish of both Costa Rica and Nicaragua, where the question of its origin is a matter of friendly (and sometimes not-so-friendly) national rivalry. The name refers not to any chicken in the recipe but to the speckled appearance produced by mixing dark beans with white rice.

Preparation and regional versions

Gallo pinto is typically made by frying day-old cooked rice with cooked beans — black beans in Costa Rica's Central Valley, red beans on its Caribbean coast and in Nicaragua — together with diced onion, sweet peppers and cilantro. In Costa Rica it is almost always seasoned with Salsa Lizano, a tangy Worcestershire-like sauce, and served at breakfast with eggs, fried or scrambled, natilla (sour cream), fried plantains, cheese, and tortillas or bread. The Nicaraguan version is commonly fried in coconut oil or lard on the Caribbean coast, served with eggs, cheese, plantains, and bread or tortillas, and is eaten at any meal.

Cultural significance

Gallo pinto is a staple of rural and working-class cuisine, historically a way of using leftover rice and beans. Both countries celebrate the dish: Costa Rica has set records for the largest serving of gallo pinto, feeding thousands at public festivals. Related rice-and-bean dishes across the Caribbean and Latin America include Cuban moros y cristianos, Salvadoran casamiento, and Jamaican rice and peas.
