Sustainable fishery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sustainable fishery is a fishery that can be maintained indefinitely — theoretically indefinitely — without reducing the targeted population's ability to continue producing at an optimal level, and keeping the impacts on other species and the ecosystem within acceptable tolerances. Such fisheries balance harvest with the reproductive capacity of fish stocks and are a central objective of modern fisheries management under frameworks such as the United Nations Fish Stocks Agreement and the FAO Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries.

Principles

Sustainable fisheries management rests on understanding the population dynamics of exploited species: growth, recruitment, natural mortality, and fishing mortality. Key reference points include the maximum sustainable yield (MSY), the largest catch that a stock can support over the long term, and limit and target biomass levels. Harvest control rules adjust allowable catches according to stock assessments so that fishing mortality does not exceed sustainable levels and spawning biomass remains above thresholds that safeguard recruitment.

Threats to sustainability

Major threats include overfishing (fishing beyond sustainable levels), illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing, bycatch of non-target species including juveniles and protected species, destructive gear and habitat damage, subsidies that maintain excess fleet capacity, and climate change, which shifts stock distributions and productivity. According to the FAO, the share of marine fishery stocks within biologically sustainable levels has declined to roughly two-thirds of assessed stocks in recent decades.

Management tools

Managers use catch quotas (total allowable catches), individual transferable quotas and other catch-share systems, effort controls, minimum landing sizes, closed areas and seasons, marine protected areas, and gear restrictions to keep exploitation sustainable. Ecosystem-based fisheries management additionally accounts for predators, prey, bycatch species, and habitats.

Certification and markets

Ecolabelling schemes, most prominently the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC), certify fisheries that meet standards for stock status, ecosystem impact, and effective management, allowing their products to carry a label informing consumers. Retailer and consumer demand for certified seafood, and sustainability guides published by groups such as the Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch, have created market incentives for better practice, though the rigour and effects of certification remain debated.
