Accommodation reflex

The accommodation reflex (or accommodation–convergence reflex) is a reflex action of the eye in response to focusing on a near object, followed by looking at a distant object (and vice versa). It comprises three coordinated responses: a change in lens shape (accommodation), convergence of both eyes, and pupillary constriction (miosis). Together they keep the image of a near object sharply focused on the retina.

Pathway

Blurred retinal images are relayed through the optic nerve to the visual cortex, which projects to the midbrain. Parasympathetic fibers from the Edinger–Westphal nucleus travel with the oculomotor nerve (CN III) to the ciliary ganglion; postganglionic fibers contract the ciliary muscle, relaxing the zonular fibers so the elastic lens rounds up and gains refractive power. Simultaneously, both medial rectus muscles converge the eyes, and the sphincter pupillae narrows the pupil, increasing depth of field.

Aging and disorders

The amplitude of accommodation declines with age as the lens hardens, producing presbyopia, typically noticeable in the mid-forties. A classic abnormality is light-near dissociation, in which the pupil constricts to near effort but not to light — seen in the Argyll Robertson pupil of neurosyphilis and in dorsal midbrain lesions. Damage to the reflex pathway may also cause blurred near vision after head injury or in Adie's tonic pupil.
