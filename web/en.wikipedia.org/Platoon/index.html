A platoon is a military unit typically composed of two to four squads, sections, or patrols. Platoons are the smallest military unit led by a commissioned officer, usually a lieutenant (called the platoon leader or platoon commander), assisted by a platoon sergeant. Several platoons—usually three to five—make up a company.

Organisation

In the United States Army, a rifle platoon typically consists of three rifle squads of nine soldiers and a weapons squad, with a headquarters including the platoon leader, platoon sergeant, radiotelephone operator, and medic. In the British Army and many Commonwealth forces, the equivalent unit is divided into sections of eight soldiers commanded by a corporal, with the platoon commanded by a second lieutenant or lieutenant and a platoon sergeant.

History

The word derives from the French peloton ("small ball" or detachment), from the 17th-century practice of grouping musketeers for volley fire. Platoon fire—in which subunits fired in rotation to maintain continuous fire—was a standard infantry tactic in the 18th century. The modern platoon as a permanent tactical subunit emerged as firepower and dispersion increased in the 19th and 20th centuries, particularly during the First World War, when platoons adopted their own light machine guns and grenades and became self-contained combined-arms units.

Types

Beyond infantry rifle platoons, armed forces employ tank platoons (typically four tanks), mortar platoons, reconnaissance platoons, engineer platoons, signals platoons, and anti-tank platoons, each with organization and equipment specific to its role.

Other uses

The term is also used in a few civilian contexts, such as police platoons and, in vehicle automation, "platooning" of closely following trucks. The word is also familiar from the 1986 Oliver Stone film Platoon, about the Vietnam War.
