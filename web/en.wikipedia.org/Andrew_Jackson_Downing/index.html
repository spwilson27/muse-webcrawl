Andrew Jackson Downing (31 October 1815 – 28 July 1852) was an American landscape designer, horticulturist, and writer, a prominent advocate of the Gothic Revival and Italianate styles of architecture, and editor of The Horticulturist magazine. He is considered a founder of American landscape architecture, and his writings shaped the design of middle-class suburban homes, rural cemeteries, and public parks in the United States.

Life and work

Born in Newburgh, New York, where he worked in his family's nursery business, Downing published A Treatise on the Theory and Practice of Landscape Gardening, Adapted to North America in 1841, the first such book by an American. Cottage Residences (1842) and The Architecture of Country Houses (1850) provided house plans and moral arguments that beautiful, orderly homes would improve their occupants. His designs promoted the Picturesque style, with irregular plantings, curving paths, and architecture harmonising with nature.

In 1850 President Millard Fillmore commissioned Downing to design the public grounds of Washington, D.C. — the National Mall, the Ellipse, and the Smithsonian grounds — in a romantic, naturalistic style. Downing brought the young English architect Calvert Vaux to America as his partner; Vaux later, with Frederick Law Olmsted, designed Central Park in the spirit Downing had championed.

Death and legacy

Downing died at 36 in the burning of the steamboat Henry Clay on the Hudson River on 28 July 1852. His death was widely mourned; the urn memorial in the Smithsonian Castle gardens commemorates him as a pioneer of American landscape design. His advocacy of public parks — arguing they were democratic "social advantages" — helped inspire the American parks movement.
