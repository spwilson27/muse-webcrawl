Umami

Umami (from Japanese: うま味), or savoriness, is one of the five basic tastes, alongside sweetness, sourness, saltiness, and bitterness. It is the taste of glutamates and nucleotides, experienced as a pleasant "brothy" or "meaty" flavour. Foods rich in umami include fermented and aged products, cooked meats, tomatoes, mushrooms, kombu seaweed, and cheeses such as Parmesan.

Discovery

Umami was identified by Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda of Tokyo Imperial University, who in 1908 isolated glutamic acid from kombu dashi and found it responsible for the broth's distinctive savoury taste. Ikeda patented a method of producing the seasoning monosodium glutamate (MSG), commercialised as Ajinomoto. In 1913 his student Shintaro Kodama identified inosinate in bonito flakes; in the 1950s Akira Kuninaka discovered the synergistic effect between glutamates and nucleotides such as guanylate.

Biology

Umami is detected by specialised taste receptors on the tongue, principally the T1R1/T1R3 heterodimer and truncated metabotropic glutamate receptors. The identification of these receptors in the early 2000s secured scientific acceptance of umami as a basic taste, long after it had become established in Japanese cuisine. Umami compounds occur naturally in human breast milk and in many traditional flavour bases worldwide, from Roman garum to Worcestershire sauce.

Culinary use

Chefs exploit umami to add depth to dishes; combining glutamate-rich ingredients with nucleotide-rich ones multiplies the effect. MSG is widely used in processed foods and East Asian cooking, and has been the subject of public controversy over alleged adverse effects, though regulatory reviews have consistently found it safe at normal levels of consumption.
