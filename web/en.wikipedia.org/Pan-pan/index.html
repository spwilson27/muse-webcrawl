Pan-pan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pan-pan is the international radio urgency signal used in maritime and aviation communication to indicate that a vessel or aircraft has an urgent problem but that there is no immediate danger to life or to the craft itself — as distinguished from mayday, the distress signal reserved for grave and imminent danger. The word derives from the French panne, a breakdown.

Usage

The call is spoken three times — "pan-pan, pan-pan, pan-pan" — followed by the intended recipient (for example, "all stations" or a coast guard station), the caller's identification, position, and the nature of the problem and the assistance required. Examples include a vessel with a fouled propeller or engine failure, a small fire that has been contained, or an aircraft with a minor mechanical fault or a sick, but not critical, passenger.

A medical variant, "pan-pan medico", historically requested medical advice or assistance by radio. Urgency traffic has priority over routine communications but yields to mayday distress traffic. Misuse or escalation is straightforward: if the situation deteriorates into grave and imminent danger, the caller upgrades to mayday.

Related signals

The hierarchy of radiotelephone priority in the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System runs: distress (mayday), urgency (pan-pan), safety (sécurité), and routine. Pan-pan is also used in text form (PAN PAN) in written maritime safety messages.
