Fourth Council of the Lateran

LocationLateran Palace, Rome

The Fourth Council of the Lateran or Lateran IV was convoked by Pope Innocent III in April 1213 and opened at the Lateran Palace in Rome on 11 November 1215. It is considered by the Catholic Church to be the twelfth ecumenical council and was the largest and most influential council of the Middle Ages, attended by some 400 bishops, 800 abbots and priors, and representatives of most European rulers.

Decrees

The council issued seventy constitutions. Canon 1 defined the doctrine of transubstantiation — the first conciliar use of the term — and set out a confession of faith directed against the Cathars and Waldensians. Canon 21, Omnis utriusque sexus, required every adult Christian to confess to a priest and receive communion at least once a year, a rule that shaped Western religious practice for centuries. Other canons reformed clerical discipline, regulated episcopal elections and preaching, ordered Jews and Muslims to wear distinguishing dress (canon 68), prohibited new religious orders, and barred clergy from participating in ordeals — effectively ending the judicial ordeal in much of Europe (canon 18).

Political business

Innocent used the assembly to advance a new crusade (the Fifth Crusade, proclaimed in the constitution Ad liberandam), to settle the disputed imperial succession between Frederick II and Otto IV, and to confirm the annulment disputes surrounding King John of England, whose barons were then in revolt. The council also condemned the teachings of Joachim of Fiore on the Trinity and affirmed the orthodoxy of Peter Lombard.

Legacy

Lateran IV marks the high point of the medieval papal monarchy. Its decrees entered canon law and governed Catholic life until the Council of Trent revisited many of them in the 16th century.
