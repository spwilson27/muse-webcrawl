Progressive rock

Progressive rock (shortened as prog rock or simply prog) is a broad genre of rock music that primarily developed in the United Kingdom and United States through the mid- to late 1960s, peaking in the early-to-mid 1970s. The genre fused rock with influences from classical music, jazz, and folk, favouring complex compositions, extended instrumental passages, unusual time signatures, and concept albums over standard popular song structures.

Characteristics

Progressive rock songs often abandon the verse-chorus format in favour of multi-movement suites lasting ten minutes or more. Lyrics frequently draw on science fiction, fantasy, mythology, and literature. Instrumentation expanded beyond the standard rock line-up to include Mellotron, Hammond organ, synthesizers, flute, and orchestral arrangements, and albums were often conceived as unified works with elaborate cover art, notably by Roger Dean and Hipgnosis.

History

Precursors include the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967) and albums by the Moody Blues and Procol Harum. King Crimson's In the Court of the Crimson King (1969) is widely regarded as a foundational prog album. The genre reached its commercial and critical peak in the early 1970s with records such as Pink Floyd's The Dark Side of the Moon (1973), Yes's Close to the Edge (1972), and Genesis's Selling England by the Pound (1973).

By the late 1970s, prog had fallen from favour, criticised as pretentious and displaced by punk rock and new wave. A leaner neo-progressive movement emerged in the 1980s with bands such as Marillion, and the genre enjoyed a sustained revival from the 1990s onward through acts including Porcupine Tree, Dream Theater, and Tool, alongside progressive metal.
