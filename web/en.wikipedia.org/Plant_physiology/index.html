Plant physiology is the subdiscipline of botany concerned with the functioning of plants — their internal chemistry, physical processes, and the ways they grow, develop, and respond to their environment. Where plant anatomy describes structure, physiology asks how the living machinery works: how energy and matter are captured, transported, stored, and spent.

Core processes

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in chloroplasts, splitting water, releasing oxygen, and fixing carbon dioxide into sugars via the Calvin cycle; variants such as C4 and CAM photosynthesis adapt the process to hot or arid climates. Respiration releases that stored energy as ATP. Transpiration — evaporation from stomata — drives the ascent of water and minerals through the xylem, while the phloem translocates sugars from sources to sinks. Mineral nutrition studies the uptake and roles of essential elements such as nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and iron.

Growth, hormones, and responses

Development is orchestrated by plant hormones: auxins (cell elongation, apical dominance), gibberellins (stem growth, germination), cytokinins (cell division), abscisic acid (drought and dormancy), and ethylene (fruit ripening, senescence), among others. Physiologists study how plants perceive light through photoreceptors such as phytochrome, measure time for flowering (photoperiodism), bend toward stimuli (tropisms), and defend themselves against pathogens, herbivores, drought, salinity, and temperature extremes through signalling networks involving jasmonic and salicylic acids.

Applications

Plant physiology underlies modern agriculture: breeding and engineering crops for yield, water-use efficiency, nitrogen efficiency, and stress tolerance; managing fertilisers and irrigation; and controlling ripening and storage. Stephen Hales's Vegetable Staticks (1727) founded the experimental field, and landmark contributions followed from Jan Ingenhousz (photosynthesis and light), Julius von Sachs, and Jagadish Chandra Bose. Today molecular genetics, using the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana, has fused physiology with genomics.
