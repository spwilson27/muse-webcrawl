Cockayne syndrome

Cockayne syndrome (CS), also called Neill–Dingwall syndrome, is a rare, fatal autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by growth failure, impaired development of the nervous system, abnormal sensitivity to sunlight (photosensitivity), eye disorders, and premature aging. Failure to thrive and neurological disorders are criteria for diagnosis, while photosensitivity, hearing loss, eye abnormalities, and cavities are other very common features. Problems with any or all of the internal organs are possible. It is associated with a group of disorders called leukodystrophies, which are conditions characterized by deterioration of the white matter of the brain. The underlying disorder is one of defective DNA repair.

Signs and symptoms

The severity of Cockayne syndrome varies greatly. The classical form, sometimes called type I, usually becomes apparent in the first or second year of life. Children are typically born with a normal appearance but then fail to gain weight and grow at the expected rate. Characteristic features include a small head (microcephaly), an aged-looking face with sunken eyes, a thin nose, large ears, and loss of subcutaneous fat. Neurological symptoms include intellectual disability, loss of previously acquired skills, spasticity, and hearing loss. Many affected individuals are extremely sensitive to sunlight and sunburn easily even after brief exposure.

The more severe congenital form (type II) is apparent at birth and involves little neurological development after birth. Type III is a milder, later-onset form. Another form, xeroderma pigmentosum–Cockayne syndrome (XP-CS), combines features of both disorders.

Genetics

Cockayne syndrome is caused by mutations in the ERCC6 gene (CSB), which accounts for roughly 70–75% of cases, or the ERCC8 gene (CSA), which accounts for most of the remainder. Both genes encode proteins involved in transcription-coupled nucleotide excision repair, the process by which DNA damage that blocks transcription is removed. The disorder is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern.

Diagnosis and prognosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical features and can be confirmed by molecular genetic testing or by assays measuring defective recovery of RNA synthesis after ultraviolet irradiation of cultured cells. There is no cure, and treatment is supportive. Individuals with the classical form typically die in the first or second decade of life, most often from progressive neurological degeneration or infection.

History

The syndrome is named for the English physician Edward Alfred Cockayne (1880–1956), who first described it in 1936 in two siblings and reported their follow-up in 1946. Mary M. Dingwall and Catherine A. Neill also described cases, giving rise to the alternative name Neill–Dingwall syndrome.
