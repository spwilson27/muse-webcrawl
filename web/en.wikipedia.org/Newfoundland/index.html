Newfoundland is a large island in the North Atlantic Ocean off the east coast of Canada. With an area of 108,860 square kilometres, it is the world's 16th-largest island and Canada's fourth-largest. Together with Labrador on the mainland, it forms the province of Newfoundland and Labrador, and about 94 percent of the province's population lives on the island. The capital and largest city is St. John's, on the Avalon Peninsula in the southeast.

History

The island was inhabited by Indigenous peoples, including the ancestors of the Beothuk, for thousands of years. Around the year 1000, Norse explorers built a settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows, the only confirmed Viking site in North America. In 1497, John Cabot, sailing for England, reached the island and claimed it for Henry VII — one of the earliest European landfalls in the Americas since the Norse voyages. The rich cod fisheries of the Grand Banks drew European fishing fleets for centuries, and permanent English settlement began in the early 1600s.

Newfoundland became a British colony and later a self-governing dominion. After financial crisis in the 1930s it was ruled by a Commission of Government, and in 1949, following two referendums, it joined Canada as the tenth province.

Geography and culture

The island's rugged coastline is indented by numerous bays and inlets, and its interior is a plateau of lakes, bogs, and low hills; Gros Morne National Park, on the west coast, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Newfoundland has a distinctive culture and dialect, shaped by centuries of relative isolation and by Irish and English settlement, with strong traditions of music, storytelling, and fishing. The collapse of the cod fishery, closed by moratorium in 1992, profoundly reshaped its economy, which now also depends on offshore oil, mining, hydroelectricity, and tourism.
