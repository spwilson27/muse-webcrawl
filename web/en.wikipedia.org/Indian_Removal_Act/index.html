The Indian Removal Act was signed into law by President Andrew Jackson on May 28, 1830, authorizing the president to grant lands west of the Mississippi in exchange for Indian lands within existing state borders. A few tribes went peacefully, but many resisted the relocation policy. During the fall and winter of 1838 and 1839, the Cherokees were forcibly moved west by the United States government. Approximately 4,000 Cherokees died on this forced march, which became known as the "Trail of Tears".

Background

As European settlers spread westward after the American Revolution and the Louisiana Purchase, pressure grew for the removal of Native American peoples — particularly the "Five Civilized Tribes" (Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Seminole, Chickasaw and Choctaw) — whose fertile lands in Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and Florida were coveted for cotton cultivation. Andrew Jackson, a long-standing advocate of removal, made it a central policy of his presidency.

Passage

The Act passed the Senate 28–19 and the House of Representatives 102–97 after fierce debate; opponents, including Congressman Davy Crockett and many religious and humanitarian groups, denounced it as unjust. Jackson argued removal would protect tribes from extinction and open land to settlement, framing it as benevolent — a view challenged then and widely rejected by historians today.

Implementation

Under the Act and subsequent treaties — many negotiated under duress or with unrepresentative factions — the Choctaw (Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, 1830), Creek, Chickasaw, Seminole and Cherokee ceded their eastern homelands. Removal was enforced by the U.S. Army; the Seminole resisted in the Second Seminole War (1835–1842). The Cherokee removal of 1838–39 followed the disputed Treaty of New Echota (1835) and the Supreme Court's earlier decision in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), which affirmed tribal sovereignty but was not enforced. Tens of thousands were relocated to "Indian Territory" (present-day Oklahoma); many thousands died of disease, exposure and starvation.
