Freedom of religion

Freedom of religion, also known as religious liberty or freedom of belief, is a principle that supports the freedom of an individual or community, in public or private, to manifest religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship, and observance. It also includes the right not to profess any religion or belief, or to change one's religion, and the freedom from coercion or discrimination on the grounds of religion.

The concept has been recognised in international law since the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), Article 18 of which provides:

Everyone has the right to freedom of thought, conscience and religion; this right includes freedom to change his religion or belief, and freedom, either alone or in community with others and in public or private, to manifest his religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship and observance.

The right is also guaranteed by Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (1966) and Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights. In the United States, it is protected by the First Amendment to the Constitution, which prohibits Congress from making any law respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.

Historical development

Religious tolerance has ancient precedents. The Edict of Ashoka (3rd century BCE) in India enjoined respect for other faiths, and the Cyrus Cylinder has sometimes been cited as an early charter of toleration in the Persian Empire. In Europe, the Peace of Augsburg (1555) allowed rulers of the Holy Roman Empire to choose Catholicism or Lutheranism for their territories, and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) extended recognition to Calvinism. The Edict of Nantes (1598) granted rights to French Huguenots before its revocation in 1685.

Enlightenment thinkers such as John Locke, in his Letter Concerning Toleration (1689), argued that religious belief could not be compelled by force and that civil government should not concern itself with the salvation of souls. The Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1786), drafted by Thomas Jefferson, disestablished the Anglican Church in Virginia and became a model for the First Amendment. The French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) likewise protected religious opinions provided their manifestation did not disturb public order.

Scope and limitations

Freedom of religion is generally understood to comprise two dimensions: the forum internum — the inner freedom to hold, adopt, or change a belief — which is considered absolute, and the forum externum — the freedom to manifest religion in worship, observance, practice, and teaching — which may be subject to limitations prescribed by law and necessary to protect public safety, order, health, morals, or the rights of others. Freedom of religion also protects atheists, agnostics, and other non-believers, and includes the right not to be compelled to participate in religious observance.

Related concepts include the separation of church and state, secularism, religious pluralism, and the prohibition of religious discrimination. Debates continue over the boundaries of religious freedom in areas such as conscientious objection, religious dress in public institutions, faith-based schooling, blasphemy laws, and conflicts between religious liberty and anti-discrimination protections.

Status around the world

The degree of religious freedom varies widely. Many liberal democracies constitutionally guarantee free exercise and prohibit an established church or religious tests for office, though some retain established churches (such as the Church of England) alongside broad toleration. In other states, a single religion is privileged and conversion from it, proselytism, or public worship by minorities may be restricted or punished. International monitoring bodies, including the UN Special Rapporteur on freedom of religion or belief and the Pew Research Center, regularly report government restrictions and social hostilities involving religion, which by several measures have risen in the twenty-first century.
