Sarmatians

The Sarmatians were a group of Iranian nomadic peoples who dominated the Pontic steppe north of the Black Sea from about the 5th century BC to the 4th century AD. Speaking an Iranian language related to that of the Scythians, whom they gradually displaced, the Sarmatians comprised several tribal groups, among them the Iazyges, Roxolani, Alans and Aorsi.

History

Originating east of the Don River, the Sarmatians moved westward across the steppe during the 4th and 3rd centuries BC, absorbing or pushing aside Scythian tribes. By the 1st century AD they controlled the grasslands from the Danube to Central Asia and were in frequent contact — diplomatic and military — with the Roman Empire. Sarmatian cavalry fought both against and for Rome: in AD 175 the emperor Marcus Aurelius settled some 5,500 Sarmatian horsemen in Britain after defeating the Iazyges, a contingent some writers have linked with later Arthurian legend. The Roxolani raided Moesia, and the Alans joined the great migrations of late antiquity, sweeping westward with Vandals and Suebi into Gaul and Spain during the Crossing of the Rhine in 406.

Culture and warfare

Sarmatian society was pastoral and nomadic, organised around kinship and warrior elites buried in kurgans with rich grave goods of gold, weapons and horse gear. Greek and Roman writers noted the prominent role of women, some of whom fought as warriors — Herodotus traced the custom to the Sarmatians' legendary descent from Scythians and Amazons, and archaeology confirms a notable proportion of female burials with weapons.

Their armies were famous for heavy cavalry: armoured lancers (cataphracts) riding horses protected by scale armour, wielding the long two-handed lance called the kontos, alongside horse archers. This cavalry tradition influenced Roman and Byzantine heavy cavalry and, through the Alans, the warfare of early medieval Europe.

Legacy

Sarmatian groups were largely absorbed by the Goths, Huns and Slavs, but their descendants include the Alans of the Caucasus, whose language survives as modern Ossetian. In early modern Poland the nobility (szlachta) cultivated a "Sarmatian" ideology claiming descent from these steppe warriors.
