Tam-tam

The tam-tam is a large metal percussion instrument of indefinite pitch, similar in appearance to a gong but with no raised central boss and a sound that builds to a crashing, shimmering roar rather than a definite tone. Originating in China and related to instruments such as the chau gong, it entered the Western orchestra in the late 18th century and is now a standard colouristic instrument in orchestral and band music.

Construction and technique

Tam-tams are made of bronze or brass, roughly circular, with a turned-back rim. They are suspended vertically in a frame and struck with a large padded mallet; strokes may be allowed to bloom slowly or damped, scraped, or bowed for special effects. Unlike the tuned nipple gong, the tam-tam's spectrum is inharmonic, producing its characteristic wash of sound.

Use in music

François-Joseph Gossec is credited with one of the first orchestral uses (1791), and the instrument became a favourite for scenes of solemnity, terror, or catastrophe. Famous moments include the climaxes of Tchaikovsky's Sixth Symphony, the end of Shostakovich's symphonies, Mahler's symphonies, and the "Mars" movement of Holst's The Planets. It also appears widely in film scores and, in rock music, on a few celebrated recordings.

Distinction from gongs

In common speech "gong" is often used for the tam-tam, but organologically the two differ: gongs have a raised centre and (in many Asian traditions) definite pitch, while the tam-tam has a flat or slightly profiled face and produces an indefinite, swelling crash.
