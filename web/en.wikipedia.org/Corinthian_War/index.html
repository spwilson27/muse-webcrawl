The Corinthian War was an ancient Greek conflict lasting from 395 to 387 BC, pitting Sparta against a coalition of four allied states — Thebes, Athens, Corinth, and Argos — backed initially by the Persian Empire. The war arose from resentment of Sparta's high-handed dominance after its victory in the Peloponnesian War.

Course of the war

Fighting began in central Greece, where the Spartan commander Lysander was killed at Haliartus in 395 BC. The allies concentrated at Corinth, from which the war took its name; major land battles at Nemea and Coronea (394 BC) were Spartan victories but not decisive. At sea, a Persian-funded fleet under the Athenian admiral Conon destroyed Spartan naval power at Cnidus (394 BC), ending Sparta's brief maritime empire and enabling Athens to rebuild its Long Walls and fortifications of Piraeus.

On land, the war degenerated into raids and stalemate around the Isthmus of Corinth. The Athenian general Iphicrates, using light-armed peltasts, destroyed a Spartan hoplite battalion near Lechaeum in 390 BC, an event that signalled changing tactics in Greek warfare. Persia, alarmed by Athens' revival, switched support to Sparta, and a Spartan fleet under Antalcidas, backed by the threat of Persian intervention, forced the allies to terms.

Peace of Antalcidas

The war ended in 387/386 BC with the Peace of Antalcidas, also called the King's Peace because it was dictated by the Persian king Artaxerxes II. The settlement declared the Greek cities of Asia Minor Persian, guaranteed the autonomy of other Greek cities, and left Sparta as the enforcer — and for the moment the dominant power — of the Greek world.
