Biocentrism (ethics)

Biocentrism (from Greek bios, "life", and kentron, "centre") in its ethical sense is an ethical point of view that extends inherent value to all living things. It stands in contrast to anthropocentrism, which accords moral standing primarily or exclusively to human beings, and is related to but distinct from ecocentrism, which values whole ecosystems and species as well as individual organisms.

History and proponents

The idea draws on Albert Schweitzer's ethic of "reverence for life" and was developed systematically by philosophers such as Paul W. Taylor, whose book Respect for Nature (1986) set out a biocentric theory of environmental ethics, and Kenneth Goodpaster, who argued that being alive is a sufficient criterion for moral considerability. Biocentrism provides a philosophical foundation for animal rights and deep ecology, though its proponents differ over whether all organisms have equal value or whether values form a hierarchy.

Principles

Taylor's biocentric outlook holds that humans are members of Earth's community of life; that all species are part of a system of interdependence; that each individual organism is a "teleological centre of life" pursuing its own good; and that humans are not inherently superior to other living things.
