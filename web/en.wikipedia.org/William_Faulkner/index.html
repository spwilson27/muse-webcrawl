William Faulkner

William Cuthbert Faulkner (born Falkner; September 25, 1897 – July 6, 1962) was an American writer known for his novels and short stories set in the fictional Yoknapatawpha County, based on Lafayette County, Mississippi, where he spent most of his life.

Life

Faulkner was born in New Albany, Mississippi, and grew up in Oxford. He briefly served in the Royal Canadian Air Force in 1918 but saw no combat. After early poetry and the novels Soldiers' Pay (1926) and Mosquitoes (1927), he found his true subject with The Sound and the Fury (1929).

Major works

His masterpieces include As I Lay Dying (1930), Light in August (1932), Absalom, Absalom! (1936) and the Snopes trilogy. Employing stream of consciousness, multiple narrators and fractured chronology, he explored the legacy of slavery, the Civil War and Southern decline. His fiction was commercially neglected for years until Malcolm Cowley's The Portable Faulkner (1946) revived his reputation.

Honours and later years

Faulkner received the 1949 Nobel Prize in Literature, delivering a celebrated acceptance speech on "the human heart in conflict with itself". He also won two Pulitzer Prizes for Fiction, for A Fable (1954) and posthumously for The Reivers (1962), and worked intermittently as a Hollywood screenwriter. He died in Byhalia, Mississippi, in 1962.
