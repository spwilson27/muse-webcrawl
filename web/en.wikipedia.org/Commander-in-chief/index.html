Commander-in-chief

A commander-in-chief or supreme commander is the person who exercises supreme command and control over an armed force or a military branch. As a technical term, it refers to military competencies that reside in a country's executive leadership, a head of state or a head of government.

Role and powers

The role of commander-in-chief is usually vested in the head of state, providing an example of civilian control of the military. Often the powers are formally exercised on the advice of ministers, or are largely ceremonial, with real operational command delegated to professional military officers such as a chief of the defence staff. In other systems, notably that of the United States, the president's role as commander-in-chief is substantive.

By country

In the United States, Article II of the Constitution makes the president "Commander in Chief of the Army and Navy of the United States, and of the Militia of the several States, when called into the actual Service of the United States." In the United Kingdom, the monarch is the head of the British Armed Forces, though command is exercised through the Defence Council and the prime minister. In France, the president is head of the armed forces (chef des armées). In Russia, the president is the Supreme Commander-in-Chief. In India, the president is the supreme commander of the armed forces, acting on the advice of the council of ministers.

History

The term was used in the English Civil War era for the supreme commander of an army, and was applied to Oliver Cromwell and later to commanders such as George Washington, who was commander-in-chief of the Continental Army before becoming president.
