The pancreas is an organ of the digestive system and endocrine system of vertebrates. In humans, it is located in the abdomen behind the stomach and functions as a gland. It is a mixed gland, with both exocrine and endocrine components: about 99% of its mass is devoted to secreting digestive enzymes into the small intestine, while small clusters of endocrine cells, the islets of Langerhans, release hormones directly into the blood.

Anatomy

The pancreas is a soft, elongated, lobulated organ roughly 15–25 cm long, divided into a head (tucked into the C-curve of the duodenum), a neck, a body extending across the posterior abdominal wall, and a tail reaching toward the spleen. Its main duct, the pancreatic duct (of Wirsung), usually joins the common bile duct and empties into the duodenum at the major duodenal papilla. Daily secretion of pancreatic juice amounts to roughly 1.5 litres.

Exocrine function

Acinar cells produce a potent mixture of digestive enzymes — trypsinogen and other proteases, pancreatic lipase, amylase, and nucleases — together with bicarbonate-rich fluid that neutralises acidic chyme from the stomach. Secretion is stimulated by the hormones secretin and cholecystokinin released by the intestinal mucosa, and by vagal activity. Enzymes are secreted as inactive zymogens to protect the gland from autodigestion.

Endocrine function

The islets of Langerhans contain several cell types: beta cells secreting insulin, which lowers blood glucose; alpha cells secreting glucagon, which raises it; delta cells secreting somatostatin; and PP cells secreting pancreatic polypeptide. The balance of insulin and glucagon is central to glucose homeostasis. The isolation of insulin from pancreatic extracts by Banting, Best, Macleod, and Collip in 1921–22 transformed type 1 diabetes from a fatal disease into a manageable one.

Diseases

Inflammation of the pancreas, pancreatitis, may be acute — most often caused by gallstones or alcohol — or chronic, leading to malabsorption and diabetes. Cystic fibrosis thickens pancreatic secretions and destroys exocrine function. Pancreatic cancer, usually ductal adenocarcinoma, is among the deadliest cancers because it is typically detected late and responds poorly to chemotherapy; risk factors include smoking, chronic pancreatitis, obesity, and diabetes. Surgical removal of the pancreatic head (the Whipple procedure) is used for periampullary tumours.
