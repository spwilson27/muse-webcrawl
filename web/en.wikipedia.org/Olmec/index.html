Olmecs

RegionGulf Coast of Mexico (Veracruz and Tabasco)
Periodc. 1500 – 400 BCE
Major sitesSan Lorenzo, La Venta, Tres Zapotes
Known forColossal heads; earliest Mesoamerican civilization

The Olmecs were the earliest known major Mesoamerican civilization, flourishing in the tropical lowlands of the modern Mexican states of Veracruz and Tabasco during the Formative period, roughly 1500 to 400 BCE. Their heartland lay in the swampy basins of the Coatzacoalcos and Tonalá rivers. The name "Olmec" derives from the Nahuatl word for "rubber people", after the region's rubber trees.

Civilization

At great centres such as San Lorenzo and La Venta, the Olmecs built monumental earthen pyramids, plazas, and drainage systems, and carved enormous basalt sculptures — most famously the colossal heads, seventeen of which have been found, each weighing many tons and believed to portray individual rulers. The basalt was transported great distances from the Tuxtla Mountains.

Culture and legacy

Olmec art features distinctive motifs including were-jaguar infants, and the culture produced fine jade carvings, early calendar and glyphic traditions, and possibly the first writing in Mesoamerica, suggested by the Cascajal Block. The Olmecs are often called the "mother culture" of Mesoamerica: the ballgame, ritual bloodletting, and major deities of later civilizations such as the Maya and Aztecs have roots in Olmec practice. Their language is unknown but is often linked to the Mixe–Zoquean family.

Decline

La Venta was abandoned around 400 BCE and Olmec culture dissolved, for reasons that remain debated, possibly including environmental change, volcanic activity, and shifting trade networks.
