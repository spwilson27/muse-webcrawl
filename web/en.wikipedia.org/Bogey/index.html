Bogey

Bogey is a term with several meanings, most commonly in golf, where it denotes a score of one stroke over par on an individual hole. The word and its related forms have acquired additional senses in military aviation, railway engineering, and popular culture.

In golf

The term originated in late 19th-century Britain. "Par" was originally the score an expert player would expect for a hole; golfers also compared themselves against a notional consistent player, the "Bogey Man" or "Colonel Bogey", inspired by a popular music-hall song. Scores are described relative to par: a bogey is one over par, a double bogey two over, a triple bogey three over, and so on, while one under par is a birdie. As standards improved, "bogey" shifted from denoting the target score to its modern meaning of a mediocre, one-over result.

Other uses

In military brevity codes, a bogey is an air contact whose identity is unknown, as distinct from a "bandit", which is confirmed hostile. On railways, a bogie (often spelled "bogey" historically) is the swiveling wheeled undercarriage beneath a carriage or wagon. In folklore, "bogey" is linked to the bogeyman, a monster used to frighten children; the word is also a British slang term for dried nasal mucus.

Culture

The 1914 march "Colonel Bogey" by Kenneth J. Alford became famous worldwide, later whistled in the film The Bridge on the River Kwai and given comic lyrics in various countries.
