Sympathetic nervous system

Sympathetic nervous system
Part ofAutonomic nervous system
OriginThoracolumbar spinal cord (T1–L2)
Postganglionic transmitterNoradrenaline (mostly)
Function"Fight or flight" mobilisation

The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) is one of the three divisions of the autonomic nervous system, alongside the parasympathetic and enteric systems. It prepares the body for action — the "fight or flight" response — by increasing heart rate and blood pressure, dilating the airways and pupils, mobilising energy stores and diverting blood from the gut and skin to skeletal muscle.

Anatomy

Preganglionic neurons arise from the thoracolumbar spinal cord (segments T1 to L2). Their short myelinated axons synapse in the sympathetic trunk (paravertebral ganglia) or in prevertebral ganglia near the abdominal aorta. Long postganglionic fibres then reach the target organs. The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion whose chromaffin cells release adrenaline and noradrenaline directly into the bloodstream.

Neurotransmission

Preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors. Postganglionic neurons mostly release noradrenaline, acting on alpha and beta adrenergic receptors; an exception is the sympathetic supply to sweat glands, which is cholinergic. Drugs that mimic or block these receptors — beta blockers, for example — have wide clinical use in cardiovascular medicine.

Functions

  • Increased heart rate and contractility; vasoconstriction raising blood pressure.
  • Bronchodilation and pupillary dilation (mydriasis).
  • Glycogenolysis and lipolysis to fuel activity.
  • Inhibition of digestion and salivation; sweating and piloerection.
