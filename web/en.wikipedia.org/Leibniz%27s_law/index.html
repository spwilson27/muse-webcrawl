Leibniz's law

Also known as: Identity of indiscernibles; indiscernibility of identicals
Named after: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
Field: Metaphysics, logic

Leibniz's law is a principle associated with the German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz concerning identity. It is usually divided into two parts: the indiscernibility of identicals — if x and y are identical, then every property of x is a property of y — and the identity of indiscernibles — if x and y share all properties, then x is identical to y.

Formulation

Formally, the indiscernibility of identicals states that for any objects x and y, if x = y then for every property F, Fx if and only if Fy. It is widely used as a substitution principle in formal logic.

Debates

The identity of indiscernibles is more controversial: Max Black proposed a counterexample involving a universe containing only two perfectly identical spheres. The principle also plays a role in debates about personal identity and the philosophy of mind.
