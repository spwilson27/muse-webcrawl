William Lloyd Garrison (December 10, 1805 – May 24, 1879) was an American abolitionist, journalist and social reformer. He is best known for his widely read abolitionist newspaper The Liberator, which he founded in 1831 and published in Boston until slavery in the United States was abolished after the Civil War.

Abolitionism

Apprenticed as a printer, Garrison worked on reform papers in Vermont and Baltimore, where his attacks on the domestic slave trade led to a libel conviction and a brief imprisonment. Unlike many early opponents of slavery, he rejected gradual emancipation and colonisation, demanding the immediate, unconditional abolition of slavery and equal rights for Black Americans. In the first issue of The Liberator he declared: "I will be as harsh as truth, and as uncompromising as justice... I will not equivocate — I will not excuse — I will not retreat a single inch — AND I WILL BE HEARD."

Activism and later life

Garrison co-founded the New England Anti-Slavery Society (1832) and the American Anti-Slavery Society (1833). His uncompromising stance — including denouncing the U.S. Constitution as "a covenant with death and an agreement with hell" for tolerating slavery, and advocating Northern secession — divided the movement but kept abolition at the centre of national debate. He also championed women's rights, which caused a split in the society in 1840. After the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) he retired The Liberator and spent his later years supporting women's suffrage and other reforms.
