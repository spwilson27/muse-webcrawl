Everglades

LocationFlorida, United States
AreaAbout 20,000 km² (historic extent)
DesignationUNESCO World Heritage Site; Ramsar wetland

The Everglades is a natural region of tropical wetlands in the southern portion of the U.S. state of Florida, comprising the southern half of a large drainage basin within the Neotropical realm. The system begins near Orlando with the Kissimmee River, which discharges into the vast but shallow Lake Okeechobee. Water leaving the lake in the wet season forms a slow-moving river 60 miles (97 km) wide and over 100 miles (160 km) long, flowing southward across a limestone shelf to Florida Bay at the southern end of the state — the famous "River of Grass".

Ecology

The Everglades is a mosaic of habitats: sawgrass marshes, cypress swamps, mangrove forests, pinelands, hardwood hammocks, and marine estuaries. Its wildlife includes the American alligator, over 350 bird species, the endangered Florida panther, and the West Indian manatee. The ecosystem depends on seasonal flooding and fire, and on sheet flow — a broad, shallow movement of fresh water across the marsh. Invasive species such as the Burmese python have severely reduced mammal populations in the southern Everglades.

Human history and drainage

Indigenous peoples, including the Calusa and Tequesta and later the Seminole and Miccosukee, lived in and around the Everglades for thousands of years. From the late 19th century, developers and the state of Florida dug canals to drain the region for agriculture and settlement, and by the mid-20th century roughly half the original Everglades had been converted, principally to sugarcane fields in the Everglades Agricultural Area and to urban development along the Atlantic coast. Flood-control works after the hurricanes of 1928 and 1947, administered by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, re-plumbed the whole system.

Restoration

Everglades National Park was established in 1947, protecting the southern fifth of the historic ecosystem. The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan (CERP), authorized by Congress in 2000, is one of the largest ecosystem restoration projects ever undertaken, aiming to restore more natural water flow and quality while maintaining flood protection and water supply for South Florida's cities and farms. Challenges include nutrient pollution (especially phosphorus), sea-level rise pushing salt water inland, and continuing competition for fresh water.
