André Le Nôtre

André Le Nôtre (French pronunciation: [ɑ̃dʁe lə notʁ]; 12 March 1613 – 15 September 1700), often rendered in English as André Le Notre, was a French landscape architect and the principal gardener of King Louis XIV of France. He is celebrated as the defining designer of the French formal garden (the jardin à la française), whose masterpiece is the Gardens of Versailles. His vast, geometrically ordered vistas — with their parterres, canals, fountains and long axial perspectives — set the pattern for European court gardens for a century.

Early life

Le Nôtre was born in Paris into a family of royal gardeners: his grandfather Pierre was a market gardener to Catherine de' Medici, and his father Jean was head gardener of the Tuileries under Louis XIII. André trained in mathematics, perspective, painting (in the studio of Simon Vouet) and architecture, a combination that underpinned his command of vast outdoor space. In 1637 he succeeded his father at the Tuileries.

Major works

Le Nôtre's breakthrough came at Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661), the château of finance minister Nicolas Fouquet, where with architect Louis Le Vau and painter Charles Le Brun he created the first fully realised French formal garden; the splendour of its inaugural fête helped provoke Fouquet's arrest. Louis XIV then engaged the whole team for Versailles, where from 1661 Le Nôtre laid out the great east–west axis, the Grand Canal (1.6 km long), the parterre d'eau, the bosquets and the fountain system that defined the Sun King's image. He also designed or remodelled gardens at the Tuileries, Saint-Germain-en-Laye, Saint-Cloud, Chantilly, Sceaux, Fontainebleau and Marly, and advised abroad, including work for Greenwich Park and St James's Park in London and the Turin region in Italy.

Style

The Le Nôtre garden subordinates nature to geometry and royal perspective: a long central axis vanishing to the horizon, embroidered box parterres near the château, clipped cone and ball topiary, still sheets of water mirroring the sky, and groves (bosquets) concealing fountains and sculpture as surprises along the walks. It was a political as much as aesthetic statement — the landscape itself ordered by the king.

Later life and legacy

Le Nôtre accumulated offices, wealth and a notable art collection, much of which he gave to the king in 1693, and was ennobled in 1675. He died in Paris in 1700 and was buried in the church of Saint-Roch. His style dominated European garden design until the English landscape garden reacted against it in the 18th century, and his Versailles axis later influenced city planning from Washington, D.C. to Chandigarh.
