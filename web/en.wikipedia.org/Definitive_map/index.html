Definitive map

A definitive map is a legal record of public rights of way — footpaths, bridleways, restricted byways, and byways open to all traffic — maintained by surveying authorities (usually county councils) in England and Wales under the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981. Together with the accompanying definitive statement, it is conclusive evidence of the existence and location of the rights of way it shows.

History

The system originated with the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, which required councils to survey and map all public paths in their areas. The resulting maps were built up over decades from parish surveys and public inquiry processes.

Modification and lost ways

Rights of way can be added, altered, or deleted by definitive map modification orders when evidence shows the map is wrong. Because unrecorded historic paths risked being extinguished, the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 set a cut-off date (originally 2026, later extended and then slated for repeal in England) after which pre-1949 rights not shown on the map would be lost, prompting volunteers to research and claim thousands of "lost ways".
