Melancholia

Melancholia (from Greek μελαγχολία, "black bile") is a historical concept of a mood disorder characterized by profound sadness, despondency and withdrawal. In ancient and medieval medicine it was attributed to an excess of black bile, one of the four humours. For centuries melancholia encompassed conditions that modern psychiatry treats as depressive disorders, and in the DSM-5 "melancholic features" remains a specifier for major depressive disorder, describing symptoms such as anhedonia, lack of reactivity, early-morning waking, weight loss and excessive guilt.

History

Hippocratic physicians described melancholia as a state of fear and despondency lasting a long time. Aretaeus of Cappadocia and Galen developed the concept, and it flourished in medieval Islamic and European medicine. Robert Burton's encyclopedic The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621) surveyed its causes and cures. In Renaissance thought — notably Marsilio Ficino's circle and Dürer's engraving Melencolia I — melancholy was also linked with genius and contemplation. Sigmund Freud's essay "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917) contrasted pathological melancholia with normal grief, attributing it to ambivalence toward a lost object turned inward against the self.

Modern usage

By the late 19th century Emil Kraepelin had folded melancholia into manic-depressive illness, and 20th-century psychiatry largely replaced the term with "depression", though it survives in diagnostic specifiers, in psychoanalysis, and in everyday usage for a pensive, wistful sadness.
