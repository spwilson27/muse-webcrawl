The Royal Geographical Society (with the Institute of British Geographers), often shortened to RGS, is the United Kingdom's learned society and professional body for geography. Founded in 1830, it supports geographical research, education, expeditions and public engagement, and represents geographers in British public life.

History

The Geographical Society of London was founded in 1830 by members of the Raleigh Dining Club, including John Barrow, Sir John Franklin and Francis Beaufort, absorbing earlier bodies such as the African Association. It received its royal charter in 1859. In the nineteenth century the Society was a driving force behind British exploration, backing expeditions associated with David Livingstone, Richard Burton, John Hanning Speke and Henry Morton Stanley, and later the Everest expeditions of the 1920s and the 1953 first ascent. In 1995 it merged with the Institute of British Geographers, the professional association of academic geographers.

Activities

The Society accredits geography degree programmes, awards the professional status of Chartered Geographer, and supports school geography through training, resources and the Geography Teacher Training Scholarships. It publishes journals including the Geographical Journal, Transactions of the Institute of British Geographers and Area, and holds an annual international conference attended by around 2,000 delegates.

Its collections at Lowther Lodge — some two million maps, photographs, manuscripts and artefacts — form one of the world's great geographical archives, including material from the Everest expeditions and the travels of Shackleton and Scott. Grants from the Society continue to fund dozens of field research projects and expeditions each year.

Medals

The Society's premier awards are the two royal Gold Medals, the Founder's Medal and the Patron's Medal, given annually since the 1830s for the encouragement and promotion of geographical science and discovery.
