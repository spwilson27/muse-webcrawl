The Henotikon (Greek: ἑνωτικόν, "act of union" or "uniting edict") was a Christological edict issued by the Byzantine emperor Zeno in 482, drafted in collaboration with Acacius, Patriarch of Constantinople. It attempted to reconcile the differences between the supporters of the Council of Chalcedon (451) and its opponents (the Miaphysites), who were numerous in Egypt and Syria, and thus to heal a deepening division within the church and empire.

Contents

The edict affirmed the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed as the sufficient and sole expression of the faith, anathematised both Nestorius and Eutyches, and endorsed the Twelve Chapters (Anathemas) of Cyril of Alexandria. Crucially, it condemned "anyone who has held or holds any other belief, whether at Chalcedon or at any synod whatsoever" — a studied ambiguity that avoided either affirming or rejecting the Chalcedonian definition of Christ's two natures. It also ignored the Tome of Pope Leo I, and made no mention of the controversial phrase "in two natures".

Consequences

The Henotikon was accepted by the patriarchs of Alexandria, Antioch, and Jerusalem, and temporarily restored communion among the eastern sees. However, it satisfied neither side fully: hardline Miaphysites considered it insufficient, while Rome regarded it as an unacceptable abandonment of Chalcedon. Pope Felix III condemned the edict and excommunicated Acacius in 484, beginning the Acacian schism between Rome and Constantinople, which lasted until 519.

In that year the emperor Justin I, seeking reconciliation with Rome, repudiated the Henotikon; the schism ended when Patriarch John of Constantinople accepted the Formula of Hormisdas and removed the names of Zeno and Acacius from the diptychs. The episode is an early example of imperial attempts to impose doctrinal compromise (sometimes called "Zeno's compromise") in the interest of political unity.
