Proton therapy, or proton beam therapy, is a type of particle therapy that uses a beam of protons to irradiate diseased tissue, most often to treat cancer. The chief advantage of proton therapy over other types of external beam radiotherapy is that the dose of protons is deposited over a narrow range of depth; hence in minimal entry, exit, or scattered radiation dose to healthy nearby tissues.

When evaluating whether to treat a tumor with photon or proton therapy, physicians may choose proton therapy if it is important to deliver a higher radiation dose to targeted tissues while significantly decreasing radiation to nearby organs at risk. Protons are accelerated to therapeutic energies, typically 70–250 MeV, using a cyclotron or synchrotron.

Description

The standard depiction of dose deposition contrasts photons, which deposit maximum dose near the surface and continue depositing an exit dose beyond the tumor, with protons, which deposit their maximum dose at the Bragg peak — a depth determined by the beam energy — with almost no exit dose beyond it. By superimposing beams of different energies (a spread-out Bragg peak), the high-dose region can be conformed to the shape of the tumor volume.

Application

Proton therapy is especially valued for pediatric cancers, where sparing developing tissue reduces the risk of secondary malignancies and developmental harm, and for tumors close to critical structures such as the eye (ocular melanoma), the base of the skull (chordomas and chondrosarcomas), and the spine. It is also used for prostate, lung, liver, and some head and neck cancers, though evidence of superiority over modern photon techniques for many common indications remains an active research question.

History

The use of protons for medical treatment was first proposed by Robert R. Wilson in 1946. The first patients were treated in the 1950s at particle accelerators built for physics research, including at Berkeley Radiation Laboratory and at Harvard's cyclotron laboratory. The first hospital-based proton therapy center opened at Loma Linda University Medical Center in California in 1990. Since then dozens of centers have opened worldwide, though the cost of construction — typically well over $100 million for a multi-room facility — has limited their spread.

Comparison with other treatments

Proton therapy facilities are considerably more expensive than conventional photon (X-ray) radiotherapy centers. Clinical benefit is clearest where the photon exit dose would unavoidably irradiate sensitive structures; for many common cancers, trials comparing outcomes with intensity-modulated photon therapy are ongoing.
