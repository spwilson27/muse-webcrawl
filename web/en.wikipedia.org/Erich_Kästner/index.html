Erich Kästner

Emil Erich Kästner (23 February 1899 – 29 July 1974) was a German writer, poet, screenwriter and satirist, known primarily for his humorous, socially astute poems and for children's books such as Emil and the Detectives (1929) and Lottie and Lisa (1949), the latter adapted into the films The Parent Trap.

Life and career

Kästner was born in Dresden, the son of a master saddler and a seamstress — the unusually close bond with his mother is a recurring theme of his autobiographical writing, including the memoir When I Was a Little Boy (1957). He trained as a teacher but turned to writing, studying German literature and theatre in Leipzig, where he began publishing poems and feuilletons. Moving to Berlin in 1927, he became one of the most distinctive voices of the Weimar Republic's Neue Sachlichkeit ("New Objectivity"), writing satirical verse collections such as Herz auf Taille (1928) and the novel Fabian (1931), a mordant portrait of Berlin on the eve of the Nazi takeover.

Under National Socialism

Despite his pacifism and anti-militarism, Kästner remained in Germany after 1933, later saying he wished to witness events and care for his mother. His books were among those burned in the Nazi book burnings of May 1933 — Kästner famously stood in the crowd and watched. He was banned from publishing, twice arrested by the Gestapo, and survived by writing under pseudonyms, including the screenplay for the 1943 UFA film Münchhausen (credited to "Berthold Bürger").

Post-war

After the war Kästner settled in Munich, edited the youth magazine Pinguin, wrote cabaret material and the anti-war poem cycle, and continued his children's classics with The Flying Classroom reissues and new works. He received the Georg Büchner Prize in 1957 and the international Hans Christian Andersen Award for writing in 1960. He died in Munich in 1974.
