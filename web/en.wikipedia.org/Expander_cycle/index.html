Expander cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The expander cycle is a power cycle of a bipropellant rocket engine. In this cycle, some of the cryogenic propellant — usually the fuel, such as liquid hydrogen — is circulated through cooling channels around the combustion chamber and nozzle to cool them (regenerative cooling). In doing so the fuel absorbs heat and changes phase to a gas, and the pressure from this expansion is used to drive the turbopumps that feed propellant into the chamber, before the warmed fuel is itself injected and burned.

Characteristics

Because all of the fuel passes through the chamber and is burned, the expander cycle is a closed cycle with good efficiency: typical specific impulses are high for chemical engines, around 440–465 seconds for hydrogen/oxygen engines in vacuum. Its performance is limited by the amount of heat that can be absorbed from the chamber walls, which scales with chamber surface area while thrust scales with volume; this "square–cube" constraint has historically restricted expander-cycle engines to relatively modest thrust. Variants include the bleed (or open) expander cycle, in which a small fraction of the heated fuel is dumped overboard after driving the turbines, allowing larger engines at a small efficiency cost.

Examples

Well-known expander-cycle engines include the Pratt & Whitney RL10, which has powered Centaur upper stages since the 1960s, the Vinci engine for Ariane 6, Japan's LE-5B and the expander-bleed LE-9, and China's YF-75D. The approach is also used by the BE-3U upper-stage engine and was studied for many proposed reusable launch vehicles.
