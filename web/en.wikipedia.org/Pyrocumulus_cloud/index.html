Pyrocumulus cloud

A pyrocumulus cloud (also called a fire cloud, or cumulus flammagenitus in the International Cloud Atlas) is a dense cumulus cloud that forms above a source of intense, localized heat such as a wildfire, volcanic eruption, or industrial combustion. The World Meteorological Organization formally recognized the flammagenitus classification in 2017.

Formation

Rising hot air above the heat source carries water vapor — released from burning vegetation and from the combustion process itself — and smoke particles that act as condensation nuclei. As the air ascends and cools to the dew point, the vapor condenses to form a cloud that caps the smoke column. The color of the cloud ranges from white to dark gray or brown depending on the amount of ash and smoke entrained.

Effects

Pyrocumulus clouds can affect the fire that creates them: downdrafts and gusts beneath the cloud may spread flames unpredictably, posing dangers to firefighters, while precipitation from a mature cloud occasionally helps dampen a fire. When a pyrocumulus grows into a thunderstorm, it becomes a pyrocumulonimbus, which can produce lightning capable of igniting new fires far from the original blaze, as well as fire-generated tornadoes and downbursts.

Other sources

Smaller pyrocumulus clouds form above cooling towers, industrial flares, and large explosions. The firestorms produced by firebombing raids in the Second World War generated towering fire clouds, and similar phenomena were observed after the 2020 Beirut explosion and large volcanic eruptions such as Hunga Tonga in 2022.
