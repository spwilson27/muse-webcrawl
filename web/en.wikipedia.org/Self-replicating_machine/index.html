Self-replicating machine

A self-replicating machine is a hypothetical device capable of manufacturing copies of itself using raw materials from its environment. The concept bridges engineering, biology and computation: living organisms are existence proofs of self-replication, and theorists have asked whether an artificial machine could achieve the same feat, with applications from industrial automation to the exploration and colonisation of space.

Von Neumann's theory

The foundational analysis is John von Neumann's theory of self-reproducing automata, developed in the 1940s and published posthumously in 1966. Von Neumann showed that a machine containing a universal constructor, guided by a description (a "program") that it both interprets as instructions and copies as data, can reproduce itself — a logical architecture biology independently embodies in the ribosome and DNA. His kinematic model imagined a machine assembling itself from a stock of parts; his cellular-automaton model, completed by Arthur Burks, required a configuration of 29 states per cell.

Engineering studies

In 1980 a NASA summer study, published as Advanced Automation for Space Missions, examined self-replicating lunar factories in detail. The RepRap project pursues a modest version: a 3D printer that prints many of its own components, though not its motors or electronics. Full "closure" — manufacturing every part, including control systems — remains the central difficulty, and real systems fall back on externally supplied "vitamin" components.

Risks and speculation

Uncontrolled replication is the hazard behind the "grey goo" scenario for molecular nanotechnology popularised by K. Eric Drexler and later qualified by him and others. Self-replicating spacecraft, or von Neumann probes, feature in discussions of interstellar exploration and in the Fermi paradox, where their apparent absence is itself a datum. Computer programs that copy themselves, such as worms and quines, are a software analogue, usually classed separately.
