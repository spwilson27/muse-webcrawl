Le Corbusier

Charles-Édouard Jeanneret (6 October 1887 – 27 August 1965), known as Le Corbusier, was a Swiss-French architect, designer, painter, urban planner and writer, and one of the pioneers of modern architecture. He championed the use of reinforced concrete, open floor plans and functional design, and his "Five Points of Architecture" reshaped twentieth-century building around the world.

Early career

Jeanneret trained as an engraver and painter in La Chaux-de-Fonds, travelled widely in Europe, and worked briefly with Auguste Perret in Paris and Peter Behrens in Berlin. Settling in Paris in 1917, he adopted the pseudonym Le Corbusier and co-founded the journal L'Esprit Nouveau. His 1923 book Vers une architecture ("Toward an Architecture") declared that a house is "a machine for living in".

Five Points and major works

His Five Points — pilotis (supports), free plan, free façade, horizontal ribbon windows and roof garden — were realised in the Villa Savoye (1928–31). After the Second World War he developed the Modulor system of proportions and built the Unité d'habitation in Marseille (1952), a self-contained "vertical city". He planned much of Chandigarh, the new capital of Punjab in India, and designed the sculptural chapel of Notre-Dame du Haut at Ronchamp (1955). Seventeen of his works in seven countries were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2016.

Urbanism and legacy

Le Corbusier's urban schemes, such as the Ville Radieuse of towers in parkland, profoundly influenced post-war planning and public housing, though critics later blamed the model for soulless estates and the destruction of historic districts; his authoritarian vision and wartime dealings with the Vichy regime remain controversial. He died in 1965 while swimming at Roquebrune-Cap-Martin.
