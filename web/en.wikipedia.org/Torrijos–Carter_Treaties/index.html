The Torrijos–Carter Treaties are a pair of treaties signed by the United States and Panama in Washington, D.C., on 7 September 1977, which superseded the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty of 1903. The treaties were named after the two signatories, Panamanian leader Omar Torrijos and U.S. President Jimmy Carter. They abolished the Panama Canal Zone and provided for the transfer of the Panama Canal to Panamanian control.

The two treaties

The Panama Canal Treaty provided for the phased withdrawal of the United States: the Canal Zone ceased to exist on 1 October 1979, Panama assumed increasing responsibility for operations, and at noon on 31 December 1999 Panama took full control of the canal, its administration passing to the Panama Canal Authority. The Treaty Concerning the Permanent Neutrality and Operation of the Panama Canal guarantees the canal's permanent neutrality and open access to vessels of all nations; the United States retained the right to defend the canal's neutrality.

Background and ratification

Renegotiation had been sought by Panama for decades, intensified by the deadly 1964 flag riots in the Canal Zone. Negotiations under several U.S. administrations culminated under Carter. In Panama the treaties were approved in a plebiscite in October 1977. In the U.S. Senate, ratification in spring 1978 was fiercely contested, passing each treaty by a single vote above the two-thirds threshold (68–32), with reservations (the DeConcini reservation) asserting a U.S. right to intervene to keep the canal open.

Handover

The transfer was completed on 31 December 1999 in a ceremony attended by former President Carter. The canal has since been operated by Panama and was expanded with a third set of locks in 2016.
