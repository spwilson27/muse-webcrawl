Paul Joseph Cohen (April 2, 1934 – March 23, 2007) was an American mathematician best known for his proof of the independence of the continuum hypothesis and the axiom of choice from Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory, for which he invented the technique of forcing. Together with Kurt Gödel's earlier result on consistency, Cohen's work settled the first of Hilbert's problems: the continuum hypothesis can neither be proved nor disproved from the standard axioms of set theory.

Early life and education

Cohen was born in Long Branch, New Jersey, into a Jewish family that had immigrated from Poland. He graduated from Stuyvesant High School in New York in 1950, studied at Brooklyn College and then the University of Chicago, where he received his master's degree in 1954 and his PhD in 1958 under Antoni Zygmund, with a dissertation on uniqueness in the factorization of trigonometric series.

Career and the continuum hypothesis

After a year at MIT as a Moore Instructor and a year at the Institute for Advanced Study, Cohen joined Stanford in 1961, remaining there for the rest of his career. In 1963–64 he developed forcing, a method for constructing models of set theory with prescribed properties, and used it to show that both the continuum hypothesis and the axiom of choice are independent of ZF set theory — completing the work begun by Gödel, who had shown in 1940 that neither can be disproved. Cohen received the Fields Medal in 1966 and the National Medal of Science in 1967; he remains the only mathematician to have won a Fields Medal for work in mathematical logic.

Other work

Cohen also made contributions to harmonic analysis, including work on idempotent measures, and published the influential monograph Set Theory and the Continuum Hypothesis (1966). He had wide interests in analysis and number theory and worked on the Littlewood conjectures and factorization of operators.

Death

Cohen died on March 23, 2007, in Stanford, California, of a rare lung disease, aged 72.
