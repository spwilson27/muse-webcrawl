The Iroquoian languages are an Indigenous language family of North America, spoken historically around the Great Lakes, the St Lawrence River valley, and — in the case of Cherokee — the southern Appalachian Mountains. They are known for their general lack of labial consonants, their polysynthetic morphology (including extensive noun incorporation), and their use of pronominal prefixes encoding agent and patient.

Classification

The family divides into Northern Iroquoian and Southern Iroquoian. Northern Iroquoian includes the languages of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) — Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga, Oneida and Mohawk, with Tuscarora joining the confederacy in the 18th century — as well as extinct languages such as Huron-Wendat, Petun, Neutral and Laurentian. Southern Iroquoian consists solely of Cherokee, which diverged from the northern group perhaps 3,500–4,000 years ago.

History and current status

At European contact, Iroquoian peoples farmed maize, beans and squash and lived in longhouse villages. Colonisation, disease and displacement severely reduced speaker numbers. Cherokee is notable for its syllabary, devised by Sequoyah in the 1820s, which made the Cherokee literate within years. All Iroquoian languages are now endangered to varying degrees, though immersion schools, language nests and documentation projects — including the Kanien'kehá:ka (Mohawk) programmes and Cherokee immersion schools — support revitalisation. Mohawk has the largest speaker base, with several thousand speakers.

Features

Iroquoian languages typically have small phoneme inventories (Mohawk has no labial consonants at all in native words) and verb-heavy, agglutinative grammar in which a single verb word can express an entire proposition. They are head-marking, lack a separate class of adjectives, and classify nouns in ways reflected in verb agreement.
