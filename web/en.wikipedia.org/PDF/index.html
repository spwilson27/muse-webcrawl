PDF

Portable Document Format (PDF), standardized as ISO 32000, is a file format developed by Adobe in 1992 to present documents, including text formatting and images, in a manner independent of application software, hardware, and operating systems. Based on the PostScript language, each PDF file encapsulates a complete description of a fixed-layout flat document, including the text, fonts, vector graphics, raster images and other information needed to display it.


PDF documents render identically across platforms.

History

PDF was developed from "The Camelot Project" initiated by Adobe co-founder John Warnock in 1991, which set out to create a universal way to view and print documents across computers. Adobe introduced PDF 1.0 and Adobe Acrobat at the Windows and OS/2 Conference in June 1993, winner of a best-of-show presentation. Early adoption was slow: Acrobat was expensive, the free Reader initially had limited features, and the format competed with rivals such as DjVu and Envoy. Adobe progressively added support for hyperlinks, free Reader distribution, and plug-ins, and PDF became the de facto standard for printable documents, forms, and electronic publishing. In 2008 Adobe published the complete specification to ISO, and PDF became an open standard maintained by the International Organization for Standardization; PDF 2.0 (ISO 32000-2) was published in 2017 and updated in 2020.

Technical overview

A PDF file describes pages whose content streams draw text and graphics using operators derived from PostScript. Text is encoded with embedded or referenced fonts, including subsetted glyphs, allowing documents to display correctly without the fonts installed on the viewing system. Images may be stored in formats such as JPEG and JPEG 2000, and vector graphics support transparency (from PDF 1.4). Files use a structure of indirect objects referenced by a cross-reference table (or compressed object streams in later versions), enabling incremental updates that append changes without rewriting the whole file.

PDF supports interactive features: hyperlinks, bookmarks, form fields (AcroForms and the XML-based XFA), annotations, digital signatures, embedded JavaScript, layers, and multimedia. Variants of the standard serve specialized needs: PDF/A for long-term archiving, PDF/X for print production, PDF/E for engineering documents, PDF/UA for accessibility, and PDF/H for healthcare.

Security and criticism

Because PDF supports embedded scripts, attachments, and complex parsing, it has historically been a vector for malware, and viewer vulnerabilities have been common attack targets; modern readers sandbox documents and restrict JavaScript. Digital rights management and encryption features allow authors to restrict printing, copying, or editing, though these restrictions are enforced by compliant software rather than being cryptographically strong. PDFs have also been criticized for poor reflow on small screens compared with formats such as EPUB, and for variable accessibility when documents lack proper tagging.

Usage

PDF is ubiquitous in business, government, and academia: contracts, invoices, tax forms, scholarly articles, e-books, manuals, and archived records are routinely exchanged as PDFs. Most operating systems and web browsers now include built-in PDF viewing, and tools for creating, editing, signing, and converting PDFs are widely available from many vendors.
