Greyfriars Bobby

Greyfriars Bobby (1855/1856 – 14 January 1872) was a Skye Terrier who became known in 19th-century Edinburgh, Scotland, for supposedly spending 14 years guarding the grave of his owner until his own death. The story continues to be represented in film and literature and is commemorated by the famous statue of Bobby outside Greyfriars Kirkyard.

The story

John Gray, a night watchman for the Edinburgh City Police, took on Bobby as a watchdog around 1856. Gray died of tuberculosis on 15 February 1858 and was buried in Greyfriars Kirkyard. According to the famous account, Bobby refused to leave the grave, surviving in the kirkyard through harsh winters, leaving only at the sound of the one o'clock gun from Edinburgh Castle to take his midday meal — fed by local residents and at a nearby eating house. In 1867 a new bye-law required dogs to be licensed or destroyed; Sir William Chambers, the Lord Provost of Edinburgh, paid Bobby's licence and presented him with a collar inscribed "Greyfriars Bobby from the Lord Provost 1867 licensed". When Bobby died in 1872 he was buried just inside the gate of Greyfriars Kirkyard, not far from John Gray's grave.

Commemoration

A year after Bobby's death, the philanthropist Lady Burdett-Coutts commissioned a drinking fountain topped by a life-size statue of Bobby, sculpted by William Brodie, at the junction of George IV Bridge and Candlemaker Row. It is a Category A listed monument and one of Edinburgh's best-loved landmarks; a red granite headstone over Bobby's grave was erected by the Dog Aid Society of Scotland in 1981. Bobby's collar and feeding bowl are displayed in the Museum of Edinburgh.

Historical accuracy

Modern researchers have questioned details of the tale. Jan Bondeson, in a 2011 study, argued that contemporary records suggest Bobby may have been a stray "kirkyard dog" encouraged to stay by the kirkyard's curator and local businesses, and that the story was embellished as he became a tourist attraction — with possibly two different dogs playing the role across the 14 years. The story remains celebrated regardless, and has inspired Eleanor Atkinson's 1912 novel Greyfriars Bobby, a 1961 Disney film, and a 2005 film adaptation.
