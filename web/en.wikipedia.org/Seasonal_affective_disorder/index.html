Seasonal affective disorder

Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a mood disorder subset in which people who have normal mental health throughout most of the year exhibit depressive symptoms at the same time each year, most commonly in winter. In the DSM-5 it is classified not as a separate disorder but as a specifier — major depressive disorder with seasonal pattern — applied to recurrent depression that follows a seasonal cycle.

Symptoms

Winter-pattern SAD is associated with oversleeping, overeating (especially carbohydrate craving), weight gain, fatigue, and social withdrawal — sometimes described as a wish to "hibernate". A rarer summer pattern involves insomnia, poor appetite, and agitation. Symptoms remit in the opposite season.

Causes

The disorder is linked to reduced sunlight in winter, which can disrupt circadian rhythms and serotonin activity and alter melatonin production. Prevalence rises with latitude, and the condition is more common in women and in people with a family history of depression.

Treatment

First-line treatments include bright light therapy using a light box, typically for 30–45 minutes each morning, cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for SAD, and antidepressant medication such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Regular daylight exposure and exercise are also recommended.
