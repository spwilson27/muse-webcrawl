A coroner is a government or judicial official who is empowered to conduct or order an inquest into the manner or cause of death. The role originated in medieval England — the office of custos placitorum coronae ("keeper of the pleas of the Crown") is documented from 1194 — and spread throughout the Commonwealth and the United States.

Duties

Coroners investigate deaths that are sudden, unexplained, violent, or occurring in custody or state detention. They may order post-mortem examinations (performed by pathologists), summon witnesses, and sit with a jury. In England and Wales, coroners are independent judicial officers, usually lawyers or doctors of at least five years' standing; their inquests determine who the deceased was and how, when, and where they died, without assigning criminal liability to named persons. Coroners there also handle treasure trove cases.

Coroners vs. medical examiners

In the United States both systems coexist: roughly half of the population is served by coroners — often elected county officials, not necessarily physicians — and half by appointed medical examiners, who are forensic pathologists. The distinction and the variability in training and independence have long been debated by forensic-science bodies.
