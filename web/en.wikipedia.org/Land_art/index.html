Land art

Land art, also known as Earth art, environmental art, and Earthworks, is an art movement that emerged in the 1960s and 1970s largely in the United States and the United Kingdom, in which landscape and the work of art are inextricably linked.

Works are often created in remote locations using materials taken directly from the landscape — rock, earth, timber and water — and are left to erode or change with the environment. The movement arose partly in reaction against the commercial gallery system and the perceived sterility of minimalism.

Major works and artists

Robert Smithson's Spiral Jetty (1970), a 460-metre coil of basalt in Utah's Great Salt Lake, is the movement's most famous work. Other leading figures include Michael Heizer (Double Negative, 1969; City, completed 2022), Walter De Maria (The Lightning Field, 1977), Nancy Holt (Sun Tunnels, 1973–76), James Turrell (Roden Crater), and in Britain Richard Long and Andy Goldsworthy, whose ephemeral works made from leaves, stone and ice emphasise walking and impermanence.

Characteristics

Land art often involved documentation — photographs, maps and films — as the only gallery-accessible record, since the works themselves were vast, remote or temporary. The movement influenced later environmental and ecological art, and has been criticised for the scale of its earth-moving interventions in fragile landscapes.
