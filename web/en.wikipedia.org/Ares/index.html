Ares (Ancient Greek: Ἄρης) is the Greek god of war and courage. He is one of the Twelve Olympians, and the son of Zeus and Hera. The Greeks were ambivalent toward him: although he embodied the physical valor necessary for success in war, he was a dangerous force, "overwhelming, insatiable in battle, destructive, and man-slaughtering". His value as a war god is placed in doubt, since during the Trojan War he sided with the losing Trojans, and was frequently wounded or humiliated.

Mythology

Ares's attendant deities included his sons Phobos (Fear) and Deimos (Terror), his sister Eris (Strife) and the war goddess Enyo. His affair with Aphrodite, wife of Hephaestus, was famously exposed when the smith trapped the lovers in an unbreakable net and displayed them to the laughing gods. In the Iliad, Ares is wounded by the mortal hero Diomedes, aided by Athena, and flees bellowing to Olympus. He was father of the Amazons' queen Penthesilea and, by Aphrodite, of Eros in some traditions and of Harmonia.

Ares was associated with Thrace, considered a wild and warlike land, and his worship in Greece proper was limited; Sparta, however, kept a statue of Ares in chains to ensure the war god would never leave the city.

Roman counterpart

The Roman god Mars, identified with Ares, was far more central to Roman religion: father of Romulus, guardian of soldiers and agriculture, and one of the most important deities of the Roman state. The month of March (Latin Martius) is named for him, as is the planet Mars.
