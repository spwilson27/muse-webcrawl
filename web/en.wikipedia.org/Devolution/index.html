Devolution

Devolution is the statutory delegation of powers from the central government of a sovereign state to govern at a subnational level, such as a regional or local level. It is a form of administrative decentralisation. Devolved territories have the power to make legislation relevant to the area within the delegated sphere, thus granting them a higher level of autonomy.

Devolution versus federalism

Devolution differs from federalism in that the devolved powers of the subnational authority ultimately reside in central government, which can in principle revoke or override them by ordinary legislation; the central state remains legally supreme. In a federal system, by contrast, the division of powers is constitutionally entrenched and subnational governments are sovereign in their spheres. In practice, devolved settlements may become politically irreversible even where legally revocable.

United Kingdom

The best-known modern example is the United Kingdom, where devolution was introduced following referendums in 1997–98. The Scotland Act 1998 created the Scottish Parliament with primary legislative powers (extended since, including over income tax), the Government of Wales Act established what is now the Senedd, and the Northern Ireland Act 1998 underpins the power-sharing Northern Ireland Assembly created by the Good Friday Agreement. Devolution is asymmetric: England, the largest component, has no devolved parliament, giving rise to the "West Lothian question" about English representation.

Other examples

Unitary states such as Spain (autonomous communities), France, Italy and the Philippines have adopted various forms of devolved or regionalised government. Devolution is often advanced as a response to regional nationalism and demands for self-government short of independence.
