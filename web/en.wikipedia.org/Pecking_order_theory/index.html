Pecking order theory


Firms prefer internal financing first

In corporate finance, the pecking order theory (or pecking order model) postulates that the cost of financing increases with asymmetric information. Managers know more about their firm's value than outside investors do, so investors interpret the issue of new shares as a signal that the shares may be overvalued, and discount them accordingly. To avoid this adverse-selection cost, firms follow a hierarchy of financing:

  1. Internal funds (retained earnings) — no information problem, used first.
  2. Debt — only a modest information problem, issued before equity.
  3. External equity — subject to severe underpricing, used only as a last resort.

Origins and predictions

The idea was first suggested by Gordon Donaldson in 1961 and was formalised by Stewart Myers and Nicolas Majluf in their 1984 paper on financing under asymmetric information. A key prediction is that firms do not aim at a target debt ratio: leverage instead reflects the cumulative history of external financing needs. Profitable firms tend to borrow less simply because they generate cash internally, while growing firms exhaust internal funds and turn to debt.

Evidence and criticism

Empirical tests are mixed. The theory explains the negative stock-market reaction to equity announcements and the inverse relation between profitability and leverage, but studies of small high-growth firms often find equity used heavily, contrary to the strict hierarchy. The pecking order is usually contrasted with the trade-off theory, in which firms balance the tax advantages of debt against the costs of financial distress to reach an optimal capital structure.
