Cytoskeleton

The cytoskeleton is a complex, dynamic network of interlinking protein filaments present in the cytoplasm of all cells, including those of bacteria and archaea. It extends from the cell nucleus to the cell membrane and is composed of similar proteins in different organisms. In eukaryotes it consists of three principal types of protein filaments: microfilaments (actin filaments), microtubules, and intermediate filaments.

Components

Microfilaments

Microfilaments are polymers of the protein actin, about 7 nm in diameter — the thinnest cytoskeletal fibres. They are concentrated just beneath the plasma membrane, where they maintain cell shape, form surface projections such as microvilli, and drive cell crawling through cycles of polymerisation and depolymerisation. Together with the motor protein myosin, actin forms the contractile machinery of muscle and the contractile ring that divides a cell in cytokinesis.

Microtubules

Microtubules are hollow cylinders about 25 nm in diameter, built from α- and β-tubulin dimers. They are nucleated mainly at the centrosome and show dynamic instability — stochastic switching between growth and rapid shrinkage. Microtubules form the tracks along which motor proteins (kinesins and dyneins) haul vesicles and organelles, build the mitotic spindle that segregates chromosomes, and form the structural core (axoneme) of cilia and flagella.

Intermediate filaments

Intermediate filaments, about 10 nm in diameter, are rope-like polymers that provide mechanical strength. They include the keratins of epithelial cells, vimentin in connective tissue, neurofilaments in neurons, and the lamins that line the inner nuclear membrane. Unlike actin and microtubules they are not polarised and do not serve as tracks for motors.

Functions

  • Maintaining and changing cell shape
  • Enabling cell movement (crawling, swimming)
  • Intracellular transport of vesicles and organelles
  • Chromosome segregation during mitosis and meiosis
  • Cytokinesis
  • Mechanical resilience of tissues
  • Signalling and organisation of the cell interior

Prokaryotic cytoskeleton

Once thought to be unique to eukaryotes, cytoskeletal proteins were later found in bacteria and archaea: FtsZ (a tubulin homologue) forms the division ring, MreB (an actin homologue) maintains rod shape, and crescentin resembles intermediate filaments. These discoveries showed the cytoskeleton is an ancient feature of cellular life.
