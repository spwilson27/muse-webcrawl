Père Lachaise Cemetery

Père Lachaise Cemetery (French: Cimetière du Père-Lachaise) is the largest cemetery in Paris, France, covering about 44 hectares (110 acres) in the 20th arrondissement. With more than 3.5 million visitors per year, it is the most visited necropolis in the world, and it contains the remains of many famous people, including Oscar Wilde, Jim Morrison, Édith Piaf, Frédéric Chopin and Marcel Proust.

History

The cemetery takes its name from Père François de la Chaise (1624–1709), confessor to King Louis XIV, who lived in a Jesuit house on the site. The city of Paris purchased the land in 1803, and the cemetery was established by Napoleon Bonaparte in 1804, when burial within the city limits had been banned for health reasons. Designed by the neoclassical architect Alexandre-Théodore Brongniart, it was laid out as an English-style garden cemetery — the first of its kind in Paris.

At first Parisians were reluctant to be buried so far from the city centre. To raise its prestige, the authorities transferred the remains of the playwright Molière and the poet Jean de La Fontaine in 1804, and later those of the medieval lovers Héloïse and Abélard. The marketing succeeded: by 1830 more than 33,000 graves filled the cemetery, and it was expanded five times during the 19th century.

Notable burials

  • Frédéric Chopin, composer and pianist
  • Oscar Wilde, Irish playwright and poet
  • Jim Morrison, lead singer of The Doors
  • Édith Piaf, singer
  • Marcel Proust, novelist
  • Honoré de Balzac, novelist
  • Maria Callas, opera singer (ashes later scattered)
  • Amedeo Modigliani, painter
  • Camille Pissarro and Georges Seurat, painters
  • Sarah Bernhardt, actress

War memorials

The cemetery contains numerous memorials to the dead of the First and Second World Wars, as well as monuments to victims of the Nazi concentration camps. Against its north-east wall, the Communards' Wall (Mur des Fédérés), 147 members of the Paris Commune were shot on 28 May 1871, during the suppression of the Commune.
