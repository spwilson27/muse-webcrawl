Jackup rig

A jackup rig or jack-up rig is a type of mobile offshore drilling unit that consists of a buoyant hull fitted with a number of moveable legs, capable of raising the hull above the surface of the sea. The barge-like hull is floated to location, the legs are lowered to the seabed, and the hull is then jacked up out of the water, providing a stable platform unaffected by waves and currents.

Design

Most jackups have three or four legs, either open-truss lattice structures or cylindrical columns, terminating in spud cans or mats that rest on the seabed. Rack-and-pinion or hydraulic jacking systems raise and lower the hull. Modern jackups can operate in water depths up to about 170 metres (550 ft) and drill wells more than 10,000 metres deep. Cantilevered drill floors allow the derrick to be skidded out over a fixed platform.

Uses

Jackups are the most common type of mobile offshore drilling unit and are used for exploratory and development drilling in shallow waters. They are also used in modified form as offshore accommodation units, and for installing and servicing offshore wind turbines, where purpose-built wind turbine installation vessels use the same jacking principle.

History

The first jackup was built in 1954, and designers such as R. G. LeTourneau pioneered the electric rack-and-pinion jacking system. One of LeTourneau's 1955 rigs, Scorpion, was lost in a storm off the Texas coast, spurring improved storm criteria and leg design.
