Polyvinyl chloride

Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is the world's third-most widely produced synthetic polymer, after polyethylene and polypropylene. It is made by polymerizing the monomer vinyl chloride, forming chains of the repeating unit –CH2–CHCl–. Global production is on the order of 40–50 million tonnes per year.

Forms and uses

PVC comes in two basic forms. Rigid PVC (uPVC, where "u" stands for unplasticized) is used extensively in construction for window and door frames, pipes, and siding, thanks to its durability, low cost, and resistance to weathering. Flexible PVC is made softer by adding plasticizers — historically phthalates — and is used for electrical cable insulation, inflatable products, flooring, synthetic leather, and medical tubing and blood bags.

History

Vinyl chloride was first observed by Henri Victor Regnault in 1835, and the polymer was described by Eugen Baumann in 1872, but commercial development only followed Waldo Semon's work at B.F. Goodrich in the 1920s and 1930s, when he discovered how to plasticize PVC into a useful flexible material, sold as Koroseal.

Health and environment

The vinyl chloride monomer is a known human carcinogen, and its production and the incineration of PVC — which can release hydrogen chloride and, under poor conditions, dioxins — have made the material controversial. Certain phthalate plasticizers have also faced restrictions, especially in toys and medical devices. Recycling of PVC is established but limited compared with its production volume.
