Los Angeles Basin

The Los Angeles Basin is a sedimentary basin located in Southern California, in a region known as the Peninsular Ranges. The basin is connected to the ocean through a narrow channel and contains the cities of Los Angeles and Long Beach, as well as a large portion of the Greater Los Angeles metropolitan area.

Geology

The basin is a structurally deep, sediment-filled depression bounded by faults, including the Newport–Inglewood Fault, the Whittier Fault, and the Palos Verdes Fault. It began forming in the Miocene epoch and contains rock units that reach more than 10,000 metres (33,000 ft) in thickness in places, making it one of the deepest sedimentary basins in the world relative to its area. Compressional tectonics associated with the bend in the San Andreas Fault system continue to deform the basin.

Petroleum

The Los Angeles Basin is one of the most prolific petroleum-producing regions per unit volume in the world. Fields such as the Los Angeles City, Long Beach (Wilmington), Signal Hill, Huntington Beach, and Inglewood oil fields have produced billions of barrels of oil since the late nineteenth century. Oil seeps at places like the La Brea Tar Pits were known to Indigenous peoples long before commercial drilling began.

Geography and climate

The basin forms a broad coastal plain ringed by the Santa Monica Mountains, the San Gabriel Mountains, the Santa Ana Mountains, and the Palos Verdes Hills. Its topography and position along a cold ocean current contribute to the region's Mediterranean climate and its susceptibility to temperature inversions, which historically trapped smog over the basin.
