Independence (mathematical logic)

In mathematical logic, independence is the property of a sentence that can be neither proved nor refuted from a given set of axioms. A sentence φ is independent of a theory T if T does not prove φ and T does not prove the negation of φ; equivalently, there are models of T in which φ is true and models in which it is false.

Famous examples

The best-known independence result concerns the continuum hypothesis: Kurt Gödel showed in 1940 that it cannot be disproved from the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms with the axiom of choice (ZFC), assuming ZFC is consistent, and Paul Cohen showed in 1963, using the method of forcing, that it cannot be proved either. The parallel postulate in geometry is independent of the other Euclidean axioms, as demonstrated by models of non-Euclidean geometry; and the axiom of choice is independent of ZF set theory.

Relation to Gödel's theorems

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem implies that any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory capable of expressing elementary arithmetic contains independent sentences. Independence results shape modern set theory, where many questions about the infinite are settled only relative to additional axioms.
