Heparan sulfate (HS) is a linear polysaccharide found on the surface of virtually all animal cells and in extracellular matrices. It is a member of the glycosaminoglycan family and occurs covalently attached to core proteins, forming heparan sulfate proteoglycans (HSPGs) such as syndecans, glypicans, perlecan and agrin.

Structure and biosynthesis

HS chains are built from repeating disaccharide units of a uronic acid — either β-D-glucuronic acid or its epimer α-L-iduronic acid — linked to D-glucosamine. Biosynthesis begins in the Golgi apparatus when a tetrasaccharide linker is assembled on a serine residue of a core protein; the exostosin enzymes EXT1 and EXT2 then polymerize the chain. Subsequent modification by N-deacetylase/N-sulfotransferases, C5 epimerase and O-sulfotransferases creates a highly variable pattern of sulfation along the chain, giving HS a large repertoire of protein-binding specificities. Because modification is incomplete and non-template-driven, HS structure is highly heterogeneous.

Functions

By binding hundreds of proteins, HS regulates growth-factor signalling (including FGF, VEGF, Wnt, Hedgehog and BMP pathways), chemokine presentation, cell adhesion, basement-membrane organization, lipoprotein metabolism and blood coagulation. HS acts as a co-receptor that stabilizes ligand–receptor complexes at the cell surface. Many viruses and other pathogens exploit cell-surface HS as an initial attachment factor.

Clinical significance

Defects in HS biosynthesis cause developmental disorders: loss-of-function variants in EXT1 or EXT2 cause hereditary multiple exostoses (multiple osteochondromas). Altered HS is implicated in Alzheimer's disease, cancer progression and inflammation. Heparin, the highly sulfated pharmaceutical anticoagulant produced by mast cells, is a closely related molecule; a specific pentasaccharide sequence in HS and heparin binds and activates antithrombin.
