Ghirardi–Rimini–Weber theory

The Ghirardi–Rimini–Weber theory (GRW) is a collapse theory in quantum mechanics proposed in 1985 by Italian physicists Gian Carlo Ghirardi, Alberto Rimini, and Tullio Weber. It modifies the Schrödinger equation by adding a stochastic, nonlinear process that causes the wavefunction of a system to spontaneously localise in space. GRW was the first precisely formulated objective-collapse model and aims to solve the measurement problem by describing quantum measurement as an ordinary physical process, without invoking observers or consciousness.

Theory

In GRW, each elementary constituent of matter undergoes random spontaneous localisation events — "hits" — at an average rate of about once every 1016 seconds per particle (λ ≈ 10−16 s−1), localising its wavefunction to a region of about 10−7 metres (the length scale a). For a single particle such events are vanishingly rare and quantum behaviour is preserved, but for a macroscopic object containing ~1023 particles, some constituents localise almost constantly; because the particles are entangled, the whole object's centre of mass is thereby localised almost continuously. This "amplification mechanism" explains why macroscopic objects always have definite positions while microscopic ones can exist in superposition.

Testing and variants

Because GRW modifies quantum dynamics, it makes testable predictions that differ slightly from standard quantum mechanics: spontaneous collapses should produce tiny amounts of heating and spontaneous radiation, and should limit the size and duration of achievable superpositions. Experiments searching for these effects have progressively constrained the theory's parameters. Variants include the continuous spontaneous localisation (CSL) model and the mass-proportional ontology of the "GRWm" and flash-ontology "GRWf" versions, debated among philosophers of physics.
