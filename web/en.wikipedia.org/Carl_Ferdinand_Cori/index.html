Carl Ferdinand Cori

Carl Ferdinand Cori (5 December 1896 – 20 October 1984) was a Czech-American biochemist. Together with his wife Gerty Cori, he elucidated how glycogen is broken down and resynthesised in the body. The Coris shared the 1947 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Bernardo Houssay "for their discovery of the course of the catalytic conversion of glycogen".

Life

Cori was born in Prague, then in Austria-Hungary, and studied medicine at the German University there, where he met fellow student Gerty Radnitz; they married in 1920. Facing post-war hardship and antisemitism in Europe, the couple emigrated to the United States in 1922, working at the State Institute for the Study of Malignant Diseases in Buffalo before moving in 1931 to Washington University in St. Louis, where Carl became professor and later chair of biochemistry.

Research

The Coris discovered glucose 1-phosphate (the "Cori ester") and the enzyme glycogen phosphorylase, and worked out the cycle by which muscle glycogen is converted to lactic acid, transported to the liver, and rebuilt into glucose — the Cori cycle. Their laboratory became a training ground for a generation of biochemists, including several future Nobel laureates such as Arthur Kornberg and Earl Reece Stadtman.

Later life

After Gerty's death in 1957, Carl continued research at Harvard Medical School and the Massachusetts General Hospital, working on enzymes and glycogen storage diseases. He died in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1984. The Cori crater on the Moon and the Cori cycle both bear the couple's name.
