Schmidt–Cassegrain telescope

The Schmidt–Cassegrain is a catadioptric telescope that combines a Cassegrain reflector's optical path with a Schmidt corrector plate to make a compact astronomical instrument that offers a wide field of view. The design uses spherical primary and secondary mirrors combined with an aspherical correcting plate at the front of the tube, which keeps manufacturing costs low while correcting spherical aberration.

History

The optical design combines elements of the Schmidt camera, invented by Bernhard Schmidt in 1930, with the Cassegrain reflector. The commercial Schmidt–Cassegrain was developed by the American company Celestron, whose founder Tom Johnson patented key production techniques in the 1960s and 1970s; Meade Instruments soon became a competing manufacturer. The design became the most popular type of amateur astronomy telescope from the 1970s onward.

Design

Light entering the tube passes through a thin Schmidt corrector plate, reflects off a spherical primary mirror at the back, then off a convex secondary mirror mounted on the corrector, and finally through a hole in the primary to the focal plane behind the telescope. Because the light path is folded, a telescope with a focal length of 2,000 mm may be only about 400 mm long, typically operating at f/10. Focusing is usually accomplished by moving the primary mirror.

Applications

Schmidt–Cassegrains are general-purpose instruments used for observing the Moon, planets, and deep-sky objects, and for astrophotography. Common apertures range from 5 to 16 inches. Modern versions are often mounted on computerized fork or equatorial mounts with GoTo pointing. Variants such as Meade's Advanced Coma-Free optics and Celestron's EdgeHD correct the coma and field curvature of the basic design.
