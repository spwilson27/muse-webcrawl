Senecan tragedy

Senecan tragedy refers to the ten tragic plays attributed to the Roman Stoic philosopher and statesman Lucius Annaeus Seneca the Younger (c. 4 BC – AD 65). Written in verse in the first century AD, these plays — including Hercules Furens, Medea, Phaedra, Thyestes, Agamemnon, Oedipus, Phoenissae, Troades, Hercules Oetaeus and the disputed Octavia — became the principal surviving model of Roman tragedy and profoundly influenced Renaissance drama, especially Elizabethan revenge tragedy.

Characteristics

Senecan tragedies are noted for their rhetorical declamation, long monologues, sententious maxims (sententiae), gruesome violence, supernatural elements such as ghosts and witches, and a five-act structure mediated by a chorus. Stoic themes of fate, passion and the destructive power of unrestrained emotion dominate the plays.

Influence

Recovered and widely read in the Renaissance, Seneca's tragedies supplied English dramatists such as Thomas Kyd, Christopher Marlowe and William Shakespeare with conventions including the revenge plot, the ghost demanding vengeance, soliloquies, and stichomythic dialogue. The Thyestes in particular shaped the revenge tragedy genre.
