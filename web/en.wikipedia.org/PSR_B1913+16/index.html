PSR B1913+16 (also known as the Hulse–Taylor pulsar) is a binary star system composed of a pulsar in orbit around another neutron star. It was discovered in 1974 by Russell A. Hulse and Joseph H. Taylor Jr. using the Arecibo radio telescope — the first binary pulsar found.

Test of general relativity

The system provided the first indirect evidence for the existence of gravitational waves. Precise timing of the pulsar's pulses showed that the orbit is decaying — the two neutron stars spiral toward each other — at exactly the rate predicted by Einstein's general theory of relativity for energy lost to gravitational radiation. The match between observation and prediction, refined over decades, agrees to within a fraction of a percent.

Nobel Prize

Hulse and Taylor received the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics "for the discovery of a new type of pulsar, a discovery that has opened up new possibilities for the study of gravitation". The pulsar itself has a rotation period of about 59 milliseconds, and both components have masses near 1.4 solar masses.
