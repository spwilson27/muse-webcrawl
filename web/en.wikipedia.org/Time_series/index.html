Time series

In mathematics, a time series is a series of data points indexed in time order — a sequence of discrete-time data taken at successive, usually equally spaced, points in time. Examples include the daily closing value of a stock index, monthly rainfall at a weather station, quarterly gross domestic product, and the voltage trace of an electrocardiogram. Time series analysis comprises methods for analysing such data to extract meaningful characteristics and to forecast future values.

Components

A time series is conventionally decomposed into a trend (long-term direction), seasonal variation (regular patterns tied to the calendar, such as higher retail sales in December), cyclical variation (longer waves such as business cycles) and an irregular or noise component. A series is called stationary if its statistical properties — mean, variance and autocorrelation — do not change over time; many modelling techniques assume stationarity, or transform the series (for example by differencing) to achieve it.

Methods

Classical methods include moving averages and exponential smoothing for short-term forecasting. The Box–Jenkins methodology models series with autoregressive (AR), moving-average (MA) and combined ARIMA models, extended to SARIMA for seasonal data. Multivariate extensions such as vector autoregression (VAR) model several series jointly, and cointegration analysis handles series that move together over the long run. Analysis in the frequency domain, using the periodogram and Fourier methods, reveals hidden periodicities. Modern practice also uses state-space models and Kalman filtering, volatility models such as GARCH in finance, and machine-learning approaches including recurrent neural networks.

Applications

Time series methods are used throughout statistics and applied fields: economic and financial forecasting, weather and climate analysis, epidemiology, quality control, signal processing, astronomy and operations management. Their central difficulty is that successive observations are usually correlated (autocorrelation), so methods designed for independent samples give misleading results when applied uncritically.
