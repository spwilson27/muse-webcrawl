Isabella I (Spanish: Isabel I; 22 April 1451 – 26 November 1504), also called Isabella the Catholic (Spanish: Isabel la Católica), was Queen of Castile and León from 1474 until her death in 1504. Together with her husband, King Ferdinand II of Aragon, she was a driving force in the unification of Spain. Their marriage in 1469 formed the dynastic union that laid the foundations of the Spanish Empire, and their reign saw the completion of the Reconquista, the institution of the Spanish Inquisition, and the sponsorship of Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage to the Americas.

Early life and succession

Isabella was born in Madrigal de las Altas Torres to King John II of Castile and his second wife, Isabella of Portugal. After her father's death in 1454, her half-brother Henry IV became king. Isabella's claim to the throne was contested, and upon Henry's death in 1474 she proclaimed herself queen, precipitating the War of the Castilian Succession (1475–1479) against the supporters of Henry's daughter Joanna la Beltraneja, who was backed by Portugal. Isabella's eventual victory, sealed by the Treaty of Alcáçovas in 1479, secured her crown.

Reign

Reforms

Isabella and Ferdinand moved to centralise power in the crown, reforming the administration, the legal system, and the finances of Castile. They established the Santa Hermandad, a police force that curtailed the power of the nobility, and brought the military orders under royal control. Isabella also promoted learning and supported the printing of books, including the Latin grammar of Antonio de Nebrija, the first grammar of a modern European vernacular.

Reconquista and the conquest of Granada

In 1482 the Catholic Monarchs began the final campaign of the Reconquista against the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, the last Muslim state in Iberia. After a decade of warfare, Granada surrendered on 2 January 1492, completing the Christian reconquest of the peninsula. The Alhambra Decree, issued later that year, ordered the expulsion of practising Jews from Spain.

The Inquisition

In 1478 Pope Sixtus IV granted the monarchs permission to establish the Spanish Inquisition, intended initially to identify conversos — descendants of converted Jews — suspected of secretly practising Judaism. Under its first inquisitor general, Tomás de Torquemada, the Inquisition became notorious for its severity.

Columbus and the Americas

After initially rejecting Christopher Columbus's proposal, Isabella and Ferdinand agreed to sponsor his voyage in 1492, following the capitulations of Santa Fe. Columbus's landfall in the Caribbean opened the Americas to Spanish colonisation, an event with world-transforming consequences. Isabella took a keen interest in the welfare of the indigenous peoples of the new lands, instructing that they be treated as free subjects of the crown.

Children and death

Isabella and Ferdinand had five surviving children, including Catherine of Aragon, who married first Arthur, Prince of Wales and then Henry VIII of England; Joanna of Castile, who succeeded her mother; and John, Prince of Asturias, whose early death in 1497 was a devastating blow. Isabella died on 26 November 1504 at Medina del Campo and was buried in the Royal Chapel of Granada. Her daughter Joanna succeeded her in Castile, though effective power passed to Ferdinand and later to Isabella's grandson, Charles V.

Legacy

Isabella is regarded as one of the most consequential monarchs in European history. Her reign united Spain dynastically, launched its overseas empire, and reshaped the religious map of the peninsula. In 1974 the Roman Catholic Church granted her the title Servant of God, and her cause for canonisation remains a subject of debate.
