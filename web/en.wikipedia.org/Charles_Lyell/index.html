Charles Lyell, the leading geologist of the Victorian era.

Sir Charles Lyell, 1st Baronet (14 November 1797 – 22 February 1875) was a Scottish geologist who did more than any other scientist to establish the modern discipline of geology. He championed uniformitarianism — the doctrine that the Earth's features are the product of natural processes operating at present-day rates over immense spans of time — and expounded it in his three-volume Principles of Geology (1830–1833), one of the most influential scientific books of the 19th century.

Life and work

Lyell was born at Kinnordy House in Angus, the eldest of ten children of a botanist father. He read law at Exeter College, Oxford, but turned to geology under the influence of William Buckland's lectures and travels in Europe with his family, during which he studied the volcanic districts of France and Italy. He was called to the bar in 1825 but practised little, supporting himself through geology and writing. Fieldwork in France with Roderick Murchison on the freshwater deposits of the Auvergne, and his studies of Mount Etna, convinced him that geological change was gradual and still ongoing.

Principles of Geology

Published by John Murray from 1830, the Principles argued that "the present is the key to the past": rivers, volcanoes, earthquakes, and erosion, acting steadily, could account for the whole of the geological record without recourse to universal catastrophes. Lyell subdivided the Tertiary strata using the proportions of still-living mollusc species in their fossil faunas, coining the names Eocene, Miocene, and Pliocene, which remain in use. The book passed through twelve editions in his lifetime.

Influence on Darwin

Charles Darwin took the first volume of the Principles aboard HMS Beagle and received the second in South America; Lyell's deep time and gradualism supplied the framework within which Darwin developed evolution by natural selection. The two became close friends, and although Lyell long hesitated over human origins, he endorsed the antiquity of man in The Geological Evidence of the Antiquity of Man (1863). Lyell was president of the Geological Society twice, was knighted in 1848 and made a baronet in 1864, and is buried in Westminster Abbey.
