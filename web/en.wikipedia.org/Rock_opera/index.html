Rock opera

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rock opera is a collection of rock songs with lyrics that relate to a common story. Rock operas are typically released as albums but may also be staged as theatrical productions or films. Unlike a concept album, whose songs share only a loose theme, a rock opera tells a continuous narrative with characters, often using recurring musical motifs in the manner of classical opera.

History

Early candidates include the Pretty Things' S.F. Sorrow (1968), but the form was established by the Who's Tommy (1969), about a "deaf, dumb and blind" pinball champion, written primarily by Pete Townshend. Andrew Lloyd Webber and Tim Rice's Jesus Christ Superstar began as a 1970 album before becoming a long-running stage musical. Pink Floyd's The Wall (1979) combined a double album, elaborate stage show, and a 1982 feature film. The Kinks, Genesis, and Frank Zappa also produced narrative rock works in the 1970s.

Later developments

The form persisted in works such as Meat Loaf's Bat Out of Hell albums, Queensrÿche's Operation: Mindcrime (1988), Green Day's American Idiot (2004), later adapted for Broadway, and My Chemical Romance's The Black Parade (2006). Arjen Lucassen's Ayreon project produced a series of science-fiction rock operas with large casts of vocalists.

Rock opera vs rock musical

A rock opera is defined by its music carrying the story, usually originating on record; a rock musical such as Hair or Rent is written for the stage with dialogue between songs, though the boundary is often blurred once albums are adapted for theatre or film.
