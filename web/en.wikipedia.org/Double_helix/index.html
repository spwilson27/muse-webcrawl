Double helix


The DNA double helix, with two strands wound around a common axis.

In molecular biology, the term double helix refers to the structure of DNA, in which two complementary strands wind around each other like a twisted ladder. Each strand consists of nucleotides — adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G), and cytosine (C) — with A pairing to T and G pairing to C by hydrogen bonds. The two strands are antiparallel, running in opposite directions.

Discovery

The structure was proposed in 1953 by James Watson and Francis Crick at Cambridge, drawing crucially on X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins at King's College London — including Franklin's "Photo 51." Watson, Crick, and Wilkins received the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Geometry

The most common form, B-DNA, is right-handed with a diameter of about 2 nanometres and a helical repeat of about 10.5 base pairs per turn. Two spiral grooves, the major and minor grooves, run along the molecule and provide binding surfaces for proteins. Alternate forms include the left-handed Z-DNA and the more compact A-DNA.
