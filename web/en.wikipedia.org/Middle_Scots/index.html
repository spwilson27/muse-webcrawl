Middle Scots was the Anglic language of Lowland Scotland in the period from 1450 to 1700. It developed from Early Scots, itself descended from the Northumbrian dialect of Old English, and during the Middle Scots period Scots served as the language of the Scottish court, parliament and literature.

Golden age

The reign of James IV and the early sixteenth century saw a flowering of Scots literature by the poets known as the Makars, including William Dunbar, Gavin Douglas — whose Eneados (1513) was the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into an Anglic language — Robert Henryson and David Lyndsay. Middle Scots orthography was distinctive, with spellings such as quh- for wh- and characteristic vocabulary drawn from Old Norse, Dutch, French and Gaelic sources.

Decline

Several forces anglicised Scotland's written language: the printing press and the adoption of the English Geneva Bible and King James Bible after the Reformation, and the Union of the Crowns in 1603, when James VI moved his court to London. By the later seventeenth century, formal writing had largely shifted to English, while Scots survived as a spoken vernacular, later revived literarily by Allan Ramsay and Robert Burns in its modern form.

Features

Middle Scots retained many features lost in southern English, such as the Scottish Vowel Length Rule's precursor distinctions, and developed its own legal and administrative terminology visible in the records of the Scottish Parliament.
