Cumulate rock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Cumulate layering in the Bushveld Igneous Complex

Cumulate rocks are igneous rocks formed by the accumulation of crystals that settle out from a magma by gravity (or, less often, float) during fractional crystallisation. They are the principal constituents of layered intrusions such as the Bushveld Complex of South Africa, the Skaergaard intrusion of Greenland and the Stillwater Complex of Montana.

Formation and terminology

As magma cools, early-formed crystals may sink or float and concentrate on the floor of the magma chamber, producing layers dominated by one or two minerals — for example olivine (dunite), pyroxene (pyroxenite), plagioclase (anorthosite) or chromite (chromitite). Petrologists classify cumulates by the proportion of cumulus crystals to trapped intercumulus liquid: adcumulates (93–100% cumulus minerals, with the intercumulus melt expelled or reacted away), mesocumulates and orthocumulates. Rhythmic layering in such intrusions records pulses of magma, convection and crystal sorting.

Economic importance

Cumulate horizons host major ore deposits: the chromitite layers and the Merensky Reef and Platreef of the Bushveld contain much of the world's chromium and platinum-group element reserves, while magnetitite layers carry vanadium and iron.
