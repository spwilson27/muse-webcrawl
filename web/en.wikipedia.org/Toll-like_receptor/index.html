Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are a class of proteins that play a central role in the innate immune system. They are single-pass membrane-spanning pattern recognition receptors, expressed on immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells (and some non-immune cells), that recognise structurally conserved molecules derived from microbes—pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)—as well as damage-associated patterns from injured host cells.

Signalling and function

TLRs sit either on the cell surface (e.g. TLR4, which senses bacterial lipopolysaccharide with the help of CD14 and MD-2) or in endosomes (e.g. TLR3, TLR7, TLR9, which sense nucleic acids). Ligand binding causes the receptors to dimerise and recruit adaptor proteins such as MyD88 and TRIF through their intracellular TIR domains, activating transcription factors including NF-κB and interferon-regulatory factors. The result is production of inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons, linking innate detection to the adaptive immune response.

Discovery and significance

The name derives from the Drosophila Toll protein, identified in development by Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard (whose exclamation "Das ist ja toll!"—"that's amazing!"—named the gene) and shown in 1996 by Jules Hoffmann's group to defend flies against fungal infection. Bruce Beutler identified TLR4 as the LPS sensor in mammals. Beutler and Hoffmann shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for these discoveries. TLR agonists are used as vaccine adjuvants, and TLR dysfunction is implicated in sepsis, autoimmunity and cancer.
