Battle of the Thames

Date5 October 1813
LocationNear present-day Chatham-Kent, Ontario, Canada
ResultAmerican victory; death of Tecumseh
BelligerentsUnited States vs. British Empire and Tecumseh's Confederacy
CommandersWilliam Henry Harrison vs. Henry Procter; Tecumseh (killed)

The Battle of the Thames, also known as the Battle of Moraviantown, was fought on 5 October 1813 in Upper Canada during the War of 1812. United States forces under Major General William Henry Harrison decisively defeated a retreating British and Indigenous force under Major General Henry Procter. The Shawnee leader Tecumseh was killed in the battle, and his confederacy of northwestern tribes collapsed, securing the American Old Northwest for the remainder of the war.

The mounted charge of the Kentucky volunteers broke the British line.

Background

American victory at the Battle of Lake Erie in September 1813 cut the British supply line to Detroit and Amherstburg. Procter abandoned both posts and retreated up the Thames River with some 800 regulars, followed by Tecumseh and about 1,000 warriors who were increasingly disillusioned by the retreat. Harrison pursued with about 3,500 men, many of them Kentucky volunteers.

The battle

Procter made a stand near the Moravian mission village of Moraviantown, placing his regulars in line across the road with Tecumseh's warriors in the swampy woods on his flank. Harrison, instead of a frontal infantry assault, ordered a charge by Colonel Richard Mentor Johnson's mounted riflemen. The thin British line fired one volley and broke; Procter fled the field. In the swamp, the fighting was fiercer and Tecumseh was killed — by whom remains disputed, Johnson himself being among the claimants. Once word of Tecumseh's death spread, Indigenous resistance ceased.

Aftermath

The battle restored American control of the Detroit frontier and ended the Anglo-Indigenous alliance in the Northwest. Tecumseh's confederacy, the great project of his life, disintegrated. Harrison's victory later propelled him to the presidency, with the campaign slogan drawing on his earlier Battle of Tippecanoe, and Johnson's claimed killing of Tecumseh helped make him Vice President.
