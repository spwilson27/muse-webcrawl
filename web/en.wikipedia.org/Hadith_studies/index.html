Hadith studies

Hadith studies (Arabic: علم الحديث, ʿilm al-ḥadīth, "science of hadith") is the academic and traditional religious discipline concerned with the hadith — reports of the words, actions and tacit approvals of the Islamic prophet Muhammad — including their collection, transmission, authentication and interpretation. Within Islam, hadith form the second source of law and guidance after the Quran, embodying the sunna (example) of the Prophet.

Methods

Classical scholarship evaluates each hadith on two components: the isnād, the chain of named transmitters, and the matn, the text itself. Critics developed an elaborate biographical science (ʿilm al-rijāl) to assess each transmitter's reliability, memory and contemporaneity with those they cited. Hadith are graded as ṣaḥīḥ (sound), ḥasan (good) or ḍaʿīf (weak), with further categories such as mutawātir (mass-transmitted) and āḥād (single-chain). Major Sunni collections include the Ṣaḥīḥ works of al-Bukhārī and Muslim and the four Sunan books; Shia Islam relies principally on its own "Four Books", such as al-Kulaynī's al-Kāfī.

Modern academic study

Western academic study, beginning with Ignác Goldziher and Joseph Schacht, raised sceptical questions about whether isnāds reliably reach back to the 7th century, proposing that many hadith reflect later legal and theological debates "projected back" onto the Prophet. Later scholars — among them Harald Motzki, using isnād-cum-matn analysis — have argued for earlier dating of substantial material. The field remains an active area of debate between traditional, sceptical and intermediate positions.
