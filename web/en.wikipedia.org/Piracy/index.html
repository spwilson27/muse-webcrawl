Piracy

Piracy on the high seas was a persistent threat to maritime trade

Piracy is an act of robbery or criminal violence by ship or boat-borne attackers upon another ship or a coastal area, typically with the goal of stealing cargo and other valuable goods. Those who conduct acts of piracy are called pirates, and vessels used for piracy are called pirate ships. The earliest documented instances of piracy were in the 14th century BC, when the Sea Peoples, a group of ocean raiders, attacked the ships of the Aegean and Mediterranean civilisations.

Under international law, piracy is defined — notably in the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS) — as illegal acts of violence or detention committed for private ends on the high seas or in a place outside the jurisdiction of any state. Pirates operating under a government licence were known as privateers, a distinction recognised in law until the nineteenth century.

History

Piracy flourished wherever valuable cargo moved by sea and naval power was weak. In the Mediterranean, Cilician pirates plagued Roman shipping until Pompey the Great suppressed them in 66 BC. Viking raiders combined trade and plunder in early medieval Europe. The so-called "Golden Age of Piracy" in the late 17th and early 18th centuries saw famous figures such as Blackbeard and Bartholomew Roberts operating in the Caribbean and Atlantic, often out of ports like Nassau during the era covered in maritime folklore and popular culture.

The Barbary corsairs of North Africa raided Mediterranean and even Atlantic shipping into the early 1800s. In East and Southeast Asia, wokou pirates and the great pirate confederations of the South China Sea, such as that of Ching Shih, commanded fleets of hundreds of junks. Concerted naval campaigns and steam warships largely suppressed classical piracy by the mid-nineteenth century.

Modern piracy

Piracy re-emerged as a serious problem in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries, most notoriously off the coast of Somalia from about 2005, where hijackings for ransom drew international naval patrols, and in the Strait of Malacca and the Gulf of Guinea. Anti-piracy measures include naval escorts, vessel hardening, citadels, and embarked armed guards. The term has also been extended by analogy to unauthorised copying of copyrighted works ("copyright piracy") and to pirate radio broadcasting.
