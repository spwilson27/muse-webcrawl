Dysexecutive syndrome

Dysexecutive syndrome (DES) is a pattern of impairments in executive functions — the cognitive processes responsible for planning, organizing, initiating, monitoring, and regulating goal-directed behavior. It is most often associated with damage to the frontal lobes, particularly the prefrontal cortex, though it can also arise from injury to connected subcortical structures and white-matter pathways.

Symptoms

People with dysexecutive syndrome may have difficulty planning and sequencing activities, poor judgment and insight, impulsivity or apathy, distractibility, perseveration (repeating responses), and problems shifting between tasks or rules. Emotional and personality changes are common: some patients become disinhibited and socially inappropriate, while others become flat and unmotivated. Paradoxically, patients may perform well on structured IQ tests yet fail badly in unstructured everyday life, where they must organize their own behavior.

Causes

Causes include traumatic brain injury, stroke, tumors, neurodegenerative disease (such as frontotemporal dementia), multiple sclerosis, and psychiatric conditions. The famous 19th-century case of Phineas Gage, whose personality changed after an iron rod destroyed part of his frontal lobes, is an early description of the syndrome.

Assessment and treatment

Assessment uses tests sensitive to executive function, such as the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test, verbal fluency, Trail Making, and questionnaires like the Dysexecutive Questionnaire (DEX) from the Behavioural Assessment of the Dysexecutive Syndrome (BADS) battery developed by Barbara Wilson and colleagues. Treatment emphasizes rehabilitation strategies: external structure, goal-management training, errorless learning, and compensatory aids rather than restoration of the damaged functions themselves.
