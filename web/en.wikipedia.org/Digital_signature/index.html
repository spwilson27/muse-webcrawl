Digital signature

A digital signature is a mathematical scheme for verifying the authenticity of digital messages or documents. A valid digital signature, where the prerequisites are satisfied, gives a recipient very strong reason to believe that the message was created by a known sender (authentication), that the sender cannot deny having sent the message (non-repudiation), and that the message was not altered in transit (integrity).

Digital signatures are a standard element of most cryptographic protocol suites, and are commonly used for software distribution, financial transactions, contract management software, and in other cases where it is important to detect forgery or tampering.

History

The concept was first described by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976. The RSA algorithm, published in 1977, provided the first practical implementation. Other schemes followed, including the Digital Signature Algorithm (DSA), standardised by NIST in 1994, and elliptic-curve variants such as ECDSA.

How it works

A signature scheme consists of three algorithms: key generation, which produces a private signing key and a public verification key; signing, which uses the private key and a cryptographic hash of the message to produce a signature; and verification, which uses the public key to check that the signature matches the message. Hashing the message first makes signing efficient and binds the signature to the exact content.

Legal status

Many jurisdictions grant digital signatures legal equivalence to handwritten signatures, including the United States (ESIGN Act, 2000), the European Union (eIDAS regulation, 2014), and India (Information Technology Act, 2000).
