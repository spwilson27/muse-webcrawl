The rise of the Ottoman Empire is the period, conventionally dated from about 1299 to 1453, in which a small Turkish frontier principality (beylik) in northwestern Anatolia grew into a major power spanning southeastern Europe and western Asia, culminating in the conquest of Constantinople by Mehmed II.

Foundations under Osman and Orhan

Around 1299 Osman I established an independent principality near Söğüt, on the border of the weakening Byzantine Empire. His son Orhan captured Bursa in 1326, making it the first Ottoman capital, and extended Ottoman control across northwestern Anatolia. Ottoman forces crossed into Europe in the 1350s, seizing Gallipoli, and under Murad I won major victories over Balkan coalitions at the Maritsa (1371) and Kosovo (1389), annexing much of Serbia and Bulgaria and moving the capital to Edirne (Adrianople).

Crisis and recovery

Expansion was nearly undone in 1402, when Timur (Tamerlane) crushed Sultan Bayezid I at the Battle of Ankara. Bayezid's sons fought an eleven-year civil war (the Ottoman Interregnum) before Mehmed I reunified the state. Under Murad II the empire recovered, defeating crusading armies at Varna (1444) and again at Kosovo (1448), helped by the new standing infantry corps, the Janissaries, and by gunpowder artillery.

Conquest of Constantinople

Mehmed II, "the Conqueror," besieged Constantinople in April–May 1453 with a huge army and the largest cannon yet built, cast by the founder Orban. The city fell on 29 May 1453, ending the Byzantine Empire; Mehmed made it his capital (later Istanbul) and styled himself Kayser-i Rûm, "Caesar of the Romans." The date is conventionally taken to end the Empire's rise and begin its classical age of further expansion under Selim I and Suleiman the Magnificent.

Factors in Ottoman success

Historians cite the beylik's frontier position attracting ghazi warriors, pragmatic tolerance and gradual incorporation of conquered peoples, an efficient military-fiscal state built on the timar land-grant system, and the fragmentation of its Byzantine, Balkan and Anatolian rivals.
