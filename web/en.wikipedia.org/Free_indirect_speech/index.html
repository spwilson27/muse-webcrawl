Free indirect speech
Also calledFree indirect discourse; style indirect libre
TypeNarrative technique
PioneersJane Austen, Gustave Flaubert
FeaturesThird person, past tense, character's idiom and deictics

Free indirect speech, also called free indirect discourse or style indirect libre, is a narrative technique in which a character's thoughts or speech are presented within third-person, past-tense narration, without quotation marks or reporting clauses, while retaining the character's own vocabulary, exclamations, and sense of "here" and "now". It blurs the boundary between narrator and character.

Example

Direct speech: She thought, "Tomorrow I will leave this dreadful place!" Indirect speech: She thought that she would leave that dreadful place the next day. Free indirect speech: Tomorrow she would leave this dreadful place! — the narration keeps third person and past tense but adopts her words ("this dreadful place") and her emotional emphasis.

History and use

Although anticipations occur earlier, the technique was developed extensively by Jane Austen, whose novels use it to render characters' consciousness with irony. Gustave Flaubert made it central to the modern novel in Madame Bovary (1857). It became a hallmark of literary modernism in the work of Virginia Woolf, James Joyce, and Katherine Mansfield, allowing seamless movement between external narration and interior life.

Effects

Free indirect speech creates intimacy with a character's viewpoint while preserving the narrator's grammatical frame, enabling irony when the character's self-deceptions are rendered in their own terms. Its ambiguity — whose voice is speaking? — has been a major topic in narratology.
