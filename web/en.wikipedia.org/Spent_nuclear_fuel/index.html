Spent nuclear fuel

Spent nuclear fuel, occasionally called used nuclear fuel, is nuclear fuel that has been irradiated in a nuclear reactor (usually at a nuclear power plant) and has reached the end of its useful life because the buildup of fission products and depletion of fissile material make it inefficient. It remains highly radioactive and thermally hot, requiring cooling and shielding for years and isolation for far longer.

Spent nuclear fuel
Typical composition: ~95% uranium, ~1% plutonium, ~4% fission products and minor actinides
Initial storage: spent fuel pools
Long-term options: dry cask storage, reprocessing, deep geological repository

Characteristics

When discharged, a fuel assembly's radioactivity is dominated by short-lived fission products and it generates substantial decay heat. After a year the heat and activity have fallen dramatically, but isotopes such as caesium-137 and strontium-90 (half-lives about 30 years) and long-lived actinides like plutonium-239 make the material hazardous for thousands of years.

Storage

Newly discharged fuel is stored under water in spent fuel pools at the reactor site for several years of cooling. It can then be transferred to dry cask storage — sealed steel canisters in concrete overpacks. The 2011 Fukushima Daiichi accident, in which a spent fuel pool lost cooling, focused worldwide attention on pool safety and accelerated transfers to dry storage in several countries.

Reprocessing and disposal

Some countries (France, Russia, Japan) reprocess spent fuel to recover uranium and plutonium for reuse, notably as MOX fuel, while vitrifying the remaining high-level waste. Others, including the United States, plan direct disposal in deep geological repositories; Finland's Onkalo repository became the world's first licensed deep disposal facility for spent fuel, while the proposed Yucca Mountain repository in the US was never completed.
