In monotheistic belief systems, God is usually viewed as the supreme being, creator, and principal object of faith. In the Abrahamic religions — Judaism, Christianity, and Islam — God is conceived as eternal, transcendent yet involved with creation, and the source of moral obligation. God is usually conceived of as being omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, and omnibenevolent, as well as having an eternal and necessary existence.

Conceptions

Conceptions of God vary widely. Classical theism, developed by thinkers such as Augustine, Maimonides, Avicenna and Aquinas, describes God as simple, immutable, impassible and timeless. Other conceptions include deism (a creator who does not intervene), pantheism (God identical with the universe), panentheism, and the personal God of popular devotion who hears prayer and acts in history. In Islam, God (Allah) is strictly one (tawhid) and incomparable; in Judaism, God is the covenant partner of Israel; in Christianity, God is a Trinity of Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

Arguments for and against

Philosophers have debated God's existence for millennia. Arguments in favor include the cosmological argument (from the existence and contingency of the universe), the teleological argument (from apparent design, classically William Paley's watchmaker), the ontological argument (Anselm), and arguments from morality and religious experience. Arguments against include the problem of evil — how a wholly good, all-powerful God permits suffering — and critiques of divine hiddenness, as well as naturalistic explanations advanced by atheism. Positions on the question include theism, atheism, agnosticism, and ignosticism.

Names and worship

God is addressed by many names, including Yahweh and the Tetragrammaton in Judaism, the Father in Christianity, and Allah with 99 names in Islam. Worship practices include prayer, liturgy, sacrifice historically, meditation, and ethical obedience, conducted in synagogues, churches, mosques and homes.
