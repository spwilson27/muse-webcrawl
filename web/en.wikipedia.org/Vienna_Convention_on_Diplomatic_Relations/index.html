SubjectDiplomatic relations and immunity

The Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations of 1961 is an international treaty that defines a framework for diplomatic relations between independent countries. It specifies the privileges of a diplomatic mission that enable diplomats to perform their function without fear of coercion or harassment by the host country, forming the legal basis for diplomatic immunity. The Convention was adopted on 18 April 1961 in Vienna and entered into force on 24 April 1964; with 193 parties, it is among the most widely ratified of all treaties and is largely regarded as codifying customary international law.

Key provisions

The Convention provides that diplomatic agents are inviolable and immune from criminal jurisdiction, and generally from civil and administrative jurisdiction, of the receiving state (Articles 29 and 31). The premises of a mission are inviolable and may not be entered by the host state without consent (Article 22), and the mission's archives, official correspondence and the diplomatic bag are likewise protected (Articles 24 and 27). Diplomats are exempt from most taxation (Article 34) and from public service and military obligations.

In return, diplomats must respect the laws of the receiving state and must not interfere in its internal affairs (Article 41). A diplomat may be declared persona non grata, in which case the sending state must recall them (Article 9). The Convention also covers the establishment of missions by mutual consent, the functions of a mission, classes of heads of mission, accreditation, and the protection of missions in armed conflict or when relations are broken off.

Related instruments

The Convention is complemented by the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963), which governs consular services and affords consular officers a narrower, function-based immunity. An Optional Protocol concerning the Compulsory Settlement of Disputes allows cases to be brought before the International Court of Justice, which has applied the Convention in cases such as United States Diplomatic and Consular Staff in Tehran (1980).
