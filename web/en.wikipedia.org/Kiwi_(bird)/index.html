Kiwi (Māori: kiwi) are flightless birds endemic to New Zealand, belonging to the genus Apteryx in the family Apterygidae. About the size of a domestic chicken, kiwi are the smallest living ratites (the group that includes ostriches, emus and rheas). They are a national symbol of New Zealand, and New Zealanders are widely nicknamed "Kiwis".

Kiwi are unique among birds in several respects: they have nostrils at the very tip of their long bills, an exceptionally keen sense of smell, tiny vestigial wings hidden in their plumage, no tail, and hair-like feathers. Females lay the largest egg relative to body size of any bird — a single egg may weigh around 20 per cent of the female's body mass.

Species

Five species are generally recognised:

  • Great spotted kiwi (Apteryx haastii)
  • Little spotted kiwi (Apteryx owenii)
  • Rowi or Okarito kiwi (Apteryx rowi)
  • Tokoeka (Apteryx australis)
  • North Island brown kiwi (Apteryx mantelli)

Evolution

Kiwi belong to the palaeognath birds. DNA studies suggest their closest relatives were the extinct elephant birds of Madagascar, indicating that the ancestors of kiwi may have flown to New Zealand (or reached it via Antarctica) rather than walking across Gondwana. Fossil kiwi-like birds, such as Proapteryx, are known from the Miocene of Otago.

Behaviour and ecology

Kiwi are nocturnal and largely flightless, sheltering by day in burrows and emerging at night to probe the leaf litter and soil for invertebrates — earthworms, insect larvae, spiders and fallen fruit. They have excellent hearing and smell but poor eyesight. Most species are monogamous, forming pair bonds that may last for decades. In most species the male alone incubates the egg for around 75–85 days, one of the longest incubation periods of any bird. Chicks hatch fully feathered and independent, receiving no feeding from their parents.

Conservation

Before human settlement, kiwi numbered in the millions. Habitat clearance and, above all, predation by introduced mammals — stoats, ferrets, dogs, cats and brushtail possums — have reduced numbers drastically; unmanaged chick survival can be as low as 5 per cent. All five species are classified as vulnerable or endangered to varying degrees. Conservation measures include predator control, predator-free offshore island sanctuaries, and "Operation Nest Egg", in which eggs are collected, hatched in captivity and the juveniles released once large enough to defend themselves against stoats.

Cultural significance

The kiwi has been a symbol of New Zealand since the 19th century, appearing on coins, stamps, military insignia and the logo of the Royal New Zealand Air Force. Its image is so closely associated with the country that "Kiwi" is an internationally recognised nickname for New Zealanders.
