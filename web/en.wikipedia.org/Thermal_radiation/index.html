Thermal radiation

TypeElectromagnetic radiation
Governing lawsPlanck's law, Stefan–Boltzmann law, Wien's displacement law, Kirchhoff's law

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic radiation emitted by the thermal motion of particles in matter. All matter with a temperature greater than absolute zero emits thermal radiation. The radiation is a conversion of a body's thermal energy into electromagnetic energy, and is propagated without a material medium, including through vacuum.

Properties

The spectrum and intensity of thermal radiation depend on the body's temperature and surface properties. An idealized emitter, a black body, emits radiation described by Planck's law; its total power output follows the Stefan–Boltzmann law (proportional to the fourth power of absolute temperature), and the peak wavelength shifts with temperature according to Wien's displacement law — which is why heated objects progress from dull red to orange, yellow, and white as they get hotter. Real surfaces emit a fraction of blackbody radiation determined by their emissivity, and by Kirchhoff's law good absorbers are good emitters at each wavelength.

Applications

Thermal radiation dominates heat transfer at high temperatures and across vacuum. It underlies infrared thermography and remote temperature sensing, the Earth's energy balance and greenhouse effect, incandescent lighting, radiative cooling and heating in engineering, and the thermal design of spacecraft, which can shed heat only by radiation.
