United States v. Carroll Towing Co.

CourtUnited States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Full nameUnited States v. Carroll Towing Co., Inc., et al.
DecidedJanuary 9, 1947
Citation159 F.2d 169
JudgesLearned Hand, Chief Judge
KeywordsNegligence; burden of precaution; B < PL formula

United States v. Carroll Towing Co., 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947), is a landmark decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, written by Judge Learned Hand. The case is famous for articulating an algebraic test for negligence — the "Hand formula" or "B < PL" formula — under which a party is negligent if the burden of adequate precautions (B) is less than the probability of harm (P) multiplied by the gravity of the resulting injury (L).

Facts

In January 1944, during the Second World War, the barge Anna C was moored at a pier in New York Harbor along with several other barges. The tug Carroll, owned by the Carroll Towing Company, was sent to remove another vessel from the string. In the process the moorings were disturbed; the Anna C, which had been taking on water and had no bargee aboard during working hours, broke adrift, struck a tanker, and sank with its cargo of flour, which belonged to the United States.

Holding

Judge Hand held that the barge owner was contributorily negligent in failing to have a bargee aboard during the daytime, reducing the owner's recovery. In reaching this conclusion he wrote that since navigating waters crowded with barge traffic involved risks, the owner's duty was a function of three variables: (1) the probability that the barge would break away; (2) the gravity of the resulting injury; and (3) the burden of adequate precautions. "Possibly it serves to bring this notion into relief to state it in algebraic terms: if the probability be called P; the injury, L; and the burden, B; liability depends upon whether B is less than L multiplied by P: i.e., whether B < PL."

Significance

The Hand formula became a cornerstone of the law-and-economics analysis of tort law, central to the work of scholars such as Guido Calabresi and Richard Posner, and it remains one of the most frequently cited formulations of the negligence standard in American law.
