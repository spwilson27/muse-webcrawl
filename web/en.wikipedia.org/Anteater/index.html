Anteaters are the four extant mammal species in the suborder Vermilingua ("worm tongue"), part of the order Pilosa alongside the sloths. They are known for feeding almost exclusively on ants and termites. The four species are the giant anteater (Myrmecophaga tridactyla), the silky anteater (Cyclopes didactylus), and the northern and southern tamanduas (Tamandua tetradactyla and Tamandua mexicana).

Characteristics

Anteaters have elongated heads and snouts, small eyes, and no teeth. Their long, sticky tongues — in the giant anteater up to 60 cm (24 in) long and capable of flicking in and out as many as 150 times per minute — are used to lap up insects by the thousands each day. Powerful forelegs armed with large claws open concrete-hard termite mounds; the giant anteater walks on its knuckles to keep its claws sharp. Anteaters have low body temperatures for placental mammals, around 32.7 °C (90.9 °F), and correspondingly low metabolic rates.

Distribution and behaviour

Anteaters are native to Central and South America, inhabiting grasslands, savannas and rainforests. The giant anteater is terrestrial, the silky anteater almost entirely arboreal and nocturnal, and the tamanduas both terrestrial and arboreal. Anteaters are generally solitary except for mothers with young; pups ride on the mother's back. Their predators include jaguars and pumas. The giant anteater is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN, threatened by habitat loss and roadkill.

Evolution

Anteaters belong to the superorder Xenarthra, an ancient lineage of placental mammals that diverged roughly 59 million years ago. Their closest living relatives are the sloths, with which they form the order Pilosa; armadillos are the other main xenarthran group.
