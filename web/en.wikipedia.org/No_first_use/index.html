In nuclear strategy, no first use (NFU) refers to a pledge or policy by a nuclear-armed state not to use nuclear weapons as a means of warfare unless first attacked by an adversary using nuclear weapons. The concept also extends in some doctrines to a pledge not to use or threaten nuclear weapons against non-nuclear states. NFU contrasts with policies that reserve the option of nuclear first use in response to conventional attack or other extreme circumstances.

No first use: a commitment to employ nuclear weapons only in retaliation

Countries with NFU policies

China declared a no first use policy immediately after its first nuclear test in 1964, pledging not to be the first to use nuclear weapons at any time or under any circumstances, and has repeatedly reaffirmed the commitment. India adopted NFU after its 1998 tests and formalized it in its 2003 nuclear doctrine, though it reserves retaliation for major chemical or biological attacks and Indian officials have periodically debated the policy. North Korea announced in 2016 that it would not use nuclear weapons first unless its sovereignty was threatened by hostile forces with nuclear weapons.

Countries without NFU

The United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, and Pakistan have declined to adopt NFU, maintaining deliberate ambiguity about first use to deter conventional or other attacks. NATO strategy during and after the Cold War relied on the possibility of nuclear escalation in response to overwhelming conventional attack. The Soviet Union pledged no first use in 1982, but Russia abandoned the pledge in 1993.

Debate

Supporters argue NFU reduces the risk of accidental or mistaken nuclear war, strengthens the nuclear taboo, and is credible because nuclear forces structured for retaliation can still deter. Critics contend that declaratory pledges may not be believed in a crisis, and that abandoning first-use options could weaken deterrence of large-scale conventional, chemical, or biological attack against one's country or allies.
