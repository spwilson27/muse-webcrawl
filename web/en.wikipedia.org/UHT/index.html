Ultra-high-temperature processing

Ultra-high-temperature processing (UHT), also known as ultra-heat treatment or ultra-pasteurisation, is a food processing technology that sterilises liquid food by heating it above 135 °C (275 °F) — the temperature required to kill bacterial endospores — for 2 to 5 seconds. The heat used in the process can cause Maillard browning and change the taste and smell of dairy products.

UHT is most commonly used in milk production, but the process is also applied to fruit juices, cream, soy milk, wine, soups, honey and stews. UHT milk was first developed in the 1960s and became generally available in the 1970s. When packaged aseptically, UHT products can remain shelf-stable for six to nine months without refrigeration, until opened.

Process

Two main methods are used:

  • Direct heating — steam is injected into the product, or the product is infused into steam, rapidly raising its temperature; the added water is later removed by flash cooling in a vacuum.
  • Indirect heating — the product is heated through a heat exchanger, most often a plate heat exchanger, without direct contact with steam.

After heating, the product is flash-cooled and aseptically packaged, typically in cartons lined with layers of paperboard, polyethylene and aluminium foil (such as Tetra Pak cartons), which protect the contents from light and oxygen.

Comparison with pasteurisation

Conventional (HTST or "flash") pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds, killing pathogenic bacteria but not spores, so the milk must be refrigerated and keeps for only a few weeks. UHT treatment achieves commercial sterility, allowing ambient storage at the cost of a slightly "cooked" flavour, caused by changes to milk proteins and the caramelisation of some lactose, and modest losses of some heat-sensitive vitamins.

Use worldwide

UHT milk accounts for the majority of milk consumed in many European countries — including France, Spain, Portugal and Belgium — while standard pasteurised milk predominates in the United Kingdom, Ireland, the United States and Australia. Its long shelf life makes UHT milk valuable in regions with limited refrigeration or long supply chains.
