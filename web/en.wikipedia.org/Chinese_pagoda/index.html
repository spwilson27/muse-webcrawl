Chinese pagoda

A Chinese pagoda (Chinese: 塔; pinyin: tǎ) is a tiered tower with multiple eaves common to China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and other parts of East Asia. Most pagodas were built for religious, usually Buddhist, purposes — to house relics and sacred writings — though some served as watchtowers, landmarks, or feng shui markers. The form evolved from the Indian stupa combined with Chinese Han-dynasty watchtower architecture.

History

Pagodas appeared in China during the Eastern Han dynasty as Buddhism spread along the Silk Road. Early examples were wooden towers modelled on native multi-storeyed watchtowers, topped with the rings of the stupa. Brick and stone construction became common from the Tang and Song dynasties for durability. Surviving masterpieces include the Songyue Temple Pagoda (523 CE), the oldest extant brick pagoda in China; the Giant Wild Goose Pagoda in Xi'an (652); and the timber Yingxian Pagoda (1056), the world's tallest wooden pagoda.

Structure

A pagoda's storeys decrease in size as they rise, each defined by a projecting roof or eave ring. Storey counts are usually odd numbers, reflecting yang symbolism. Some pagodas have a solid core, others internal staircases to a viewing platform. Decorative finials (xianglun) crown the roof, preserving the symbolism of the stupa's spire.

Spread

From China the pagoda form spread to Japan (where it became the five-storeyed gojū-no-tō), Korea, and Vietnam, each culture adapting proportions and materials to local conditions and seismic concerns.
