Robert I (11 July 1274 – 7 June 1329), popularly known as Robert the Bruce, was King of Scots from 1306 until his death in 1329. One of the most famous warriors of his generation, he led Scotland during the First War of Scottish Independence against England, most famously defeating the English at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314. He is regarded in Scotland as a national hero.

Background and early life

Robert de Brus was born at Turnberry Castle in Ayrshire, heir to the lordship of Annandale and, through his mother Marjorie, to the earldom of Carrick. The Bruce family, of Norman descent, held a claim to the Scottish throne through descent from David I. When the succession crisis of 1290–92 (the "Great Cause") ended with Edward I of England installing John Balliol as king, the Bruces were passed over. After Balliol's humiliation and deposition in 1296, Edward attempted to rule Scotland directly, provoking the Wars of Independence.

Robert's early career was ambiguous: he at times supported William Wallace's resistance, at times submitted to Edward I, and served as a Guardian of Scotland alongside his rival John Comyn.

The road to the throne

On 10 February 1306, Robert met John Comyn in the church of the Greyfriars at Dumfries; the meeting ended in Comyn's killing — an act of sacrilege and murder that left Robert no path but rebellion. Six weeks later, on 25 March 1306, he was crowned King of Scots at Scone. Edward I responded with force: Robert's army was defeated at Methven, his wife and daughter were captured, and several of his brothers were executed. Robert himself fled, spending the winter of 1306–07 as a fugitive, possibly on Rathlin Island or in the Hebrides.

Guerrilla war and recovery

Returning to mainland Scotland in 1307, Robert began a campaign of guerrilla warfare, defeating English forces at Glen Trool and Loudoun Hill. The death of Edward I in 1307 and the accession of the less capable Edward II gave him breathing space. Between 1308 and 1313 he and his captains — notably his brother Edward Bruce, James Douglas, and Thomas Randolph — systematically captured English-held castles, usually by surprise escalade, and demolished them so they could not be re-garrisoned. By 1314 only Stirling Castle, Bothwell, and Berwick remained significant English strongholds in the south.

Bannockburn

When Edward II marched north in the summer of 1314 with a large army to relieve Stirling, Robert met him at the Battle of Bannockburn on 23–24 June 1314. Choosing ground carefully south of Stirling, Robert's schiltron formations of spearmen, supported by a small cavalry contingent and the terrain of the Bannock Burn, destroyed the English army in one of the most celebrated victories in Scottish history. Edward II fled the field and barely escaped capture.

Later reign

After Bannockburn, Scottish raids deep into northern England and Edward Bruce's campaign in Ireland (1315–18) pressed England toward negotiation. In 1320 the Scottish barons sent the Declaration of Arbroath to the Pope, asserting Scotland's independence and Robert's kingship. Pope John XXII eventually recognised Robert as king, and in 1328 England concluded the Treaty of Edinburgh–Northampton, formally recognising Robert I as King of Scots and Scotland as fully independent.

Death and legacy

Robert died on 7 June 1329 at Cardross, probably of leprosy according to contemporary accounts, though modern scholars have suggested other causes. His body was buried in Dunfermline Abbey; his heart was embalmed and carried on crusade by James Douglas, who died fighting in Spain in 1330, and was later buried at Melrose Abbey. Robert was succeeded by his young son, David II. His reputation as the restorer of Scottish independence has endured for seven centuries, and he remains one of the defining figures of Scottish national identity.
