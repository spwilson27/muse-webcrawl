Carrington Event

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Carrington Event was the most intense geomagnetic storm in recorded history, occurring on 1–2 September 1859 during solar cycle 10. It was caused by a powerful coronal mass ejection from the Sun that struck Earth's magnetosphere, producing spectacular aurorae visible as far south as the Caribbean and causing telegraph systems across Europe and North America to fail — some operators received electric shocks, and telegraph paper caught fire. Several systems reportedly continued to send and receive messages with their power supplies disconnected, running on aurora-induced current alone.

The solar flare

On 1 September 1859, English astronomer Richard Carrington was sketching sunspots when he witnessed a sudden, intense brightening — the first solar flare ever observed — which he reported to the Royal Astronomical Society. Richard Hodgson independently saw the same event. Roughly 17.6 hours later, the associated coronal mass ejection reached Earth, an exceptionally fast transit that suggested the interplanetary medium had been cleared by an earlier ejection.

Effects

Magnetometers worldwide recorded extreme disturbances; the Bombay (Colaba) observatory measured a horizontal field decrease of roughly 1,600 nT. Auroras were reported in Hawaii, Cuba, Mexico and Queensland, and gold miners in the Rocky Mountains reportedly woke and prepared breakfast in the middle of the night, mistaking the bright aurora for dawn. Ice-core evidence suggests an event of this magnitude occurs roughly once every 500 years, although later storms (May 1921, March 1989) have caused significant regional damage.

Modern significance

A Carrington-class storm striking today could damage satellite electronics, GPS timing, aviation communications and — through geomagnetically induced currents — high-voltage power transformers, potentially causing prolonged regional blackouts. Studies by insurers and space-weather agencies cite possible economic losses in the trillions of dollars, making solar storm forecasting and grid hardening an active policy concern. In July 2012 a coronal mass ejection of comparable strength crossed Earth's orbit nine days after the planet had passed the spot, a widely cited near-miss.
