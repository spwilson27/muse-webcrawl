Scottish literature

Scottish literature is the literature written in Scotland or by Scottish writers, in English, Scots, Scottish Gaelic, Latin and other languages. It stretches from early medieval Gaelic and Latin chronicles through the poetry of the makars to the world-famous works of the Enlightenment and beyond.

History

Medieval Scotland produced major poets such as Robert Henryson and William Dunbar, and the epic verse of Blind Harry's The Wallace. In the eighteenth century, Robert Burns became Scotland's national poet, writing in Scots, while Allan Ramsay revived vernacular verse.

Sir Walter Scott effectively invented the modern historical novel with works such as Waverley (1814) and Ivanhoe, achieving immense international fame. Later in the century, Robert Louis Stevenson wrote Treasure Island and Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, and Arthur Conan Doyle created Sherlock Holmes.

Modern literature

The twentieth-century Scottish Renaissance, led by Hugh MacDiarmid, sought to revive Scots as a literary language. Contemporary Scottish writers include Irvine Welsh (Trainspotting), Ian Rankin, Muriel Spark and Alasdair Gray.
