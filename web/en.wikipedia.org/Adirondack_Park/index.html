The Adirondack Park is a park in northeastern New York protecting the Adirondack Mountains and surrounding lands. Created by the state legislature in 1892 out of concern for the region's water and timber resources, it covers some 6.1 million acres — larger than Yellowstone, Yosemite, Grand Canyon, Glacier and Great Smoky Mountains national parks combined — and is the largest publicly protected area in the contiguous United States.

Uniquely, roughly half the park is privately owned: it contains about 130,000 year-round residents, hamlets, farms, timberlands and resorts, interwoven with the state-owned Forest Preserve. The Forest Preserve lands are protected by Article XIV of the New York State Constitution — the famous "forever wild" clause, adopted in 1894 — which requires that they "be forever kept as wild forest lands" and that timber on them not be sold or cut. Land use in the park is overseen by the Adirondack Park Agency, created in 1971, whose zoning of private land has been both a model for and a flashpoint in American land-use planning.

Geography and recreation

The park includes the High Peaks region, crowned by Mount Marcy, New York's highest summit; more than 2,000 lakes and ponds; and thousands of miles of hiking trails and canoe routes, including the Saranac Lakes and Lake Placid, host of the 1932 and 1980 Winter Olympics. Forty-six High Peaks rise above 4,000 feet, and hikers who climb all of them are known as "Forty-Sixers". The park is a UNESCO-recognised part of the Champlain–Adirondack Biosphere Reserve.
