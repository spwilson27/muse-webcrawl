Field: Partial differential equations; computational fluid dynamics
Named after: Bernhard Riemann

Riemann problem

A Riemann problem is a fundamental initial-value problem in the theory of conservation laws: a hyperbolic system of partial differential equations together with piecewise constant initial data having a single jump discontinuity. Named after Bernhard Riemann, who studied related problems in gas dynamics, it serves as a building block for understanding shock waves, rarefaction waves, and contact discontinuities.

Structure of the solution

For the Euler equations of compressible flow, the solution consists of waves separating constant states: typically a shock or rarefaction wave on each side and a contact discontinuity in the middle across which pressure and velocity are continuous but density jumps. The classic example is the shock tube (Sod) problem, in which a diaphragm separating high- and low-pressure gas is suddenly removed.

Numerical methods

Riemann problems are central to modern computational fluid dynamics: Godunov-type finite-volume schemes compute fluxes at cell interfaces by solving (exactly or approximately) a Riemann problem at every step. Approximate Riemann solvers, such as those of Roe and Harten–Lax–van Leer (HLL), are standard tools in aerodynamics and astrophysics codes.
