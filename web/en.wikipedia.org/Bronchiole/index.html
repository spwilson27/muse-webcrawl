The bronchioles are the smaller branches of the bronchial airways in the lower respiratory tract. They include the terminal bronchioles and finally the respiratory bronchioles that mark the start of the respiratory zone delivering air to the gas-exchanging units of the lungs, the alveoli. Bronchioles are distinguished from bronchi by the absence of cartilage and submucosal glands in their walls.

Structure

As bronchi branch repeatedly within the lungs, their diameter decreases and cartilage disappears; passages less than about 1 mm in diameter are termed bronchioles. Their walls contain smooth muscle that regulates airflow: contraction causes bronchoconstriction and relaxation bronchodilation. The epithelium is lined with ciliated cells and club cells (formerly Clara cells), which secrete protective components of the epithelial lining fluid.

Types

Conducting bronchioles, including terminal bronchioles, carry air without participating in gas exchange. Respiratory bronchioles have alveoli budding from their walls and so mark the transition to the respiratory zone, continuing into alveolar ducts and alveolar sacs.

Clinical significance

Bronchioles are the principal site of airflow obstruction in asthma, where smooth-muscle spasm, inflammation, and mucus narrow the lumen. Inflammation of the bronchioles is called bronchiolitis, most commonly caused in infants by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). Obliterative bronchiolitis is a serious complication of lung transplantation.
