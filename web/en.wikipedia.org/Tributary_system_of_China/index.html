Tributary system of China

The tributary system of China (simplified Chinese: 中华朝贡体系; traditional Chinese: 中華朝貢體系), or Cefeng system (册封体制) was a network of loose international relations centered around China which facilitated trade and foreign relations by acknowledging China's predominant role in East Asia. It emerged during the Han dynasty and was fully developed by the Ming dynasty. Participants in the tributary system were ruled by the concept of Sinocentrism, under which China was the cultural and political centre of the world. Other states, known as vassals to the Chinese system, had to send tribute missions and acknowledge the supremacy of the Chinese emperor in return for protection and trading rights.

Operation

Tributary states sent periodic missions bearing gifts (tribute) to the Chinese court, performed ritual obeisances such as the kowtow, and received in return imperial investiture and gifts often more valuable than the tribute itself, along with privileged access to Chinese markets. Major tributary relationships included Korea (Joseon), Vietnam, the Ryukyu Kingdom, and at times Japan, Siam, and various Central Asian states, though the economic and political substance of each relationship varied considerably.

Interpretation

The concept was systematised by John King Fairbank and other scholars in the mid-20th century to describe a distinctively Chinese world order. Later historians have debated how unified, hierarchical, or merely rhetorical the "system" was in practice. The system effectively ended in the late 19th century as China was integrated into the Westphalian state system following the Opium Wars and the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95).
