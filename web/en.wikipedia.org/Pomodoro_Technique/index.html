Pomodoro Technique

The Pomodoro Technique is a time management method developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s. It uses a kitchen timer to break work into intervals, typically 25 minutes in length, separated by short breaks. Each interval is known as a pomodoro, from the Italian word for tomato, after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer Cirillo used as a university student.

Method

The technique comprises six steps: decide on the task to be done; set the pomodoro timer (typically 25 minutes); work on the task until the timer rings; end work when the timer rings and take a short break (typically 5–10 minutes); then go back to step two and repeat until four pomodoros have been completed; finally, after the fourth pomodoro, take a longer break (typically 20–30 minutes) and reset the count.

Principles

The aims of the technique are to reduce the impact of internal and external interruptions on focus and flow, to provide an effective planning tool, and to make time an ally in estimation rather than a source of anxiety. Interruptions arising during a pomodoro are recorded ("inform, negotiate, schedule, call back") rather than acted upon. A pomodoro is considered indivisible: if it is interrupted and cannot be resumed, it is voided.

Tools and adoption

Originally practised with a mechanical kitchen timer, pencil and paper, the method is now supported by numerous software timers and apps, and is widely used by students, programmers and remote workers. It is frequently combined with other methods such as timeboxing and the Getting Things Done workflow.
