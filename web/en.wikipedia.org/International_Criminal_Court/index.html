The International Criminal Court (ICC) is an intergovernmental organisation and international tribunal seated in The Hague, Netherlands. It is the first and only permanent international court with jurisdiction to prosecute individuals for the international crimes of genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression. It began functioning on 1 July 2002, when the Rome Statute entered into force.

History and structure

The idea of a permanent criminal court followed the ad hoc tribunals at Nuremberg and Tokyo and, in the 1990s, for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda. The Rome Statute was adopted on 17 July 1998 by 120 states. The ICC is a court of last resort: under the principle of complementarity, it acts only when national courts are unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute. Cases reach the court by referral from a state party, from the UN Security Council, or by the prosecutor acting proprio motu with judicial authorisation. The court has 18 judges, an Office of the Prosecutor, and a Registry; its Assembly of States Parties oversees governance and budget.

Cases and controversies

Early situations concerned Uganda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Central African Republic and Darfur (Sudan, a Security Council referral). Thomas Lubanga (2012) was the first person convicted, for enlisting child soldiers; later convictions included Germain Katanga, Ahmad al-Mahdi and Bosco Ntaganda. Arrest warrants have been issued for serving heads of state including Sudan's Omar al-Bashir (2009) and Russia's Vladimir Putin (2023, over deportation of Ukrainian children), and in 2024 for Israeli and Hamas leaders in the Gaza war situation — moves that drew sharp political reactions. The United States, Russia, China, India and Israel are not members; Burundi and the Philippines withdrew. Critics have charged the court with slowness, high cost, selective focus on Africa, and dependence on state cooperation for arrests.
