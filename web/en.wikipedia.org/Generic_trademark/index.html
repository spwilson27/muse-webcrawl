A generic trademark, also known as a genericized trademark or proprietary eponym, is a trademark or brand name that, because of its popularity or significance, has become the generic term for, or synonymous with, a general class of products or services, usually against the intentions of the trademark's owner.

Former trademarks such as "aspirin" and "escalator" became generic terms in many jurisdictions.

A trademark is said to be "genericized" when it begins as a distinctive product identifier but changes in meaning to become generic. This typically happens when the products or services with which the trademark is associated have acquired substantial market dominance or mind share, such that the primary meaning of the genericized trademark becomes the product or service itself rather than an indication of source. A company may lose its trademark rights if it fails to police its mark properly; however, genericization can occur even where the owner campaigns against it.

Examples

Well-known examples of marks that became generic in at least some jurisdictions include:

  • Aspirin — originally a Bayer trademark for acetylsalicylic acid; declared generic in the United States in 1921, though it remains protected in many other countries.
  • Escalator — a trademark of the Otis Elevator Company, lost in the United States in 1950.
  • Thermos — declared generic in the United States in 1963.
  • Heroin — originally a Bayer trademark.
  • Cellophane, dry ice, trampoline, zipper, kerosene and videotape also began as trademarks or brand-associated terms.

Other marks, such as Band-Aid, Kleenex, Google (as a verb) and Xerox, are sometimes used generically in everyday speech but remain legally protected trademarks; their owners actively campaign to keep the public using them as adjectives ("Kleenex brand tissues") rather than nouns.

Legal doctrine

Under the United States Lanham Act, a registration may be cancelled when the mark becomes the generic name for the goods or services for which it is registered. Courts apply the "primary significance" test: if the primary significance of the term to the relevant public is the product category rather than the producer, the mark is generic. European Union law similarly permits revocation where, in consequence of the proprietor's acts or inactivity, the mark has become the common name in the trade for a product or service.

Protection against genericization

Trademark owners fight genericide by using the mark as an adjective followed by a generic noun, running corrective advertising (Xerox's famous campaign: "You can't Xerox a Xerox"), using the ® symbol, and objecting to dictionary entries and journalistic usage that treat the mark as a common noun or verb.
