The Spanish Army (Spanish: Ejército de Tierra, lit. 'Land Army') is the land warfare branch of the Spanish Armed Forces. One of the oldest standing armies in the world, it dates its continuous existence to the reign of the Catholic Monarchs in the late 15th century. The Army is responsible for land-based military operations and, together with the Spanish Navy and the Spanish Air and Space Force, defends the sovereignty and territorial integrity of Spain.

History

The unified Spanish Army emerged from the amalgamation of the forces of Castile and Aragon under Ferdinand and Isabella. During the 16th and early 17th centuries the Spanish tercios — combined-arms infantry formations of pikemen, swordsmen and arquebusiers — dominated European battlefields, fighting in Italy, the Low Countries, France and the Mediterranean. The Spanish Road supplied these armies in Flanders from Spanish Italy.

Spanish military power declined relative to France in the later 17th century, and the army was reorganised along French lines under the Bourbon monarchy in the 18th century. Spain fought in the Peninsular War against Napoleon, the Carlist Wars, colonial campaigns in Africa, and the Spanish–American War of 1898. The Army was central to the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), after which it formed the backbone of the Franco regime. Following the transition to democracy, Spain professionalised the force, ending conscription in 2001.

Modern role

The modern Ejército de Tierra numbers roughly 70,000–80,000 personnel. It contributes to NATO missions and has deployed to the Balkans, Afghanistan, Iraq, Lebanon and Mali. Notable components include the Legion (La Legión), the Paratrooper Brigade, mountain troops, and the garrisons of Ceuta and Melilla.

Organisation

The Army is headed by the Chief of Staff of the Army (JEME), based at the Buenavista Palace in Madrid. Its operational structure includes the Land Force headquarters in Seville, divisional and brigade commands, and support commands for logistics, training and doctrine.
