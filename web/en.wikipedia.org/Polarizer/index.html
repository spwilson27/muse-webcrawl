A polarizer or polariser is an optical filter that lets light waves of a specific polarization pass through while blocking light waves of other polarizations. It can filter a beam of light of undefined or mixed polarization into a beam of well-defined polarization, known as polarized light. Common types of polarizers are linear polarizers and circular polarizers.

Polarizers are used in many optical techniques and instruments. Polarizing filters find applications in photography and in liquid crystal display (LCD) technology. Polarizers for visible light can be made from a birefringent material such as calcite, or from polymer sheets containing oriented iodine-doped polyvinyl alcohol chains, as in the Polaroid sheet invented by Edwin Land.

Linear polarizers

Linear polarizers can be divided into two general categories: absorptive polarizers, where unwanted polarization states are absorbed by the device, and beam-splitting polarizers, where the unpolarized beam is split into two beams with opposite polarization states. The simplest example of an absorptive polarizer is a wire-grid polarizer, consisting of many thin parallel wires; electromagnetic radiation that is polarized along the wires is reflected or absorbed, while radiation polarized perpendicular to the wires passes through.

Circular polarizers

A circular polarizer can be created with a linear polarizer and a quarter-wave plate. Unpolarized light entering the system is first linearly polarized; the quarter-wave plate, oriented at 45 degrees to the polarization axis, converts it into circularly polarized light. Circular polarizers are used in 3D cinema glasses and in photography to reduce glare while avoiding the darkening of autofocus and metering sensors.

Malus's law

When a perfect polarizer is placed in a polarized beam of light, the irradiance transmitted obeys Malus's law: I = I₀ cos² θ, where I₀ is the initial intensity and θ is the angle between the light's initial polarization direction and the axis of the polarizer. When two ideal polarizers are crossed at 90 degrees, essentially no light is transmitted.

Applications

Polarizing sunglasses reduce glare reflected from horizontal surfaces such as water and roads. In photography, polarizing filters darken skies, manage reflections, and enhance color saturation. Stress analysis uses polariscopes to reveal strain patterns in transparent materials. In astronomy, polarization measurements reveal information about magnetic fields and scattering dust in space.
