Pixel art

Pixel art is a form of digital art drawn with graphical software where images are built using pixels as the only building block. It is widely associated with the low-resolution graphics of early computers, home video game consoles, and arcade machines of the 1970s, 1980s, and 1990s, in which artists worked within strict technical constraints of resolution and colour palette.

Techniques

Pixel artists deliberately place individual pixels and use restricted palettes, sometimes of only a handful of colours. Characteristic techniques include dithering — interleaving pixels of two colours to suggest intermediate shades or textures — and manual anti-aliasing, in which intermediate-colour pixels soften transitions. Works are typically saved in lossless formats such as GIF or PNG to avoid compression artefacts.

Related concepts include the sprite (a small, movable graphic used in games), tilesets (repeating squares assembled into larger scenes), and isometric pixel art, which renders scenes in a dimetric projection popularised by games such as Habbo Hotel and early strategy titles.

History

Pixel art arose from the constraints of early display hardware, from the vector and raster displays of the 1970s to the 8-bit and 16-bit eras of the Nintendo Entertainment System, Sega Genesis, and home computers. As hardware constraints eased, the style survived as a deliberate aesthetic: communities such as the demoscene, sites devoted to sprite archives, and a revival in independent games (titles like Superbrothers: Sword & Sworcery EP, Fez, and Stardew Valley) made pixel art a recognisable art form in its own right.
