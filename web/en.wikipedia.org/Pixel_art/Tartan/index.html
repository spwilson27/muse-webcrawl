Tartan

Type: Patterned woven cloth
Origin: Scottish Highlands
Material: Wool (traditionally)
Pattern: Criss-crossed horizontal and vertical bands

Tartan is a patterned cloth consisting of criss-crossed horizontal and vertical bands in multiple colours, typically woven from wool. Tartans originated in woven wool, but are now made in many other materials and reproduced in countless media — including pixel art, where the regular grid of the weave maps naturally onto a pixel grid. Tartan is particularly associated with Scotland and Scottish kilts, where particular patterns (setts) are linked to clans and families.

History

Checked and striped woven cloth has been produced in Scotland for centuries; a surviving fragment known as the Falkirk tartan dates to around the 3rd century AD. By the 16th century, tartan was characteristic dress in the Scottish Highlands. After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the Dress Act of 1746 banned Highland dress, though it was repealed in 1782. In the 19th century, tartan became fashionable across Britain, popularised by Sir Walter Scott and the visit of George IV to Edinburgh in 1822.

Clan tartans

The association of specific setts with specific clans largely crystallised in the 19th century, partly through works such as the Vestiarium Scoticum (1842), whose designs are of doubtful authenticity but great influence. Today the Scottish Register of Tartans, maintained by the National Records of Scotland, records thousands of designs, including clan, district, regimental, and corporate tartans.

Construction

Tartan is woven with a two-over, two-under twill, producing diagonal lines where colours meet and a halved tone where they cross. The sequence of coloured threads, the threadcount, determines the pattern, which repeats along both warp and weft.
