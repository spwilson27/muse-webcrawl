Pity

Pity is a sympathetic emotion or feeling of sorrow and compassion caused by the suffering and misfortune of others. The word pity comes from the Latin pietas, via Old French pitié, and is also related to the concept of piety. In philosophical and ethical discussions, pity is often distinguished from compassion and empathy: pity can imply a sense of superiority or distance toward the sufferer, whereas compassion and empathy are generally understood to involve shared feeling and a desire to help.

Philosophical perspectives

Ancient philosophers examined pity closely. Aristotle analysed pity in his Rhetoric as a kind of pain at the sight of apparent destructive or painful evil befalling one who does not deserve it — an evil that one might expect to befall oneself or a friend. In the Stoic tradition, pity was regarded with suspicion as an irrational emotional disturbance, although the sage might still act mercifully.

Later thinkers took varied positions. Jean-Jacques Rousseau treated pity as a natural, pre-rational sentiment that tempers self-love and underpins morality. Friedrich Nietzsche, by contrast, criticised pity as a depressive, contagious feeling that multiplies suffering and preserves what ought to perish, arguing that it stands opposed to the tonic emotions that heighten one's sense of power.

In literature and religion

Pity is a central theme in tragedy, where Aristotle held that the hero's misfortune should arouse pity and fear in the audience, leading to catharsis. In Christian thought, pity is closely linked with mercy and charitable love, frequently portrayed in devotional art through images such as the Pietà, showing the Virgin Mary cradling the dead body of Jesus.

Self-pity

Self-pity is the feeling of sorrow directed at oneself, often accompanied by a sense of victimhood. Psychological literature generally treats persistent self-pity as an unhelpful mindset that can hinder resilience and problem-solving, distinguishing it from healthy self-compassion.
