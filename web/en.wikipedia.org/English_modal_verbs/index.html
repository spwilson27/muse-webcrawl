English modal verbs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The English modal verbs are a subset of the English auxiliary verbs used mostly to express modality — that is, likelihood, ability, permission, and obligation. The core modal verbs of English are can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, and must. A modal verb generally modifies a main verb in a clause; for example, in "I can swim", can expresses the ability of the subject to perform the action of swimming.

Characteristics

English modals form a distinctive grammatical class. They are defective: they have no infinitive, no participles, and no third-person singular -s ("he can", not "he cans"). They are followed by the bare infinitive of the main verb without to, they form negatives by simply adding not ("cannot", "will not"), and they invert with the subject in questions ("Can you swim?") without do-support. They have only one morphological form each for present and past, and the "past" forms (could, might, should, would) often express tentativeness or hypothetical meanings rather than past time.

Meanings

Modals express two broad families of meaning. Epistemic modality concerns the speaker's judgement of likelihood: "It may rain", "He must be tired". Deontic modality concerns permission and obligation: "You may leave", "You must stop". Meanings of ability (can), volition and futurity (will), and habit in the past (would) complete the system. Semi-modals such as have to, ought to, be able to, need and dare fill gaps in the defective paradigm.

History

Most modals descend from Old English "preterite-present" verbs, verbs whose present tense was formed like a past tense; their former past tenses became separate modals or acquired modal meanings, a development known as modal remoteness. Usage continues to change: shall is declining in many varieties, while must increasingly gives way to have to.
