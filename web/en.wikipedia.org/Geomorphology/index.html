Geomorphology

Geomorphology (from Ancient Greek γῆ, gê, "earth"; μορφή, morphḗ, "form"; and λόγος, lógos, "study") is the scientific study of the origin and evolution of topographic and bathymetric features generated by physical, chemical, or biological processes operating at or near Earth's surface. Geomorphologists investigate landforms such as mountains, valleys, dunes, river channels, and coastlines, and seek to understand why landscapes look the way they do, through field observation, physical experiments, remote sensing, and numerical modelling of landscape evolution.

Processes

The primary surface processes that shape landforms are weathering, the transport of material by water, ice, wind, and gravity, and deposition. These are driven by climate, tectonics, and rock type. Major subfields study specific environments and agents: fluvial geomorphology (rivers), glacial geomorphology (ice), aeolian geomorphology (wind), coastal geomorphology (waves and tides), hillslope processes and mass wasting, and tectonic geomorphology, which examines how uplift and faulting interact with erosion to build relief. The balance between uplift and erosion determines the height and form of mountain ranges.

History

Ideas about landscape change are ancient — Chinese scholar Shen Kuo inferred land formation from marine fossils found in mountains in the eleventh century — but the modern discipline developed in the nineteenth century. William Morris Davis proposed the "geographical cycle," in which landscapes pass from youth through maturity to old age, a framework that dominated the field for decades. In the mid-twentieth century the focus shifted from historical description to quantitative process studies, led by figures such as Arthur Strahler and Luna Leopold. Since the late twentieth century, digital elevation models, satellite imagery, and cosmogenic nuclide dating have transformed the ability to measure rates of landscape change over timescales from single storms to millions of years.

Applications

Applied geomorphology supports hazard assessment for floods, landslides, and coastal erosion, river restoration, soil conservation, and planning. Planetary geomorphology applies the same principles to the surfaces of Mars, the Moon, Titan, and other bodies, where landforms record processes operating under very different conditions.
