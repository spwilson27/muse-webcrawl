3D display

A 3D display is a display device capable of conveying depth perception to the viewer by means of stereopsis for binocular vision. The basic requirement is to present offset images that are shown to the left and right eye separately; the brain fuses these two slightly different 2D images into a single perception of three dimensions.

Types

Stereoscopic displays require the viewer to wear glasses or a headset. Anaglyph systems use coloured filters, polarized systems use polarizing filters and linearly or circularly polarized light, and active shutter systems alternate frames between the eyes synchronized with LCD shutter glasses. Head-mounted displays, including virtual reality headsets, present a separate small screen to each eye.

Autostereoscopic displays deliver the effect without glasses by directing different views to each eye using parallax barriers or lenticular lenses. More advanced volumetric displays form images within a volume of space, and holographic displays reconstruct a light field resembling that of the original scene.

History

Stereoscopy dates to the 19th-century stereoscopes of Wheatstone and Brewster. Modern consumer interest peaked around 2010–2013 with 3D televisions and the Nintendo 3DS autostereoscopic handheld, after which home 3D television faded, while stereoscopic display technology remained central to virtual and augmented reality.

Health considerations

Some viewers experience eye strain or headaches from the vergence-accommodation conflict, in which the eyes converge at one distance while focusing at another.
