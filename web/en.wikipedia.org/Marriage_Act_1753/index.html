Marriage Act 1753

Marriage Act 1753
Long titleAn Act for the Better Preventing of Clandestine Marriage
Citation26 Geo. II c. 33
Effective25 March 1754
Also known asHardwicke's Marriage Act

The Marriage Act 1753, also called Hardwicke's Marriage Act after its sponsor, Lord Chancellor Hardwicke, was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that for the first time imposed strict legal formalities on marriage in England and Wales. Its purpose was to suppress clandestine marriages, particularly the irregular ceremonies performed around the Fleet Prison in London.

Provisions

The Act required marriages to be performed in a parish church after the publication of banns, or by a licence, and to be recorded in a register signed by the parties and witnesses. Couples under 21 needed parental consent when marrying by licence, and clergy who performed irregular marriages faced transportation. Jews and Quakers were exempted, but Roman Catholics and other dissenters were required to marry in Anglican churches.

Effects

The Act did not apply to Scotland, whose law continued to recognise irregular marriages by declaration. This discrepancy made the Scottish border village of Gretna Green famous as a destination for eloping English couples. The Act remained the foundation of English marriage law until reforms in the 19th century.
