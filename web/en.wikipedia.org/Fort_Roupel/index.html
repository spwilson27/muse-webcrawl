Fort Roupel (Greek: Οχυρό Ρούπελ, Ochyro Roupel) is a Greek fortress in the Belasica mountain range near the border with Bulgaria, overlooking the Strymon (Struma) river valley and the Roupel gorge. It was the largest stronghold of the Metaxas Line, the chain of fortifications built by Greece in the 1930s to defend its northern frontier.

Construction

Named after the nearby Roupel pass, the fort was an extensive underground complex of tunnels, galleries, machine-gun positions, artillery emplacements, observation posts, and underground barracks capable of operating independently for extended periods. Together with the neighbouring forts of the Metaxas Line, it covered the main invasion route from Bulgaria into the Strymon valley toward Thessaloniki.

Battle of April 1941

During the German invasion of Greece in April 1941, Fort Roupel was attacked from April 6 to April 9 by German mountain troops supported by dive-bombers. Under its commander, Major Georgios Douratsos, the garrison repelled repeated assaults and inflicted heavy casualties. The fort was surrendered only after the German army outflanked the Metaxas Line through Yugoslavia and the armistice was signed; the Germans, acknowledging the defenders' resistance, allowed the garrison to march out with honours of war. A famous exchange is attributed to the surrender, in which the Greek commander replied to a demand to hand over the fort that forts are taken, not handed over.

Legacy

Roupel became a symbol of Greek resistance in the Second World War. An earlier fort of the same name had also seen fighting in the Balkan Wars and First World War battles in the Struma valley. Part of the complex is preserved today as a museum, with annual commemorations held at the site.
