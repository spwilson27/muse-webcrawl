The black rat (Rattus rattus), also known as the roof rat, ship rat, or house rat, is a common long-tailed rodent of the stereotypical rat genus Rattus, in the subfamily Murinae. It likely originated in the Indian subcontinent, but is now found worldwide.

The black rat is black to light brown in colour with a lighter underside. It is a generalist omnivore and a serious pest to farmers because it feeds on a wide range of agricultural crops. It is sometimes kept as a pet. In parts of India, it is considered auspicious.

Description

A typical adult black rat is 12.75 to 18.25 cm (5.0 to 7.2 in) long, not including a 15 to 22 cm (5.9 to 8.7 in) tail, and weighs 75 to 230 g (2.6 to 8.1 oz), depending on the subspecies. Despite its name, the black rat exhibits several colour forms. It is usually black to light brown with a lighter underside.

Distribution and habitat

The black rat originated in South Asia and spread through the Near East and Egypt before reaching Europe, where it became established by the time of the Roman Empire. Travelling on ships, it colonised ports worldwide. It is an agile climber that often lives in the upper parts of buildings, in trees, and in coastal habitats, generally preferring warmer and drier environments than the brown rat (Rattus norvegicus).

Disease

Black rats are associated with the spread of bubonic plague, carried by the rat flea Xenopsylla cheopis which infests them; they are implicated in the transmission of the Black Death in 14th-century Europe, though the relative roles of rats, fleas, and human ectoparasites remain debated. They also spread diseases such as leptospirosis, typhus, and toxoplasmosis.

Ecology and impact

On islands, black rats are a major invasive threat, preying on seabirds, land birds, seeds, and invertebrates; eradication programmes on islands worldwide have been undertaken to protect native wildlife.
