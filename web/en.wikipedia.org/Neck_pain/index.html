Neck pain

Neck pain

SymptomsPain, stiffness, reduced range of motion, headache
CausesMuscle strain, poor posture, cervical spondylosis, disc disease, whiplash, pinched nerve

Neck pain, also known as cervicalgia, is pain felt in the neck and structures of the cervical spine. It is a very common complaint: a majority of adults experience neck pain at some point in their lives, and at any given time a substantial minority of the population is affected. Most episodes are benign and resolve with conservative care, but neck pain can occasionally signal serious disease.

Causes

The most frequent causes are mechanical: muscle strain and spasm, poor posture (including prolonged flexed posture during screen work), and degenerative changes of the cervical spine such as cervical spondylosis, osteoarthritis of the facet joints, and degenerative disc disease. Acute injury — classically whiplash from motor vehicle collisions — can damage muscles, ligaments, discs, and joints. Compression of a cervical nerve root (cervical radiculopathy) produces pain radiating into the arm, sometimes with numbness or weakness; compression of the spinal cord (myelopathy) is more serious.

Less common but important causes include meningitis (typically with fever and marked stiffness), fracture, infection, malignancy, rheumatoid arthritis, and referred pain from the heart or other organs.

Diagnosis

Evaluation begins with history and physical examination, including assessment of range of motion, tenderness, and neurological function. "Red flag" features — such as trauma, fever, unexplained weight loss, progressive neurological deficit, or history of cancer — prompt imaging such as X-ray, CT, or MRI and further workup. In the absence of red flags, routine imaging is generally not recommended for acute nonspecific neck pain.

Treatment

Most neck pain is managed conservatively with reassurance, staying active, heat or cold, simple analgesics such as NSAIDs or acetaminophen, and physical therapy emphasizing exercise, posture, and mobilization. Manipulation and acupuncture are used by some patients, with mixed evidence. Soft cervical collars are no longer routinely recommended. Surgery is reserved for cases with significant neurological compromise, instability, or refractory pain from a defined structural cause.

Epidemiology

Neck pain is among the leading causes of disability worldwide, ranking high in years lived with disability in the Global Burden of Disease studies. It is more common in women than men, peaks in middle age, and is strongly associated with sedentary occupations and psychological stress.
