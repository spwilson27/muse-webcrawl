BPP (complexity)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In computational complexity theory, BPP (bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time) is the class of decision problems solvable by a probabilistic Turing machine in polynomial time with an error probability of at most 1/3 on every instance. Because the error bound can be driven down exponentially by repetition, BPP is considered the standard class of problems efficiently solvable by randomized algorithms.

Definition and amplification

A language is in BPP if a randomized polynomial-time algorithm accepts members with probability at least 2/3 and rejects non-members with probability at least 2/3. By running the algorithm a polynomial number of times and taking a majority vote, the error falls exponentially; the specific constants 1/3 and 2/3 are therefore arbitrary, and the definitions using any constant gap from 1/2 coincide.

Relations to other classes

BPP contains P and is contained in PSPACE; it is known to lie within the polynomial hierarchy (in Σ₂ ∩ Π₂, by the Sipser–Gács–Lautemann theorem). Whether BPP equals P is a major open question, but strong evidence from circuit lower bounds suggests that randomness adds little power: results of Nisan and Wigderson and the Impagliazzo–Wigderson theorem imply that plausible hardness assumptions yield P = BPP ("derandomization"). Practical randomized algorithms, such as the Miller–Rabin primality test, long motivated the class; primality itself was later shown to be in P by the AKS test.

Closure properties

BPP is closed under complement, union and intersection, and its promise-problem and function analogues (FBPP) model feasible randomized computation throughout theoretical computer science.
