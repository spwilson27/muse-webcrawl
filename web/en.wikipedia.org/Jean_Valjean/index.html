Jean Valjean (French pronunciation: [ʒɑ̃ valʒɑ̃]) is the protagonist of Victor Hugo's 1862 novel Les Misérables. An ex-convict imprisoned for nineteen years for stealing a loaf of bread to feed his sister's starving children, Valjean is redeemed by an act of mercy and spends the rest of his life fleeing the obsessive police inspector Javert while devoting himself to good works and to raising the orphaned Cosette.

Character biography

Valjean, an illiterate woodcutter from Faverolles, steals bread in 1795 and is sentenced to five years in the Bagne of Toulon; repeated escape attempts extend his sentence to nineteen years. Released in 1815, embittered and shunned, he is sheltered by Bishop Myriel of Digne, whose forgiveness — after Valjean steals his silver and is given his candlesticks as well — transforms him.

Under the alias Monsieur Madeleine he becomes a prosperous manufacturer and mayor of Montreuil-sur-Mer. When another man is mistaken for the escaped convict Valjean, he reveals his identity in court to save him. He rescues Cosette, the daughter of the dying Fantine, from the abusive Thénardiers and raises her in Paris. During the June Rebellion of 1832 he carries the wounded Marius through the sewers of Paris and spares Javert's life. Valjean dies peacefully in 1833 after revealing his past to Marius and Cosette.

Themes and analysis

Valjean embodies Hugo's themes of redemption, grace and the injustice of a legal system that punishes poverty. Hugo stated that the character was partly inspired by the real ex-convict turned businessman Pierre Maurin and by Eugène François Vidocq. Valjean's moral struggle — the "tempest in a skull" — is one of the most famous interior monologues in French literature.

In adaptations

Valjean has been portrayed by many actors, including Fredric March (1935), Jean Gabin (1958), Liam Neeson (1998) and Hugh Jackman in the 2012 film of the stage musical, in which the role — featuring "Bring Him Home" and "Who Am I?" — was originated by Colm Wilkinson in London.
