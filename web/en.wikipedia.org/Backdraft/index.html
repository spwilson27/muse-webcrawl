Backdraft

A backdraft (North American English) or backdraught (British English) is an explosion that occurs when oxygen is suddenly introduced into an enclosed, oxygen-depleted fire filled with hot, unburned gases and soot particles. The rapid combustion of the accumulated fuel gases can produce a violent fireball erupting from the opening, making backdrafts one of the most dangerous phenomena encountered in structural firefighting.

Backdraft
Type: Fire phenomenon
Cause: Sudden oxygen intake into oxygen-starved fire
Warning signs: Dense smoke, no visible flames, smoke-stained windows, puffs of smoke at openings

Mechanism

In a ventilation-limited compartment fire, pyrolysis continues to generate flammable gases even after open flaming dies down for lack of oxygen, filling the space with a hot, fuel-rich mixture above its autoignition conditions. When a door or window is opened, fresh air rushes in, mixes with the gases, and ignition — often after a delay of a few seconds — produces a deflagration that can expel flame and pressure from the opening.

Prevention

Firefighters are trained to recognise backdraft indicators and to ventilate the compartment from above or apply water before entry. The phenomenon is distinct from a flashover, in which the gases ignite because the room contents reach ignition temperature under sufficient oxygen.
