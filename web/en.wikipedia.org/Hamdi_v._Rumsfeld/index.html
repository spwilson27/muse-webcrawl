Hamdi v. Rumsfeld

Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004), was a United States Supreme Court case in which the Court recognised the power of the federal government to detain individuals, including U.S. citizens, as enemy combatants, but held that detainees who are U.S. citizens must be afforded due process, including a meaningful opportunity to contest the factual basis for their detention before a neutral decision-maker.

Background

Yaser Esam Hamdi, an American citizen born in Louisiana and raised in Saudi Arabia, was captured in Afghanistan in late 2001 during the U.S. invasion following the September 11 attacks. He was transferred to the Guantánamo Bay detention camp and, once his U.S. citizenship was discovered, to naval brigs in Norfolk, Virginia, and Charleston, South Carolina, where he was held incommunicado without charges or access to counsel. His father filed a habeas corpus petition on his behalf. The government argued that separation of powers prevented judicial review of his designation as an enemy combatant.

Decision

Decided on 28 June 2004 — the same day as Rasul v. Bush — the Court rejected the government's position. Justice Sandra Day O'Connor's plurality opinion held that the Authorization for Use of Military Force Act of 2001 authorised detention of individuals who were part of or supporting Taliban or al-Qaeda forces in Afghanistan for the duration of the conflict, but that "a state of war is not a blank check for the President when it comes to the rights of the Nation's citizens." A citizen-detainee must receive notice of the factual basis for his classification and a fair opportunity to rebut the government's assertions before a neutral decisionmaker.

Aftermath

Following the decision, the government negotiated Hamdi's release rather than provide the required hearing: in October 2004 he was released to Saudi Arabia, renouncing his U.S. citizenship and agreeing to travel restrictions. The case remains a leading precedent on wartime detention and habeas corpus, later supplemented by Boumediene v. Bush (2008).
