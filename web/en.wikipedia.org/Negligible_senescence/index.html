Negligible senescence

Negligible senescence is a term coined by biogerontologist Caleb Finch in 1990 to describe organisms that do not exhibit evidence of biological aging (senescence), such as measurable reductions in reproductive capability or increases in mortality rate with age.

Examples

Animals often cited as negligibly senescent include some tortoises, the naked mole-rat, certain fish such as rockfish, and invertebrates such as lobsters and hydra. Some species, like the jellyfish Turritopsis dohrnii, may even exhibit a form of biological immortality by reverting to an earlier life stage.

Research

Study of negligibly senescent species informs research into the mechanisms of aging, including telomere maintenance, DNA repair, and proteostasis. Long-lived species such as the Greenland shark, which may live several centuries, are of particular interest.
