A Roman candle is a traditional firework that ejects one or more pyrotechnic stars — self-propelled coloured pellets — in a series of shots from a single cardboard tube. Among the oldest and most recognisable consumer fireworks, Roman candles are used at celebrations worldwide, although their use is restricted or banned in some jurisdictions because of the temptation to aim them by hand.

Construction and operation

The device consists of a sturdy cardboard or paper tube loaded with repeated layers: a small lifting (ejecting) charge of black powder, a pyrotechnic star, and a slow-burning delay composition, packed between bentonite clay plugs. When lit, the topmost charge fires its star into the sky; the delay composition burns down over a few seconds before igniting the next charge, and the sequence repeats until the tube is exhausted. Stars typically burn red, green, gold, or silver — colours produced by metal salts such as strontium, barium, and sodium compounds — and may crackle, whistle, or trail sparks depending on their composition. Small consumer candles commonly hold five to ten shots; large display versions can fire far more and include comet or mine effects.

History

Like most fireworks, the Roman candle descends from Chinese pyrotechnics transmitted through Italy, where firework craft flourished in the Renaissance and early modern period — the name likely reflects the candle-like tube and Italy's prominence in the trade. By the 19th and 20th centuries Roman candles were standard items in European and American firework catalogues and backyard displays, particularly for the Fourth of July in the United States and Bonfire Night in Britain.

Safety and regulation

Roman candles have a poor safety reputation relative to static fireworks because users sometimes hold them in the hand, turning them into improvised weapons — an activity with its own folk following ("Roman candle wars") and a record of eye injuries and burns. A star fired from a hand-held tube can recoil, misfire, or shoot backwards. Consequently several countries and states prohibit them entirely or classify them with professional fireworks; where legal, instructions universally direct that the tube be fixed upright in the ground or a support and never held. Some modern designs incorporate bases and remote firing to reduce misuse.

In culture

The Roman candle's rhythm of shot, pause, and shot has made it a literary image: Jack Kerouac used it as a metaphor for exuberant people in On the Road. In fireworks terminology, "candles" are frequently bundled into cakes and fan-shaped barrages for professional displays.
