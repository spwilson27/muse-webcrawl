Daniel Moreau Barringer (1860–1929) was an American geologist and mining engineer best known as the first person to prove the existence of an impact crater on Earth. The crater in northern Arizona that he championed is now called Meteor Crater, or Barringer Crater, in his honour.

Early life and career

Barringer was born in Raleigh, North Carolina, and studied at Princeton University and the University of Pennsylvania. He made a fortune in mining ventures, including silver mines in Arizona, before turning his attention to the Arizona crater in 1903.

The crater controversy

The prevailing scientific view, supported by the eminent geologist G. K. Gilbert of the US Geological Survey, held that the crater was a volcanic steam explosion. Barringer, convinced it was caused by the impact of a huge iron meteorite, formed the Standard Iron Company, acquired the land, and spent 26 years and a fortune drilling for the buried meteorite mass, which he hoped to mine for iron, nickel, and platinum. The main mass was never found — later research showed the impactor largely vaporised on impact — but Barringer's geological arguments, published from 1905 onward, gradually persuaded the scientific community that the crater was indeed of impact origin.

Legacy

Barringer died of a heart attack in 1929, shortly after reading a paper by Forest Ray Moulton concluding that the impactor had mostly vaporised. His family retained ownership of the crater through the Barringer Crater Company, and the site operates as a visitor attraction. The lunar crater Barringer and the Barringer Medal, awarded for work on impact cratering, commemorate him.
