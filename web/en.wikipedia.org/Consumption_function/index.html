Consumption function

Consumption function
FieldMacroeconomics
Introduced byJohn Maynard Keynes (1936)
RelatesConsumer spending to disposable income
Key parameterMarginal propensity to consume (MPC)

In economics, the consumption function describes the relationship between consumer spending and the factors that determine it, most importantly disposable income. It is commonly written C = a + bYd, where a is autonomous consumption (spending that occurs even at zero income), Yd is disposable income, and b is the marginal propensity to consume — the fraction of each extra unit of income that is spent rather than saved.

Keynes's formulation

John Maynard Keynes introduced the concept in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), proposing a "fundamental psychological law": as income rises, consumption rises too, but by less than the increase in income. The consumption function is central to the Keynesian multiplier, because a higher MPC means government spending or tax changes produce larger total effects on output.

Empirical puzzles and later theories

Early data supported Keynes, but Simon Kuznets found that the long-run average propensity to consume was roughly constant, contradicting the simple theory. Responses included James Duesenberry's relative income hypothesis, Milton Friedman's permanent income hypothesis (consumption depends on long-run expected income, not current income), and Franco Modigliani's life-cycle hypothesis (people smooth spending over their lifetimes, saving while working and dissaving in retirement).

Modern use

Consumption functions remain building blocks of macroeconomic models, estimated with variables such as wealth, interest rates, credit conditions and consumer confidence in addition to income.
