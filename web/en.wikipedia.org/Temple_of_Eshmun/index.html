Temple of Eshmun

Temple of Eshmun
Location: Bustan esh-Sheikh, near Sidon, Lebanon
Built: 7th century BCE – Roman period
Deity: Eshmun
Excavated: from 1963 (Maurice Dunand)

The Temple of Eshmun (Arabic: معبد أشمون) is an ancient place of worship dedicated to Eshmun, the Phoenician god of healing, located near Sidon in present-day Lebanon. Set in the valley of Bustan esh-Sheikh on the banks of the Awali River, about 2 kilometres northeast of Sidon, the sanctuary was built and enlarged over more than a thousand years, from the 7th century BCE through the Roman period. It is notable for its well-preserved mosaics, its monumental podium, and the many inscriptions naming Sidonian kings who patronized it.

History

The sanctuary was founded under the Sidonian king Eshmunazar II in the 6th century BCE, during the Achaemenid Persian period; inscriptions in Phoenician record that Eshmunazar and his mother Amoashtart built temples to the gods of Sidon, including the "Eshmun of the Ydll spring" near the river's sacred source. Construction continued under King Bodashtart, whose numerous dedicatory inscriptions on the podium record building works at the site. The sanctuary flourished in the Hellenistic period and remained in use under Roman rule, when colonnades, pools and mosaics were added. An earthquake in the 4th century CE damaged the site, though a healing cult associated with the god persisted into the Byzantine era, when a church was built nearby.

Description

The complex is organized around a massive trapezoidal podium, once topped by a marble temple in Greek style. Water played a central role in the healing cult: channels, basins and ritual pools were fed by the river and the sacred spring. Notable remains include the so-called "Tribune of Eshmun", a monumental structure decorated with reliefs of Greek mythological scenes, the throne of Astarte flanked by sphinxes, Roman mosaics including one depicting the four seasons, and a nymphaeum with niches for statues.

Excavation

The site was excavated from 1963 by the Lebanese archaeologist Maurice Dunand, who recovered sculptures, inscriptions and mosaics over more than a decade of work. Many finds are displayed in the National Museum of Beirut. Work was interrupted by the Lebanese Civil War, and the site was later rehabilitated and opened to visitors.
