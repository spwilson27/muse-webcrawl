Controlled atmosphere

A controlled atmosphere is an agricultural storage method in which the concentrations of oxygen, carbon dioxide and nitrogen, as well as the temperature and humidity of a storage room are regulated. Both dry commodities and fresh fruit and vegetables can be stored in controlled atmospheres.

History of use

In the 1820s the French scientist Jacques Étienne Bérard discovered that fruit stored in an oxygen-depleted environment slowed their metabolic processes, delaying ripening. However, controlled atmosphere storage was not widely used until the 1930s in the United Kingdom, and research by Franklin Kidd and Cyril West at Cambridge established the scientific basis of the technique.

Method

The gas mixture normally consists of lowered oxygen and raised carbon dioxide compared with ordinary air. Low oxygen slows respiration, ethylene production, and the growth of many spoilage organisms, extending the storage life of produce such as apples and pears from a few months to most of a year. Ultra-low oxygen storage pushes oxygen levels below 1.5 percent.

Applications

Controlled atmospheres are used for storing fruit, vegetables, grains, and seeds, and in modified forms aboard ships for long-distance transport of fresh produce. A related technique, modified atmosphere packaging, is used in retail food packaging. See also food preservation.
