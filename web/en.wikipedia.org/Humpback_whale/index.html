Humpback whale

Humpback whale
Scientific name: Megaptera novaeangliae
Family: Balaenopteridae (rorquals)
Length: 14–17 m; Mass: up to 40 t
Pectoral fins: About one-third of body length — longest in any whale
Conservation: Least Concern (recovered from whaling)

The humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae) is a species of baleen whale, a rorqual recognisable by its extraordinarily long pectoral fins, knobbly head and acrobatic breaching behaviour. Adults range in length from 14 to 17 metres and weigh up to 40 metric tons. Humpbacks are found in oceans worldwide and migrate up to 16,000 km between feeding and breeding grounds.

Description

The body is stocky, black above and variably white below, with tubercles on the head and lower jaw each bearing a stiff hair. The flippers have a scalloped leading edge with enlarged knuckles, giving the genus name Megaptera, “large wing”. The tail fluke has a unique pattern used by researchers to identify individuals.

Feeding

Humpbacks feed on krill and small schooling fish. They use bubble-net feeding, in which a group blows a circular curtain of bubbles to corral prey, then lunges upward through it with mouths open. Some populations also use their flippers and tail slaps to stun prey.

Song and migration

Males sing complex songs lasting 10–20 minutes, repeated for hours, with all males in a population singing roughly the same evolving song. Songs are thought to function in mating. Populations feed in cold productive waters in summer and breed in tropical waters in winter, fasting for much of the breeding season.

Conservation

Commercial whaling reduced some populations by over 95%, but protection from 1966 and the 1986 whaling moratorium allowed strong recoveries; the species was reclassified as Least Concern in 2008, though a few populations, such as Oceania's, remain endangered. Current threats include entanglement in fishing gear, ship strikes and ocean noise.
