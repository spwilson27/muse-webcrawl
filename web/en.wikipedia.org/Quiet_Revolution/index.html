The Quiet Revolution (French: Révolution tranquille) was a period of intense socio-political and cultural transformation in the Canadian province of Quebec during the 1960s, generally dated from the election of Jean Lesage's Liberal government in 1960. It marked the rapid modernisation and secularisation of Quebec society, the expansion of the provincial state, and the rise of modern Quebec nationalism.

Reforms

  • Nationalisation of electricity under Hydro-Québec (1962–63), championed by minister René Lévesque
  • Creation of a Ministry of Education (1964) and a modern public education system, ending the Catholic Church's dominance of schooling
  • Creation of the Caisse de dépôt et placement (1965) and the Quebec Pension Plan to manage pension funds and finance development
  • Hospital insurance and state assumption of healthcare from religious orders
  • Legal reforms, including married women's legal capacity (1964), and unionisation of public employees

Background and significance

The revolution followed the conservative, church-aligned era of Premier Maurice Duplessis (the so-called Grande Noirceur). Within a few years, church attendance collapsed, the birth rate fell sharply, francophones took control of major economic institutions, and a new Québécois identity replaced the older French-Canadian one. The period also incubated the sovereignty movement: Lévesque later founded the Parti Québécois (1968), leading to the referendums of 1980 and 1995.
