Emerging adulthood and early adulthood

Emerging adulthood is a phase of the life span between adolescence and full-fledged adulthood which encompasses late adolescence and early adulthood, proposed by psychologist Jeffrey Arnett in a 2000 article in the American Psychologist. It primarily describes people living in developed countries, but it is also experienced by young adults in wealthy urban families in the Global South. The term describes young adults who do not yet have children, do not live in their own home, and/or do not have sufficient income to become fully independent.

Characteristics

Arnett identified five features of emerging adulthood: identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and a sense of broad possibilities for the future. During this period, typically spanning roughly ages 18 to 29 in recent formulations, individuals explore possibilities in love, work and worldviews while gradually making enduring choices.

Early adulthood

Early adulthood follows emerging adulthood and is generally considered to extend through the twenties and thirties. Developmental tasks associated with this period include establishing a career, forming intimate relationships, and starting families. Erik Erikson described the central psychosocial conflict of early adulthood as intimacy versus isolation.

Criticism

Critics argue that emerging adulthood is not a universal developmental stage but a product of affluence and delayed marriage in industrialized societies, and that it risks pathologizing young people who take on adult roles earlier. Others note that its boundaries vary greatly by social class and culture.
