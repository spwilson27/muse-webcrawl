Zazen

Zazen (Japanese: 座禅, literally "seated meditation") is a meditative discipline that is the primary practice of the Zen Buddhist tradition. The word derives from Chinese zuòchán (坐禪). Zazen is typically practised seated on a cushion (zafu) in the full-lotus, half-lotus, Burmese or kneeling (seiza) posture, with the spine straight, the hands in the cosmic mudra, and the eyes half-open and lowered.

Practice

In the Sōtō school, the characteristic form of zazen is shikantaza ("just sitting"), an objectless meditation in which the practitioner maintains alert presence without focusing on any particular object or goal, following the teaching of Dōgen Zenji (1200–1253), who described zazen itself as enlightenment rather than a means to it. In the Rinzai school, zazen is commonly combined with kōan introspection — sustained contemplation of a paradoxical phrase such as "What is the sound of one hand?" — and with breath counting (susokukan) for beginners.

Sessions in a zendō (meditation hall) are highly formal: practitioners sit facing the wall (in Sōtō) or the centre of the room (in Rinzai), rounds of sitting are separated by walking meditation (kinhin), and a monitor may carry the keisaku, a flat "encouragement stick" applied to the shoulders of drowsy meditators at their request. Intensive multi-day retreats are called sesshin.

Historical background

Seated meditation descends from Indian dhyāna practices transmitted to China, where the Chan tradition made meditation its defining activity — the word Chan itself deriving from dhyāna. In Japan, Dōgen's Fukanzazengi ("Universal Recommendation for Zazen") and his Shōbōgenzō gave the practice its classic Sōtō formulation, while Hakuin Ekaku revitalised Rinzai kōan training in the eighteenth century. Zazen spread to the West in the twentieth century through teachers such as Shunryu Suzuki, whose talks were published as Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind (1970).
