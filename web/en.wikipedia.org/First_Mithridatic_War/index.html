The First Mithridatic War (89–85 BC) was fought between the Roman Republic and the Kingdom of Pontus, ruled by Mithridates VI, over control of Asia Minor and Greece. It was the first of three Mithridatic Wars and marked Rome's most serious challenge in the Greek East since the Macedonian Wars.

Outbreak and the Asiatic Vespers

Mithridates, an ambitious Hellenistic king who had built Pontus into a regional power, clashed with Rome over Bithynia and Cappadocia. Invading the Roman province of Asia in 89 BC, he was welcomed by cities resentful of Roman tax farmers. In 88 BC he orchestrated the Asiatic Vespers — the coordinated massacre of an estimated 80,000 Romans and Italians in Asia Minor — and carried the war across the Aegean into Greece, where Athens under the tyrant Aristion joined him.

Sulla's campaign

The Roman commander Lucius Cornelius Sulla, having first marched on Rome in the opening act of the Roman civil wars, crossed to Greece in 87 BC. He besieged Athens and Piraeus, sacking Athens on 1 March 86 BC, and then crushed Mithridates' armies at Chaeronea and Orchomenus in Boeotia, where the Pontic general Archelaus was decisively beaten. Sulla's quaestor Lucullus meanwhile assembled a fleet and defeated Pontic naval forces at Tenedos.

Treaty of Dardanos

Eager to return to Italy to confront his domestic enemies, Sulla concluded the Treaty of Dardanos (85 BC): Mithridates surrendered his conquests in Asia, Paphlagonia and the disputed kingdoms, paid an indemnity of 2,000 talents and gave up seventy warships — but kept his throne. The lenient settlement left the underlying conflict unresolved, and war resumed within a decade.
