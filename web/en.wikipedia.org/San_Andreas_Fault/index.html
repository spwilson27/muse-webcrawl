The San Andreas Fault is a continental right-lateral strike-slip transform fault that extends roughly 1,200 kilometres (800 mi) through the U.S. state of California. It forms the tectonic boundary between the Pacific Plate and the North American Plate. The fault was first identified in 1895 by Professor Andrew Lawson of the University of California, Berkeley, and is named after the Laguna de San Andreas, a small lake in a valley formed by the fault.

Structure

The fault is divided into three segments — northern, central, and southern — each with different characteristics and earthquake risks. The two plates move past each other at rates of roughly 20–35 mm per year. The central segment partly "creeps" continuously, while the northern and southern segments tend to lock and release energy in large earthquakes.

Major earthquakes

The fault produced the great 1906 San Francisco earthquake (estimated magnitude 7.9), in which the northern segment ruptured and offset fences and roads by several metres, and the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake (magnitude 6.9), which caused major damage in the San Francisco Bay Area. The southern segment, running from the Salton Sea toward Los Angeles, has not ruptured in a great earthquake since 1680 and is considered a major seismic hazard — the scenario of a future large rupture is popularly called "the Big One".

Study and monitoring

The San Andreas Fault Observatory at Depth (SAFOD) drilled directly into the fault near Parkfield to sample fault-zone material and install instruments at seismogenic depths. GPS networks and seismometers continuously track strain accumulation along the fault.
