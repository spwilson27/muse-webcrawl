Christianization of Scandinavia

The Christianization of Scandinavia, the process by which the peoples of Denmark, Norway and Sweden adopted Christianity in place of Norse paganism, occurred between the eighth and twelfth centuries. It was a gradual process driven by missionaries, trade contacts, royal policy and political integration with Christian Europe.

Early missions

The first recorded missions were led by the English monk Willibrord to Denmark around 710 and, more famously, by Ansgar, the "Apostle of the North", who established churches at the trading ports of Hedeby and Birka in the 820s and 850s. These early efforts won few lasting converts, and pagan worship continued to dominate.

Royal conversions

The conversion of kings proved decisive. Harald Bluetooth of Denmark was baptised around 960 and proclaimed on the Jelling stones that he had "made the Danes Christian". In Norway, the missionary kings Olaf Tryggvason and Olaf Haraldsson (Saint Olaf) promoted Christianity, sometimes by force, between 995 and 1030. Iceland adopted Christianity by a legal decision of the Althing in the year 1000, brokered by the lawspeaker Thorgeir Thorkelsson, which permitted private pagan practice for a transitional period. Sweden was the slowest to convert: King Olof Skötkonung was baptised around 1008, yet the pagan temple at Uppsala survived into the late eleventh century.

Consolidation

Archbishoprics were established at Lund in 1103, Nidaros in 1152 and Uppsala in 1164, integrating Scandinavia into the Latin Church. Pagan practices, including sacrifice (blót) and the worship of Odin, Thor and Freyr, faded over generations, though folklore preserved many older motifs. The coming of Christianity brought literacy in Latin, stone church building, and closer ties to the rest of Europe.
