Amber Road

The Amber Road was an ancient network of trade routes by which amber — fossilised tree resin prized for ornament and supposed medicinal properties — was transferred from the coasts of the North Sea and the Baltic Sea to the Mediterranean. The trade flourished from the Bronze Age onward and reached its height under the Roman Empire.

Routes

Amber gathered along the Baltic shores (notably the Samland peninsula) travelled south along the Vistula and across the Moravian Gate to the Danube, reaching Roman markets through centres such as Carnuntum and Aquileia at the head of the Adriatic. Other branches crossed Gaul toward Massalia (Marseille) or followed the Elbe and Oder. Tacitus described the Aestii of the Baltic coast collecting amber, and Pliny the Elder recorded an expedition under Nero that brought back huge quantities of it.

Significance

Amber objects from the Baltic are found in Mycenaean tombs and in Etruscan and Roman jewellery, showing the route's reach as early as the second millennium BCE. The trade connected northern Europe with Mediterranean civilisations centuries before written records in the region, moving not only amber southward but also bronze, glass, wine and coinage northward. The name "Amber Road" is a modern coinage, modelled on the Silk Road, and survives today as a heritage and cycling route designation in several countries.
