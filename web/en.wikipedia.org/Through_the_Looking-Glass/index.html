Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There (also known as Through the Looking-Glass) is a novel published on 27 December 1871 (though dated 1872) by Lewis Carroll, and the sequel to Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865). Alice again enters a fantastical world, this time by climbing through a mirror placed over the fireplace in her home, and discovers a world divided into squares like a great chessboard.

Plot summary

On a snowy November evening, Alice plays with her kittens and wonders what the world beyond the mirror is like. She climbs through the looking-glass and finds a world where everything is reversed, including the logic by which things work. Books are printed in mirror writing, and running fast keeps one in the same place. Travelling across a landscape laid out as a chessboard, Alice meets the Red Queen, who promises that if Alice reaches the eighth square she will become a queen herself. Alice proceeds as a pawn through a series of encounters: the flowers that talk, Tweedledum and Tweedledee, Humpty Dumpty, the Lion and the Unicorn, and the White Knight. Along the way she hears the poems “Jabberwocky” and “The Walrus and the Carpenter”. Reaching the eighth square, she is crowned queen, attends a chaotic banquet, and seizes the Red Queen, checking the Red King — whereupon she wakes back in her armchair, holding her black kitten.

Themes

The novel is structured around the game of chess, with Alice's journey mirroring a pawn's progress to promotion. Mirrors and reversals run throughout: time runs backwards in the White Queen's memory, and the country is divided by brooks into squares. The book is generally considered darker and more melancholic than its predecessor, with themes of growing up and the passage of time.

Legacy

The book introduced “Jabberwocky”, often regarded as one of the finest nonsense poems in English, and coinages such as “portmanteau word”. Its characters and episodes have been adapted in numerous films, plays, and works of music.
