Orthogonality thesis

The thesis holds that an agent's intelligence and its final goals can vary independently of one another.

The orthogonality thesis is a proposition in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, most prominently formulated by philosopher Nick Bostrom, which states that an agent's intelligence — its ability to achieve goals in complex environments — and its final goals are orthogonal: more or less any level of intelligence could in principle be combined with more or less any final goal.

Statement

Bostrom sets out the thesis in his work on superintelligence, contrasting it with the common assumption that sufficiently intelligent agents will naturally converge on goals humans regard as moral or reasonable. Under the thesis, a highly capable system might pursue a trivial or bizarre objective — Bostrom's example is a superintelligence dedicated to manufacturing paperclips — with extreme competence and persistence.

Relation to the instrumental convergence thesis

The orthogonality thesis is usually paired with the instrumental convergence thesis, which holds that intelligent agents with very different final goals will nonetheless converge on similar instrumental sub-goals, such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resisting modification of their goals. Together the two theses form a standard argument for why advanced AI systems might pose risks if their goals are not aligned with human values.

Criticism

Critics question whether the thesis applies to minds that are sufficiently human-like, whether "intelligence" can be cleanly separated from goals, and whether very general reasoners would in practice adopt goals independently of their reasoning capabilities.
