Rain gutter

A rain gutter, eavestrough, eaves-shoot or surface water collection channel is a component of a water discharge system for a building. It consists of a horizontal trough, usually fixed just below the edge of the roof, that collects rainwater and channels it to downspouts (downpipes), which carry it to a drain or a collection vessel. Gutters protect a building's foundations, siding, and landscaping from uncontrolled roof runoff.

A K-style sectional gutter fitted to the fascia below the eaves.

History

Gargoyles on Roman and Gothic buildings served as early rain-disposal devices, throwing water clear of walls. Lead, wood, and cast-iron gutters became common in the 18th and 19th centuries. Modern gutters are usually made of aluminum, galvanized steel, copper, zinc, or vinyl (PVC/uPVC), and may be formed seamlessly on site from coil stock using a gutter machine.

Types and profiles

Common profiles include K-style (ogee), half-round, and box gutters integrated into the roof structure. Gutters are sized according to roof area and expected rainfall intensity, and are installed with a slight slope (commonly about 1:500) toward the downspouts. Accessories include gutter guards, which reduce clogging by leaves, leaf catchers, and rain chains used as decorative alternatives to downspouts.

Maintenance

Gutters require periodic cleaning because accumulated debris and standing water cause overflow, corrosion, and ice dams in cold climates. Clogged gutters are a frequent cause of basement water intrusion and foundation damage.
