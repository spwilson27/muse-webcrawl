Changi Prison

Changi Prison Complex, often known simply as Changi Prison, is a prison complex in Changi, in the eastern part of Singapore. Built by the British colonial government in 1936, it is the oldest and largest prison in the country and remains in use as the main facility of the Singapore Prison Service.

Second World War

After the fall of Singapore in February 1942, the Japanese used Changi as a prisoner-of-war camp for Allied soldiers and civilian internees. Conditions were harsh, and the area became synonymous with the suffering of POWs in Southeast Asia, although most prisoners were later moved elsewhere, including to work on the Burma Railway. The Changi Chapel and Museum commemorate the internees, and the famous Changi Murals were painted by a POW in the prison chapel.

Modern use

The original prison was largely demolished in the 2000s and replaced by a modern high-security complex, though the historic entrance gate, wall and turrets were preserved as a national monument. Changi Prison houses Singapore's death row, and judicial executions by hanging are carried out there.
