Alternative splicing

Alternative splicing (AS) is a regulated process during gene expression in which particular exons of a gene may be included in, or excluded from, the final messenger RNA produced from that gene. As a result, a single gene can encode multiple distinct proteins (isoforms), greatly expanding the coding capacity of eukaryotic genomes.

One pre-mRNA, several mature mRNAs: exon skipping, intron retention and alternative splice sites.

Mechanism and modes

Splicing is carried out by the spliceosome, which removes introns and ligates exons. The principal modes of alternative splicing are exon skipping (cassette exons), mutually exclusive exons, alternative 5′ and 3′ splice sites, and intron retention. The choice among them is governed by cis-acting sequences — exonic and intronic splicing enhancers and silencers — and trans-acting RNA-binding proteins, including SR proteins and hnRNPs, whose concentrations vary between tissues.

Significance

More than 90 percent of human multi-exon genes undergo alternative splicing, and the phenomenon is especially prominent in the nervous system: the Drosophila gene Dscam can theoretically generate over 38,000 isoforms, more than the total number of genes in the fly genome. Errors in splicing cause or contribute to many diseases, including spinal muscular atrophy and numerous cancers; conversely, splicing can be therapeutically manipulated, as in the antisense drug nusinersen, which corrects splicing of the SMN2 gene.

Discovery

Alternative splicing was first observed in adenoviruses in 1977, shortly after the discovery of split genes, and was soon demonstrated for cellular genes such as calcitonin/CGRP, whose tissue-specific splicing produces different peptide hormones.
