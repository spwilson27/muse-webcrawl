Proscription

A proscription (Latin: proscriptio) is a public condemnation of individuals, typically political enemies, who are declared outlaws and whose property is confiscated. The term derives from the practice in ancient Rome of posting lists of the condemned in public places. Those named could be killed with impunity — often for a reward — and their estates seized by the state.

Sulla's proscriptions

The dictator Lucius Cornelius Sulla introduced the first systematic proscriptions in 82 BC after winning the civil war against the followers of Marius. Thousands of senators, equestrians, and their supporters were listed, executed, and stripped of their property, which was auctioned off — enriching Sulla's loyalists, including the young Crassus.

The Second Triumvirate

In 43 BC the Second Triumvirate — Octavian, Mark Antony, and Lepidus — issued a new proscription to eliminate enemies and fund their armies. Its most famous victim was the orator Cicero, whose head and hands were displayed on the Rostra in the Forum. Ancient sources put the victims at around 300 senators and 2,000 equestrians.

Legacy

Proscription became a byword for politically motivated purge and confiscation, and the word survives in modern usage for any official prohibition or ban.
