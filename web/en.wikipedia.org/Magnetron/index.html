Magnetron

The cavity magnetron is a high-power vacuum tube that generates microwaves using the interaction of a stream of electrons with a magnetic field while moving past a series of cavity resonators. Electrons are emitted from a heated cathode, and a permanent magnet forces them into spiral paths so that they excite resonant oscillations in the anode cavities.

History

Early magnetrons were developed in the 1920s and 1930s, but the breakthrough multi-cavity design came in 1940 from John Randall and Harry Boot at the University of Birmingham. Their device produced far more power at centimetric wavelengths than anything before it, making compact airborne and naval radar practical. Shared with the United States under the Tizard Mission, it was called by one historian "the most valuable cargo ever brought to American shores," and it transformed Allied radar in the Second World War.

Applications

After the war the magnetron found a second life heating food: Percy Spencer at Raytheon noticed a chocolate bar melt near a radar set, leading to the microwave oven, in which a magnetron typically generates 2.45 GHz radiation at around a kilowatt. Magnetrons also drive industrial heating and plasma processes and are used in some radar transmitters to this day, though solid-state devices are replacing them.
