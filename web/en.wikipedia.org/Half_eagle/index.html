The half eagle is a United States coin that was produced for circulation from 1795 to 1929 and in commemorative and proof formats from 1983 to the present day. Sometimes referred to as the "workhorse of the US economy", the half eagle was authorized by the Coinage Act of 1792, and it was the first gold coin minted by the United States. It had a face value of five dollars ($5), half that of the ten-dollar eagle.

Specifications

As originally issued in 1795, the half eagle weighed 8.75 grams and was composed of 91.7% gold and 8.3% silver and copper alloy. The Coinage Act of 1834 reduced the weight to 8.36 grams and the fineness to .8992, and the Act of 1837 set the composition at 90% gold and 10% copper, a standard that remained until production for circulation ended in 1929. The diameter was reduced over time from 25 mm to 21.6 mm.

Designs

The half eagle passed through more major design changes than any other United States denomination:

  • Turban Head (1795–1807), designed by Robert Scot, in right-facing and left-facing variants.
  • Draped Bust (1807–1812) and Capped Head Left (1813–1834), designed by John Reich.
  • Classic Head (1834–1838), by William Kneass, which removed the motto E PLURIBUS UNUM.
  • Liberty Head (Coronet) (1839–1908), by Christian Gobrecht — the longest-running design; the motto IN GOD WE TRUST was added above the eagle in 1866.
  • Indian Head (1908–1929), by Bela Lyon Pratt, whose incuse (sunken-relief) design was shared with the quarter eagle.

Collecting

The 1822 half eagle is one of the great rarities of American numismatics: of three known specimens, two are held by the Smithsonian Institution, and the third sold at auction in 2021 for $8.4 million, then a record for a United States gold coin. The early-date Capped Head issues of 1815–1834 are also extremely rare because most were melted when their bullion value exceeded face value. Half eagles were minted at Philadelphia and at the branch mints of Charlotte, Dahlonega, Carson City, Denver, New Orleans, and San Francisco.
