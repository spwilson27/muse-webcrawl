Carbon nanotube

A single-walled carbon nanotube: a graphene sheet rolled into a seamless cylinder.

CompositionCarbon (sp²-bonded)
Diameter~1 nm (single-walled) to tens of nm (multi-walled)
TypesSingle-walled (SWCNT), multi-walled (MWCNT)
DiscoveryPopularised by Sumio Iijima, 1991

Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are cylindrical molecules made of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice, essentially sheets of graphene rolled into seamless tubes. They are allotropes of carbon related to fullerenes, and have diameters of the order of nanometres with lengths that can reach centimetres, giving them enormous aspect ratios.

Structure and properties

A nanotube's electronic behaviour depends on how the graphene sheet is rolled (its "chirality"): roughly one-third of single-walled nanotubes are metallic, the rest semiconducting. CNTs are among the strongest and stiffest materials known — with tensile strengths tens of times that of steel at a fraction of the weight — and they exhibit exceptional electrical and thermal conductivity.

Synthesis

Nanotubes are produced by arc discharge, laser ablation, and, most commonly today, chemical vapour deposition (CVD), in which a carbon-containing gas decomposes over metal catalyst particles. Controlling the chirality and purity of the product remains a major technical challenge.

Applications

Current and proposed uses include conductive and reinforcing additives in composites and plastics, lithium-ion battery electrodes, field-emission displays, transistors and sensors, and research into cables for a hypothetical space elevator. Possible health effects resembling those of asbestos fibres for certain long, rigid multi-walled nanotubes are an active subject of study and regulation.
