Cupid

In Roman mythology, Cupid (Latin: Cupīdō, "passionate desire") is the god of desire, erotic love, attraction and affection. He is usually portrayed as the son of the love goddess Venus and the war god Mars, and is the Roman counterpart of the Greek god Eros. In popular culture Cupid is frequently depicted as a chubby winged infant wielding a bow and arrows, whose wounds inspire love in those struck — imagery especially associated with Valentine's Day.

Attributes and myths

Cupid carries two kinds of arrows: golden, sharp-tipped arrows that fill a person with love, and blunt, lead-tipped arrows that cause aversion. A famous story in Ovid's Metamorphoses tells how Cupid, insulted by Apollo, struck the god with a golden arrow and the nymph Daphne with a leaden one, causing the hopeless pursuit that ended with Daphne's transformation into a laurel tree.

The most celebrated Cupid tale is Cupid and Psyche, from Apuleius' novel The Golden Ass (2nd century AD). Psyche, a mortal of extraordinary beauty, is loved by Cupid, who visits her only in darkness. Breaking his prohibition, she looks upon him by lamplight and a drop of hot oil wakes him; she must then undergo trials set by Venus — sorting grains, fetching the golden fleece, and descending to the underworld — before being granted immortality and marriage to the god. The story has been read as an allegory of the soul (Greek psychē) purified through love.

In art and culture

Cupid appears throughout classical and Renaissance art — by Caravaggio, Bernini, Botticelli and many others — variously as a mischievous child, a beautiful youth, or a sleeping figure whose repose signifies love at rest. The related figures of the Amores or putti, winged cherubic children, populate Baroque ceilings. In modern times Cupid's image anchors the commercial iconography of romance, from greeting cards to matchmaking "Cupid" references.
