Combustion

Combustion
TypeChemical reaction (redox)
ProductsHeat, light, CO2, H2O (complete)
ExamplesBurning of wood, methane, gasoline
RelatedFire, Oxidation, Internal combustion engine

Combustion, or burning, is a high-temperature exothermic redox chemical reaction between a fuel (the reductant) and an oxidant, usually atmospheric oxygen, that produces oxidized, often gaseous products, in a mixture termed as smoke. Combustion does not always result in fire, because a flame is only visible when substances undergoing combustion vaporize, but when it does, a flame is a characteristic indicator of the reaction. While activation energy must be supplied to initiate combustion (e.g., using a lit match to light a fire), the heat from a flame may provide enough energy to make the reaction self-sustaining.

Types

Complete combustion

In complete combustion, the reactant burns in oxygen and produces a limited number of products. When a hydrocarbon burns completely in oxygen, the reaction primarily produces carbon dioxide and water. For example, the combustion of methane: CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O.

Incomplete combustion

Incomplete combustion occurs when there is not enough oxygen to allow the fuel to react completely, producing carbon monoxide, soot (carbon), and other partially oxidized products. It is a major source of air pollution and of carbon-monoxide poisoning.

Rapid, slow, and spontaneous combustion

Rapid combustion produces large amounts of heat and light energy, as in a fire. Slow combustion occurs at low temperatures, as in cellular respiration and rusting of fuels. Spontaneous combustion results from self-heating of a material (such as oily rags or hay) to its ignition temperature.

Applications

Combustion powers most road vehicles through internal combustion engines, generates much of the world's electricity in thermal power stations, and is used for heating, cooking, and industrial processes such as smelting. Managing combustion efficiency and emissions (including nitrogen oxides and particulates) is a central concern of energy engineering.
