Alexander Duncan Langmuir (12 September 1910 – 22 November 1993) was an American epidemiologist who is widely regarded as one of the founders of modern applied epidemiology. As chief epidemiologist of the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) from 1949 to 1970, he founded the Epidemic Intelligence Service (EIS) in 1951 and transformed disease surveillance into a central tool of public health practice.

Career

Langmuir studied at Harvard College and Harvard Medical School, and earned a master's degree in public health at Johns Hopkins. During the Second World War he worked on respiratory disease control for the U.S. Army. In 1949 he joined the Communicable Disease Center (later the CDC) in Atlanta as its first chief epidemiologist, a post he held for two decades.

Epidemic Intelligence Service

Motivated in part by Cold War fears of biological warfare, Langmuir founded the EIS in 1951 to train physicians and other health professionals in field epidemiology — the rapid investigation of epidemics, famous for the image of the "disease detective". EIS officers investigated outbreaks ranging from polio and influenza to foodborne illness, and the programme's response to the 1955 Cutter incident, in which incompletely inactivated polio vaccine caused cases of paralytic poliomyelitis, helped establish modern vaccine safety monitoring. Langmuir promoted surveillance as the systematic collection, analysis and dissemination of health data, and championed the use of epidemiological methods in family planning and other fields beyond infectious disease.

Later life

After leaving the CDC in 1970, Langmuir taught at Harvard Medical School and at Johns Hopkins, where he had earlier held academic appointments. He remained an influential voice on epidemic investigation and public health practice until his death in 1993.
