Statistical hypothesis test

A statistical hypothesis test is a method of statistical inference used to decide whether the data provide sufficient evidence to reject a particular hypothesis. The procedure begins with a null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis; a test statistic is computed from the sample, and its observed value is compared with the distribution it would have if the null hypothesis were true.

p-values and significance levels

The p-value is the probability, under the null hypothesis, of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed. If the p-value falls below a pre-chosen significance level (commonly 0.05), the null hypothesis is rejected. Two kinds of error are possible: a Type I error (rejecting a true null hypothesis, with probability equal to the significance level) and a Type II error (failing to reject a false null hypothesis, related to statistical power).

Examples and criticism

Widely used tests include Student's t-test, the chi-squared test, analysis of variance (ANOVA), and non-parametric alternatives such as the Mann–Whitney U test. Hypothesis testing emerged from the work of Ronald Fisher, Jerzy Neyman, and Egon Pearson in the early 20th century. It has been criticized for encouraging binary decisions, misinterpretation of p-values, and publication bias; alternatives and complements include confidence intervals, effect sizes, and Bayesian methods.
