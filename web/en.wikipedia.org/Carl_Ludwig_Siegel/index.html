Carl Ludwig Siegel

Carl Ludwig Siegel (31 December 1896 – 4 April 1981) was a German mathematician specialising in analytic number theory and celestial mechanics. He is regarded as one of the most important mathematicians of the 20th century, known for landmark results on Diophantine approximation, transcendental numbers, and quadratic forms. In 1978 he was among the first recipients of the Wolf Prize in Mathematics.

Career

Born in Berlin, Siegel studied at the Humboldt University and the University of Göttingen, completing his doctorate in 1920 under Edmund Landau. After a professorship at Frankfurt, he joined Göttingen in 1938. A committed anti-Nazi, he emigrated in 1940 via Norway and a dramatic escape from an internment camp to the United States, where he worked at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton alongside Einstein and von Neumann. He returned to Göttingen in 1951 and remained there until his death.

Work

Siegel's 1929 paper on Diophantine equations proved that curves of genus greater than zero have only finitely many integral points — a deep extension of Thue's theorem. He proved results on the transcendence of values of Bessel and exponential functions, advanced the theory of quadratic forms in many variables, and created the theory of Siegel modular forms and Siegel upper half-space, which became central to the Langlands programme. His work on celestial mechanics, including Siegel's theorem on small divisors, proved foundational for KAM theory. The Thue–Siegel–Roth theorem and Siegel's lemma bear his name.
