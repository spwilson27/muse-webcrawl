Atrium (heart)


Diagram of the heart chambers

In anatomy, an atrium (plural: atria) is one of the two upper chambers of the heart that receives blood from the veins and passes it into a ventricle. The human heart has a right atrium and a left atrium, which contract together shortly before the ventricles. Atrial contraction contributes the "atrial kick", topping up ventricular filling, especially important when heart rates are high or ventricles are stiff.

Right atrium

The right atrium receives deoxygenated blood from the body via the superior and inferior vena cava and from the heart muscle itself via the coronary sinus. It contains the sinoatrial node, the heart's natural pacemaker, and passes blood through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs. A flap-like depression in its wall, the fossa ovalis, is the remnant of the foramen ovale of fetal circulation.

Left atrium

The left atrium receives oxygenated blood from the lungs through the four pulmonary veins and passes it through the mitral valve into the left ventricle. It is the most posterior of the heart's chambers, lying in front of the oesophagus — a relationship used in transoesophageal echocardiography. Its small ear-shaped pouch, the left atrial appendage, is a common site of blood clot formation in atrial fibrillation, a major cause of stroke.

Evolution

In fish, the heart has a single atrium; amphibians have two atria but one ventricle, while most reptiles, birds and mammals possess fully separated right and left atria as part of a four-chambered heart with complete separation of pulmonary and systemic circulation.
