Narcissistic personality disorder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Narcissistic personality disorder
Specialty: Psychiatry, clinical psychology
Symptoms: Grandiosity, excessive need for admiration, reduced empathy
Usual onset: Early adulthood
Treatment: Psychotherapy

Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) is a personality disorder characterised by a long-term pattern of exaggerated feelings of self-importance, an excessive desire for admiration, and a reduced ability to empathise with other people's feelings. People with NPD often spend much time thinking about achieving power and success, or on their appearance. The condition takes its name from the Greek myth of Narcissus, the youth who fell in love with his own reflection.

Signs and symptoms

According to the DSM-5, NPD is indicated by a pervasive pattern of grandiosity (in fantasy or behaviour), need for admiration, and lack of empathy, beginning by early adulthood and present in a variety of contexts. Diagnostic features include a grandiose sense of self-importance, preoccupation with fantasies of unlimited success or power, belief that one is "special", a need for excessive admiration, a sense of entitlement, interpersonally exploitative behaviour, envy, and arrogant or haughty attitudes. Beneath the outward grandiosity, self-esteem is often fragile, and individuals may react to criticism with rage, shame, or humiliation.

Causes

The causes of NPD are not fully understood. Research suggests a combination of genetic factors, neurobiological differences, and environmental influences such as parenting styles — either excessive pampering and overvaluation or, conversely, neglect and harsh criticism in childhood.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by a qualified mental health professional through clinical interview, usually using DSM-5 or ICD-11 criteria. Treatment is primarily psychotherapy, including approaches such as transference-focused psychotherapy, schema therapy, and mentalization-based treatment. There are no medications approved specifically for NPD, though medication may be used for co-occurring conditions such as depression or anxiety. People with NPD often do not seek treatment, as they may not recognise their behaviour as problematic.

Prevalence

Estimates of lifetime prevalence vary; a large United States survey estimated about 6.2% of adults, with higher rates in men than in women.
