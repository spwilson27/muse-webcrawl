An electric arc furnace (EAF) is a furnace that heats material by means of an electric arc. Industrial arc furnaces range in size from small units of approximately one-tonne capacity (used in foundries for producing cast iron products) up to about 400-tonne units used for secondary steelmaking.

Arc furnaces are central to "mini-mill" steel production, melting scrap and direct-reduced iron rather than iron ore. Because they run on electricity, their carbon footprint depends on the grid mix and is generally lower than that of the blast furnace–basic oxygen route, especially when melting scrap.

History

The electric arc furnace was invented in 1907 by Paul Héroult of France and first applied commercially in the United States that year for specialty steels. Adoption expanded greatly after the Second World War as scrap became plentiful, and from the 1960s the rise of continuous casting enabled the mini-mill model pioneered by companies such as Nucor. EAFs now produce roughly a third of the world's steel, and a large majority of U.S. steel.

Construction

The furnace consists of a refractory-lined steel shell with a removable roof through which pass one (DC furnaces) or three (AC furnaces) graphite electrodes. Modern furnaces use water-cooled panels to extend lining life. A rocking mechanism tilts the furnace for slag removal and for tapping steel into a ladle.

Operation

Scrap is loaded by overhead crane in charging buckets. The electrodes are lowered and an arc is struck; currents of tens of thousands of amperes melt the charge. Oxygen lancing and carbon injection refine the melt and create a foaming slag that shields the walls and improves efficiency. Chemical analysis guides additions before tapping.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: flexibility (start/stop, varied feedstock), lower capital cost, strong scrap-recycling capability
  • Disadvantages: high electricity demand, electrode consumption, noise and flicker on the power grid
