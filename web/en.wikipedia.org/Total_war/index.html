Total war

Mass industrial mobilisation is a hallmark of total war

Total war is a type of warfare that includes any and all (including civilian-associated) resources and infrastructure as legitimate military targets, mobilises all of the resources of society to fight the war, and gives priority to warfare over non-combatant needs. The term has been defined as "a war that is unrestricted in terms of the weapons used, the territory or combatants involved, or the objectives pursued, especially one in which the laws of war are disregarded."

Characteristics

Historians commonly identify several features of total war: the complete mobilisation of a society's manpower and economy, including conscription and the direction of industry and labour; the erosion of the distinction between combatants and civilians, making civilian populations and infrastructure targets; the radicalisation of war aims, often toward unconditional surrender; and centralised state control over most aspects of public life. The American Civil War general William Tecumseh Sherman's "March to the Sea" and Philip Sheridan's campaign in the Shenandoah Valley are frequently cited as early examples, as are the French Revolutionary levée en masse and the campaigns of the Napoleonic Wars.

Twentieth century

The term gained currency around the First World War, in which industrial mobilisation, blockade and mass conscription drew entire societies into the conflict, and was popularised by German general Erich Ludendorff's 1935 memoir Der totale Krieg ("The Total War"). The Second World War is generally regarded as the quintessential total war, with strategic bombing of cities, the Holocaust and other genocides, rationing and total economic mobilisation on all sides. Scholars debate whether total war is a modern product of industrialisation and nationalism or whether comparable practices can be found in earlier eras, such as the Mongol conquests or the Thirty Years' War.
