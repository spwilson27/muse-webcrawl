Dirichlet's approximation theorem

In number theory, Dirichlet's approximation theorem, also called Dirichlet's theorem on Diophantine approximation, states that for any real numbers α and N, with 1 ≤ N, there exist integers p and q such that 1 ≤ q ≤ N and |qα − p| < 1/N. It is a fundamental result in Diophantine approximation, showing that every real number has a sequence of rational approximations that are good relative to the denominators involved.

Statement and proof

The classical proof applies the pigeonhole principle: the N+1 fractional parts of 0, α, 2α, …, Nα are placed into N subintervals of [0,1) of length 1/N, so two of them fall in the same subinterval; their difference yields the desired approximation. An important consequence is that for every irrational α there are infinitely many rationals p/q with |α − p/q| < 1/q².

History and generalizations

The theorem was proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in the mid-19th century. It was later sharpened by Hurwitz's theorem, which gives the best possible constant for the inequality |α − p/q| < 1/(√5 q²). Dirichlet's theorem generalizes to simultaneous approximation of several real numbers, a cornerstone of the geometry of numbers.
