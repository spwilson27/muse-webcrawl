Government corporation

A government corporation is a legal entity that undertakes commercial activities on behalf of an owner government. Such organizations are typically established when a government wishes to deliver a public service or conduct a business activity with a degree of operational independence from day-to-day political control, while remaining accountable to the state as owner.

Government corporations differ from ordinary government departments in that they usually have a separate legal personality, can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued in their own name, and often generate their own revenue rather than depending entirely on annual budget appropriations. They also differ from privately owned companies because their shares—if any—are held by the government, and their governing boards are typically appointed by ministers or the head of government.

Characteristics

Common features of government corporations include:

  • Creation by a specific statute, charter, or executive act;
  • A board of directors appointed by the government;
  • A mandate to provide a public service, manage a public asset, or operate in a strategic sector such as transport, energy, postal services, or broadcasting;
  • Financial reporting and audit requirements imposed by the state;
  • Some degree of exemption from the civil service rules that govern ordinary ministries.

Examples

Well-known examples include the United States Postal Service analogues in many countries, national railway companies, public broadcasters such as the BBC, and corporations such as Amtrak and the Tennessee Valley Authority in the United States. In the United Kingdom, bodies established under statute with commercial functions have historically been called public corporations. Many countries also create government corporations to run lotteries, manage sovereign investment, or operate ports and airports.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue that the corporate form allows professional management, borrowing on capital markets, and insulation from short-term political pressure. Critics respond that government corporations can combine the worst features of both sectors: they may enjoy implicit state guarantees that distort competition, face weaker accountability than ministries, and accumulate liabilities that ultimately fall on taxpayers. Debates over privatization and nationalization frequently centre on whether a given activity is better performed by a government corporation, a regulated private firm, or a government department.
