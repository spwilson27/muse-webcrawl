Indian English is the set of English-language dialects native to India and the Indian subcontinent. English has an official status in India alongside Hindi: it is an associate official language of the Union government and is widely used in law, higher education, business, and the media. India is often said to have one of the largest English-speaking populations in the world, although estimates of fluent speakers vary widely.

History

English was introduced to India under British rule, particularly through the East India Company and the colonial education system promoted by Thomas Babington Macaulay's 1835 "Minute on Indian Education". After independence in 1947, the Constitution of India provided for Hindi as the official language with English continuing as an associate official language, a status made indefinite by the Official Languages Act of 1963.

Features

Indian English has distinctive vocabulary drawn from Indian languages, including words such as prepone (the opposite of postpone), co-brother (brother-in-law), out of station (away from home), and terms like lakh (100,000) and crore (10 million) used in counting. Pronunciation is influenced by regional mother tongues; most varieties are syllable-timed rather than stress-timed, and retroflex consonants are common. Spellings generally follow British conventions.

Varieties

Regional varieties reflect the dominant local language, including Hindi-influenced English in the north, Bengali English in the east, Tamil English in the south, and others. Sociolinguists have also described a spectrum from acrolectal standard Indian English through mesolectal forms to basilectal varieties sometimes informally called "Babu English" or "Butler English".

Literature

Indian English literature includes internationally acclaimed authors such as R. K. Narayan, Salman Rushdie, Vikram Seth, Arundhati Roy, and Amitav Ghosh. Rushdie's Midnight's Children (1981) is frequently cited as a landmark that reshaped postcolonial writing in English.
