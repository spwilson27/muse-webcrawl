Pneumonia is an inflammatory condition of the lung primarily affecting the small air sacs known as alveoli. It is usually caused by infection with viruses or bacteria, and less commonly by other microorganisms, certain medications or conditions such as autoimmune diseases. When the alveoli fill with pus and fluid, oxygen exchange is impaired, producing cough, fever, chest pain and difficulty breathing.

Causes and classification

The most common bacterial cause is Streptococcus pneumoniae; other frequent pathogens include Haemophilus influenzae, Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Legionella, respiratory viruses such as influenza and RSV, and — in immunocompromised patients — fungi such as Pneumocystis jirovecii. Pneumonia is commonly classified by where it was acquired: community-acquired, hospital-acquired, healthcare-associated, or aspiration pneumonia following inhalation of food, drink or vomit. It may also be described anatomically as lobar pneumonia, bronchopneumonia, or interstitial pneumonia.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on symptoms and physical examination (including crackles heard through a stethoscope), confirmed typically by chest X-ray showing infiltrates; blood tests, sputum cultures and pulse oximetry assess severity. Bacterial pneumonia is treated with antibiotics; viral pneumonia is usually managed supportively, with antivirals for influenza in some cases. Severe cases may require hospitalisation, supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation.

Epidemiology and prevention

Pneumonia affects approximately 450 million people a year and results in about 4 million deaths, making it a leading cause of death in children under five and in the elderly. Vaccines against pneumococcus, Haemophilus influenzae type b and influenza, together with smoking cessation and hand hygiene, reduce risk. William Osler called pneumonia "the captain of the men of death"; its mortality fell dramatically with the introduction of antibiotics in the 20th century.
