Mitral valve

The mitral valve, also known as the bicuspid valve or left atrioventricular valve, is one of the four valves of the heart. It lies between the left atrium and the left ventricle and allows blood to flow from the atrium into the ventricle while preventing backflow during ventricular contraction. Its name derives from its resemblance to a bishop's mitre.

Anatomy

Unlike the three-cusped tricuspid valve on the right side of the heart, the mitral valve has two leaflets, an anterior and a posterior. The leaflets are tethered by fibrous cords, the chordae tendineae, to the papillary muscles of the left ventricle, which prevent the leaflets from prolapsing into the atrium during systole. The valve orifice is surrounded by a fibrous ring, the mitral annulus.

Function

During diastole the valve opens and oxygenated blood flows from the left atrium into the left ventricle. When the ventricle contracts, rising pressure closes the leaflets; the resulting sound contributes to the first heart sound (S1) heard on auscultation.

Diseases

  • Mitral valve prolapse — one or both leaflets bulge into the atrium, common and often benign.
  • Mitral regurgitation — leakage of blood back into the atrium, caused by prolapse, endocarditis, or ventricular dilation.
  • Mitral stenosis — narrowing of the valve, most often a late consequence of rheumatic fever.

Severe disease is treated by surgical repair or valve replacement, or increasingly by transcatheter procedures.
