Sex hormone

ClassSteroid hormones (gonadal steroids)
Main typesAndrogens, estrogens, progestogens
Produced byGonads (testes, ovaries), adrenal glands, placenta
Regulated byHypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis (GnRH, LH, FSH)
FunctionsSexual development, reproduction, secondary sex characteristics

Sex hormones, also known as sex steroids or gonadal steroids, are steroid hormones that interact with vertebrate androgen and estrogen receptors and govern sexual development and reproduction. The principal classes are androgens (such as testosterone), estrogens (such as estradiol) and progestogens (such as progesterone). All are synthesised from cholesterol, mainly in the gonads, with smaller amounts from the adrenal glands; both sexes produce all three classes, in different proportions.

Functions

Before birth, sex hormones direct the differentiation of the reproductive tract and external genitalia. At puberty, rising levels drive the development of secondary sexual characteristics — breast development and menstruation in females, voice deepening, facial hair and increased muscle mass in males — and the maturation of gametes. In adults, they regulate the menstrual cycle and ovulation, spermatogenesis, libido, bone density, fat distribution and aspects of mood and cognition. Anti-Müllerian hormone and inhibin also participate in reproductive regulation, though they are peptides rather than steroids.

Regulation and medicine

Secretion is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis: GnRH from the hypothalamus stimulates pituitary LH and FSH, which act on the gonads, with negative feedback from the sex steroids themselves. Synthetic sex hormones are widely used medicines: combined oral contraceptives (estrogen plus progestin), hormone replacement therapy for menopause and hypogonadism, anabolic steroids (abused in sport), anti-androgens, and hormone therapies for prostate and breast cancers. Endocrine-disrupting chemicals in the environment can mimic or block sex hormones, a topic of ongoing research.
