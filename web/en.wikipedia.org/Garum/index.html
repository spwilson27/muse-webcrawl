Garum

Image placeholder: fermentation vats at a Roman garum factory in Spain

Garum is a fermented fish sauce that was used as a condiment in the cuisines of Phoenicia, ancient Greece, Rome, Carthage, and later Byzantium. Liquamen is a similar preparation, and at times they are considered synonymous. Although garum enjoyed its greatest popularity in the Roman world, earlier variants existed among the Greeks and Phoenicians.

Production

Garum was made by crushing and fermenting the intestines and other parts of fish — such as mackerel, tuna, anchovies, or sardines — in brine in stone vats exposed to the sun for weeks or months. Enzymes from the fish's own gut digested the mixture, yielding a clear amber liquid rich in glutamates and hence strongly savoury (umami). The liquid was drawn off and sold in amphorae, often labelled with its maker, origin, and grade, while the solid residue, called allec, was sold cheaply as a paste for the poor.

Use and trade

Production centres ringed the Mediterranean, notably in Hispania (Baelo Claudia, Gades), Lusitania, North Africa, and Pompeii, whose garum producer Aulus Umbricius Scaurus grew wealthy from the trade. Garum was used in the great majority of recipes in the Roman cookbook Apicius, seasoned wine sauces, and was valued as a medicine as well as a condiment. Its popularity waned in the early Middle Ages in the West, though fermented fish sauces survived elsewhere — modern analogues include Southeast Asian fish sauces, Italian colatura di alici, and Worcestershire sauce.
