Vitrification

Vitrification
DefinitionTransformation of a substance into a glass (amorphous solid)
ApplicationsGlassmaking, cryopreservation, nuclear waste immobilization

Vitrification (from Latin vitrum, "glass") is the transformation of a substance into a glass, that is to say, a non-crystalline amorphous solid. In the production of ceramics, vitrification is the progressive partial fusion of a clay, or of a body, as a result of a firing process. In cryobiology and other physical contexts, vitrification refers to cooling a liquid so rapidly that it solidifies without forming ice crystals.

In materials science

When a melt is cooled, it may either crystallize or, if viscosity rises fast enough near the glass transition temperature, become a glass. Silicate glasses such as window glass are the classic example. In ceramics, vitrification of the clay body during firing gives porcelain its strength, hardness, and near-zero porosity; the degree of vitrification is assessed through water absorption.

In cryopreservation

In cryobiology, vitrification uses high concentrations of cryoprotectants (such as glycerol, ethylene glycol, or DMSO) and very rapid cooling so that water forms an amorphous "glass" rather than damaging ice crystals. It is now standard for cryopreserving human oocytes and embryos in assisted reproduction and is researched for larger tissues and organs. Cryonics organizations use vitrification solutions in an attempt to preserve the brain and body after legal death.

Nuclear waste

Vitrification is a mature technology for immobilizing high-level radioactive waste: the waste is mixed with glass-forming materials and melted into borosilicate glass, which is poured into steel canisters for long-term storage, as practised at facilities such as Sellafield in the UK and La Hague in France.
