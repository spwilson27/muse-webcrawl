Homo Sacer

Homo sacer (Latin for "sacred man" or "accursed man") is a figure of Roman law: a person who is banned and may be killed by anybody, but may not be sacrificed in a religious ritual. The status resulted from being judged for a crime; the person was expelled from the community and their life was placed outside both human and divine law.

Agamben's Homo Sacer project

The term gained wide currency through the Italian philosopher Giorgio Agamben's book Homo Sacer: Sovereign Power and Bare Life (1995), the first volume of his Homo Sacer series. Agamben uses the figure to analyze the structure of sovereign power and the concept of "bare life" (zoē as opposed to bios, qualified political life). Drawing on Carl Schmitt's definition of the sovereign as the one who decides on the state of exception, Agamben argues that the production of bare life — life that can be killed but not sacrificed — is the originary activity of sovereignty, and controversially describes the concentration camp as the "nomos of the modern".

Influence

Agamben's reading has been influential, and contested, in political theory, legal studies, and refugee studies, where the figure of the stateless person and the refugee camp is often analyzed in terms of the state of exception.
