MALT lymphoma (mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma), formally extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of MALT type, is a low-grade B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma that arises in lymphoid tissue associated with mucosal sites. It most often affects the stomach, but also occurs in the salivary glands, lungs, ocular adnexa, thyroid, skin and other organs.

Causes

MALT lymphomas develop in the setting of chronic antigenic stimulation. Gastric MALT lymphoma is strongly associated with chronic Helicobacter pylori infection; salivary gland cases arise with Sjögren syndrome, thyroid cases with Hashimoto thyroiditis, and ocular cases have been linked to Chlamydia psittaci in some populations. Recurrent chromosomal translocations, notably t(11;18) involving API2–MALT1, occur in a substantial minority and predict resistance to antibiotic therapy.

Treatment

Remarkably, early localised gastric MALT lymphoma often regresses completely after eradication of H. pylori with antibiotics alone. Other localised cases are treated with radiotherapy or surgery; disseminated disease may be managed with watchful waiting, rituximab or chemotherapy.
