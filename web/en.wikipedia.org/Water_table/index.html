Water table

The water table is the upper surface of the zone of saturation in the ground, the level below which the soil and rock are saturated with water. It separates the groundwater zone below from the capillary fringe and the unsaturated (vadose) zone above, through which water percolates downward from precipitation.

Characteristics

The water table generally follows, in subdued form, the topography of the land surface, rising beneath hills and sloping toward valleys, where it may intersect the surface to feed springs, streams, lakes and wetlands. It is the level at which water stands in a shallow well dug into an unconfined aquifer, and at that level water pressure equals atmospheric pressure.

Fluctuations

The depth of the water table varies seasonally with rainfall and snowmelt, and over longer periods with climate and pumping. Heavy groundwater abstraction for irrigation and cities lowers water tables, producing cones of depression around wells, drying springs and causing land subsidence in some regions; conversely, irrigation return flows and leaking urban infrastructure can raise local water tables. In coastal areas, pumping can induce saltwater intrusion as fresh water is drawn down.

Importance

Knowledge of the water table is essential to groundwater supply, agriculture, construction (where a high water table affects basements and foundations), contaminant hydrogeology and ecosystem management. “Perched” water tables can occur above the main one where a local low-permeability layer traps infiltrating water.
