Fond

In classical French cuisine, a fond is a concentrated stock or base used in the preparation of sauces, soups and braised dishes. The word is French for "base" or "foundation", reflecting the stock's role as the foundation of a sauce. Fonds are made by simmering bones, meat and aromatic vegetables in water, then straining and often reducing the liquid to intensify its flavour.

Types

The principal fonds include fond blanc (white stock, from raw or blanched bones), fond brun (brown stock, from roasted bones, giving a darker colour and deeper flavour), veal stock (fond de veau), chicken stock and fish stock (fumet). A closely related technique is deglazing: the browned residue left in a pan after searing meat — also called fond in English-language cooking — is dissolved with wine or stock to form the base of a pan sauce.

Use

Stocks formed the backbone of the classical French repertoire codified by Auguste Escoffier. A well-made fond supplies body through dissolved gelatin, savouriness through amino acids, and a neutral background that supports the flavours of the finished dish. Modern kitchens often substitute commercial stock cubes or concentrated pastes, though chefs generally regard freshly made fond as superior.
