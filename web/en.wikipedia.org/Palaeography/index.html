Palaeography

Palaeography (US spelling paleography; from Greek παλαιός, palaiós, 'old', and γράφειν, gráphein, 'to write') is the study of historic writing systems and the deciphering and dating of historical manuscripts. It includes the study of the history of handwriting and the forms of letters, abbreviations, punctuation and materials used by scribes.

History

The discipline is conventionally traced to Jean Mabillon's De re diplomatica (1681), which established systematic criteria for authenticating medieval charters. Bernard de Montfaucon's Palaeographia Graeca (1708) gave the field its name and extended the method to Greek manuscripts. In the 19th century, scholars such as Ludwig Traube and E. A. Lowe developed detailed classifications of Latin scripts — uncial, half-uncial, Caroline minuscule, and the Gothic hands of the later Middle Ages.

Method

Palaeographers analyse the ductus (the order and direction of strokes), letter forms, ligatures, abbreviations and layout of a hand, comparing them with dated examples to assign a manuscript a date and place of origin. The study extends across writing cultures: Greek and Latin palaeography, papyrology for texts on papyrus, and analogous disciplines for Arabic, Hebrew, Chinese and other scripts. Dating by script is relative and is combined with codicology — the study of the physical book — and with textual and historical evidence.

Applications

Palaeographical skills underpin the editing of ancient and medieval texts, the authentication of documents, and archival work. Decipherments such as Michael Ventris's reading of Linear B drew on careful analysis of sign forms. In the digital era, handwriting-recognition software trained on transcribed manuscripts is extending palaeographical analysis to entire archives.
