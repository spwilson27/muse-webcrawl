Sefer Torah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Sefer Torah adorned with a silver crown and breastplate.

A Sefer Torah (Hebrew: סֵפֶר תּוֹרָה, "Book of Torah"; plural Sifrei Torah) is a handwritten copy of the Torah, the holiest book in Judaism, containing the Five Books of Moses: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. It is written by hand in Hebrew calligraphy on parchment by a specially trained scribe, a sofer, according to strict rules.

Production

A Sefer Torah contains 304,805 letters, and a skilled scribe may take about a year to complete one. The parchment (klaf) is prepared from the skin of a kosher animal, and the text is written with a quill and specially prepared ink. Every letter must be perfectly formed; a single error can render the scroll pasul (unfit) until corrected. The sheets are sewn together and attached to two wooden rollers called atzei chaim ("trees of life").

Use and honors

The scroll is stored in the Torah ark (aron hakodesh) in a synagogue, dressed in an ornate mantle, breastplate, and crown when not in use. Portions of the Torah are read aloud in synagogue services on Mondays, Thursdays, Shabbat, and festivals, completing the entire scroll over a yearly cycle celebrated at Simchat Torah. When a new scroll is completed, its dedication (hachnasat sefer Torah) is celebrated festively, and fulfilling the commandment to write a Torah scroll is traditionally the last of the 613 commandments.
