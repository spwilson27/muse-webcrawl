Dirichlet series

In mathematics, a Dirichlet series is any series of the form Σ an / ns, where s is a complex number and an is a complex sequence. It is a special case of a general Dirichlet series. Dirichlet series play a central role in analytic number theory, where they encode arithmetic information in a form amenable to complex analysis.

Examples

The most famous Dirichlet series is the Riemann zeta function, ζ(s) = Σ 1/ns, corresponding to an = 1 for all n. Other examples include the Dirichlet L-functions, formed with a Dirichlet character as coefficients, which Dirichlet himself used in 1837 to prove his theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, and the Dirichlet eta function.

Convergence

A Dirichlet series converges in some right half-plane Re(s) > σc, where σc is the abscissa of convergence; there is also an abscissa of absolute convergence σa, and the strip between them is a region of conditional convergence. Unlike power series, which converge in discs, Dirichlet series converge in half-planes because the variable appears in the exponent.

Euler products and analytic continuation

When the coefficients an are multiplicative, the series factors over the primes as an Euler product, Πp (1 + ap/ps + …), linking the series to the distribution of prime numbers. Many Dirichlet series admit analytic continuation and a functional equation relating values at s and 1 − s; the Riemann hypothesis concerns the zeros of the continued zeta function.
