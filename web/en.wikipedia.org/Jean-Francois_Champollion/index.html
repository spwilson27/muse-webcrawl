Jean-Francois Champollion

Jean-François Champollion (23 December 1790 – 4 March 1832), also known as Champollion le jeune ("the Younger"), was a French philologist and orientalist who deciphered the Egyptian hieroglyphs, most famously through his work on the Rosetta Stone. His breakthrough, announced in 1822, founded the science of Egyptology.

Early life

Champollion was born in Figeac in southwestern France, the youngest son of a bookseller. A precocious linguist, he was largely guided by his elder brother Jacques-Joseph, an archaeologist, and by his teens had studied Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, Chaldean, Persian and, crucially, Coptic — the latest form of the ancient Egyptian language, which he correctly believed held the key to the hieroglyphs.

Decipherment

Working from copies of the Rosetta Stone, whose parallel texts in hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek had been known since its discovery in 1799, Champollion built on the work of predecessors such as Thomas Young. In his Lettre à M. Dacier of September 1822 he demonstrated that hieroglyphic writing was a mixed system of phonetic, ideographic and determinative signs, and that the cartouches contained the names of rulers such as Ptolemy and Cleopatra written largely phonetically. His Précis du système hiéroglyphique (1824) laid out the system in full.

Later career and death

Champollion became the first curator of the Egyptian collections at the Louvre in 1826 and led a Franco-Tuscan expedition to Egypt in 1828–1829, copying inscriptions throughout the Nile valley. In 1831 he was appointed to the first chair of Egyptology at the Collège de France. Exhausted by his Egyptian journey, he died in Paris in 1832, aged only 41. His Egyptian grammar and dictionary were published posthumously by his brother.
