Viral evolution

Viral evolution is a subfield of evolutionary biology and virology concerned with the evolution of viruses. Viruses evolve rapidly because of their short generation times and large population sizes, combined — especially in RNA viruses — with high mutation rates due to the lack of proofreading in their polymerases. The study of viral evolution underpins the prediction of influenza strains for vaccines, the tracking of epidemics such as HIV and SARS-CoV-2, and the understanding of how viruses jump between host species.

Mechanisms

Key mechanisms include mutation (point mutations, insertions and deletions), genetic drift and selection, recombination, and — in segmented viruses such as influenza — reassortment ("antigenic shift"), which can produce pandemic strains. Gradual accumulation of point mutations in surface proteins is termed antigenic drift. Viruses within a host also exist as a quasispecies, a cloud of related variants subject to selection.

Origins of viruses

The deep origins of viruses remain debated among three main hypotheses: the virus-first (coevolution) hypothesis, the regressive (reduction) hypothesis, in which viruses are degenerate descendants of cellular parasites, and the escape (progressive) hypothesis, in which viruses arose from fragments of cellular genetic material that gained the ability to move between cells.
