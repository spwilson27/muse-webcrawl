Monarchy

The crown, enduring symbol of monarchy

A monarchy is a form of government in which a person, the monarch, reigns as head of state for life or until abdication, with the position usually inherited within a family or dynasty. Monarchies are one of the oldest forms of government, arising in the earliest states of Mesopotamia, Egypt, and China. Today some 43 sovereign states have a monarch as head of state, including 15 Commonwealth realms that share King Charles III.

Types

In an absolute monarchy the monarch holds supreme authority unconstrained by law or legislature, as in Saudi Arabia, Brunei, and Eswatini. In a constitutional monarchy the monarch's powers are limited by a constitution, and real governing authority rests with elected officials — the United Kingdom, Japan, Spain, and the Netherlands are examples. Intermediate "semi-constitutional" systems, such as Jordan and Morocco, give the monarch substantial powers. Monarchs are styled king or queen, emperor, sultan, emir, prince, or — in the case of the Pope in Vatican City — an elected theocratic sovereign.

Succession

Succession is typically hereditary, governed by rules such as primogeniture — historically male-preference, increasingly absolute (eldest child regardless of sex) in European monarchies. Elective monarchy, once common in the Holy Roman Empire and Poland, survives in Malaysia, Cambodia, and the Vatican.

History and decline

Monarchy was the dominant form of government for millennia, often justified by divine right. The American and French Revolutions began its modern decline; the First World War ended the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman monarchies, and the 20th century replaced most remaining monarchs with republics. Surviving monarchies have generally endured by ceding political power while retaining ceremonial and symbolic roles.
