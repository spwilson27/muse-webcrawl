Special purpose entity
Abbreviations: SPE, SPV
Also called: Special purpose vehicle, special purpose company
Uses: Securitisation, project finance, risk isolation

A special purpose entity (SPE), also called a special purpose vehicle (SPV) or special purpose company (SPC), is a legal entity — typically a limited company or limited partnership — created to fulfil a narrow, specific objective, most often to isolate financial risk. By design the entity is "bankruptcy remote": its assets and liabilities are legally separated from those of its sponsoring company, so that the sponsor's creditors cannot reach them (and vice versa).

Uses

SPEs are fundamental to securitisation, in which mortgages or other receivables are transferred to an SPV that issues asset-backed securities against them. They are also used in project finance to hold a single infrastructure project, in structured finance and leasing, in property development, and to hold intellectual property or conduct research joint ventures. The special-purpose acquisition company (SPAC) is a publicly listed relative of the concept.

Abuses and regulation

Because SPEs could be kept off a sponsor's balance sheet, they were notoriously abused: Enron used entities such as Chewco and the LJM partnerships to hide debt and inflate profits, a central mechanism of its 2001 collapse. The scandal prompted accounting reforms — notably US standard FIN 46 and later FAS 166/167 — requiring consolidation of entities a company effectively controls, and IFRS 10 does likewise. SPEs nonetheless remain a legitimate and ubiquitous tool of modern finance.
