Rent-seeking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rent-seeking is the expenditure of resources in order to bring about an uncompensated transfer of goods or services from another person or persons to oneself as the result of a decision in one's favour. It is an economic concept that occurs when an entity seeks to gain added wealth without any reciprocal contribution of productivity. Typically, it revolves around government actions: the term was developed by the economist Gordon Tullock in 1967 and popularised by Anne Krueger in 1974.

The concept is closely related to public choice theory and to the study of lobbying, whereby interest groups expend resources to influence legislation, tariffs, quotas, licences or monopolies in their favour. Unlike profit-seeking in a competitive market, rent-seeking is generally considered socially wasteful, because the resources spent capturing the transfer produce nothing of value for society as a whole.

History of the concept

The underlying idea predates the term. Adam Smith observed that merchants and manufacturers often sought privileges from the state, and nineteenth-century critics of tariffs described similar behaviour. The modern analysis began with Gordon Tullock's 1967 paper "The Welfare Costs of Tariffs, Monopolies, and Theft," which argued that the social cost of a monopoly or tariff includes not only the conventional deadweight-loss triangle but also the resources spent to obtain or defend the privilege — later called the "Tullock rectangle."

Anne Krueger coined the term "rent-seeking" in her 1974 article "The Political Economy of the Rent-Seeking Society," which estimated that rents from import licences amounted to a substantial share of national income in India and Turkey. Jagdish Bhagwati later generalised the idea under the label "directly unproductive profit-seeking" (DUP) activities.

Mechanisms

Rent-seeking typically operates through political channels. Examples include lobbying for subsidies, import protection, occupational licensing, government contracts, tax loopholes, or regulations that restrict competition. Because the beneficiaries of a rent are usually a small, organised group while the costs are spread thinly over taxpayers or consumers, rent-seekers often succeed despite the total losses exceeding their gains.

Consequences

Economists argue that rent-seeking diverts entrepreneurial talent from innovation toward political influence, raises consumer prices, entrenches incumbent firms, and can corrupt institutions. In developing economies, pervasive rent-seeking is associated with slower growth. Proposed remedies include transparency, competitive procurement, sunset clauses on privileges, constitutional limits on government discretion, and trade liberalisation.
