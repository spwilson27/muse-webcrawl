The Social Credit Party of Alberta was a provincial political party in Alberta, Canada, that governed the province from 1935 to 1971—one of the longest unbroken runs in Canadian political history. It was founded on the social credit monetary theories of Major C. H. Douglas and was led to power by radio evangelist and school principal William Aberhart.

Origins

During the Great Depression, Alberta farmers faced collapsing wheat prices, drought, and crushing debt. Aberhart, host of a popular Calgary radio ministry, began preaching social credit economics on air, promising a "$25 monthly dividend" to every Albertan financed by provincial credit. Study groups sprang up across the province, and in the 1935 provincial election the new party stunned the country by winning 56 of 63 seats, destroying the incumbent United Farmers of Alberta government.

In government

The Aberhart government's attempts to issue "prosperity certificates" and control banking were repeatedly struck down or disallowed: the Supreme Court of Canada and the federal government ruled provincial banking legislation unconstitutional, and in 1937 backbenchers revolted over the failure to deliver the dividends. The government survived and gradually moderated. After Aberhart's death in 1943, Ernest Manning became premier and led the party to seven consecutive election victories, reorienting it toward free-enterprise conservatism and prudent management of Alberta's growing oil revenues after the 1947 Leduc discovery.

Decline

Manning retired in 1968 and was succeeded by Harry Strom. In the 1971 election, Peter Lougheed's Progressive Conservatives ended 36 years of Social Credit rule. The party rapidly collapsed, losing its remaining seats by 1982, and became a marginal presence, though it continued to field candidates for decades. It was deregistered in 2017 after failing to meet requirements, ending an 82-year presence in Alberta politics.

Legacy

Alberta Social Credit pioneered the province's pattern of long-lived political dynasties and populist, anti-establishment politics. Its monetary experiments remain a landmark case in Canadian federalism and constitutional law.
