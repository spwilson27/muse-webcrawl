Aliyah

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aliyah (Hebrew: עֲלִיָּה, "ascent") is the immigration of Jews from the diaspora to the Land of Israel (Eretz Yisrael) and, since 1948, to the State of Israel. A Jew who makes aliyah is called an oleh (male) or olah (female); the opposite act, emigration from Israel, is called yerida ("descent"). Aliyah is a central concept of Zionism and is facilitated by Israel's Law of Return, which grants Jews the right to Israeli citizenship.

Waves before 1948

Organised immigration to Ottoman and Mandate Palestine is conventionally divided into waves. The First Aliyah (1882–1903) brought pioneers largely from Russia and Yemen who founded agricultural settlements. The Second Aliyah (1904–1914) included many socialist pioneers who founded the kibbutz movement and revived Hebrew culture. Further waves followed under the British Mandate: the Third (1919–1923), the Fourth (1924–1929, driven partly by economic pressures and American immigration restrictions) and the Fifth Aliyah (1929–1939, heavily composed of German Jews fleeing Nazism). Illegal immigration (Aliyah Bet) continued despite British quotas, including after the Second World War, when Holocaust survivors sought entry.

After statehood

Following Israeli independence in 1948, mass immigration transformed the new state: Holocaust survivors and nearly the entire Jewish populations of countries such as Iraq, Yemen and Libya arrived within a few years, doubling Israel's Jewish population. Later major waves included Moroccan and other North African Jews in the 1950s–60s, the airlifts of Ethiopian Jews (Operations Moses and Solomon), and approximately one million immigrants from the former Soviet Union after 1989, who reshaped Israeli society, culture and politics. Aliyah continues today, encouraged by organisations such as the Jewish Agency and Nefesh B'Nefesh.
