John Porter Lucas (April 14, 1890 – December 24, 1949) was a senior United States Army officer who fought in the First and Second World Wars. He is most remembered for commanding the VI Corps at the Anzio beachhead in Italy in early 1944, where his caution in consolidating the landing rather than advancing rapidly on Rome led to his relief — one of the most debated command decisions of the war.

Early career

Lucas graduated from West Point in 1911 and served on the Mexican border and in the Philippines. In the First World War he was wounded while commanding a battalion in France. Between the wars he held staff and teaching posts, and in 1942–43 served as Dwight D. Eisenhower's deputy in North Africa and as commander of the 3rd Infantry Division during the invasion of Sicily's aftermath, before taking over VI Corps for the Salerno landings in September 1943.

Anzio

In January 1944 Lucas commanded Operation Shingle, the amphibious landing at Anzio behind the German Gustav Line. Ordered to secure a beachhead and advance on the Alban Hills, he chose first to consolidate his two divisions; German forces under Field Marshal Kesselring rapidly contained the beachhead, which was then besieged for four months. Winston Churchill famously complained that he had hoped to hurl a wildcat ashore and instead got a stranded whale. Lucas was relieved in February 1944 and replaced by Lucian Truscott.

Later service

Lucas finished the war commanding the Third Army's successor formation in the United States and afterwards the Fifth Army in Chicago. He died on Christmas Eve 1949. His diaries show a commander conscious of his mission's risks and constrained by resources, and historians remain divided over whether bolder action at Anzio would have succeeded or ended in disaster.
