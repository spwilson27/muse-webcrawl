A dwarf planet is a small planetary-mass object that is in direct orbit of the Sun, massive enough to be gravitationally rounded, but insufficient to achieve orbital dominance like the eight classical planets of the Solar System. The term was adopted in 2006 by the International Astronomical Union (IAU) as a category of sub-planetary objects, following the discovery of more trans-Neptunian objects comparable to Pluto.

The IAU recognised five dwarf planets: Ceres (in the asteroid belt), and the trans-Neptunian objects Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and Eris. Many more bodies probably qualify—candidates include Orcus, Quaoar, Sedna, Gonggong, and Chariklo—and estimates range into the hundreds or thousands.

Definition

Under IAU Resolution 5A (2006), a dwarf planet is a celestial body that (a) is in orbit around the Sun, (b) has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid-body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape, (c) has not cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit, and (d) is not a satellite. Criterion (c) distinguishes dwarf planets from the eight planets, which have gravitationally dominated their orbital zones.

History

From its discovery in 1930, Pluto was classified as the ninth planet. The discovery of the Kuiper belt from 1992 onward, and especially of the massive scattered-disc object Eris in 2005—initially reported as larger than Pluto—forced a formal definition of "planet." The 2006 IAU vote reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet, a decision that remains popularly and scientifically contested; NASA's New Horizons mission flew by Pluto in 2015, revealing a geologically active world.

Recognised dwarf planets

  • Ceres — the largest object in the asteroid belt, visited by the Dawn spacecraft (2015–2018); diameter ~940 km.
  • Pluto — the largest known Kuiper belt object, with five moons including Charon; diameter ~2,377 km.
  • Haumea — an elongated, rapidly rotating body with a ring and two moons, source of a large collisional family.
  • Makemake — a large classical Kuiper belt object with one known moon.
  • Eris — the most massive dwarf planet, in the scattered disc, with the moon Dysnomia.

Controversy

Some planetary scientists, notably Alan Stern (who coined "dwarf planet"), argue for a geophysical definition of planet based on intrinsic properties rather than orbital dynamics, under which dwarf planets are a subtype of planet and many moons would also count as planets. The IAU has not revisited the definition, and public debate—particularly over Pluto's status—continues.
