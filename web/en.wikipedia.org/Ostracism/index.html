Ostracism


Origin: Athens, instituted c. 508/507 BC
Mechanism: Vote on pottery shards (ostraka)
Penalty: Ten years' exile

In ancient Athens, ostracism (Greek: ostrakismos) was a democratic procedure under which any citizen could be expelled from the city-state for ten years. A vote was held once a year; citizens wrote the name of the person they wished to exile on a shard of pottery, an ostrakon. If at least 6,000 votes were cast, the man with the most votes had to leave Athens within ten days, remaining exiled for ten years while retaining his property and citizenship.

Purpose and history

The institution, traditionally attributed to Cleisthenes around 508/507 BC but first used in 487 BC, was intended to prevent any individual from gaining enough power to establish a tyranny, and to settle major political disputes non-violently. Prominent victims included Aristides, Themistocles, Cimon and Thucydides son of Melesias. The procedure fell into disuse after 415 BC, when rivals Nicias and Alcibiades combined their supporters to ostracise the demagogue Hyperbolus instead, discrediting the institution.

Modern usage

In modern usage, "ostracism" has come to mean exclusion from a society or group in general, and in social psychology it denotes the experience of being ignored or excluded, which research shows threatens fundamental needs for belonging and self-esteem.
