Non-cooperation movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The non-cooperation movement was a mass political campaign of civil disobedience launched in British India in 1920 under the leadership of Mahatma Gandhi and the Indian National Congress. It sought to obtain self-government (swaraj) by withdrawing Indian cooperation from British institutions: boycotting foreign goods, government schools, law courts, titles, and legislative councils, and promoting hand-spun khadi and national education. The movement is also the name given to later campaigns of non-cooperation in other countries and contexts, and in India the term is associated with subsequent phases including the 1942 and later protest movements as well as a movement of the same name in Bangladesh in 2024.

Background

The campaign grew out of discontent with the Rowlatt Acts, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919, and Muslim anger over the treatment of the Ottoman Caliphate, which Gandhi linked with the Khilafat movement. At the Calcutta special session of Congress in September 1920, and again at Nagpur in December, the programme of non-violent non-cooperation was adopted.

Course

Lawyers such as C. R. Das and Motilal Nehru gave up their practices, students left government institutions for national schools, and bonfires of foreign cloth were held across the country. The movement was suspended by Gandhi in February 1922 after the Chauri Chaura incident, in which a mob killed 22 policemen; Gandhi held that the movement had departed from strict non-violence.

Legacy

Although swaraj was not immediately achieved, the movement transformed Congress into a mass organisation and established non-violent civil disobedience as the central method of the Indian independence struggle.
