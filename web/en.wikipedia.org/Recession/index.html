Recession

Recessions appear as sustained declines in output, employment and trade across the whole economy.

In economics, a recession is a business cycle contraction that occurs when there is a widespread drop in economic activity. Recessions generally occur when consumer spending falls sharply, and they are associated with rising unemployment, falling industrial production and retail sales, and declining real gross domestic product (GDP). A commonly cited rule of thumb defines a recession as two consecutive quarters of negative GDP growth, although official dating bodies use a broader set of indicators.

Definitions

In the United States, recessions are officially dated by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months, normally visible in real GDP, real income, employment, industrial production and wholesale-retail sales. Because the NBER weighs many indicators, its turning points need not match the two-quarter rule; the 2001 recession, for example, did not include two consecutive quarters of falling GDP. The euro area uses a similar committee-based approach through the CEPR.

Causes

Recessions have many possible triggers. They may follow the bursting of an asset price bubble, a sudden tightening of monetary policy to control inflation, a financial crisis that impairs lending, a collapse in consumer or business confidence, or external shocks such as sharp increases in energy prices. The Great Recession of 2007–2009 followed the collapse of a housing bubble and a crisis in the banking system, while the brief COVID-19 recession of 2020 resulted from the pandemic and the public health restrictions imposed in response.

Effects and policy responses

During recessions, unemployment rises, business investment falls, government tax revenues shrink and welfare spending grows, widening budget deficits. Central banks typically respond by cutting interest rates and, in recent episodes, by purchasing assets (quantitative easing), while governments may deploy fiscal stimulus such as tax cuts and increased public spending. Economists debate the balance between such interventions and the risks of inflation and public debt.

Shapes and severity

Commentators describe recoveries with letters: a V-shaped recession falls and rebounds quickly; a U-shaped one lingers at the bottom; a W-shaped or "double-dip" recession recovers briefly before falling again; and an L-shaped recession fails to regain its previous trend for many years. A recession that is especially deep and prolonged is termed a depression, although there is no universally agreed threshold separating the two.
