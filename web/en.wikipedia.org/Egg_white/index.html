Egg white

Egg white is the clear liquid (also called the albumen or the glair/glaire) contained within an egg. In chickens it is formed from the layers of secretions of the anterior section of the hen's oviduct during the passage of the egg. It forms around fertilized or unfertilized egg yolks. The primary natural purpose of egg white is to protect the yolk and provide additional nutrition for the growth of the embryo (when fertilized).

Composition

Egg white consists primarily of about 90% water into which about 10% proteins are dissolved, including albumins, mucoproteins, and globulins. Its principal protein is ovalbumin (54% of total protein), followed by ovotransferrin (conalbumin, 12%) and ovomucoid (11%). Others include lysozyme, an enzyme with antibacterial action, and avidin, which binds biotin tightly. Unlike the yolk, which is high in lipids, egg white contains almost no fat and negligible carbohydrate. Raw egg white is transparent and slightly opalescent; cooking denatures its proteins, turning it white and firm.

Culinary uses

When beaten, egg whites form stable foams — the foam's volume can expand six to eight times — making them essential for meringues, soufflés, angel food cake, mousses, and cocktails such as the whiskey sour. Fresh egg white has a pH that rises from about 7.6 to about 9.2 as carbon dioxide escapes during storage, which affects whipping behavior. Egg whites are also used as a clarifying agent for consommés and, historically, as a binder in gilding and photography (albumen prints).

Nutrition and allergy

Egg whites are a low-calorie, fat-free source of high-quality protein popular in sports nutrition, though most of the egg's vitamins and minerals are in the yolk. Egg allergy, most commonly to ovomucoid, is among the most frequent food allergies in young children, many of whom outgrow it. Raw egg whites carry a small risk of salmonella contamination and their avidin can impair biotin absorption if consumed raw in quantity.
