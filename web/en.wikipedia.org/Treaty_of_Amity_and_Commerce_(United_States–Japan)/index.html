Treaty of Amity and Commerce (United States–Japan)

The Treaty of Amity and Commerce between the United States and Japan, sometimes called the Harris Treaty, was signed on 29 July 1858 at Edo (now Tokyo) by the first American consul general to Japan, Townsend Harris, and representatives of the Tokugawa shogunate. Following the Convention of Kanagawa of 1854, the Harris Treaty opened full trade between the two countries and established the framework of Japan's "unequal treaties" with the Western powers.

Terms

The treaty opened the ports of Kanagawa (Yokohama), Nagasaki, Niigata, and Hyōgo (Kobe) to American trade, with Edo and Osaka opened to residence. It fixed low import and export duties largely beyond Japanese control, granted extraterritoriality to American citizens in Japan, permitted free trade without interference by Japanese officials, and allowed an American minister to reside in Edo. Similar treaties — the Ansei Treaties — were soon concluded with the Netherlands, Russia, Britain, and France. Resentment of the treaties' unequal terms fueled the sonnō jōi movement and contributed to the fall of the shogunate; revision was achieved only in 1894–1899, and full tariff autonomy in 1911.
