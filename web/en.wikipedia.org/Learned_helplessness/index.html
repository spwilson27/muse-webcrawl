Learned helplessness

Learned helplessness is a psychological state in which a person or animal, after experiencing repeated uncontrollable aversive events, fails to avoid or escape them even when escape becomes possible, having learned that outcomes are independent of its responses.

The phenomenon was described by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier in experiments in the 1960s at the University of Pennsylvania, in which dogs exposed to inescapable electric shocks later failed to escape shocks they could avoid. Seligman extended the theory, with an attributional reformulation by Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale (1978), to help explain human depression: people who attribute negative events to stable, global and internal causes are more prone to helplessness and depressive symptoms.

The concept has been applied in education, health, organisational behaviour and animal welfare, and is related to locus of control and self-efficacy.
