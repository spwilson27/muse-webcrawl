Popped rice, also widely known as puffed rice, is a type of puffed grain made from rice, prepared by applying high temperature and pressure, or by roasting pre-cooked and dried rice grains so that the moisture inside each grain flashes to steam and expands the kernel. It has been a traditional food in South and East Asia for centuries and is used both as a snack and as a breakfast cereal, and as an ingredient in sweets and street foods.

Traditional preparation

In the traditional method, raw or parboiled rice is dried — often after being soaked and steamed — and then roasted in very hot sand or salt in a wok or deep pan, sometimes with a little oil. The grains puff within seconds and are sieved from the sand. Industrial methods use steam under high pressure in "puffing guns", which release the pressure suddenly to achieve a larger, more uniform expansion. Only certain varieties of rice with a hard outer shell and suitable moisture content pop satisfactorily.

Regional uses

Popped rice is a staple snack across the Indian subcontinent, where it is known by many names — muri in Bengali, kurmura or mumra in Hindi and Marathi, pori in Tamil. In West Bengal it is the base of the street food jhal muri, tossed with spices, mustard oil, onion, chilli and peanuts; elsewhere it is mixed with jaggery to form sweet balls (lai/kurmura laddoo) and offered in Hindu rituals, where puffed paddy (laja) has ancient ceremonial significance. In the West, puffed rice is best known as a breakfast cereal (immortalised by the "Snap, Crackle and Pop" of Rice Krispies, introduced in 1928) and in chocolate-coated treats and cereal bars.

Nutrition

Popped rice is mostly carbohydrate with very little fat and modest protein; because the grains are greatly expanded, it is low in energy density by volume. Salted or sweetened commercial preparations add sodium or sugar accordingly.
