Automorphism

[Image placeholder: a structure mapped onto itself]

In mathematics, an automorphism is a structure-preserving bijection from a mathematical object to itself — an isomorphism whose domain and codomain are the same object. The set of all automorphisms of an object forms a group under composition, called its automorphism group, often denoted Aut(X).

Examples

In graph theory, an automorphism of a graph is a permutation of the vertices that preserves adjacency. In group theory, conjugation by a fixed element gives an inner automorphism; automorphisms not of this form are outer. For a field such as the complex numbers, complex conjugation is an automorphism. In Galois theory, the automorphisms of a field extension fixing the base field form the Galois group, the central object of study.

Automorphism group

The automorphism group measures the symmetry of an object: the larger the group, the more symmetric the object. A classic result, Frucht's theorem, states that every finite group can be realised as the automorphism group of some finite graph. For a set with no further structure, the automorphism group is the full symmetric group of permutations; for a rigid object it may be trivial, consisting only of the identity map.

Inner and outer automorphisms

An automorphism is inner if it arises from the object's own internal operation — for groups, conjugation. The quotient of the full automorphism group by the inner automorphism group is the outer automorphism group, which plays an important role in the classification of finite simple groups and in the study of mapping class groups.
