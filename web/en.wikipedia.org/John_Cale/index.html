John Cale

[Image placeholder: portrait of John Cale]

John Davies Cale OBE (born 9 March 1942) is a Welsh musician, composer, and record producer who was a founding member of the American rock band the Velvet Underground. Over a six-decade career he has embraced genres from rock and drone to classical and electronic music, and has released numerous solo albums.

Cale was born in Garnant, Wales, and studied music at Goldsmiths College, London, where he was drawn to the avant-garde. In 1963 he moved to New York, working with experimental composers such as John Cage and La Monte Young, whose Theatre of Eternal Music ensemble introduced him to sustained drone techniques. In 1965 he co-founded the Velvet Underground with Lou Reed; Cale played viola, bass, and keyboards on the band's first two albums, The Velvet Underground & Nico (1967) and White Light/White Heat (1968), before leaving in 1968.

Solo career and production

Cale's solo work ranges from the orchestral pop of Paris 1919 (1973) to the abrasive Fear (1974) and Sabotage/Live (1979), and includes a celebrated cover of Leonard Cohen's "Hallelujah" (1991) that strongly influenced Jeff Buckley's version. As a producer he worked on landmark albums including the Stooges' debut, Patti Smith's Horses, and albums by Nico, with whom he also recorded. Later albums include Music for a New Society (1982), Words for the Dying, and Mercy (2023).

Cale was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame as a member of the Velvet Underground in 1996.
