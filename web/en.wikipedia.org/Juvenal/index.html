Decimus Junius Juvenalis, known in English as Juvenal, was a Roman poet active in the late first and early second century AD. He is the author of the Satires, a collection of sixteen satirical poems in dactylic hexameter, divided into five books, which survey the follies, vices, and dangers of life in Rome with sustained indignation.

Life

Almost nothing certain is known of Juvenal's biography; the ancient Lives are late and unreliable, and the poems themselves offer only a fictionalized persona. He was plausibly born at Aquinum in Latium around 55 AD and may have practised as a rhetorician. His surviving work appears to have been written in the reigns of Trajan and Hadrian, looking back with bitterness at the tyranny of Domitian.

The Satires

The sixteen satires range widely in tone and length. Satire 1 announces the poet's programme: it is hard not to write satire amid the corruption of the city. Satire 3 laments the impossibility of honest life in Rome; Satire 6, the longest, attacks women and marriage; Satire 7 addresses the poverty of intellectuals; and Satire 10, on the vanity of human wishes, gave Samuel Johnson the model for The Vanity of Human Wishes.

Famous phrases from Juvenal have entered common usage: panem et circenses ("bread and circuses", Satire 10), mens sana in corpore sano ("a healthy mind in a healthy body", Satire 10), and the question quis custodiet ipsos custodes? ("who will guard the guards themselves?", Satire 6).

Style and influence

Juvenal's manner — declamatory, indignant, dense with historical and mythological exempla — set the template for the "Juvenalian" mode of formal verse satire, contrasted with the gentler "Horatian" mode. His work was little read in late antiquity's early centuries but was a school author by the fourth century, deeply influenced medieval and Renaissance satire, and shaped English satirists from John Dryden to Johnson.
