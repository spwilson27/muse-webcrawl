Full-rigged ship

A full-rigged ship (or fully rigged ship) is a sailing vessel with a sail plan of three or more masts, all of them square-rigged. The phrase is also written as "ship-rigged", since a vessel with this rig was simply called a "ship" during the age of sail.

Sail plan

A full-rigged ship has a bowsprit and three masts — fore, main, and mizzen — each carrying a lower mast, topmast, topgallant mast, and usually a royal mast. Square sails are set on yards across all three masts, supplemented by triangular staysails between the masts and a fore-and-aft sail, the spanker, on the mizzen. A large ship of the line or clipper might carry more than 30 individual sails.

History

The three-masted square rig developed from the carrack and galleon of the 15th and 16th centuries and became the standard rig for ocean-going warships and merchantmen from the 17th to the 19th century. Famous examples include HMS Victory, the tea clipper Cutty Sark (a preserved museum ship in London), and the American clipper Flying Cloud.

Related rigs

A vessel with square sails on the fore and main masts but a fore-and-aft sail on the mizzen is a barque; if only the foremast is square-rigged, it is a brigantine. Full-rigged ships are still used as sail-training vessels by several navies, including the Chilean Esmeralda and the Argentine Libertad.
