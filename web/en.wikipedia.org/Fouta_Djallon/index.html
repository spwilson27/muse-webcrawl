Fouta Djallon (Fula: Fuuta Jaloo) is a highland region in the centre of Guinea, West Africa. The region consists largely of rolling grassland and deeply incised sandstone plateaux at an average elevation of about 1,000 metres, and is often called the "water tower of West Africa" because several major rivers — including the Niger, Senegal, and Gambia — rise in its abundant rainfall.

History

The area was settled by the Fula people, who migrated into the highlands over several centuries. In the 18th century a jihad led by Karamokho Alfa and Ibrahima Sory established the Imamate of Futa Jallon, a theocratic state organized into provinces, which became a regional centre of Islamic scholarship and trade, including in enslaved people. The imamate was defeated and annexed by France in 1896 after the Battle of Porédaka.

Geography and people

The highlands cover roughly 77,000 km² and are home mainly to Fula pastoralists and farmers. The climate is cooler and wetter than the surrounding lowlands, supporting crops such as fonio, potatoes, and citrus. Spectacular waterfalls, including the Kambadaga and Ditinn falls, and hiking around towns such as Labé, Dalaba, and Pita have made the region Guinea's principal tourist area.
