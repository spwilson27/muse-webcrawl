Psycholinguistics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psycholinguistics or psychology of language is the study of the interrelation between linguistic factors and psychological aspects. The discipline is mainly concerned with the mechanisms by which language is processed and represented in the mind and brain; that is, the psychological and neurobiological factors that enable humans to acquire, use, comprehend, and produce language.

Psycholinguistics is concerned with how speakers produce language and how listeners comprehend it, how children acquire their first language, how second languages are learned, and how language breaks down in disorders such as aphasia. The field is interdisciplinary, drawing on linguistics, cognitive psychology, neuroscience, and computer science, and it employs experimental methods including reaction-time studies, eye tracking, and neuroimaging (such as ERP and fMRI).

History

The field coalesced in the 1950s during the cognitive revolution. The 1953 Social Science Research Council seminar at Cornell and the subsequent volume Psycholinguistics: A Survey of Theory and Research Problems (1954), edited by Charles E. Osgood and Thomas A. Sebeok, helped define the discipline. Noam Chomsky's 1959 review of B. F. Skinner's Verbal Behavior is often taken as a landmark that shifted the field toward cognitivist accounts of language as a mental faculty. Chomsky's distinction between linguistic competence and performance shaped decades of research.

Main areas

  • Language comprehension — how words are recognized (lexical access), how sentences are parsed, and how meaning and discourse are integrated. Phenomena such as garden-path sentences illuminate parsing mechanisms.
  • Language production — how speakers plan and articulate utterances; studied through speech errors ("slips of the tongue") and models such as Levelt's Speaking (1989).
  • Language acquisition — how children acquire phonology, vocabulary, and grammar, and the role of innate structure versus input.
  • Second-language acquisition and bilingualism.
  • Reading and the neural basis of language, including language disorders.

Methods

Classic paradigms include lexical decision and priming tasks, self-paced reading, eye-tracking during reading and listening (the visual world paradigm), event-related potentials components such as the N400 and P600, and computational modelling of processing. Corpus and naturalistic studies complement laboratory experiments.
