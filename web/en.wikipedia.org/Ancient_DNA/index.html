Ancient DNA

Ancient DNA (aDNA) is DNA isolated from ancient specimens, such as archaeological bones, teeth, and preserved tissues, as well as from environmental samples such as sediments. While not truly ancient in the geological sense — recovered sequences are typically thousands to tens of thousands of years old — the field has transformed the study of human history, evolution, and extinct species. Because aDNA molecules are short and chemically damaged, their recovery requires dedicated clean laboratories, stringent contamination controls, and computational methods to authenticate results.

History

The first aDNA sequences were reported in 1984 from a museum specimen of the extinct quagga, using molecular cloning. Early enthusiasm produced claims of DNA tens of millions of years old that were later attributed to contamination. The development of high-throughput sequencing in the 2000s revolutionised the field. In 2010, Svante Pääbo's team published the draft genome of the Neanderthal; the same year, DNA from a Siberian finger bone identified a previously unknown human population, the Denisovans. Pääbo received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2022 for these discoveries.

Key findings

Ancient DNA showed that modern humans interbred with Neanderthals (leaving roughly 1–4% ancestry in most non-African genomes) and with Denisovans (up to about 5% in some Oceanian populations). Large-scale studies have reconstructed major prehistoric migrations, including the spread of farming from Anatolia into Europe and the steppe migrations of the Bronze Age associated with the Yamnaya culture. aDNA has also illuminated extinct animals (mammoths, ground sloths), the domestication of horses, dogs and crops, and the evolution of pathogens such as Yersinia pestis. In 2022, two-million-year-old environmental DNA from Greenland sediments set a new record for the oldest DNA recovered.

Methods and challenges

Post-mortem damage — short fragments, cytosine deamination producing characteristic C-to-T miscoding lesions — both hinders analysis and serves to authenticate sequences as genuinely ancient. The petrous bone and tooth cementum are especially rich sources. The field also raises ethical questions concerning the sampling of Indigenous human remains, consent, and repatriation.
