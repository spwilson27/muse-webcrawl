Menopause

Menopause, also known as the climacteric, is the time when menstrual periods permanently cease, marking the end of a woman's reproductive years. It is diagnosed retrospectively after twelve months without a period and typically occurs between ages 45 and 55, with a median age of about 51 in Western countries. Menopause results from the depletion of ovarian follicles and the resulting decline in oestrogen and progesterone production. The years of irregular cycles and symptoms leading up to it are called perimenopause or the menopausal transition; the years after are postmenopause.

Signs and symptoms

During the transition, cycles become irregular in length and flow. Common symptoms include:

  • Hot flushes (flashes) and night sweats — sudden sensations of heat affecting up to 80% of women
  • Sleep disturbance and fatigue
  • Mood changes, irritability, and difficulty concentrating ("brain fog")
  • Vaginal dryness and genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)
  • Joint aches, weight redistribution, and thinning hair or skin

Long term, low oestrogen accelerates bone loss, increasing the risk of osteoporosis and fractures, and contributes to changes in cardiovascular risk. Menopause before age 40 is called premature ovarian insufficiency; surgical menopause follows removal of both ovaries.

Management

Many women need no treatment. For troublesome symptoms, menopausal hormone therapy (MHT/HRT) — oestrogen, combined with a progestogen if the uterus is present — is the most effective option for hot flushes and bone protection; guidelines generally favour it for symptomatic women under 60 or within ten years of menopause, weighing individual risks such as venous thromboembolism and breast cancer. Non-hormonal options include certain SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin, clonidine, and the neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist fezolinetant. Lifestyle measures, calcium and vitamin D, and bone-density monitoring support long-term health.

Evolutionary and cultural aspects

A long post-reproductive lifespan is rare in nature, shared notably with some toothed whales, and the "grandmother hypothesis" proposes that helping descendants raised inclusive fitness enough to select for it. Attitudes toward menopause vary widely across cultures, from a feared loss to a welcome liberation from menstruation and childbearing, and symptom reporting differs accordingly.
