Carcinogen

A carcinogen is any substance, organism, or agent capable of causing cancer. Carcinogens may be chemical (such as benzene or asbestos), physical (such as ionising radiation and ultraviolet light), or biological (certain viruses, bacteria, and parasites). They act by damaging DNA or by disrupting cellular processes that control growth.

Mechanisms

Genotoxic carcinogens interact directly with DNA, forming adducts or causing mutations in genes such as oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes. Non-genotoxic carcinogens promote cancer indirectly, for example through chronic inflammation, hormonal effects, or immunosuppression. Cancer typically develops through the accumulation of multiple genetic changes over years, explaining the long latency between exposure and disease.

Classification

The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifies agents into Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans, e.g. tobacco smoke, asbestos, plutonium, processed meat), Group 2A (probably carcinogenic), Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic), and Group 3 (not classifiable). These groups describe the strength of evidence, not the level of risk. The US National Toxicology Program similarly lists "known" and "reasonably anticipated" human carcinogens.

Common carcinogens

Well-established carcinogens include tobacco smoke (the leading preventable cause of cancer), asbestos, radon gas, aflatoxins in contaminated food, hepatitis B and C viruses, human papillomavirus, Helicobacter pylori, alcohol, and ultraviolet radiation from sunlight and tanning devices.
