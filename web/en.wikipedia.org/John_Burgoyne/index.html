John Burgoyne (24 February 1722 – 4 August 1792) was a British army officer, dramatist, and politician best known for his role in the American War of Independence, in which his invasion from Canada ended in surrender at Saratoga in 1777—one of the war's decisive turning points.

Early career

Educated at Westminster School, Burgoyne bought a commission, eloped with the Earl of Derby's daughter, and rebuilt a debt-ridden career through able service. He distinguished himself in the Seven Years' War, notably in the 1762 campaign in Portugal, and sat in Parliament, where he took an interest in military reform and in Indian affairs, serving on the committee investigating the East India Company.

The Saratoga campaign

In 1777 Burgoyne led some 8,000 British, German, Canadian, and Native troops south from Quebec along Lake Champlain, intending to cut New England off from the other rebelling colonies. He retook Fort Ticonderoga, but his advance slowed in the wilderness, supplies ran short, and detachments were cut off at Bennington. Surrounded by a growing American army under Horatio Gates, and weakened by the battles of Freeman's Farm and Bemis Heights, he surrendered his army at Saratoga on 17 October 1777 under the terms of a "convention." The American victory persuaded France to enter the war as an American ally.

Later life

Paroled home, Burgoyne defended his conduct in print and Parliament, briefly held command in Ireland, and turned successfully to the theatre; his comedy The Heiress (1786) was a major hit. He died in London in 1792.
