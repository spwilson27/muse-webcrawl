Aksum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aksum (also spelled Axum) is a town and historic city in the Tigray Region of northern Ethiopia, near the base of the Adwa mountains. It was the capital of the Aksumite Empire, a major trading power of the ancient world from around the 1st to the 7th century AD.

The Aksumite Empire

The Kingdom of Aksum controlled northern Ethiopia, Eritrea, Djibouti, parts of Sudan and, at its height, South Arabia (Yemen). It traded with the Roman Empire, Persia, India and Egypt through the Red Sea port of Adulis, exporting ivory, gold, frankincense and slaves and importing silk, spices and manufactured goods. Aksum was among the first states to mint its own coins bearing its rulers' portraits.

Christianity

Under King Ezana in the 4th century AD, Aksum adopted Christianity — among the earliest states in the world to do so officially — through the mission of Frumentius, who became the first bishop of the Ethiopian church. Aksumite tradition holds that the Ark of the Covenant is kept in the Chapel of the Tablet at the Church of Our Lady Mary of Zion in Aksum.

Monuments

The city is famous for its monumental granite stelae — carved obelisks marking royal tombs, the largest of which toppled and broke in antiquity. The Northern Stelae Field, with the still-standing King Ezana's stele, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The ruins of palaces, tombs and the church of Mary of Zion remain major religious and archaeological sites; Aksum is one of the holiest cities of Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity.

Decline

The empire declined from the 7th century as Red Sea trade routes shifted following the rise of Islam, and the capital eventually moved south. Aksum retained religious significance as the coronation site of Ethiopian emperors.
