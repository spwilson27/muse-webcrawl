Fair use

Fair use is a doctrine in United States copyright law that allows limited use of copyrighted material without having to first acquire permission from the copyright holder. Fair use is one of the limitations to copyright intended to balance the interests of copyright holders with the public interest in the wider distribution and use of creative works by allowing as a defence the unlicensed use of copyrighted works in, for example, criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, or research. It is codified at 17 U.S.C. § 107.

The four factors

Courts weigh four statutory factors: (1) the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial and whether it is "transformative"; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the whole; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for the work. No single factor is decisive, and fair use is decided case by case.

History and landmark cases

The doctrine developed in case law beginning with Folsom v. Marsh (1841) and was codified in the Copyright Act of 1976. In Sony Corp. v. Universal City Studios (1984), the Supreme Court held home time-shifting of television broadcasts to be fair use. Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (1994) held that a commercial parody (2 Live Crew's version of "Pretty Woman") could be fair use, elevating transformative purpose. Authors Guild v. Google (2015) upheld mass book scanning for search and snippets, and Google LLC v. Oracle America (2021) held Google's copying of Java API declaring code in Android to be fair use. In Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023), the Court narrowed the transformative-use inquiry where the secondary use shares the original's commercial purpose.

Influence

Although fair use is distinctively American, its flexibility has been influential: Israel adopted a fair-use-style provision, and debates in the EU, UK, and elsewhere over text-and-data mining, parody, and AI training frequently invoke it. On the internet, fair use is central to search engines, thumbnails, memes, and remix culture.
