Cell cycle checkpoint

Cell cycle checkpoints are control mechanisms in the eukaryotic cell cycle which ensure its proper progression. Each checkpoint serves as a potential point of termination for the cycle if conditions are unfavorable — for example, if DNA is damaged or chromosomes are misaligned — allowing time for repair or triggering apoptosis. Checkpoint failure is a major contributor to cancer.

Major checkpoints

The G1 checkpoint (restriction point) decides whether the cell commits to division, integrating signals about cell size, nutrients, and DNA integrity; the tumor suppressor p53 is a key mediator of arrest here. The G2/M checkpoint prevents entry into mitosis with damaged or incompletely replicated DNA, acting mainly through the ATM/ATR kinases and Chk1/Chk2, which inhibit the Cdc25 phosphatases that activate cyclin B–CDK1. The spindle assembly checkpoint (metaphase checkpoint) delays anaphase until every kinetochore is properly attached to spindle microtubules, preventing chromosome missegregation.

Molecular machinery

Checkpoints are implemented by sensor proteins that detect lesions, transducer kinases (ATM, ATR, Chk1, Chk2), and effectors that halt cyclin-dependent kinase activity, often via CDK inhibitors such as p21. The anaphase-promoting complex is restrained by the mitotic checkpoint complex until attachment is complete.

Clinical significance

Mutations in checkpoint genes — p53, BRCA1, ATM — predispose to cancer, and many chemotherapy and targeted drugs exploit checkpoint defects, for example Wee1 and ATR inhibitors that force cancer cells with faulty G1 control into lethal mitosis.
