Meiji Constitution

The Constitution of the Empire of Japan, known informally as the Meiji Constitution, was the constitution of the Empire of Japan from 11 November 1890 until 2 May 1947. Promulgated by the Emperor Meiji on 11 February 1889 as a gift from the sovereign to his subjects, it established a form of constitutional monarchy influenced chiefly by the conservative Prussian (German Empire) model. It created Japan's first parliament, the Imperial Diet, while reserving sweeping authority to the emperor, in whose person sovereignty resided.

Drafting

After the Meiji Restoration of 1868, pressure for a constitution grew from the Freedom and People's Rights Movement. Itō Hirobumi, the leading drafter, travelled to Europe in 1882 to study constitutional systems, working with Rudolf von Gneist in Berlin and Lorenz von Stein in Vienna. He deliberately rejected the British and American liberal models in favour of Prussia's, which protected monarchical authority. The draft was prepared in secret by Itō's committee, deliberated by the newly created Privy Council, and granted by the emperor as an imperial gift — a fact with lasting consequences for Japanese constitutional theory.

Provisions

The constitution declared the emperor "sacred and inviolable", head of the Empire, combining the rights of sovereignty and exercising legislative power with the consent of the Imperial Diet. The Diet comprised an elected House of Representatives and a House of Peers composed largely of nobility. Ministers of state advised the emperor and were responsible to him, not to the Diet; the armed services answered directly to the emperor as supreme commander, and the requirement that service ministers be active-duty officers (introduced by regulation in 1900 and restored in 1936) gave the army and navy a veto over cabinets. Subjects were granted rights — speech, religion, property, due process — but qualified by statute ("within the limits of law").

Operation and replacement

In practice the system allowed significant parliamentary development in the Taishō era, when party cabinets governed (1918–1932), but its ambiguities about sovereignty enabled the military's growing autonomy in the 1930s. Minobe Tatsukichi's "emperor-organ theory", which treated the emperor as an organ of the state, was orthodox scholarship until ultranationalist pressure suppressed it in 1935. Following Japan's defeat in the Second World War, the constitution was wholly revised: the new Constitution of Japan, effective 3 May 1947, transferred sovereignty to the people and reduced the emperor to a symbol of the state. Formally it was passed as an amendment under Article 73 of the Meiji Constitution to preserve legal continuity.
