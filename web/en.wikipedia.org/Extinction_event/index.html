Extinction event

Mass extinctions mark sharp drops in Earth's biodiversity.

An extinction event (also known as a mass extinction or biotic crisis) is a widespread and rapid decrease in biodiversity on Earth. Such events are identified by sharp changes in the diversity and abundance of multicellular organisms in the fossil record, occurring over geologically short intervals.

The "Big Five"

Paleontologists traditionally recognize five major mass extinctions: the End-Ordovician (~444 million years ago), the Late Devonian (~375 Ma), the End-Permian "Great Dying" (~252 Ma, the largest, eliminating most marine species), the End-Triassic (~201 Ma), and the Cretaceous–Paleogene event (~66 Ma), caused by an asteroid impact that ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs.

Causes

Proposed causes include massive volcanism (flood basalts such as the Siberian Traps and Deccan Traps), asteroid or comet impacts, rapid climate change, sea-level fluctuation, ocean anoxia, and combinations of these stresses. The End-Permian and End-Triassic events are closely associated with flood-basalt eruptions and ensuing global warming.

Recovery and the present

Recovery of ecosystems after a mass extinction typically takes millions of years, and the species that radiate afterward reshape the biosphere—mammals rose to prominence only after the Cretaceous–Paleogene event. Many scientists argue that human activity—habitat destruction, overexploitation, pollution, and climate change—is driving extinction rates far above background levels, potentially constituting a sixth mass extinction.
