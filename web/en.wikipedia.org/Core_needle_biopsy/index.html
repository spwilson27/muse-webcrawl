Core needle biopsy

A core needle biopsy is a medical procedure in which a hollow needle, larger than that used in fine-needle aspiration, is inserted into a lesion to remove a thin cylindrical sample — a "core" — of tissue for pathological examination. Because the core preserves the architecture of the tissue, the technique provides more diagnostic information than cytology alone while remaining far less invasive than open surgical biopsy.

Procedure

After local anaesthesia, a spring-loaded or vacuum-assisted biopsy gun advances a notched needle into the target; tissue fills the notch and an outer cutting cannula slices and traps the core. Several cores are usually taken. In the breast the procedure is guided by the imaging modality that best shows the lesion — ultrasound, mammography (stereotactic biopsy) or MRI — and cores from calcifications are X-rayed to confirm sampling. Core biopsy is also routine for liver, prostate (usually transrectal or transperineal), lymph-node, soft-tissue and bone lesions.

Diagnostic value

For breast lesions, core needle biopsy distinguishes invasive from in-situ carcinoma, provides tumour grade and hormone-receptor and HER2 status, and has largely replaced surgical excision for diagnosis. Discordance between imaging and pathology findings, or high-risk lesions such as atypical ductal hyperplasia, may still prompt surgical excision.

Risks

Complications are uncommon and include bleeding and haematoma, infection, pneumothorax for thoracic targets, and discomfort. Needle-tract seeding of tumour is considered rare and generally does not alter management. Compared with excisional biopsy, the technique is cheaper, quicker, leaves little or no scar, and usually allows a definitive treatment plan to be made in one step.
