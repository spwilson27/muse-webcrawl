Timaeus (dialogue)

The Timaeus (Greek: Τίμαιος) is one of Plato's dialogues, mostly in the form of long monologues given by Critias and by the title character Timaeus of Locri, written in about 360 BC. The work puts forward a speculative account of the origin and structure of the physical universe and of human beings, and includes the earliest known mention of Atlantis, introduced in its companion framing by Critias.

Content

Timaeus explains that the cosmos was fashioned by a divine craftsman, the Demiurge, who, being good, imposed order on pre-existing chaos by looking to an eternal model. The world is a living creature with a soul (the World Soul), built from the elements earth, air, fire, and water, whose proportions are governed by mathematics. Timaeus famously associates the four elements with four of the Platonic solids — fire with the tetrahedron, earth with the cube, air with the octahedron, and water with the icosahedron — and the universe as a whole with the dodecahedron. The dialogue goes on to discuss the creation of the gods, time ("a moving image of eternity"), the human body, sensation, and disease.

Influence

Uniquely among Plato's works, the Timaeus remained continuously known in the Latin West through late antiquity and the Middle Ages, thanks to the partial translation and commentary of Calcidius (4th century). It profoundly shaped medieval cosmology, natural philosophy, and theology, and its geometric atomism influenced early modern thinkers including Kepler.
