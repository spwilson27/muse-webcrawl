Pulse oximetry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pulse oximetry
Output: SpO₂ (peripheral oxygen saturation)
Normal values: 95–100% (on room air)
Wavelengths used: red (660 nm), infrared (940 nm)
Inventor: Takuo Aoyagi (1970s)

Pulse oximetry is a non-invasive method for monitoring the oxygen saturation of a person's blood. A sensor, often clipped to a fingertip or earlobe, shines red and infrared light through the tissue and measures how much is absorbed; because oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin absorb the two wavelengths differently, the device can compute the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen, displayed as SpO2.

Principle

The technique exploits the pulsatile component of the signal: absorbance that varies with the heartbeat is attributed to arterial blood, separating it from the constant absorbance of tissue and venous blood. The ratio of absorbances at the two wavelengths is converted to a saturation using empirical calibration curves obtained from healthy volunteers.

Clinical use

Pulse oximetry is standard in operating theatres, recovery rooms, intensive care, emergency medicine and neonatal care, and is often called the "fifth vital sign". It allows rapid detection of hypoxemia and continuous monitoring during sedation and anaesthesia. Readings can be inaccurate with poor peripheral perfusion, motion, nail polish, carbon monoxide poisoning (which the device may read as oxyhaemoglobin), and, studies have shown, may be less accurate in patients with darker skin pigmentation.

History

The underlying principle was developed in the 1940s; the modern pulse oximeter was invented in the 1970s by Japanese bioengineer Takuo Aoyagi, and the devices became widespread in clinical practice during the 1980s.
