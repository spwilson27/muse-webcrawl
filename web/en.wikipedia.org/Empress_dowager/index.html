Empress dowager

An empress dowager is the widow of an emperor, typically the mother (or grandmother) of the reigning emperor, who holds a position of high honour and, in several historical monarchies, real political power. The title is particularly associated with imperial China, where the empress dowager (Chinese: 皇太后, huángtàihòu) ranked above the reigning emperor in familial precedence and could serve as regent for a child emperor. Equivalent dowager titles existed in Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and in European monarchies such as Russia and Austria.

Empress Dowager Cixi, who effectively ruled China for nearly five decades

In China

In imperial China, the empress dowager was usually the late emperor's principal consort and the new emperor's mother, whether biological or adoptive. Confucian norms of filial piety gave her moral authority over the emperor himself. During minorities, empress dowagers routinely governed as regents, in some eras ruling "from behind the curtain". Powerful examples include Empress Lü of the Han dynasty, who dominated the court after 195 BC, and Empress Dowager Cixi of the Qing, who controlled the Chinese government from 1861 until her death in 1908. Other notable dowagers include Empress Dowager He, Empress Dowager Xiaozhuang, and Empress Dowager Longyu, who signed the abdication edict ending the Qing dynasty in 1912.

Elsewhere in Asia

Japan's imperial house recognised dowager empresses (皇太后, kōtaigō), and the title regained prominence in the Heian period, when Fujiwara regents governed through imperial mothers and grandmothers. Korea's Joseon dynasty had royal queen dowagers (大妃, daebi) and grand queen dowagers (大王大妃, daewangdaebi), several of whom acted as regents — most famously Queen Jeongsun and Queen Sunwon. Vietnamese dynasties followed the Chinese pattern.

In Europe

Dowager empresses in Europe included the Russian empresses consort who outlived their husbands — most notably Maria Feodorovna, widow of Alexander III, who retained immense court prestige under Nicholas II — and the Austrian and Byzantine dowagers who sometimes served as regents, such as the Byzantine empresses Irene (as regent) and Theodora. The status carried income, households, and precedence, though formal regency power varied by realm.
