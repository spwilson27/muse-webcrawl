The Philippine–American War, known in the United States at the time as the Philippine Insurrection, was an armed conflict between the First Philippine Republic and the United States that lasted from February 4, 1899, to July 2, 1902. The conflict arose when the United States, having taken the Philippines from Spain in the Spanish–American War, refused to recognise the independence proclaimed by Filipino revolutionaries under Emilio Aguinaldo. The war is regarded as a continuation of the Philippine Revolution and as one of the first major American counterinsurgency wars.

Background

The Philippine Revolution against Spain began in 1896 under the Katipunan movement. In 1898 the United States, at war with Spain, brought Aguinaldo back from exile; Filipino forces liberated most of Luzon while Commodore Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay. Filipino leaders proclaimed independence on June 12, 1898, and convened the Malolos Congress, which established the First Philippine Republic in January 1899. However, the Treaty of Paris (December 1898) transferred the Philippines to the United States for $20 million without Filipino consent, and US policy under President William McKinley claimed sovereignty over the archipelago.

Conventional war, 1899

Fighting began on February 4, 1899, when an American sentry shot a Filipino soldier at the San Juan Bridge in Manila. In the conventional phase that followed, US forces under General Elwell Otis captured Malolos and drove Aguinaldo's army northward; Filipino general Antonio Luna, the republic's ablest commander, was assassinated by Aguinaldo's own guards in June 1899 amid factional disputes. At the Battle of Tirad Pass (December 2, 1899), the young general Gregorio del Pilar died covering Aguinaldo's retreat. By late 1899 the Filipino army, outmatched in artillery and logistics, dissolved into guerrilla warfare.

Guerrilla war and counterinsurgency

From 1900 the war became a guerrilla conflict. Filipino forces ambushed patrols and drew support from the population; US forces under General Arthur MacArthur Jr. responded with population control, reconcentration of civilians into protected zones in some provinces, destruction of crops and supplies, and, especially after the Balangiga massacre of September 1901 — in which Filipino fighters killed 48 US soldiers on Samar — severe reprisals. On Samar, General Jacob H. Smith's orders to turn the interior into a "howling wilderness" led to his court-martial. Aguinaldo was captured in March 1901 by a ruse engineered by General Frederick Funston and swore allegiance to the United States; organised resistance in most regions collapsed during 1901–02. President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war over on July 4, 1902.

Resistance after 1902 and the Moro conflict

Armed resistance continued after the official end: Macario Sakay proclaimed a Tagalog Republic and fought until 1907, and in the Muslim south the Moro Wars continued against American rule until 1913, ending with the Battle of Bud Bagsak. The Philippines remained an American colony — as an unincorporated territory, later a Commonwealth (1935) — until independence on July 4, 1946.

The war was controversial in the United States, where the Anti-Imperialist League — counting Mark Twain among its members — opposed annexation. Senate hearings in 1902 publicised atrocities including the "water cure." Filipino death tolls remain contested: demographers estimate at least 200,000 civilian deaths from the war's violence, disease and famine, with some historians arguing for much larger figures. In the Philippines the war is commemorated as a heroic, if tragic, struggle for independence; in the United States it was long overshadowed by the Spanish–American War and is sometimes called "America's first Vietnam."
