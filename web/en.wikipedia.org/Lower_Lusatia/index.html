Lower Lusatia (German: Niederlausitz; Lower Sorbian: Dolna Łužyca; Upper Sorbian: Delnja Łužica; Polish: Łużyce Dolne) is a historical region in Central Europe, the northern part of Lusatia, stretching from the southeastern part of the German state of Brandenburg into the Lubusz Voivodeship of western Poland. Its traditional capital is Cottbus (Lower Sorbian: Chóśebuz).

Geography

Lower Lusatia is a low-lying, sandy plain shaped by the Ice Age, crossed by the rivers Spree, Neisse and Schwarze Elster. Its best-known landscape is the Spreewald, a network of natural and artificial waterways where the River Spree splits into hundreds of small channels, now a UNESCO biosphere reserve. Extensive pine forests cover much of the region, and vast areas have been transformed by open-cast lignite (brown coal) mining, which left artificial lakes — the emerging Lusatian Lakeland — in former pits.

History

The area was settled by West Slavic tribes, among them the Lusici from whom the region takes its name. In the Middle Ages the Margraviate of Lusatia passed among Meissen, Bohemian and Brandenburg rulers. From 1367 it belonged to the Lands of the Bohemian Crown, and in 1635, by the Peace of Prague, Lower Lusatia was transferred to the Electorate of Saxony. Most of it passed to Prussia at the Congress of Vienna in 1815. After 1945, the portion east of the Oder–Neisse line was assigned to Poland, and the German population there was expelled.

The Sorbs

Lower Lusatia is part of the homeland of the Sorbs, a West Slavic minority officially recognised in Germany. The Lower Sorbian language, distinct from Upper Sorbian spoken further south, is endangered but taught in some schools and used in bilingual signage and media around Cottbus. The region's cultural landscape is marked by Sorbian customs, costumes and Easter traditions.

Economy

For more than a century the region's economy centred on lignite mining and power generation, first developed on an industrial scale in the 19th century. Germany's planned coal phase-out has brought deep structural change, with investment directed toward tourism in the Spreewald and Lakeland, renewable energy, and new industry around Cottbus, where a university (BTU Cottbus–Senftenberg) anchors research.
