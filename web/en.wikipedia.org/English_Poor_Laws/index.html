The English Poor Laws were a system of poor relief in England and Wales that developed out of late-medieval and Tudor statutes. They provided a compulsory, tax-funded system for relieving the destitute, administered locally by parishes and financed by a levy on property, the poor rate. The system lasted, in changing forms, from the sixteenth century until the creation of the modern welfare state after the Second World War.

The Old Poor Law

The Poor Relief Act 1601 consolidated earlier legislation and remained the basis of relief for over two centuries. Parishes appointed overseers of the poor to relieve the "impotent" poor (the aged, sick and orphaned), set the able-bodied to work, and apprentice pauper children. The Settlement Acts tied entitlement to a parish of settlement, and the Workhouse Test Act 1723 allowed parishes to deny relief to those refusing the workhouse. In the 1790s the Speenhamland system of wage supplements spread in southern England, later blamed — contentiously — for depressing wages and inflating costs.

The New Poor Law

A Royal Commission of 1832–34, influenced by Edwin Chadwick and Nassau Senior, condemned the old system. The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 grouped parishes into unions run by elected boards of guardians, supervised by a central Poor Law Commission. Relief for the able-bodied was to be given only in workhouses, run under the principle of "less eligibility" — conditions deliberately less attractive than the lowest-paid independent labour. The regime, memorably depicted in Dickens's Oliver Twist, was fiercely resisted, especially in the industrial north.

Decline and abolition

From the later nineteenth century the harshness of the system softened; outdoor relief remained widespread, and new services — infirmaries, schools, and later old-age pensions (1908) and national insurance (1911) — took over its functions. The boards of guardians were abolished in 1929, and the National Assistance Act 1948 formally repealed the Poor Law.
