Rankine cycle

The Rankine cycle is an idealized thermodynamic cycle describing the process by which certain heat engines, such as steam turbines or reciprocating steam engines, allow mechanical work to be extracted from a fluid as it moves between a heat source and a heat sink. It is named after William John Macquorn Rankine, a Scottish polymath and professor at Glasgow University. Most of the world's electricity — from coal, nuclear, natural gas and concentrated solar plants — is generated with Rankine-cycle steam turbines.

The four processes

The cycle consists of four processes in a closed loop: (1) isentropic compression of liquid water in a pump; (2) constant-pressure heat addition in a boiler, producing superheated steam; (3) isentropic expansion through a turbine, producing work; and (4) constant-pressure heat rejection in a condenser, returning the steam to liquid water. In a temperature–entropy (T–s) diagram these form the familiar loop with a two-phase region inside the vapour dome.

Efficiency and improvements

Thermal efficiency increases with higher boiler temperature and pressure and with lower condenser pressure. Practical refinements include superheating the steam, reheating between turbine stages, and regenerative feedwater heating, in which steam bled from the turbine preheats the feedwater. Supercritical plants operate above the critical point of water (22.1 MPa, 374 °C) and reach efficiencies above 40%.

Organic Rankine cycle

The organic Rankine cycle replaces water with an organic fluid of lower boiling point, enabling power generation from low-temperature heat sources such as geothermal wells, biomass and industrial waste heat.
