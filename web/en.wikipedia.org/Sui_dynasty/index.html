Sui dynasty

The Sui dynasty (Chinese: 隋朝; Wade–Giles: Sui ch'ao) was a short-lived imperial dynasty of China that ruled from 581 to 618. Founded by Emperor Wen (Yang Jian), it reunified China in 589 after nearly three centuries of division since the fall of the Han, and laid institutional foundations inherited by the flourishing Tang dynasty.

History

Yang Jian, a regent of the Northern Zhou, usurped the throne in 581 and in 589 conquered the Chen dynasty in the south, ending the Northern and Southern dynasties period. Under Emperor Wen the Sui standardised coinage (the Kaihuang-era reforms), codified law in the Kaihuang Code, reorganised government into the Three Departments and Six Ministries, developed the imperial examination system, and maintained the equal-field system of land distribution.

His son, Emperor Yang, undertook vast construction projects — completing the Grand Canal linking the Yellow and Yangtze rivers, rebuilding Luoyang, and extending roads and walls — but his costly and disastrous campaigns against the Korean kingdom of Goguryeo (612–614) and heavy corvée burdens provoked widespread rebellion. He was assassinated in 618, and the general Li Yuan founded the Tang dynasty the same year.

Legacy

Despite its brevity, the Sui is often compared to the Qin dynasty: a harsh unifying dynasty whose reforms and infrastructure enabled a long-lived successor. The Grand Canal remained China's economic artery for a millennium.
