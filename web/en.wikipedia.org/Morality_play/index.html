The morality play is a genre of medieval and early Tudor theatrical entertainment. In their most characteristic form, morality plays personify abstract qualities — virtues such as Good Deeds and Mercy, vices such as Greed and Lechery — who struggle for possession of the soul of a representative human protagonist, usually called Everyman, Mankind or Humanum Genus. The plays were intended to teach audiences Christian moral lessons about repentance and salvation.

Characteristics

Morality plays dramatise the spiritual struggle described as psychomachia — the battle for the soul. Characters are personifications rather than individuals, and the plot typically follows the protagonist from innocence through temptation and sin to repentance and, ideally, salvation. Comic scenes involving the Vice figure, a mischievous tempter who addresses the audience directly, were a popular feature and an ancestor of later stage clowns.

Major examples

The Castle of Perseverance (c. 1405–1425) is among the earliest surviving English morality plays and includes a famous stage diagram showing a castle surrounded by scaffolds. Mankind (c. 1470) mixes bawdy comedy with moral instruction. The best known is Everyman (c. 1510), adapted from the Dutch Elckerlijc, in which Death summons Everyman to his reckoning and he discovers that only Good Deeds will accompany him.

Influence

The morality play influenced Elizabethan drama: Marlowe's Doctor Faustus retains the structure of a soul contested by good and evil angels, and Shakespeare's Vice-like figures such as Richard III and Iago descend from the morality tradition.
