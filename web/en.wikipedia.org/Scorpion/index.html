Scorpion

A scorpion in defensive posture, metasoma raised.

Scorpions are predatory arachnids of the order Scorpiones. They have eight legs and are characterised by a pair of grasping pincers (pedipalps) and a narrow, segmented tail—often carried in a characteristic forward curve over the back and ending in a venomous stinger. About 2,500 species have been described, ranging in size from about 9 mm to over 20 cm. Scorpions occur on every continent except Antarctica, mainly in deserts but adapted to a wide range of environments.

Anatomy and behaviour

The body is divided into the cephalothorax and the abdomen, the rear part of which forms the five-segmented metasoma ("tail") ending in the telson, which contains the venom glands and stinger. Scorpions are nocturnal predators that seize prey with their pincers and subdue it with venom when necessary. They fluoresce vividly under ultraviolet light owing to chemicals in their cuticle.

Reproduction

After an elaborate courtship "dance" (the promenade à deux), females give birth to live young, which climb onto the mother's back and remain there until their first moult.

Venom

All known scorpion species possess venom, but only about 25 species—mostly of the family Buthidae, such as the deathstalker and the Indian red scorpion—have venom capable of killing a human. Scorpion stings are a significant public-health problem in parts of Mexico, North Africa, and the Middle East; antivenom is available for the most dangerous species.

Evolution

Scorpions have an ancient fossil record extending back some 435 million years to the Silurian; early forms were aquatic or amphibious, and scorpions may have been among the first animals to colonise land.
