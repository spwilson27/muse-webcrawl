Arnold Schoenberg

Arnold Schoenberg (13 September 1874 – 13 July 1951) was an Austrian and American composer, music theorist and painter. A leader of the Second Viennese School, he revolutionized 20th-century music by abandoning tonality and developing the twelve-tone technique of composition. His pupils included Alban Berg and Anton Webern.

Life and career

Born in Vienna to a Jewish family, Schoenberg was largely self-taught, receiving guidance from Alexander Zemlinsky. His early works — the string sextet Verklärte Nacht (1899), the tone poem Pelleas und Melisande and the monumental Gurre-Lieder — extended the late-Romantic language of Wagner and Brahms. From about 1908 he moved into free atonality in works such as the Three Piano Pieces, Op. 11, the monodrama Erwartung and the melodrama cycle Pierrot lunaire (1912), famous for its use of Sprechstimme.

In the early 1920s Schoenberg systematized his approach into the method of composing with twelve tones related only to one another, first used in the Suite for Piano, Op. 25, and applied in the opera Moses und Aron (left unfinished). Dismissed from his Berlin post by the Nazis in 1933, he emigrated to the United States, settled in Los Angeles, and taught at UCLA. Late works reconciled twelve-tone writing with tonal elements; they include the Violin and Piano Concertos, A Survivor from Warsaw (1947) and the Phantasy for violin and piano. Schoenberg, who suffered from triskaidekaphobia, died on Friday 13 July 1951, aged 76.

Influence

His treatise Theory of Harmony (1911) and his teaching shaped generations of composers, and his ideas — including the "emancipation of the dissonance" and "developing variation" — remain central to music theory.
