Back-of-the-envelope calculation

A back-of-the-envelope calculation is a rough, informal mathematical estimate performed quickly with minimal data, traditionally scribbled on whatever paper is at hand — such as the back of an envelope. Its aim is not precision but a plausible order of magnitude that can guide decisions, test feasibility or sanity-check a more detailed result.

Method

Such calculations combine simplification (treating shapes and populations as round numbers, ignoring second-order effects), dimensional analysis and known reference values. They are closely related to Fermi problems, in which an answer is built by chaining several rough estimates. Enrico Fermi was famous for them, for example estimating the yield of the first atomic bomb from the distance scraps of paper were blown by the blast.

Use

Scientists, engineers, investors and policymakers use back-of-the-envelope reasoning to decide whether a project or theory is worth pursuing before committing to detailed analysis. The phrase is sometimes extended to describe any sketchy plan drawn up hastily — "written on the back of an envelope" — as in the (apocryphal) origin story of the Beveridge Report's welfare state.
