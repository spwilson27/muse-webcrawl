A digital camera is a camera that captures photographs in digital memory, using an electronic image sensor — a charge-coupled device (CCD) or a CMOS sensor — in place of photographic film. Images can be displayed immediately on a screen, stored on removable media, transferred to a computer and shared electronically. Digital cameras displaced film cameras for most purposes during the 2000s, and the same technology underlies the cameras now built into nearly all smartphones, which have in turn largely displaced dedicated compact cameras.

History

The first self-contained digital camera was built in 1975 by Kodak engineer Steven Sasson: it weighed 3.6 kg, recorded 0.01-megapixel black-and-white images onto cassette tape over 23 seconds. CCD sensors had been invented at Bell Labs in 1969; Fuji's DS-1P (1988) was the first camera to record to a semiconductor memory card, and the Kodak DCS-100 (1991) brought digital capture to professionals. Consumer milestones followed through the 1990s — the Apple QuickTake, Casio QV-10 (with an LCD preview screen, 1995) and megapixel compacts — before Canon's EOS 300D (2003) put a digital SLR below $1,000. Mirrorless interchangeable-lens systems, beginning with Panasonic's G1 in 2008, removed the mirror box and have since become the mainstream professional format. Smartphone cameras, from the Sharp J-SH04 (2000) onward and boosted by computational photography, collapsed the market for separate compact cameras after about 2010.

How it works

Light passing through the lens is focused onto the sensor, an array of millions of photosites covered by a Bayer colour filter array (or, in Foveon designs, layered sensors). Each photosite accumulates charge proportional to the light falling on it; the analogue signal is amplified (the gain setting determines ISO sensitivity), digitised, demosaiced and processed into a JPEG/HEIF image or stored as raw sensor data. Key specifications include resolution in megapixels, sensor size (from tiny phone sensors through APS-C and full-frame to medium format), dynamic range and low-light performance.

Market and culture

Shipments of dedicated digital cameras peaked at over 120 million units around 2010 before smartphones cut annual volumes by more than 90%. Photography nonetheless became far more widespread: digital capture removed the cost per shot, enabled instant review and social media sharing, and created new practices such as computational night modes, burst stacking and machine-learning-based enhancement.
