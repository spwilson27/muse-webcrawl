Attorney General for England and Wales

His Majesty's Attorney General for England and Wales is the chief legal adviser to the Crown and the Government of the United Kingdom, and a minister in the government. The attorney general is the senior of the two law officers of the Crown, the other being the solicitor general, who acts as deputy. The office is one of the oldest in English government, with holders recorded from the 13th century.

The attorney general is the Crown's chief legal adviser and superintends the prosecuting departments.

Functions

The attorney general advises the government on legal matters, represents the Crown in important litigation, and superintends the Crown Prosecution Service, the Serious Fraud Office and the Government Legal Department, while remaining operationally independent of individual prosecutorial decisions. The office also exercises functions in the public interest, such as referring unduly lenient sentences to the Court of Appeal, intervening in certain litigation, and acting in relation to charities and contempt of court.

Constitutional position

By convention the attorney general attends Cabinet when legal or constitutional issues arise but is not a full member. The holder is almost always a Member of Parliament and a barrister of senior standing, frequently a King's Counsel. The Shawcross doctrine, articulated in 1951, states that the attorney general's decisions on prosecutions must not be influenced by political considerations or Cabinet colleagues.

Devolution

Since devolution, the attorney general advises the UK government on matters concerning England and Wales and certain UK-wide issues; Scotland has its own Lord Advocate, and Northern Ireland its own attorney general. The attorney general also serves as the Advocate General for Northern Ireland.
