Glycogen

Glycogen is a multibranched polysaccharide of glucose that serves as a form of energy storage in animals, fungi, and bacteria. It is the main storage form of glucose in the human body, accumulated chiefly in the liver and skeletal muscles. Structurally similar to amylopectin in plants but more extensively branched, a single glycogen granule can contain around 30,000 glucose units.

Structure

Glycogen consists of chains of glucose residues linked by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds, with branch points formed by α(1→6) bonds roughly every 8–12 residues. Synthesis starts on a primer protein, glycogenin, at the core of each granule. The highly branched structure gives many ends from which glucose can be released rapidly when energy is needed.

Metabolism

Glycogen is synthesised by the enzyme glycogen synthase after meals, under the stimulation of insulin. Breakdown (glycogenolysis) is catalysed by glycogen phosphorylase and a debranching enzyme, releasing glucose-1-phosphate. In the liver, glucose-6-phosphatase converts this to free glucose for export to the blood, a process switched on by glucagon during fasting; muscle glycogen, lacking that enzyme, is reserved for the muscle's own use. Inherited defects in these enzymes cause the glycogen storage diseases.
