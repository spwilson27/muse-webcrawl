Dennis MacAlistair Ritchie (September 9, 1941 – October 12, 2011) was an American computer scientist. He created the C programming language and, with long-time colleague Ken Thompson, the Unix operating system and B programming language. Ritchie and Thompson were awarded the Turing Award from the ACM in 1983, the Hamming Medal from the IEEE in 1990 and the National Medal of Technology from President Bill Clinton in 1999.

Ritchie was the head of Lucent Technologies System Software Research Department when he retired in 2007. He was the "R" in K&R C, and commonly known by his username dmr.

Career

Ritchie studied physics and applied mathematics at Harvard University. In 1967 he began working at the Bell Labs Computing Sciences Research Center, following his father Alistair Ritchie, a longtime Bell Labs scientist. After the Multics project was abandoned, Ritchie worked with Thompson on the early Unix system; to port Unix to a new machine he developed the C language between 1969 and 1973, evolving it from Thompson's B language. Unix was rewritten in C in 1973 — a landmark in portable operating-system design.

With Brian Kernighan he wrote The C Programming Language (1978), widely known as "K&R", which served for years as the informal specification of the language and popularised the "Hello, World!" example program. Ritchie later contributed to the Plan 9 and Inferno operating systems at Bell Labs.

Legacy

C remains one of the most widely used programming languages in history, and its syntax influenced C++, Java, JavaScript, C# and many others. Unix and its descendants — including Linux and the systems underlying macOS and the internet's servers — shaped modern computing. Ritchie's death, a week after that of Steve Jobs, drew tributes emphasising that much of the technology Jobs's products depended upon rested on Ritchie's work. In 2011 he shared the Japan Prize for the development of Unix.
