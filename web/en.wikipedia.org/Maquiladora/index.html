Maquiladora

A maquiladora plant in northern Mexico

A maquiladora (Spanish: [makilaˈðoɾa]), also known as a maquila, is a factory in Mexico — and by extension similar operations elsewhere in Latin America — run by a foreign company that imports materials and equipment on a duty-free and tariff-free basis for assembly, processing, or manufacturing, and then exports the assembled product back to the materials' country of origin, historically most often the United States.

History

The system began with Mexico's Border Industrialization Program of 1965, launched after the end of the U.S. bracero program left many Mexican workers unemployed along the border. The program allowed foreign-owned plants in a zone along the U.S.–Mexico border to import inputs duty-free, provided the output was exported. Ciudad Juárez, Tijuana, Reynosa, and Matamoros became major maquila centres, employing predominantly young women in the early decades, though the workforce later became more mixed by gender.

Expansion and NAFTA

The industry expanded rapidly after the peso devaluations of the 1980s and under the North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA, 1994), employing well over a million workers at its peak around 2000 in electronics, automotive parts, textiles, and medical devices. After 2001, competition from China caused a sharp decline, but the sector recovered as a key part of integrated North American supply chains under the USMCA (2020), which replaced NAFTA.

Criticism

Maquiladoras have been criticized for low wages, weak labour protections, rapid workforce turnover, unsafe conditions, and environmental damage in border cities where infrastructure failed to keep pace with growth. Feminist scholars and journalists have also drawn attention to the feminicides of Ciudad Juárez, many of whose victims were maquila workers. Defenders point to the jobs, foreign exchange, and industrial skills the sector brings to Mexico.
