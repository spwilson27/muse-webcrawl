Tequila

TypeDistilled spirit
OriginTown of Tequila, Jalisco, Mexico
Base ingredientBlue Weber agave (Agave tequilana)
Alcohol by volume35–55% (typically 40% in the US)

Tequila is a distilled beverage made from the blue agave plant, primarily in the area surrounding the city of Tequila in the Mexican state of Jalisco. It is protected by a denomination of origin: only spirits distilled from blue Weber agave grown in Jalisco and limited municipalities of several other states may be called tequila.

Production

Agave plants mature for five to eight years before harvesters (jimadores) strip the leaves with a coa knife, leaving the heart, or piña. The piñas are cooked to convert inulin to sugars, crushed, fermented, and distilled — traditionally in pot stills, usually twice. Tequila must contain at least 51% agave sugars; "100% agave" bottlings are considered premium.

Categories

By aging, tequila is classed as blanco (unaged), reposado (rested 2–12 months in oak), añejo (1–3 years), and extra añejo (3+ years). Mezcal is a related, broader category of agave spirits from other regions and agave species.

History

Spanish settlers introduced distillation to Mexico in the 16th century, applying it to fermented agave drinks with roots in the pre-Hispanic beverage pulque. The town of Tequila was founded in 1666, and industrial production by families such as Cuervo and Sauza grew in the 18th and 19th centuries.
