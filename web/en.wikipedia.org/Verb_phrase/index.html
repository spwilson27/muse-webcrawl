In linguistics, a verb phrase (VP) is a syntactic unit composed of a verb and its dependents — objects, complements, and modifiers such as adverbs — but not including the subject. It forms the predicate of a clause: in the sentence "Mary saw the man through the telescope", the verb phrase is "saw the man through the telescope".

Two senses

The term is used in two related ways. In traditional grammar and in much school instruction, the verb phrase is the group of verb forms alone — the main verb together with its auxiliaries, as in "will have been working". In phrase structure grammar, the VP is a full constituent containing the verb plus everything it selects: its objects, predicative complements, and associated adverbials, excluding only the external subject.

Structure

Within generative grammar, the verb is the head of the VP and projects its structure. The arguments a verb requires — for instance, a direct object for a transitive verb like devour — appear inside the VP, a fact supported by constituency tests such as VP-fronting, coordination, and replacement by the pro-form do so. In many modern analyses the subject originates inside a larger verbal projection (the VP-internal subject hypothesis) and raises to the subject position. Light-verb and small-clause structures, auxiliary sequences, and the behaviour of phrasal verbs have all been central topics in the study of VP structure across languages.
