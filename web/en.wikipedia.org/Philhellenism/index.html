Philhellenism

Philhellenism ("the love of Greek culture") was an intellectual movement prominent in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. It contributed to the support of European and American opinion, and in many cases the personal participation of volunteers, on the side of the Greeks during the Greek War of Independence against the Ottoman Empire. Philhellenes idealized ancient Greek civilization and saw modern Greeks as its heirs, deserving of political freedom.

Origins

Philhellenism grew out of the European Enlightenment and the revival of classical learning. Writers such as Johann Joachim Winckelmann shaped an ideal image of ancient Greek art and society, while Romantic authors popularized sympathy for the Greek cause. The movement was closely tied to classical education: for educated Europeans, Greek antiquity represented the cradle of democracy, philosophy, and the arts.

The Greek War of Independence

When the Greek revolution broke out in 1821, philhellene committees formed across Europe and the United States to raise money, supplies, and volunteers. The English poet Lord Byron became the movement's most famous figure; he traveled to Greece, helped organize forces, and died of fever at Missolonghi in 1824, becoming a martyr-hero of the cause. Artists such as Eugène Delacroix depicted Greek suffering, most famously in The Massacre at Chios, shaping public sympathy that eventually pressured Britain, France, and Russia to intervene, culminating in the Battle of Navarino in 1827 and Greek independence.

Later influence

Philhellenism left a lasting mark on European culture and politics, influencing education, archaeology, and the neoclassical style in art and architecture. Within Greece itself, the philhellenic ideal of continuity with antiquity shaped national identity, cultural policy, and debates about language and heritage that continued into the modern era.
