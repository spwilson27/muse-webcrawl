Pasta is a type of food typically made from an unleavened dough of wheat flour mixed with water or eggs, formed into sheets or other shapes, and cooked by boiling or baking. In Italian cuisine it is the archetypal first course (primo piatto). Italian law requires dried pasta sold in Italy to be made from durum wheat semolina, whose high gluten content gives the pasta its firm bite when cooked al dente ("to the tooth").

Types

There are several hundred distinct pasta shapes, broadly grouped into long pasta (spaghetti, linguine, fettuccine), short tubular pasta (penne, rigatoni, maccheroni), shaped pasta (fusilli, farfalle, conchiglie), sheet pasta (lasagne) and filled pasta (ravioli, tortellini, agnolotti). Small soup pastas include orzo and ditalini; dumpling-like gnocchi are made from potato or semolina dough. Fresh egg pasta is traditional in northern Italy, especially Emilia-Romagna, while dried semolina pasta dominates in the south.

History

Dried pasta developed in medieval southern Italy and Sicily — the geographer al-Idrisi described pasta manufacture near Palermo in 1154 — benefiting from durum wheat and a climate suited to drying. The popular story that Marco Polo brought noodles from China is a myth; pasta was established in Italy before his return. Industrial production in Naples and later mechanised extrusion made pasta cheap and ubiquitous in the 19th century, and Italian emigration spread it worldwide; the pairing with tomato sauce became canonical in the same period.

Production and consumption

Italy is the world's largest producer and consumer of pasta, with annual consumption per person of roughly 23 kilograms. International production exceeds 16 million tonnes. Pasta is typically boiled in well-salted water and finished in its sauce; classic dishes include spaghetti aglio e olio, carbonara, ragù alla bolognese, cacio e pepe and pasta al pomodoro.
