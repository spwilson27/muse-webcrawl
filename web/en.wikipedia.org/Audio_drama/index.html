Audio drama

An audio drama, or radio play, is a dramatised, purely acoustic performance. With no visual component, it depends on dialogue, music and sound effects to help the listener imagine the characters and story. Radio drama achieved widespread popularity within a decade of its initial development in the 1920s, and by the 1940s it was a leading international popular entertainment.

Radio drama was a dominant form of home entertainment before television.

History

Early milestones include the BBC's A Comedy of Danger (1924), written for a blind audience of listeners. In the United States, network radio of the 1930s and 1940s — the "Golden Age of Radio" — produced soap operas, mysteries, westerns and comedies. Orson Welles's Mercury Theatre on the Air broadcast of The War of the Worlds in 1938 became the most famous radio drama of all, noted for its faux-news format. Television displaced radio drama in the United States in the 1950s, but it remained strong in Britain, Germany (as the Hörspiel) and elsewhere; BBC Radio 4's soap The Archers has run since 1951.

Modern audio drama

The rise of podcasting and streaming has produced a revival of audio drama since the 2010s, with fiction podcasts in genres from horror to science fiction, and companies producing full-cast drama series both for broadcast and direct release.

Technique

Because the medium is blind, writers and sound designers establish scenes through dialogue, narration, music cues and sound effects — doors, footsteps, weather and crowd noise. Stereo and binaural recording are used to place sounds in space around the listener.
