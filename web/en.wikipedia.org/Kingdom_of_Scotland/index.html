Kingdom of Scotland

Existed: 843 – 1707
Capital: Edinburgh (from c. 1452)
Traditional founder: Kenneth MacAlpin
Succeeded by: Kingdom of Great Britain (Acts of Union 1707)

The Kingdom of Scotland (Scottish Gaelic: Rìoghachd na h-Alba; Scots: Kinrick o Scotland) was a sovereign state in north-west Europe, traditionally said to have been founded in 843 with the unification of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata and the Pictish kingdom under Kenneth MacAlpin. It endured until the Acts of Union 1707 merged it with the Kingdom of England into the Kingdom of Great Britain.

History

The early kingdom, known as Alba, gradually absorbed the British kingdom of Strathclyde and English-speaking Lothian. David I (r. 1124–1153) transformed Scotland through burghs, feudal tenures, and church reform. After Alexander III's death in 1286 and the demise of his granddaughter Margaret, the Maid of Norway, the succession crisis triggered the Wars of Scottish Independence against Edward I of England, in which William Wallace and Robert the Bruce — victor at Bannockburn in 1314 — became national heroes.

The Stewart monarchy brought Scotland into European diplomacy; James IV and James V expanded royal authority, while James VI inherited the English throne in 1603, creating the Union of the Crowns. The Scottish Reformation of 1560 established a Presbyterian national church. The 17th century saw the Covenanters, civil wars, and the Cromwellian occupation. Financial disaster in the Darien scheme and English political pressure preceded the Union of 1707.

Government and society

Scotland was governed by the monarch with the Parliament of Scotland at Edinburgh, the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland, and a distinct legal system — Scots law — which survived the Union. Its culture blended Gaelic Highland and Scots-speaking Lowland traditions.
