Propaganda model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The propaganda model is a conceptual model in political economy advanced by Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky to explain how propaganda and systemic biases function in corporate mass media in democratic societies. Proposed in their 1988 book Manufacturing Consent: The Political Economy of the Mass Media, the theory argues that the way news is framed and selected results not from conspiracy or censorship by the state, but from structural economic factors that shape media output.

According to Herman and Chomsky, mass media in the United States operate through five institutional "filters" that determine what becomes news and how it is presented. Because media outlets depend on advertising revenue, access to official sources, and the goodwill of powerful interests, stories that threaten those interests tend to be marginalized, while those compatible with them are amplified.

The five filters

The model identifies five classes of filters:

  1. Size, ownership, and profit orientation. The dominant media firms are large corporations, often part of larger conglomerates, whose primary imperative is profit. Their output is therefore shaped by the interests of owners and shareholders.
  2. Advertising as the primary income source. Because advertisers, rather than audiences, provide most media revenue, outlets must produce content that attracts affluent audiences and creates a "buying mood" favourable to advertisers.
  3. Sourcing. Media rely heavily on information provided by government, business, and expert sources funded by those powers. This gives powerful actors privileged access to the news agenda and makes challenging them costly.
  4. Flak. Negative responses to media statements or programmes — complaints, lawsuits, or organized pressure — discipline media organizations and discourage deviation from acceptable positions.
  5. Anticommunism / fear. Originally framed as anticommunism, this filter involves the invocation of a common enemy (later described by Herman as ideologies and fears more generally, such as the "war on terror") that rallies public opinion and marginalizes dissent.

Applications and case studies

Herman and Chomsky applied the model to comparative case studies, contrasting coverage of events that were similar in character but differed in their political usefulness — for example, coverage of elections in El Salvador and Guatemala versus Poland, reporting on the Khmer Rouge in Cambodia versus Indonesian atrocities in East Timor, and the disparity in coverage of "worthy" and "unworthy" victims. They also analysed coverage of the Vietnam War and the 1984 assassination attempt on Pope John Paul II.

Reception and criticism

The propaganda model has been influential in media studies and political science, and empirical studies have found support for some of its predictions about coverage patterns. Critics have argued that the model overstates media homogeneity, underestimates journalistic professionalism and audience agency, and does not fully account for cases where media coverage has challenged powerful interests. Herman and Chomsky responded that the model predicts tendencies and probabilities rather than uniform outcomes, and that exceptional cases do not refute the structural pattern.
