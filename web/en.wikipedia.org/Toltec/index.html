The Toltec culture (Nahuatl: Toltecah) is an archaeological Mesoamerican culture that dominated a state centred in Tula, Hidalgo, Mexico, in the early Postclassic period, roughly 900–1168 AD. The later Aztec culture saw the Toltecs as their intellectual and cultural predecessors and described Toltec culture emanating from Tollan (Nahuatl for Tula) as the epitome of civilisation; in Aztec Nahuatl the word toltecatl came to mean "artisan" or "artist".

Tula

At its height Tula covered some 14 square kilometres with a population estimated in the tens of thousands. Its ceremonial centre included pyramids, ballcourts, colonnaded halls and the famous Atlantean columns—four-metre-high statues of warriors that once supported a temple roof. Chacmool sculptures, serpent walls (coatepantli) and warrior imagery point to strong connections with the Maya city of Chichen Itza, whose similar architecture has long fuelled debate about the direction of influence between the two.

History and legend

Aztec and colonial sources tell of the priest-king Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl, who ruled Tollan and was exiled after conflict with devotees of Tezcatlipoca, departing eastward. Historians treat these accounts as a mixture of myth and history. Tula was burned and abandoned in the 12th century, around 1168 in traditional chronologies. The Toltecs are credited in later tradition with advances in agriculture, craft production, calendar keeping and architecture, and their prestige shaped the claims to legitimacy of later peoples, including the Mexica of Tenochtitlan, whose rulers married into lines claiming Toltec descent.
