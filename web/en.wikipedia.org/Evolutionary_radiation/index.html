Evolutionary radiation

An evolutionary radiation is an increase in taxonomic diversity that is caused by elevated rates of speciation, which may or may not be associated with an increase in morphological disparity. Radiations may affect one clade or many, and be rapid or gradual; where they are rapid and driven by a single lineage's adaptation to their environment, they are termed adaptive radiations.

Examples

The most famous example of an evolutionary radiation is that of placental mammals shortly after the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs at the end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago. At that time, most placental mammals were small nocturnal insect-eaters, but within a few million years they diversified into a wide range of forms — bats, whales, horses, and primates.

Other well-known radiations include the Cambrian explosion of animal phyla, the radiation of flowering plants (angiosperms) during the Cretaceous, the diversification of birds after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event, and the adaptive radiations of cichlid fish in the Great Lakes of East Africa and of Darwin's finches in the Galápagos Islands.

Causes

Radiations often follow the acquisition of a key innovation — a novel trait that allows a lineage to exploit new resources, such as powered flight in birds or phytophagy (plant-eating) in insects. They may also be triggered by ecological opportunity, as when a mass extinction removes competitors and frees ecological space, or when a lineage colonizes a new environment such as an island archipelago or a newly formed lake.

Radiations in the fossil record

Palaeontologists identify radiations through increases in the number of fossil taxa through time. Some radiations are concentrated bursts — such as the Cambrian explosion — while others, like the radiation of marine animal families through the Phanerozoic, unfolded over tens or hundreds of millions of years. Distinguishing genuine evolutionary radiations from artifacts of sampling and preservation remains a central challenge in palaeobiology.
