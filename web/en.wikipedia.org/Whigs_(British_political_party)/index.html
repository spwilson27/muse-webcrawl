Whigs (British political party)

The Whigs were a political party in the Parliaments of England, Scotland, Ireland, Great Britain and the United Kingdom. Between the 1680s and the 1850s, the Whigs contested power with their rivals, the Tories. The Whigs' origin lay in constitutional monarchism and opposition to absolute monarchy, and they played a central role in the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

Origins

The Whigs emerged during the Exclusion Crisis of 1678–1681, when they sought to exclude the Roman Catholic James, Duke of York, from the succession to the throne. The name "Whig" derives from "whiggamore", a term for Scottish Presbyterian rebels. Whig principles — parliamentary supremacy, resistance to arbitrary royal power, and (limited) religious toleration for Protestant dissenters — triumphed in the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and the Bill of Rights 1689.

The Whig supremacy

After the Hanoverian succession of 1714, the Whigs enjoyed decades of dominance, the so-called "Whig supremacy" (c. 1714–1760). Robert Walpole, often regarded as the first prime minister, led the government from 1721 to 1742. The Whig ascendancy rested on aristocratic landowning families, control of patronage, and support from the financial and commercial interests of the City of London.

19th century and transformation

In the 19th century the Whigs championed reform: the Reform Act 1832 under Earl Grey, the abolition of slavery in the British Empire (1833), factory and poor-law reform, and free trade, including repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 (engineered by the Tory-turned-Peelite prime minister Robert Peel with Whig support). By 1859 the Whigs merged with Peelites and Radicals to form the Liberal Party, with William Ewart Gladstone becoming its dominant figure.
