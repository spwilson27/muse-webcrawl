Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix, a small pouch attached to the large intestine. It typically presents with abdominal pain that begins around the navel and later migrates to the right lower quadrant of the abdomen, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite, and low-grade fever. It is one of the most common causes of emergency abdominal surgery worldwide.

Signs and symptoms

Classical symptoms occur in roughly half of patients. Pain at McBurney's point, rebound tenderness, and guarding may be present on examination. The psoas sign, obturator sign, and Rovsing's sign are additional clinical findings. Symptoms in young children, pregnant women, and the elderly may be atypical, making diagnosis more difficult.

Causes

Appendicitis is thought to result from obstruction of the appendiceal lumen, commonly by a fecalith, lymphoid hyperplasia, or, less often, parasites or tumors. Obstruction leads to bacterial overgrowth, increased intraluminal pressure, ischemia, and eventually perforation if untreated.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on history, physical examination, and laboratory tests showing an elevated white blood cell count. Imaging with computed tomography, ultrasound, or magnetic resonance imaging can confirm the diagnosis, particularly in atypical presentations. Scoring systems such as the Alvarado score assist clinical assessment.

Treatment

The standard treatment is appendectomy, performed laparoscopically or by open surgery. Antibiotics alone may be an option for selected cases of uncomplicated appendicitis, though recurrence rates are higher. Delayed treatment risks perforation, peritonitis, and abscess formation.
