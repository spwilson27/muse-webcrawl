Heliocentrism


The heliocentric model

Heliocentrism is a superseded astronomical model in which the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun at the centre of the universe. Historically, it was opposed to geocentrism, which places the Earth at the centre. The notion that the Earth revolves around the Sun had been proposed as early as the 3rd century BC by Aristarchus of Samos, but received no support from most other ancient astronomers. The heliocentric theory was revived and mathematically developed by Nicolaus Copernicus in the 16th century, becoming a central episode of the Scientific Revolution.

History

Aristarchus proposed that the Sun, not the Earth, stood at the centre, but his idea was rejected in favour of Aristotelian and Ptolemaic geocentrism, partly because heliocentrism predicted stellar parallax that could not be observed. Medieval Indian, Islamic, and later European astronomers developed mathematical tools used by Copernicus, whose De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (1543) presented a complete heliocentric system that explained retrograde motion naturally, though it retained circular orbits and epicycles.

Johannes Kepler replaced circles with elliptical orbits in 1609, and Galileo Galilei's telescopic observations—especially the phases of Venus and the moons of Jupiter—undermined the Ptolemaic system, though Galileo's support for heliocentrism led to his trial and house arrest in 1633. Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) supplied the physical mechanism, and direct evidence accumulated with stellar aberration (1729), stellar parallax (1838), and Foucault's pendulum (1851). The Catholic Church gradually lifted its bans on heliocentric books in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Modern view

Strictly, modern astronomy holds that no point is the centre of the universe; the Sun is the centre of the Solar System approximately, with planets orbiting the system's barycentre. "Heliocentrism" nonetheless survives as the standard working frame for the Solar System and as a landmark in the history of science.
