A powwow is a gathering of North American Indigenous peoples that centres on dancing, singing, socialising and celebrating Native American and First Nations cultures. Modern powwows may be one-day events or run for a week, and range from small community gatherings to large competitive events drawing thousands of dancers and spectators.

Etymology

The word derives from the Narragansett word powwaw, meaning "spiritual leader", and was adopted into English during the colonial period. It came to be applied broadly to Indigenous gatherings featuring dance and ceremony.

History

The modern powwow circuit developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, drawing on the dance traditions of Plains peoples, including the Omaha, Ponca and Lakota. Intertribal gatherings spread after the Second World War, and powwows became a central expression of pan-Indian identity as well as tribal tradition.

Events

A powwow typically begins with a Grand Entry, in which dancers enter the arena in procession led by flag bearers and veterans. Dance categories include men's and women's traditional, grass dance, fancy dance, jingle dress and fancy shawl. Drumming and singing are provided by drum groups seated around large drums at the edge of the arena. Competitive powwows award prize money in each category, judged on regalia, dancing style and timing with the drum.

Etiquette

Visitors are generally welcome at public powwows, but are expected to follow the guidance of the emcee, stand during the Grand Entry and flag songs, and ask permission before photographing individual dancers. Regalia should not be touched without permission.
