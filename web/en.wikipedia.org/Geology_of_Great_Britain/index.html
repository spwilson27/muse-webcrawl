Geology of Great Britain

The geology of Great Britain is renowned for its diversity across a relatively small area. Over nearly three billion years the island's rocks have recorded continental collisions, tropical seas, deserts, volcanic eruptions, and ice ages. Britain played a foundational role in the development of geology as a science: William Smith produced the first geological map of a whole country in 1815, and many divisions of the geological time scale — Cambrian, Ordovician, Silurian, Devonian, and Carboniferous among them — take their names from British places or peoples.

Overview

In broad terms the oldest and hardest rocks lie in the northwest, and successively younger, softer sedimentary rocks outcrop toward the southeast. The ancient Precambrian basement of the Lewisian gneiss complex in northwest Scotland is up to about 3 billion years old, among the oldest rocks in Europe. Scotland's northwest Highlands and islands were once part of the ancient continent of Laurentia, separated from England and Wales by the Iapetus Ocean until the Caledonian orogeny closed it in the Silurian and Devonian periods, stitching the island together along a line roughly from the Solway Firth to Berwick.

Major episodes

During the Carboniferous period, Britain lay near the equator; cyclic deltaic and shallow-marine sediments produced the limestones, sandstones, and coal measures later mined in the industrial revolution. The Variscan orogeny at the end of the Carboniferous folded the rocks of southwest England and emplaced the Cornubian granites. In the Permian and Triassic the landmass lay in an arid interior, depositing red desert sandstones, while in the Jurassic and Cretaceous shallow seas covered much of the island, laying down the fossil-rich Jurassic Coast strata and the Chalk that forms the White Cliffs of Dover and much of southern England's downland.

The opening of the North Atlantic in the Paleogene was accompanied by extensive volcanism in what is now western Scotland and Northern Ireland — the flood basalts of Skye, Mull, and the Giant's Causeway. Finally, repeated glaciations of the Pleistocene ice ages scoured the uplands, deposited till across the lowlands, and, by lowering global sea level and later flooding the land bridge of Doggerland, separated Britain from continental Europe roughly 8,000 years ago.

Economic geology

Coal, ironstone, and later North Sea oil and gas underpinned Britain's industrial history. Building stones — from Cotswold limestone to Aberdeen granite — give British towns much of their regional character.
