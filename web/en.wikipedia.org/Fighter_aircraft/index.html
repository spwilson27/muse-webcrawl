Fighter aircraft

A fighter aircraft is a fixed-wing military aircraft designed primarily for air-to-air combat. Its main tactical purpose is to establish air superiority of the battlespace; fighters are small, fast and highly manoeuvrable, and carry a mixture of cannon and guided missiles. Since the Second World War, fighters have also been widely used for ground attack, in which case they are classed as fighter-bombers or multirole fighters.

History

Fighters emerged during the First World War, when synchronised machine guns allowed aircraft to shoot along their line of flight; types such as the Fokker Eindecker, Sopwith Camel and SPAD S.XIII fought for control of the skies over the trenches. Between the wars, all-metal monoplanes replaced biplanes. The Second World War produced classic fighters including the Supermarine Spitfire, Messerschmitt Bf 109, Mitsubishi A6M Zero and North American P-51 Mustang, with speeds exceeding 700 km/h.

Jet propulsion transformed the fighter after 1945: the Korean War saw the first jet-versus-jet battles between the F-86 Sabre and MiG-15. Subsequent generations added radar and guided missiles (second and third generations), then fly-by-wire agility, pulse-Doppler radar and multirole capability (fourth generation). The current fifth generation — including the F-22 Raptor, F-35 Lightning II, Chengdu J-20 and Sukhoi Su-57 — emphasises stealth, sensor fusion and networked operations.

Characteristics

Key attributes are speed, climb rate, manoeuvrability, sensor performance and survivability. Modern fighters typically cost tens of millions of dollars each and are among the most complex machines in military service. Uncrewed combat aerial vehicles are under development and may complement or replace piloted fighters in the coming decades.
