Cryopreservation

Cryopreservation or cryo-conservation is a process where biological material — cells, tissues, organs, or any other biological constructs susceptible to damage caused by unregulated chemical kinetics — is preserved by cooling to very low temperatures, typically −80 °C using solid carbon dioxide or −196 °C using liquid nitrogen. At such temperatures, enzymatic and chemical activity is effectively halted, allowing material to be stored for extended periods and revived later.

Principles

The central problem of cryopreservation is ice. As biological material cools, water inside and outside cells can form ice crystals that mechanically damage membranes and organelles; at the same time, the exclusion of solutes from growing ice concentrates salts and other substances in the remaining liquid, causing osmotic and chemical injury. Successful protocols balance cooling rate against these two hazards: cooling too slowly exposes cells to prolonged solute concentration, while cooling too quickly traps water inside cells where it freezes lethally. Many cells have an optimal, cell-type-specific cooling rate on the order of one degree Celsius per minute.

Cryoprotectants are substances that reduce freezing damage. Permeating agents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and glycerol enter cells and limit ice formation and solute concentration; non-permeating agents such as sugars and hydroxyethyl starch protect from outside the cell. An alternative strategy, vitrification, uses very high cryoprotectant concentrations and rapid cooling to solidify the sample into a glass-like state without any ice crystals forming at all. Vitrification is now standard for human oocytes and embryos.

History

The field began in 1949, when Christopher Polge and colleagues at the National Institute for Medical Research in London accidentally discovered that glycerol protected fowl spermatozoa during freezing — a serendipitous result of a mislabelled bottle. James Lovelock proposed the underlying theory that damage was caused by salt concentration, and Peter Mazur later formalised the two-factor hypothesis of freezing injury. Human sperm banking began in the 1950s; the first human birth from a frozen embryo occurred in 1984 (Zoe Leyland, in Melbourne), and cryopreservation of blood, stem cells, and plant germplasm became routine in medicine and agriculture.

Applications

  • Reproductive medicine: sperm, oocytes, and embryos are routinely frozen for fertility treatment and fertility preservation.
  • Transfusion and transplantation: rare blood types, haematopoietic stem cells, and umbilical cord blood are banked frozen; research continues on whole organs, which remain far harder to preserve because of their size and structural complexity.
  • Conservation: seed banks (such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, though seeds there are stored cool and dry rather than cryogenic), frozen zoos holding genetic material of endangered species, and cryobanks of coral, plant meristems, and microbial cultures.
  • Research: cell lines are stored indefinitely in liquid nitrogen, preserving experimental material across decades.

Some naturally freeze-tolerant animals — wood frogs, certain insects, and tardigrades in cryptobiosis — survive internal ice or extreme desiccation, and studying their adaptations informs protocol design. The preservation of deceased humans in the hope of future revival, offered by cryonics organisations, is a separate and scientifically unproven practice; no mammal has ever been revived from cryopreservation at liquid nitrogen temperatures.
