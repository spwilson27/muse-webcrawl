Equilibrioception

The vestibular system of the inner ear, the main organ of balance

Equilibrioception, or the sense of balance, is the physiological sense that allows animals to maintain their balance and sense body movement in terms of acceleration and direction. It is one of the senses sometimes counted beyond the traditional five, and it prevents humans and other animals from falling over when standing or moving.

The vestibular system

In most mammals, the sense of balance is provided primarily by the vestibular system in the inner ear. Three semicircular canals detect rotational movements of the head in three planes, while two otolith organs — the utricle and the saccule — detect linear acceleration, including the direction of gravity. Hair cells within these structures convert mechanical movement into nerve signals carried by the vestibular nerve to the brainstem and cerebellum.

Integration with other senses

Balance is maintained by integrating vestibular input with vision and proprioception (information from muscles and joints about body position). The eyes help determine where the body is in space; sensory receptors in the neck indicate the direction in which the head is turned; and receptors in the ankles register the body's sway on the standing surface. When these inputs conflict, as in motion sickness, the result can be dizziness and nausea.

Dysfunction

Disruption of equilibrioception can result from inner ear infections, Ménière's disease, benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, stroke, head injury, or certain medications and alcohol. Symptoms include vertigo, dizziness, imbalance, and nausea. Clinicians test the balance system with procedures such as caloric testing, rotational chair testing, and posturography.

In other animals

Most vertebrates possess a vestibular system similar to that of humans. Invertebrates use different structures; many insects, for example, have halteres that function as gyroscopic sense organs, while some animals use statocysts — small organs containing a dense granule whose movement signals orientation relative to gravity.
