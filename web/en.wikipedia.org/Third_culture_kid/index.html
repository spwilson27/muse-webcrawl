Third culture kid

A third culture kid (TCK) is a person who has spent a significant part of their developmental years outside their parents' culture. The term was coined by sociologists John and Ruth Useem in the 1950s, in research on American expatriate families in India. The "third culture" is the culture a child builds for themselves that combines elements of their parents' home culture (the first culture) and the host culture in which they grow up (the second culture), without fully belonging to either.

Characteristics

Third culture kids are typically the children of expatriates, military personnel ("military brats"), diplomats, missionaries, and international business employees; as adults they are sometimes called adult third culture kids (ATCKs). Research, including the widely read book Third Culture Kids: Growing Up Among Worlds by David Pollock and Ruth Van Reken, describes common traits: multilingualism, cultural adaptability, a global outlook, and ease in cross-cultural settings, alongside challenges such as rootlessness, restlessness, unresolved grief from repeated moves and goodbyes, and difficulty answering the question "Where are you from?"

Identity and research

Because their formative experiences differ from those of monocultural peers, TCKs often report feeling most at home with others who have had similar internationally mobile childhoods. The growing population of internationally mobile families has made the TCK experience an increasingly studied topic in psychology, education, and intercultural communication.
