Kerr effect

The Kerr effect, also called the quadratic electro-optic (QEO) effect, is a change in the refractive index of a material in response to an applied electric field. The change in refractive index is proportional to the square of the electric field, which distinguishes the Kerr effect from the linear Pockels effect. The effect was discovered in 1875 by the Scottish physicist John Kerr.

Description

When a strong electric field is applied across a transparent medium, the material becomes birefringent: light polarised parallel to the field travels at a different speed from light polarised perpendicular to it. The induced birefringence is Δn = λ K E², where K is the Kerr constant of the material, λ the wavelength and E the field strength.

Applications

A Kerr cell — a liquid-filled cell (historically nitrobenzene) between crossed polarisers with electrodes — acts as a very fast optical shutter or modulator, historically used in high-speed photography, film sound recording and early television scanning (the Kerr cell featured in mechanical television systems). The related optical Kerr effect, in which the field is that of an intense light beam itself, underlies Kerr-lens modelocking in ultrafast lasers and causes self-focusing in nonlinear optics. The distinct magneto-optic Kerr effect concerns the rotation of polarised light reflected from a magnetised surface.
