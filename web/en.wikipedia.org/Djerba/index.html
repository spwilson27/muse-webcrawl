Djerba (also spelled Jerba; Arabic: جربة) is an island in the Gulf of Gabès off the south-eastern coast of Tunisia, and the largest island of North Africa. It is connected to the mainland by a Roman-era causeway, the El Kantara, and by ferry services, and is a major tourist destination known for its whitewashed villages, palm groves, sandy beaches and traditional crafts, especially pottery.

History

Djerba is traditionally identified with the island of the Lotus-Eaters in Homer's Odyssey. The island was a centre of olive oil and purple dye production in antiquity and passed successively under Carthaginian, Roman, Vandal, Byzantine and Arab rule. It was a haven for Ibadi Muslims and later became a base of Barbary corsairs, most famously the Dragut. The Ottoman Empire took control in the sixteenth century, and Djerba became part of independent Tunisia in 1956.

Culture and population

Djerba's population is largely Amazigh (Berber) and Arab, with distinctive traditions in music, dance and architecture. It is home to one of the oldest Jewish communities in the world, centred on the El Ghriba synagogue at Er Riadh, which is the focus of an annual pilgrimage and one of the few continuously inhabited Jewish communities in the Arab world. The island is also famous for its pottery village of Guellala, its markets, and its distinctive menzel courtyard houses. Since the 2010s, the island's old town of Houmt Souk has attracted street art, including the Djerbahood open-air museum project.

Economy and tourism

Tourism is the island's main industry, with a large concentration of beach resorts and hotels, particularly around Midoun and Sidi Mahrez. Djerba-Zarzis International Airport serves charter and scheduled flights. Traditional economic activities—olive cultivation, fishing and handicrafts—remain important, though the tourism sector has been affected by security concerns following the 2015 Sousse attacks and by the COVID-19 pandemic.
