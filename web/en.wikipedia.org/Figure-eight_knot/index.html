Figure-eight knot

The figure-eight knot or figure-of-eight knot is a type of stopper knot. It is a simple, reliable knot shaped like the numeral 8, used to prevent a rope from running out of a retaining device such as a block, cleat, or belay device. It is very important in both sailing and rock climbing as a method of stopping ropes from running out of retaining devices, and as the basis for the figure-eight follow-through used to tie climbers into a harness.

Tying

To tie a figure-eight on a single strand, form a loop in the rope, pass the working end behind the standing part and back down through the loop, then dress and tighten. The result is a bulky stopper that is easier to untie after loading than the simpler overhand knot, which is one reason sailors and climbers prefer it.

Climbing uses

The figure-eight follow-through (figure-eight rethreaded) is the standard tie-in knot for rock climbers: a figure-eight is tied in the rope, the end is passed through the harness tie-in points, and the knot is retraced. The figure-eight on a bight creates a strong loop for anchors and clipping. The Flemish bend (figure-eight bend) joins two ropes. These knots are strong — retaining roughly 70–80% of rope strength — self-evident to inspect, and readily checked by partners, which accounts for their dominance in climbing instruction.

In mathematics

In knot theory, the figure-eight knot is the unique knot with crossing number four besides the trefoil family: it is the simplest hyperbolic knot, amphichiral (equivalent to its mirror image), and its complement was central to William Thurston's work on hyperbolic geometry. It should not be confused with the practical rope knot, though the two share the same diagram.
