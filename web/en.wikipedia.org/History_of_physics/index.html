History of physics

The history of physics traces the attempt to understand the fundamental nature of matter, motion, energy, and the universe, from ancient natural philosophy to the modern theories of relativity and quantum mechanics. Physics emerged as a distinct experimental and mathematical science in the 17th century and has repeatedly revolutionised technology and humanity's picture of the cosmos.

Ancient and medieval physics

Greek natural philosophers such as Thales, Democritus (who proposed atoms), and Aristotle sought rational explanations of nature. Aristotle's physics — a geocentric cosmos of four elements moving toward natural places — dominated for nearly two thousand years, though Archimedes founded statics and hydrostatics with mathematical rigour. In the medieval Islamic world, Ibn al-Haytham's Book of Optics (c. 1021) pioneered experimental method, while scholars such as Avicenna and later European thinkers like Buridan and Oresme developed ideas of impetus and graphical representation of motion that foreshadowed classical mechanics.

The Scientific Revolution

Copernicus's heliocentric model (1543), Kepler's laws of planetary motion (1609–1619), and Galileo's experiments on falling bodies and telescopic observations undermined the Aristotelian world picture. The revolution culminated in Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), which unified terrestrial and celestial motion under universal gravitation and three laws of motion, presented with the new mathematics of calculus. Newton also made fundamental contributions to optics. During the 18th century, Euler, Lagrange, and Laplace recast mechanics in analytical form, and scientists such as Bernoulli and d'Alembert advanced fluid dynamics.

The 19th century: energy, heat and electromagnetism

The 19th century established the conservation of energy (Mayer, Joule, Helmholtz), the science of thermodynamics (Carnot's analysis of heat engines, 1824; the entropy concept of Clausius, 1865; the absolute temperature scale of Kelvin), and statistical mechanics (Maxwell, Boltzmann, Gibbs), which explained heat in terms of atomic motion. In parallel, Ørsted, Ampère, and Faraday uncovered the links between electricity and magnetism, and James Clerk Maxwell's equations (1865) unified them into the theory of electromagnetism, predicting electromagnetic waves — confirmed by Hertz in 1887 — and identifying light as such a wave.

The modern era: relativity and quantum theory

Anomalies at the century's turn — the Michelson–Morley experiment, blackbody radiation, radioactivity (Becquerel, 1896), and the electron (Thomson, 1897) — precipitated a revolution. Planck introduced the quantum of action (1900); Einstein's 1905 papers explained the photoelectric effect with light quanta, established special relativity, and gave the mass–energy relation E = mc². His general theory of relativity (1915) recast gravity as the curvature of spacetime. Rutherford's nuclear atom (1911) and Bohr's model (1913) led to full quantum mechanics in 1925–26 (Heisenberg, Schrödinger, Dirac), a probabilistic theory of extraordinary precision. Quantum field theory and the Standard Model of particle physics, assembled in the mid-20th century and completed with the discovery of the Higgs boson at CERN in 2012, describe the fundamental particles and forces except gravity.

Cosmology and later developments

Twentieth-century physics also transformed cosmology: Hubble's discovery of the expanding universe (1929), the prediction and 1965 detection of the cosmic microwave background, and the Big Bang model. Condensed matter physics yielded the transistor (1947) and the laser (1960), underpinning modern electronics, while nuclear physics produced both weapons and power generation. Open problems — quantum gravity, dark matter, dark energy — continue to drive the field.
