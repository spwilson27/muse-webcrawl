Venom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Venom
Definition: Toxin delivered by bite or sting
Venomous animals: Snakes, spiders, scorpions, jellyfish, cone snails, bees, some mammals
Components: Enzymes, neurotoxins, cardiotoxins, hemotoxins
Treatment: Antivenom, supportive care

Venom is a type of toxin produced by an animal and actively delivered through a wound — by bite, sting, or a specialized apparatus such as fangs or spines — to another organism. Venom differs from poison, which is ingested, inhaled, or absorbed through the skin: a venomous cobra injects, while a poisonous frog harms those who eat it.

Venom is delivered through specialized structures such as fangs.

Composition and effects

Venoms are complex cocktails of proteins and peptides: phospholipases that destroy cell membranes, neurotoxins that block nerve signals, procoagulants and anticoagulants that disrupt blood. A single snake venom may contain over 100 distinct toxins, tuned by evolution to immobilize prey and begin digestion, and secondarily to deter predators.

Evolution

Venom systems have evolved independently many times — in snakes, lizards, spiders, scorpions, centipedes, insects, jellyfish, cone snails, octopuses, and even a few mammals such as the platypus. Venom genes often arise by duplication of ordinary body proteins recruited into venom glands.

Medical use

Antivenom, produced by immunizing horses or sheep, remains the main treatment for serious envenomation. Venom components have also yielded drugs: captopril was modelled on a pit-viper peptide, and exenatide derives from Gila monster venom.
