Petroleum reserves in Saudi Arabia are among the largest in the world. The kingdom's proven oil reserves are estimated at roughly 267 billion barrels, around 17% of the global total, second only to Venezuela. Most reserves are concentrated in a relatively small number of giant fields in the Eastern Province, near the coast of the Persian Gulf, and are produced by the state-owned company Saudi Aramco.

History

Oil was first discovered in commercial quantities in 1938 at Dammam No. 7 in the Eastern Province, after King Abdulaziz granted a concession to Standard Oil of California. Production grew rapidly after the Second World War, and Saudi Arabia became a founding member of OPEC in 1960. The government gradually acquired full ownership of Aramco by 1980.

Major fields

The Ghawar Field, discovered in 1948, is the largest conventional oil field in the world, with original reserves estimated at over 70 billion barrels; it has produced more oil than any other single field. Other supergiant and giant fields include Safaniya (the world's largest offshore field), Khurais, Shaybah, Manifa, and Abqaiq. Because the fields are large, geologically favourable, and close to export terminals such as Ras Tanura, Saudi production costs are among the lowest in the world.

Reserve estimates and debate

Official reserve figures remained nearly flat at around 260 billion barrels for decades despite cumulative production, prompting periodic debate among analysts about the degree to which new discoveries and improved recovery offset output. An independent audit conducted for Saudi Aramco's 2019 initial public offering broadly supported the official figures.

Economic significance

Petroleum accounts for the dominant share of Saudi government revenue and export earnings. The kingdom's large reserves and spare production capacity give it a central role as OPEC's leading producer and the global oil market's traditional "swing producer". Efforts to diversify the economy away from oil are set out in the Saudi Vision 2030 programme.
