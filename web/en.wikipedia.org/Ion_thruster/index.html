Ion thruster

An ion thruster, ion drive, or ion engine is a form of electric propulsion used for spacecraft propulsion. It generates thrust by accelerating ions with electricity. Ion thrusters are categorized by how they ionise the propellant: electrostatic thrusters accelerate ions in an electric field, while electromagnetic types, such as the Hall-effect thruster, use combined electric and magnetic fields.

Characteristics

Ion thrusters achieve very high exhaust velocities — corresponding to specific impulses of roughly 1,500–5,000 seconds for electrostatic designs, several times those of chemical rockets — so they use far less propellant for a given change in velocity. The trade-off is extremely low thrust, often less than a newton: acceleration is gentle but can be sustained for months or years, accumulating large total Δv over a mission. Propellants are typically inert gases such as xenon, chosen for easy ionisation and high atomic mass; krypton and iodine are used in some newer designs.

Types

Gridded electrostatic ion thrusters (such as NASA's NSTAR and NEXT engines) ionise propellant in a discharge chamber and accelerate the ions through charged grids. Hall-effect thrusters accelerate ions in a crossed-field discharge and are widely used for satellite station-keeping. Radio-frequency and other variants differ mainly in the ionisation mechanism.

History and missions

The concept dates to early 20th-century theorists including Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and Robert H. Goddard, and was developed into hardware by Harold Kaufman at NASA from 1959; the first successful space test was SERT-1 in 1964. Ion propulsion flew as a primary system on NASA's Deep Space 1 (1998), JAXA's Hayabusa (2003), and NASA's Dawn mission (2007), which orbited the asteroids Vesta and Ceres on ion power. Modern communications satellites routinely use ion thrusters for station-keeping and orbit raising, and constellations such as Starlink use Hall-effect thrusters with krypton propellant.
