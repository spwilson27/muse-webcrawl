Acid house is a subgenre of house music developed around the mid-1980s by DJs and producers in Chicago. Its defining feature is the squelching, resonant bassline produced by the Roland TB-303 electronic bass synthesiser-sequencer, manipulated in real time to create a distinctive "acid" sound. The style is generally minimalist, built on repetitive four-on-the-floor drum machine patterns and hypnotic synth lines.

Origins

The genre's founding track is widely considered to be "Acid Tracks" (1987) by Phuture (DJ Pierre, Spanky and Herb J), who discovered the TB-303's sound by experimenting with the machine's knobs while a pattern played. Chicago DJs such as Ron Hardy had been playing 303-based experiments at the Music Box earlier. The term "acid" has been variously attributed to the track's corrosive sound, to "acid" as slang for LSD, and to a pun on "house" music sampling ("acid" as theft in Chicago slang).

Second Summer of Love

Imported to Britain in 1987, acid house ignited the UK rave scene. Promoters and DJs including Paul Oakenfold, Danny Rampling and Nicky Holloway — inspired by Balearic nights at Amnesia in Ibiza — launched clubs such as Shoom and Spectrum. The summers of 1988 and 1989, dubbed the "Second Summer of Love", saw huge illegal warehouse parties and orbital raves around the M25, the yellow smiley face as an emblem, and intense tabloid moral panic linking the music to the drug ecstasy. The movement laid the foundations for British rave and electronic dance music culture.

Musical characteristics

Tracks are typically in 4/4 time at 118–130 BPM, with drum patterns from Roland TR-707/808/909 machines, a TB-303 bassline whose resonance and cutoff are modulated live, and sparse elements: synth stabs, string pads, piano riffs, or short vocal samples. The aesthetic values repetition and gradual timbral change over conventional song structure.
