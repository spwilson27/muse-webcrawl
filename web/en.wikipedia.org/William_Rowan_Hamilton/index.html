William Rowan Hamilton

Sir William Rowan Hamilton (August 4, 1805 – September 2, 1865) was an Irish mathematician, physicist, and astronomer who made fundamental contributions to optics, dynamics, and algebra. He is best known for reformulating Newtonian mechanics — now called Hamiltonian mechanics — and for inventing quaternions.

Early life and education

A child prodigy, Hamilton mastered numerous languages as a boy and displayed extraordinary mathematical talent. He entered Trinity College Dublin in 1823, winning academic honors, and while still an undergraduate was appointed Andrews Professor of Astronomy and Royal Astronomer of Ireland in 1827.

Optics and dynamics

In his Theory of Systems of Rays (1828) Hamilton unified optics under a single characteristic function, and predicted conical refraction, later confirmed experimentally. He then extended the method to dynamics, producing in 1834–35 his famous equations of motion and the principle of least action in its Hamiltonian form. Hamiltonian mechanics became foundational to statistical mechanics and quantum theory.

Quaternions

After years seeking an algebra of three-dimensional rotations, Hamilton realized on October 16, 1843, while walking along Dublin's Royal Canal, that the solution required four dimensions: the quaternion, with the fundamental relation i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1, which he carved into Brougham Bridge. He devoted the rest of his life to quaternion research. He also coined the word "tensor" in a related sense and developed the calculus of quaternions that influenced vector analysis.

Later life

Hamilton was knighted in 1835. He died in Dublin in 1865. His name is attached to the Hamiltonian function, Hamiltonian paths in graph theory, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation.
