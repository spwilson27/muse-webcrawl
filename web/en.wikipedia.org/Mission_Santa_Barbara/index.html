Mission Santa Barbara is a Spanish mission in Santa Barbara, California, founded by the Franciscan order on 4 December 1786. It was the tenth of the 21 Spanish missions in California, established to Christianise the local Chumash people. Nicknamed the "Queen of the Missions" for the beauty of its twin-towered church, the mission remains an active parish church and a major landmark of the city.

History

The mission was founded by Father Fermín Lasuén on the feast day of Saint Barbara. Three adobe churches were built on the site before the present stone church, completed in 1820. The 1812 Santa Barbara earthquake destroyed the earlier church, and the towers of the new church were damaged again in the earthquake of 1925 and subsequently rebuilt.

Missionaries brought Chumash neophytes to live and work at the mission, where agriculture, livestock raising and crafts were organised around the mission quadrangle. A notable aqueduct system, partly built by Chumash labour, supplied water from nearby canyons; sections of the dam and aqueduct survive. Following Mexican secularisation in the 1830s, the mission lands were dispersed, though the church itself remained in Franciscan hands and has been continuously occupied by Franciscan friars since its founding.

Architecture

The church is built of sandstone in a neoclassical style modelled on a Roman temple described by Vitruvius, with a portico of Ionic columns and two matching bell towers — the only California mission church with twin towers. The mission complex includes a museum, gardens, a cemetery in which many Chumash and early Californian settlers are buried, and the original fountain and lavandería where mission residents washed clothes.
