253 Mathilde is a main-belt asteroid about 50 kilometres in diameter that was discovered by Austrian astronomer Johann Palisa on November 12, 1885, in Vienna. It is named after Mathilde, wife of the French astronomer Moritz Loewy. Mathilde is a carbonaceous C-type asteroid with an exceptionally slow rotation period of more than 17 days.

NEAR Shoemaker flyby

On June 27, 1997, NASA's NEAR Shoemaker spacecraft flew within 1,212 kilometres of Mathilde on its way to asteroid 433 Eros, returning the first close-up images of the asteroid. The images revealed a heavily cratered, dark world — Mathilde reflects only about 4 percent of the sunlight that strikes it, darker than charcoal — with several craters comparable in size to the asteroid's own radius, including the 20-kilometre-deep Karoo crater.

Physical characteristics

Combining Mathilde's measured volume with a mass estimate from the deflection of the spacecraft, scientists found a density of only about 1.3 g/cm³, implying a porous "rubble pile" interior with large void spaces. This loose structure explains how Mathilde could survive impacts that formed enormous craters without being shattered.
