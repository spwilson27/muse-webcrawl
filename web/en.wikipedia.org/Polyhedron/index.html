Polyhedron

A polyhedron (plural: polyhedra or polyhedrons; from Greek πολύεδρον, from poly- "many" and -hedron "base" or "seat") is a three-dimensional figure with flat polygonal faces, straight edges and sharp corners or vertices. A convex polyhedron is the convex hull of finitely many points, not all on the same plane. Cubes and pyramids are examples of convex polyhedra.

Characteristics

A polyhedron consists of polygonal faces arranged so that every edge is shared by exactly two faces. The vertices and edges form the polyhedron's graph, and for convex polyhedra Euler's formula holds: V − E + F = 2, where V is the number of vertices, E the number of edges, and F the number of faces.

Classes of polyhedra

The most symmetrical polyhedra are the five Platonic solids — the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron — whose faces are congruent regular polygons. Relaxing congruence gives the 13 Archimedean solids, with regular polygon faces and identical vertices. The duals of the Archimedean solids are the Catalan solids. The Johnson solids are strictly convex polyhedra with regular faces but no further uniformity.

Prisms, antiprisms and pyramids form simpler infinite families. Polyhedra may also be non-convex, including star polyhedra such as the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra. In four and more dimensions the analogous figures are called polytopes.

History

Polyhedra have been studied since antiquity. The final book of Euclid's Elements constructs the five Platonic solids, which Plato had associated with the classical elements. Archimedes described the semi-regular solids named after him, Kepler studied star polyhedra and duality, and Euler's formula of 1758 began the topological study of polyhedra that continues in modern combinatorics and geometry.
