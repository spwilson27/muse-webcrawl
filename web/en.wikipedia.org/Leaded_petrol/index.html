Leaded petrol

Leaded petrol, also known as leaded gasoline, is motor fuel to which the organolead compound tetraethyl lead (TEL) has been added as an antiknock agent. Introduced commercially in the 1920s, leaded petrol dramatically improved engine performance but released large quantities of toxic lead into the environment. It has been phased out worldwide; the last country, Algeria, ended its use in 2021, which the United Nations Environment Programme declared the end of the leaded petrol era.

History

Tetraethyl lead was discovered to suppress engine knocking by Thomas Midgley Jr. and colleagues at General Motors in 1921, and was marketed under the name "Ethyl" from 1923. Concerns about its toxicity arose almost immediately after workers at production plants suffered lead poisoning, and a 1925 U.S. Surgeon General conference debated a ban, but the additive was allowed to continue. For decades, lead from exhaust accumulated in soil and urban dust and entered the human body, particularly affecting children's neurological development.

Phase-out

Evidence assembled by scientists such as Clair Cameron Patterson, whose work on lead isotope dating exposed the scale of industrial lead contamination, helped drive regulation. The United States began phasing down lead in petrol in the 1970s, accelerated by the introduction of catalytic converters, which lead poisons; leaded fuel for road vehicles was banned outright in the U.S. in 1996. The European Union completed its phase-out by 2000, and developing countries followed under the UNEP Partnership for Clean Fuels and Vehicles. Lead additives remain in use in aviation gasoline (avgas) for piston-engine aircraft.

Health and environmental effects

Lead is a potent neurotoxin with no known safe level of exposure. Population blood-lead levels fell dramatically wherever leaded petrol was withdrawn, and researchers have linked its removal to measurable improvements in children's IQ and reductions in violent crime, an association known as the lead–crime hypothesis.
