Pentateuch

The Pentateuch (from Greek pentáteuchos, "five scrolls") is the first division of the Hebrew Bible, comprising the books of Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy. In Judaism it is called the Torah ("teaching" or "law") and is the most sacred part of the Tanakh. It is also the first five books of the Christian Old Testament, traditionally attributed to Moses and hence also called the "Five Books of Moses".

Contents

Genesis recounts creation, the early history of humanity, and the stories of the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and Joseph. Exodus describes the Israelites' escape from slavery in Egypt under Moses and the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai. Leviticus contains priestly legislation; Numbers narrates the wilderness wanderings; Deuteronomy presents Moses' farewell speeches restating the Law before the entry into Canaan.

Composition

Traditional belief holds that Moses authored the Pentateuch. Modern biblical scholarship, following the documentary hypothesis developed in the 19th century (notably by Julius Wellhausen) and its successors, argues that the text was compiled from multiple sources over several centuries, reaching its final form in the Persian period (roughly the 6th–5th centuries BC). The extent and dating of the sources remain debated.
