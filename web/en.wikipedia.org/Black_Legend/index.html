Black Legend

The Black Legend (Spanish: leyenda negra) is a historiographical term for a body of anti-Spanish propaganda — and, in the view of many historians, a persistent bias in historical writing — depicting Spain, the Spanish Empire, and Spaniards as uniquely cruel, fanatical, and backward.

Origins

Its sources include Bartolomé de las Casas's A Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies (1552), a Spanish Dominican's own denunciation of abuses against Indigenous peoples in the Americas, which was translated and republished across Protestant Europe, often with lurid engravings by Theodor de Bry. Spain's rivals — the Dutch during their revolt, England, and France — amplified such accounts during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries to justify their own colonial and military ambitions.

Debate

The term was coined by the Spanish historian Julián Juderías in 1914. Scholars continue to debate the balance: the atrocities of conquest, the Inquisition, and expulsion of Jews and Moriscos were real, but the Black Legend is said to exaggerate them relative to the records of other empires, and to have shaped a lasting negative stereotype of Spain in art, literature, and film.
