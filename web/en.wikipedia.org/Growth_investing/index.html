Growth investing

Growth investing is a style of investment strategy focused on capital appreciation. Those who follow this style, growth investors, invest in companies that exhibit signs of above-average growth, even if the share price appears expensive in terms of metrics such as price-to-earnings or price-to-book ratios. The style contrasts with value investing, which seeks shares trading below their intrinsic value.

Characteristics

Growth investors look for companies with rapidly increasing revenues and earnings, strong competitive advantages ("moats"), large addressable markets, and capable management. Such firms often reinvest profits into expansion rather than paying dividends, and are common in technology and other innovative sectors. A common valuation measure is the PEG ratio (price/earnings divided by earnings growth rate), popularised by Peter Lynch, who managed the Fidelity Magellan Fund with an approach blending growth and value ("growth at a reasonable price", or GARP).

History

Thomas Rowe Price Jr. is often called the father of growth investing, founding T. Rowe Price in 1937 around the philosophy of investing in well-managed companies in fertile fields whose earnings would grow over the long term. Philip Fisher's Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits (1958) also profoundly influenced growth investors, emphasising qualitative research into a company's prospects.

Risks and performance

Because growth shares are priced for high future expectations, they can fall sharply if growth disappoints, and they tend to be more volatile than the market. Growth and value styles move in long cycles of relative outperformance: growth dominated in the 1990s and again in the 2010s, driven by large technology companies, while value led in the early 2000s. Academic factor research (e.g. Fama and French) has historically found a value premium over very long periods, though results vary by era and market.
