400-series highways

The 400-series highways are a network of controlled-access freeways that form the backbone of the provincial highway system in the Canadian province of Ontario. The series is so named because all of its route numbers fall between 400 and 499. The highways are maintained by the Ministry of Transportation of Ontario and are equivalent in design standard to the Interstate Highway System in the United States.

History

The 400-series designation was introduced in 1952, when Highway 400 — the Toronto–Barrie highway — opened. Highway 401, which grew out of pre-war bypass projects around Toronto, became the network's centrepiece; its Toronto section is among the widest and busiest highways in the world, carrying several hundred thousand vehicles per day and featuring collector-express lane systems. Other early routes included Highway 402 to Sarnia and Highway 417 through Ottawa, designated in connection with the national capital.

Design standards

400-series highways are fully controlled-access, with grade-separated interchanges, divided medians, and design speeds typically of 100–110 km/h. They employ distinctive signing with the Ontario provincial highway shield and are built to standards governing lane widths, shoulders, and barrier systems. Newer additions to the series include the tolled Highway 407 ETR corridor north of Toronto and the Highway 413 and Bradford Bypass projects planned in the 2020s.
