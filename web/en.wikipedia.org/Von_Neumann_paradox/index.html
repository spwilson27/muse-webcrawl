Von Neumann paradox

In mathematics, the von Neumann paradox is the statement that one can partition the unit sphere into finitely many pieces and reassemble them, using only rotations (elements of the free group of rotations in three dimensions), into two copies of the original sphere. It is a stronger, group-theoretic forerunner of the Banach–Tarski paradox, proved by John von Neumann in 1929.

Background

The Banach–Tarski paradox (1924) showed that a ball can be decomposed and reassembled into two balls using the axiom of choice and the full group of rotations and translations. Von Neumann isolated the true source of the phenomenon: not the geometry of space itself, but the algebraic structure of the group acting on it. The rotation group SO(3) contains a free group on two generators, and free groups admit paradoxical decompositions — they can be partitioned into pieces that translations within the group reassemble into two copies of the whole group.

Amenability

Von Neumann's analysis introduced the concept now known as amenability: a group is amenable if it admits a finitely additive invariant measure (mean). The paradox shows that groups containing a free subgroup on two generators are non-amenable, which explains why the paradoxical decompositions fail in the plane for translations and rotations of an abelian or solvable group acting in low dimensions in certain settings, and it reframed the subject around the algebraic property rather than the geometry. The von Neumann–Day problem asked whether every non-amenable group contains a free subgroup; this was answered negatively decades later with the construction of non-amenable groups without free subgroups.
