Venus in fiction

The planet Venus has been used as a setting in fiction since before 1900. Its opaque cloud cover gave early science fiction writers free rein to imagine exotic worlds beneath it, and Venus was commonly depicted as a younger, warmer, wetter version of Earth, often covered in jungles or global oceans and inhabited by native life or human colonists. Discoveries by space probes in the 1960s, which revealed an extremely hot, high-pressure surface, largely ended this tradition, after which Venus appeared mainly in harder science fiction as a hostile world, or in deliberately retro settings.

Early depictions

One of the earliest fictional voyages to Venus was Achille Eyraud's Voyage à Vénus (1865). In the pulp era, Venus became a staple of planetary romance: Edgar Rice Burroughs set his Venus series, beginning with Pirates of Venus (1932), on a lush world called Amtor, and C. S. Lewis made Venus (Perelandra) the setting of a theological Eden story in Perelandra (1943). Robert A. Heinlein used a hot, swampy Venus in several works, and Isaac Asimov's juvenile novel Lucky Starr and the Oceans of Venus (1954) placed cities under a global ocean.

After the space probes

The Mariner 2 flyby of 1962 and subsequent Soviet Venera landers showed a surface hot enough to melt lead under crushing atmospheric pressure. Writers responded in different ways: some, such as Larry Niven and Poul Anderson, had already begun portraying a harsher Venus; others explored terraforming, floating cloud cities, or set stories in an imagined past. Ben Bova's Venus (2000) and later works treat the planet realistically as a deadly environment explored by hardened spacecraft.

Themes

Because Venus is named for the Roman goddess of love, early fiction often gendered the planet female and romantic; later works more often use it for stories about climate catastrophe, runaway greenhouse effects, and the fragility of Earth's environment. Venus remains, after Mars and the Moon, one of the most frequently used settings in the Solar System.
