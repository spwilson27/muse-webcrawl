Dispersal (ecology)

Biological dispersal refers to both the movement of individuals (animals, plants, fungi, bacteria, etc.) from their birth site to their breeding site (natal dispersal), as well as the movement from one breeding site to another (breeding dispersal). Dispersal is also used to describe the movement of propagules such as seeds and spores, which can establish new populations.

Types and mechanisms

Organisms disperse actively under their own power or passively by vectors such as wind (anemochory), water (hydrochory), and animals (zoochory), including transport in the gut or attached to fur. Dispersal distances are often summarized by a dispersal kernel, a probability distribution of distances moved; many kernels are fat-tailed, so rare long-distance events disproportionately drive range expansion and colonization.

Ecological and evolutionary significance

Dispersal reduces inbreeding and competition with kin, allows escape from deteriorating habitats, and enables species to track shifting climates — but it can also carry costs, including mortality in transit and arrival in unsuitable habitat. At larger scales, dispersal shapes metapopulation dynamics, gene flow, island biogeography, and the spread of invasive species and disease.
