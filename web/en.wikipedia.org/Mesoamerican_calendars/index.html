Mesoamerican calendars

Mesoamerican calendars are the calendrical systems devised and used by the pre-Columbian cultures of Mesoamerica. Besides keeping time, Mesoamerican calendars were also used in religious observances and social rituals, such as divination. The existence of Mesoamerican calendars is known from the earliest settlements; the earliest calendrical glyphs date to roughly the 6th century BC.

The 260-day and 365-day cycles

Nearly all Mesoamerican systems combined two interlocking cycles: a 260-day ritual calendar (the Tzolk'in among the Maya, the Tonalpohualli among the Aztecs), formed by combining 13 numbers with 20 named days, and a 365-day solar calendar (the Haab' or Xiuhpohualli) of eighteen 20-day months plus five extra days. A given combination of the two dates recurs every 52 years — the Calendar Round — which was marked by ceremonies such as the Aztec New Fire ceremony.

The Long Count

The Maya and some earlier cultures also used the Long Count, a continuous count of days from a mythical starting point in 3114 BC, expressed in units of 20 days (uinals), 360 days (tuns), 7,200 days (k'atuns) and 144,000 days (b'ak'tuns). The Long Count allowed events to be dated unambiguously across centuries and appears on hundreds of carved monuments. A cycle of 13 b'ak'tuns ended on 21 December 2012, an event widely but wrongly publicised as predicting the end of the world.
