Strict liability

In criminal and tort law, strict liability is a standard of liability under which a person is legally responsible for the consequences flowing from an activity even in the absence of fault or criminal intent on the part of the defendant. The claimant need only prove that the tort occurred and that the defendant was responsible; defences based on reasonable care or lack of intention are generally unavailable.

In tort law

Strict liability in tort is traditionally applied to abnormally dangerous (ultrahazardous) activities — such as blasting, keeping wild animals, or storing hazardous substances — and to harm caused by animals. The landmark English case Rylands v Fletcher (1868) imposed liability where a dangerous thing escapes from the defendant's land. Strict liability is also central to product liability, where manufacturers and sellers may be liable for injuries caused by defective products without proof of negligence.

In criminal law

In criminal law, strict liability offences dispense with the requirement of mens rea for at least one element of the offence. They are usually regulatory in nature — traffic offences, selling alcohol to minors, environmental and food-safety offences — and typically carry modest penalties. Their use is controversial because they can convict a defendant who acted blamelessly, and courts often presume Parliament or a legislature intended mens rea unless it clearly displaced it.

Rationale

Justifications include encouraging the highest standards of care for dangerous activities, placing accident costs on the party best able to insure or spread them, and easing the evidential burden on claimants. Critics argue that liability without fault is unfair and can overdeter socially useful activity.
