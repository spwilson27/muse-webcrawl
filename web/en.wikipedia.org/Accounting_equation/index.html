Accounting equation

The accounting equation, also called the basic accounting equation or balance sheet equation, is the foundation of the double-entry bookkeeping system. It states that a company's assets are financed either by borrowing money (liabilities) or by the owners' own money (equity):

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

The formula can also be rearranged as Equity = Assets − Liabilities, expressing the owners' residual claim on the business after all debts are paid. The balance sheet of a company is constructed directly from this equation: everything a company owns is listed as an asset, and the claims against those assets appear as liabilities and equity on the other side, so the two sides always balance.

Double-entry bookkeeping

In double-entry bookkeeping, every financial transaction affects at least two accounts such that the accounting equation remains in balance. If assets increase, either liabilities or equity must increase by the same amount; if an asset is exchanged for another (for example, cash spent on equipment), total assets are unchanged. This built-in check is a basic tool for detecting bookkeeping errors.

Expanded equation

For a corporation, equity can be broken down into contributed capital and retained earnings, and retained earnings further into revenues, expenses and dividends, giving the expanded accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends

History

The equation reflects the system of double-entry bookkeeping described by Luca Pacioli in his 1494 Summa de arithmetica, the first printed treatise on bookkeeping, though the underlying practices had been used by Venetian merchants earlier.
