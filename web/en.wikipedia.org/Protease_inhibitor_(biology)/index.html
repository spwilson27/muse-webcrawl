Protease inhibitor (biology)

In biology and biochemistry, protease inhibitors are a class of molecules that inhibit the function of proteases (enzymes that cleave peptide bonds). Many naturally occurring protease inhibitors are proteins. They regulate proteolytic activity in processes such as blood clotting, complement activation, inflammation, and digestion, preventing uncontrolled degradation of proteins.

Classification

Protease inhibitors are grouped by the type of protease they inhibit — serine, cysteine, aspartic, metalloproteases, or threonine — and by their mechanism, such as competitive active-site binding. Major protein families include serpins, cystatins, and Kunitz-type inhibitors; plants produce protease inhibitors in seeds as a defense against insect herbivory.

Medical significance

Deficiencies of protease inhibitors cause disease: alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency predisposes to emphysema and liver disease. Synthetic protease inhibitors are important drugs, including HIV protease inhibitors used in antiretroviral therapy and hepatitis C antivirals.
