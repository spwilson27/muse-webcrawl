Mount Carmel (Hebrew: Har HaKarmel, "God's vineyard") is a coastal mountain range in northern Israel stretching from the Mediterranean Sea towards the southeast. The range is about 39 km long and up to 8 km wide, rising to roughly 525 metres above sea level.

History and significance

The Carmel caves, including Tabun and Skhul in the Nahal Me'arot valley, preserve evidence of human habitation spanning hundreds of thousands of years, including Neanderthal and early modern human remains; they are a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In the Hebrew Bible, Mount Carmel is the site of Elijah's contest with the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18).

The Carmelite religious order was founded on the mountain in the 12th century by hermits living in imitation of Elijah; the Stella Maris Monastery in Haifa stands on the Carmel's headland. The Baháʼí World Centre's shrines and terraced gardens also occupy the mountain's northern slope in Haifa.

Ecology

Much of the range is covered in Mediterranean maquis of oak, pine, olive and laurel; large areas form the Carmel National Park. In December 2010 a major forest fire killed 44 people and consumed thousands of hectares.
