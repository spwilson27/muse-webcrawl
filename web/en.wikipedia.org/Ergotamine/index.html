Ergotamine

Ergotamine is an ergopeptine and part of the ergot family of alkaloids; it is structurally and biochemically closely related to ergoline. It possesses structural similarity to several neurotransmitters, and has biological activity as a vasoconstrictor. It is used medically for the treatment of acute migraine attacks (sometimes in combination with caffeine). The compound was first isolated from the ergot fungus Claviceps purpurea by the Swiss chemist Arthur Stoll at Sandoz in 1918 and marketed as Gynergen in 1921.

Medical use

Ergotamine is indicated for the treatment of acute migraine, with or without aura, in patients for whom other therapies are unsuitable or ineffective. It is available in oral, sublingual, rectal, and inhaled formulations, and is often combined with caffeine, which enhances its absorption and adds vasoconstrictive effect. Because of its side-effect profile and the availability of safer alternatives such as the triptans, its use has declined in many countries, but it remains on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Mechanism of action

Ergotamine acts as an agonist at serotonin 5-HT1B and 5-HT1D receptors, constricting intracranial blood vessels and inhibiting trigeminal nerve transmission, and also interacts with dopamine and adrenergic receptors. Its non-selectivity accounts for many adverse effects.

Adverse effects and toxicity

Common adverse effects include nausea and vomiting. Overdose or excessive use can cause ergotism — severe peripheral vasoconstriction that may lead to ischemia and gangrene of the extremities, historically known as "St Anthony's fire" when caused by eating ergot-contaminated grain. Ergotamine is contraindicated in pregnancy, peripheral vascular disease, and coronary artery disease, and interacts dangerously with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors such as macrolide antibiotics and azole antifungals.
