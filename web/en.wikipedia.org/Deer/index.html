A deer (pl.: deer) or true deer is a hoofed ruminant ungulate of the family Cervidae. Cervidae is divided into subfamilies Cervinae (which includes muntjac, elk, red deer, and fallow deer) and Capreolinae (which includes reindeer, white-tailed deer, roe deer, and moose). Male deer of almost all species, as well as female reindeer, grow and shed new antlers each year. These antlers are bony extensions of their skulls and contrast with the permanent horns of other ruminants, which are keratinised and borne by both sexes in some species.

Deer live in a variety of habitats, ranging from tundra to tropical rainforest, and are native to all continents except Antarctica and Australia, though they have been widely introduced. The family contains about 50 species, from the tiny northern pudú, about 38 cm tall at the shoulder, to the moose, which can stand over 2 metres.

Biology

Deer are herbivorous browsers and grazers with a four-chambered stomach that allows them to digest tough plant matter through rumination. Most species have a coat that changes with the seasons, and fawns are often born spotted for camouflage. Males are typically larger than females and compete during the rut (mating season), when stags may fight with their antlers. The main predators of deer include wolves, large cats and bears, and humans, who have hunted deer for meat (venison), hides and antlers for millennia.

Antlers

Antlers grow from structures called pedicles on the skull, covered during growth by a vascular skin known as velvet that supplies oxygen and nutrients. When growth is complete the velvet is shed, and the dead bone of the antler is carried through the mating season before being cast and regrown the following year. Antler size and branching generally increase with age and condition, making them a signal in mate choice.

Economic and cultural significance

Deer figure prominently in the mythology, art and heraldry of many cultures. They are economically important as game animals and, in some regions, as farmed livestock; reindeer are semi-domesticated and central to the cultures of northern peoples. In places where natural predators have been removed, deer populations can grow large enough to damage forests and crops, making them subjects of wildlife management.
