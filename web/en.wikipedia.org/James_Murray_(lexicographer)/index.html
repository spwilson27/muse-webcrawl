Sir James Augustus Henry Murray, FBA (7 February 1837 – 26 July 1915) was a British lexicographer and philologist. He was the first editor of the Oxford English Dictionary (OED), directing the project from 1879 until his death and personally editing roughly half of the dictionary. His crowdsourced method — appealing to the public to read books and send in quotation slips — gathered millions of quotations and set the pattern for historical lexicography.

Early life

Murray was born in Denholm in the Scottish Borders, the son of a village tailor. Largely self-educated after leaving school at fourteen, he developed an extraordinary knowledge of languages and dialects, worked as a schoolmaster in Hawick and London, and became active in the Philological Society. His dialect studies produced The Dialect of the Southern Counties of Scotland (1873), which established his reputation.

The Oxford English Dictionary

In 1879 Murray signed a contract with Oxford University Press to edit a new dictionary of English on historical principles, a project of the Philological Society begun under Herbert Coleridge and Frederick Furnivall. He issued an "Appeal to the English-Speaking and English-Reading Public" asking volunteer readers to report words and quotations on paper slips. Working first in a corrugated-iron "Scriptorium" at Mill Hill School and later in Oxford, he and his staff sorted millions of slips into pigeonholes. The first fascicle (A–Ant) appeared in 1884. The scale of the work was far greater than anyone had foreseen: the first edition was completed only in 1928, thirteen years after Murray's death. Among his most famous contributors was W. C. Minor, an American surgeon confined in Broadmoor asylum, who sent tens of thousands of quotations.

Honours and legacy

Murray was knighted in 1908 and received honorary degrees from several universities, though Oxford itself did not award him an honorary doctorate until late in his career. He died of pleurisy in Oxford in 1915, having edited the letters A–D, H–K, O and P in whole or part. His story, and Minor's, was told in Simon Winchester's The Surgeon of Crowthorne (published in the US as The Professor and the Madman), later adapted as a film.
