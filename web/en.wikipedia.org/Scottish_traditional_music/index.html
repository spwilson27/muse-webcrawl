Scottish traditional music

Scottish traditional music is music that is part of the folk tradition of Scotland. It is distinguished by characteristic forms such as the strathspey, reel and jig, by instruments including the fiddle, the accordion and the Great Highland bagpipe, and by song traditions in Scots, Scottish Gaelic and English reaching back many centuries.

Forms and instruments

Instrumental dance music is central: reels and jigs in quick duple or compound time, and the slower, rhythmically pointed strathspey with its "Scotch snap". The fiddle traditions of the Northeast, the West Highlands and Shetland each have distinct styles and repertoires, much of it recorded in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century printed collections. Pipe music divides into ceòl mòr ("great music"), the classical pibroch repertoire, and ceòl beag — marches, strathspeys and reels played by pipe bands worldwide. The clàrsach (Celtic harp) is among Scotland's oldest instruments.

Song traditions

Scotland's song heritage includes the great Border and Child ballads, Gaelic waulking songs sung to accompany cloth-working, mouth music (puirt à beul), Jacobite songs, and the comic and social commentary of the northeast bothy ballads. Robert Burns collected, adapted and wrote many songs that remain staples, including "Auld Lang Syne" and "Scots Wha Hae".

Modern era

The twentieth-century folk revival, driven by figures such as Hamish Henderson and by collectors and performers including Jeannie Robertson and the Stewarts of Blair, brought the tradition to concert and festival stages. Bands such as the Battlefield Band, Capercaillie and Shooglenifty have fused traditional material with rock, jazz and electronic influences, while institutions like the Royal Conservatoire of Scotland now offer degrees in traditional music.
