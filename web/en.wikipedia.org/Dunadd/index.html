Dunadd (Scottish Gaelic: Dùn Ad) is a hillfort in Argyll and Bute, Scotland, situated on a rocky outcrop above the Moine Mhòr ("great moss") near Kilmartin. It is best known as one of the principal royal strongholds of Dál Riata, the Gaelic kingdom that spanned north-eastern Ireland and western Scotland in the early medieval period.

History

The site was occupied from the Iron Age, but its importance peaked between about AD 500 and 900, when Dunadd served as a royal inauguration and power centre for the kings of Dál Riata. Irish annals record attacks on Dunadd, including a siege in 683 and its capture in 736 by the Pictish king Óengus I, events that chart the kingdom's shifting fortunes.

Features

On a terrace below the summit lies Dunadd's most celebrated feature: a carved footprint in the bedrock, alongside a small basin and an incised boar. The footprint is traditionally interpreted as an inauguration stone — the king placed his foot in the carving during his installation, symbolically binding him to the land, a rite paralleled at other Gaelic royal sites. The summit is enclosed by the remains of a citadel wall, with further enclosures on lower terraces that housed workshops and dwellings.

Archaeology

Excavations, notably those directed by Alan Lane in the 1980s, recovered evidence of high-status metalworking: crucibles, moulds for brooches and pins, and evidence for the production of fine jewellery. Finds also demonstrated long-distance trade links, including imported pottery from the Mediterranean world and continental Europe. The site is protected as a scheduled monument and is in the care of Historic Environment Scotland.
