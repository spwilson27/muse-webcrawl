Dutch Guiana (Dutch: Nederlands-Guiana) was the collective name for the Dutch colonies on the Guiana coast of northern South America. At various times it included the colonies of Essequibo, Demerara, Berbice and Surinam. Today "Dutch Guiana" most often refers to Suriname, the former colony of Surinam, which remained Dutch until independence in 1975.

History

The Dutch began trading and settling on the "Wild Coast" of Guiana in the late sixteenth century. In the Treaty of Breda (1667), ending the Second Anglo-Dutch War, the English formally ceded Surinam to the Dutch in exchange for New Netherland (New York). The colonies were run by chartered bodies including the Dutch West India Company and the Society of Suriname.

The plantation colonies grew sugar, coffee, cotton and cacao using the labour of enslaved Africans; the harsh conditions produced famous maroon communities, descended from escaped slaves, in the interior. Britain seized Essequibo, Demerara and Berbice during the Napoleonic Wars, and these were formally ceded in 1814, becoming British Guiana (now Guyana). Surinam remained Dutch; slavery was abolished there in 1863, followed by indentured immigration from India and Java.

Later history

Suriname became a constituent country of the Kingdom of the Netherlands in 1954 and gained full independence on 25 November 1975.
