Malaria is a mosquito-borne infectious disease that affects humans and other vertebrates. It is caused by single-celled microorganisms of the Plasmodium group and is spread exclusively through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. In humans the disease is caused mainly by five species: Plasmodium falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae and the zoonotic P. knowlesi. P. falciparum, predominant in Africa, accounts for most deaths; P. vivax is more widespread outside Africa and can lie dormant in the liver, causing relapses months or years later.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin ten to fifteen days after the infective bite. The classic attack proceeds in cycles: a cold stage of shivering, a hot stage with fever reaching 40 °C or more, headache, nausea and vomiting, and finally a sweating stage as the fever breaks. The cycles recur every two days in the "tertian" malarias (falciparum, vivax, ovale) and every three days in quartan malaria (P. malariae), timed by the synchronised rupture of infected red blood cells. Untreated or severe falciparum malaria can progress to cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, respiratory distress, organ failure, coma and death. Children under five and pregnant women are at greatest risk.

Life cycle

The parasite has a complex life cycle divided between mosquito and human hosts. Sporozoites injected with mosquito saliva travel to the liver, multiply asexually inside hepatocytes, and emerge as merozoites that invade red blood cells. Within the cells they grow through ring, trophozoite and schizont stages before bursting out to infect further cells. Some parasites instead develop into gametocytes, which a mosquito ingests during a blood meal; inside the mosquito gut they fuse, form an oocyst on the gut wall, and release new sporozoites that migrate to the salivary glands, completing the cycle.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on microscopic examination of stained blood films or on rapid antigen tests detecting parasite proteins such as HRP2. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), pairing an artemisinin derivative with a longer-acting partner drug, are the standard treatment for uncomplicated falciparum malaria; severe cases receive intravenous artesunate. Vivax and ovale infections additionally require primaquine or tafenoquine to clear dormant liver forms (hypnozoites), after testing for G6PD deficiency. Resistance to successive drug classes — chloroquine, sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine, and emerging partial artemisinin resistance in East Africa — remains a central concern.

Prevention

Vector control is the mainstay: insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying have driven much of the decline in cases since 2000. Travellers use chemoprophylaxis such as atovaquone-proguanil or doxycycline. In 2021 the World Health Organization recommended the RTS,S/AS01 vaccine for children in endemic areas — the first vaccine ever recommended against a parasitic disease — followed in 2023 by the R21/Matrix-M vaccine.

Epidemiology and history

The WHO estimated 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths in 2022, with the African Region carrying about 95 percent of cases; most deaths are in children under five. Malaria was once endemic across much of the temperate world, including the southern United States and parts of Europe, and was eliminated there through drainage, housing improvements and insecticides in the twentieth century. The disease is ancient — references appear in Chinese and Indian texts several thousand years old — and its name derives from the Italian mal'aria, "bad air", reflecting the old belief that swamp vapours caused it. The parasite was identified in blood by Charles Louis Alphonse Laveran in 1880, and Ronald Ross and Giovanni Battista Grassi established mosquito transmission in the 1890s. The evolutionary pressure of malaria is credited with maintaining genetic traits such as sickle-cell trait, thalassaemia and G6PD deficiency in human populations. Global eradication, attempted by the WHO in 1955–1969, succeeded in many regions but failed in sub-Saharan Africa; renewed campaigns under the Roll Back Malaria partnership and national elimination programmes continue, and the WHO certified a growing list of countries malaria-free in the 2010s and 2020s.
