Waggle dance

The waggle dance is a figure-eight dance performed by honey bees on the vertical comb inside the hive, by which a successful forager communicates to nestmates the direction and distance of profitable food sources, water, or a new nest site. The behaviour was decoded by the Austrian ethologist Karl von Frisch, whose work formed part of the research for which he shared the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

How the code works

The dance consists of a straight "waggle run", during which the bee vibrates her abdomen from side to side, followed by a turn and a semicircular return run, alternating left and right to trace a flattened figure eight. Because the comb is vertical and the hive is dark, gravity substitutes for the sun as a reference: the angle of the waggle run relative to straight-up equals the angle between the sun and the food source as seen from the hive. The duration of the waggle run encodes distance — roughly, a one-second waggle indicates a source on the order of a kilometre away, with the exact calibration varying by subspecies ("dialects"). The vigour and repetition of the dance reflect the quality of the source. For very near sources bees use a simpler round dance.

Other cues and debate

Followers also pick up odour cues carried on the dancer's body and tastes of the nectar she shares, and the dancer produces airborne sounds near 250–300 Hz. In the 1960s Adrian Wenner argued that recruits relied mainly on odour rather than the dance's spatial information, provoking a long controversy; subsequent experiments, including radar tracking of recruited bees by J. R. Riley and colleagues in 2005, confirmed that recruits do use the dance's directional and distance information.

Other uses

During swarming, scout bees use waggle dances on the surface of the swarm to advertise candidate nest cavities, and the colony effectively debates until a consensus site emerges — a process studied in detail by Thomas Seeley. Water collectors and bees advertising resin sources also dance.
