Short sale (real estate)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: a home offered in a short sale]

A short sale is a sale of real estate in which the proceeds from selling the property will fall short of the balance of debts secured by liens against the property, and the property owner cannot afford to repay the liens' full amounts. In such a case the lien holders agree to release their lien on the real estate and accept less than the amount owed on the debt.

Short sales became common in the United States during the housing downturn following 2007, when falling prices left many borrowers "underwater" — owing more on their mortgages than their homes were worth. Lenders may accept a short sale because it often recovers more than a foreclosure, which involves legal costs, delays, and property deterioration; borrowers may prefer it because it typically damages their credit less than a foreclosure, though both have consequences.

Process

Because the lender must approve the sale price, short sales are notoriously slow: the seller usually submits a hardship letter, financial statements, and the buyer's offer, which the lender (and any mortgage insurer or investor) reviews before accepting. Multiple lien holders, such as holders of second mortgages and home-equity lines, complicate the process further, since each must consent.

Any forgiven debt may, depending on jurisdiction and period, be treated as taxable income to the seller, though measures such as the U.S. Mortgage Forgiveness Debt Relief Act of 2007 provided temporary relief for principal residences.
