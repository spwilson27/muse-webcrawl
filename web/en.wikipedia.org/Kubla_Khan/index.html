"Kubla Khan" is a poem by Samuel Taylor Coleridge, completed in 1797 and published in 1816. Subtitled "A Vision in a Dream", it describes the Mongol emperor Kublai Khan's construction of a magnificent pleasure palace at Xanadu, and is among the most famous works of English Romantic poetry.

Composition

Coleridge claimed the poem came to him in an opium-influenced dream after reading a passage about Kublai Khan in Samuel Purchas's travel collection Purchas his Pilgrimage. According to his 1816 preface, he was writing down the vision when he was interrupted by "a person on business from Porlock", and on returning found the rest of the poem had vanished from his memory — leaving only the 54 lines that survive. The authenticity of this account has long been debated by scholars.

The poem

The poem opens with the famous lines "In Xanadu did Kubla Khan / A stately pleasure-dome decree", describing a walled paradise where the sacred river Alph runs "through caverns measureless to man / Down to a sunless sea". Its imagery contrasts the Khan's ordered pleasure grounds with wild, sublime nature and a visionary poet figure, ending with the warning to "Beware! Beware!" of the inspired bard.

Reception and influence

Early reviews were mixed, but the poem's incantatory sound and dream imagery made it one of the most anthologized poems in English. "Xanadu" and the "person from Porlock" have entered the language, and the poem has influenced writers, composers and filmmakers ever since.
