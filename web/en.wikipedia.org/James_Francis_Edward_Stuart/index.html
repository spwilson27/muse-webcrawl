James Francis Edward Stuart (10 June 1688 – 1 January 1766), nicknamed the Old Pretender, was the son of the deposed King James II of England and his second wife, Mary of Modena. As the Jacobite claimant to the thrones of England, Scotland, and Ireland, he styled himself James III of England and VIII of Scotland, and led the Jacobite rising of 1715.

Birth and exile

James's birth at St James's Palace, to a Catholic king, threatened to establish a Catholic dynasty in England; Protestant opponents spread the "warming-pan" myth that the baby had been smuggled into the bedchamber. Within months the Glorious Revolution drove his father from the throne, and the infant prince was carried to France, where he was raised at Saint-Germain-en-Laye under the protection of Louis XIV. On James II's death in 1701, Louis recognised the thirteen-year-old as king, alarming England into the War of the Spanish Succession.

The rising of 1715

James's attempts to regain the throne were repeatedly frustrated: an abortive French-backed expedition in 1708, and the rising of 1715 — "the Fifteen" — which he joined too late, arriving in Scotland in December 1715 after the Jacobite defeat at Preston and the drawn battle of Sheriffmuir. He returned to France within weeks. The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) had already forced Louis XIV to expel him from France; he settled eventually in Rome, where the Pope granted him the Palazzo Muti as a residence.

Later life

In 1719 James married Maria Clementina Sobieska, a granddaughter of John III Sobieski of Poland. Their sons, Charles Edward Stuart (the "Young Pretender") and Henry Benedict Stuart, carried on the claim; Charles led the rising of 1745 without his father's approval of its timing. James spent the rest of his long life in Rome, maintaining a court in exile and dispensing honours, while Britain treated Jacobitism as a declining threat. He died on New Year's Day 1766 and is buried in St Peter's Basilica in the Vatican, where a monument commemorates him and his sons.
