Theory of descriptions

Proposed byBertrand Russell
First published"On Denoting", Mind, 1905
FieldPhilosophy of language, logic

The theory of descriptions is Bertrand Russell's analysis of definite descriptions — phrases such as "the present King of France" — first presented in his landmark 1905 paper "On Denoting". Russell argued that definite descriptions are not genuine referring expressions but rather "incomplete symbols" that must be analysed away in the full logical form of the sentences containing them.

The problem

Descriptions generate puzzles about reference and existence: how can one truly say "the present King of France does not exist", when the sentence seems to refer to something in order to deny it? Rival theories, such as Alexius Meinong's, posited objects that subsist without existing. Russell sought an analysis that avoided such ontological commitments.

Russell's analysis

On Russell's analysis, "The present King of France is bald" is paraphrased as: there is at least one present King of France, there is at most one, and whatever is the present King of France is bald. Since no such person exists, the sentence is straightforwardly false, and no reference to a non-existent king is required. Russell regarded the theory as a paradigm of philosophical analysis and a model for how philosophical problems could be dissolved by uncovering the true logical form beneath misleading grammatical form.

Criticism and legacy

P. F. Strawson famously challenged Russell in "On Referring" (1950), arguing that sentences containing empty descriptions are neither true nor false because reference is a matter of presupposition rather than assertion. The debate between Russellian and Strawsonian approaches remains a central topic in the philosophy of language.
