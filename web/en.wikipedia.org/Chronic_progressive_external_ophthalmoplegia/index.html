Chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia

Chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO) is a condition characterised by slowly progressive paralysis of the extraocular muscles, typically accompanied by drooping of the eyelids (ptosis). It is a mitochondrial disorder, usually caused by deletions or point mutations in mitochondrial DNA, or by nuclear gene mutations that secondarily produce multiple mitochondrial DNA deletions.

Signs and symptoms

The condition usually presents in young adults with bilateral, symmetric ptosis, followed by progressive limitation of eye movements in all directions (ophthalmoparesis). Because the weakness is symmetric, patients often do not complain of double vision. In some families the eye signs are accompanied by wider features — a combination sometimes called "CPEO plus" — including weakness of the neck and limbs, dysphagia, hearing loss, ataxia and peripheral neuropathy. Kearns–Sayre syndrome is a severe early-onset variant of CPEO accompanied by pigmentary retinopathy and cardiac conduction defects.

Causes

Single large-scale deletions of mitochondrial DNA are the most common cause of sporadic CPEO. Inherited cases may follow maternal inheritance (mitochondrial point mutations) or Mendelian inheritance from mutations in nuclear genes such as POLG, TWNK, SLC25A4 and RRM2B.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is supported by muscle biopsy showing ragged-red fibres and genetically by testing for mitochondrial DNA deletions. There is no cure; treatment is supportive and may include ptosis surgery or eyelid crutches, prisms, and management of associated features such as cardiac conduction disease.
