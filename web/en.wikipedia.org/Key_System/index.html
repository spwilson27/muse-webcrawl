The Key System (or Key Route) was a privately owned company that provided mass transit in the East Bay cities of Oakland, Berkeley, Alameda, Richmond, and San Leandro in California. At its height it operated commuter rail, streetcars, buses, and ferry service to San Francisco.

History

The system was assembled by Francis Marion "Borax" Smith, whose fortune came from borax mining, and Frank C. Havens. Beginning in 1903, its transbay trains ran to a ferry pier reaching far into San Francisco Bay; the pier's shape, resembling a key, gave the network its name and its logo. From 1939, Key System trains crossed directly to San Francisco on the lower deck of the San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge.

Decline and end

In 1946 the Key System was purchased by National City Lines, which converted most streetcar and train lines to buses; the last transbay train ran in April 1958. The conversion was part of what became known as the streetcar conspiracy, for which General Motors and others were later convicted of conspiring to monopolize bus sales (though not of destroying transit systems). In 1960 the remaining bus operations were transferred to the public AC Transit district. The modern BART system follows some former Key System corridors.
