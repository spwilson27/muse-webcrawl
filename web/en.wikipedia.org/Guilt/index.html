Guilt

Guilt is a moral emotion that occurs when a person believes or realizes — accurately or not — that they have compromised their own standards of conduct or violated universal moral standards and bear significant responsibility for that violation. Guilt is closely related to the concept of remorse, as well as shame. Unlike shame, which focuses on the whole self ("I am bad"), guilt typically focuses on a specific behaviour ("I did something bad"), and it motivates reparative action such as confession, apology, and making amends.

Psychology

Psychologists regard guilt as one of the "self-conscious" or moral emotions, emerging in early childhood as children internalize social norms. Research distinguishes guilt-proneness — associated with empathy, responsibility, and repairing harm — from shame-proneness, which correlates more strongly with psychological symptoms. Excessive or misplaced guilt is a feature of depression and obsessive-compulsive disorder, and "survivor guilt" is described among people who live through disasters, combat, or epidemics in which others died. Evolutionary and social-functional accounts propose that guilt evolved to maintain cooperative relationships by deterring defection and prompting restitution.

In law

The word also denotes a legal status: a defendant is found guilty when a court determines beyond a reasonable doubt (in criminal cases) that they committed the offence charged. Legal guilt depends on both a prohibited act (actus reus) and a culpable mental state (mens rea). Legal and moral guilt may diverge — a person may be legally acquitted yet feel moral guilt, or feel none despite conviction.

In religion and philosophy

Many religious traditions frame guilt in relation to sin and provide rituals of atonement, confession, and forgiveness to relieve it. Philosophers debate collective guilt — whether nations, generations, or organizations can be guilty — a question explored prominently after the Second World War by thinkers such as Karl Jaspers in The Question of German Guilt (1947).
