George Horace Gallup (November 18, 1901 – July 26, 1984) was an American pioneer of survey sampling techniques and inventor of the Gallup Poll, a successful statistical method of survey sampling for measuring public opinion.

Gallup founded the American Institute of Public Opinion in 1935.

Career

Gallup studied journalism at the University of Iowa, where his doctoral research measured newspaper readership, then taught journalism before joining the advertising agency Young & Rubicam in 1932, where he built its research department. In 1935 he founded the American Institute of Public Opinion in Princeton, New Jersey, and began publishing the syndicated Gallup Poll.

His fame was sealed by the 1936 U.S. presidential election: using a relatively small but carefully selected quota sample, Gallup correctly predicted Franklin D. Roosevelt's victory over Alf Landon, while the Literary Digest's massive straw poll of over two million responses wrongly forecast a Landon landslide — a classic demonstration that sample representativeness beats sample size. Gallup's reputation suffered a setback in 1948, when his polls (like others) predicted Thomas Dewey would defeat Harry Truman, an error attributed to quota sampling and stopping interviewing too early; the failure pushed the field toward probability sampling.

Legacy

The company he founded, Gallup, Inc., became a global polling and management-consulting organisation, known for the Gallup World Poll, presidential approval ratings and employee-engagement research. The "Gallup poll" became a generic byword for opinion surveys worldwide. Gallup himself wrote The Pulse of Democracy (1940, with Saul Rae) and championed polling as an instrument of democracy that could give ordinary citizens a continuous voice between elections.
