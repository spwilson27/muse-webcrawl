Peasants' Revolt


Wat Tyler's death, from a medieval chronicle

The Peasants' Revolt, also called Wat Tyler's Rebellion or the Great Rising, was a major uprising across large parts of England in 1381. It was the most extreme and widespread insurrection in medieval England, driven by resentment of serfdom, high taxes — above all the poll taxes levied to pay for the Hundred Years' War — and the socio-economic tensions left by the Black Death.

Course of events

The revolt began in Essex in late May 1381 after confrontations with tax commissioners, and spread rapidly to Kent, where rebels under Wat Tyler marched on London. The radical preacher John Ball, freed from prison by the rebels, gave the movement its famous slogan: "When Adam delved and Eve span, who was then the gentleman?" On 13 June the rebels entered London, burned the Savoy Palace of John of Gaunt, stormed the Tower of London, and executed the Archbishop of Canterbury, Simon Sudbury, and the treasurer Robert Hales.

The young king Richard II met the rebels at Mile End and granted charters abolishing serfdom. The next day, 15 June, at Smithfield, Tyler was wounded by the Lord Mayor William Walworth during a confrontation and killed; Richard defused the crowd by riding forward and leading them away. Revolts in other counties, such as St Albans and East Anglia, were crushed, and the charters were revoked.

Consequences

Although the revolt failed, Parliament abandoned the poll tax, and landlords increasingly commuted labour services for money rents; serfdom in England faded over the following century. The revolt remains a famous episode in English popular history and a touchstone for later radicals.
