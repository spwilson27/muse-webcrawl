Old London Bridge

Old London Bridge was the stone bridge that crossed the River Thames in London from 1209 until 1831. Built under the direction of Peter of Colechurch, it stood on 19 arches and carried a street lined with shops and houses up to seven storeys high, making it one of the most famous inhabited bridges in the world. For more than 600 years it was the only bridge across the Thames in London downstream of Kingston.

Life on the bridge

The bridge's crowded roadway, narrowed by buildings to a few metres in places, was a notorious bottleneck; crossing could take an hour. Its buildings included the chapel of St Thomas, shops of haberdashers, booksellers, and glovers, and Nonsuch House, a prefabricated timber mansion shipped from the Netherlands. The heads of executed traitors were displayed on spikes above the south gate, a practice begun with William Wallace in 1305 and ended only in 1661. The piers' narrow starlings made the river shoot through like a weir, and "shooting the bridge" in a boat was a famous hazard.

Replacement

By the early nineteenth century the medieval bridge was structurally failing and obstructed river traffic. John Rennie designed a replacement a short distance upstream; the new London Bridge opened in 1831, and Old London Bridge was demolished in 1832. Two of its alcoves survive in Victoria Park and Guy's Hospital grounds.
