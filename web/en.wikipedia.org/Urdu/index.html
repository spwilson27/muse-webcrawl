Urdu (اُردُو, Urdū) is an Indo-Aryan language spoken chiefly in South Asia. It is the national language and lingua franca of Pakistan, where it is written, spoken and used in education and media alongside English, and it is one of the 22 scheduled languages of India, with official status in several states and union territories including Jammu and Kashmir, Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal and Telangana.

Relationship with Hindi

Urdu and Hindi share a common grammatical base and core vocabulary derived from the Khariboli dialect of the Delhi region, and their colloquial forms are mutually intelligible, together often called Hindustani. They diverge in script and in formal vocabulary: Urdu is written in a Perso-Arabic script (usually the Nastaliq style) and draws heavily on Persian and Arabic for learned words, while standard Hindi is written in Devanagari and draws on Sanskrit.

History

Urdu developed between the 12th and 18th centuries in the courts, camps and markets of northern India through contact between local Indo-Aryan speech and the Persian, Arabic and Turkic of successive Muslim-ruled states. The name derives from the Turkic word ordu ("army, camp"), the same root as English "horde". Urdu literature flowered in the Deccan and later in Delhi and Lucknow, producing a celebrated tradition of poetry — the ghazal and mushaira — associated with poets such as Mir Taqi Mir and Mirza Ghalib, and in the 20th century with Muhammad Iqbal and Faiz Ahmad Faiz.

Phonology and grammar

Urdu has a phoneme inventory typical of Indo-Aryan languages, including retroflex consonants and aspirated stops, supplemented by sounds from Persian and Arabic. It is a split-ergative, subject–object–verb language with postpositions, grammatical gender (masculine and feminine), and a system of honorific pronouns (tū, tum, āp).
