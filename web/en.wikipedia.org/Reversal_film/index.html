In photography, reversal film or slide film is a type of photographic film that produces a positive image on a transparent base. The film is processed to produce transparencies rather than negatives and prints. Reversal film is so called because after exposure it is developed first to a negative image, then the remaining unexposed silver halide is fogged and developed to produce the final positive image — the tones are "reversed".

The finished film pieces are usually mounted in individual frames (slides) for projection, or viewed on a lightbox. Colour reversal films such as Kodachrome (1935) and Ektachrome became the standard medium for colour publishing, nature photography and much professional work through the twentieth century because of their sharpness and rich colour saturation.

Mounted colour transparencies (slides) produced from reversal film.

Processing

Modern colour reversal films use the E-6 process: a first developer produces a black-and-white negative image in each emulsion layer; a reversal step fogs the remaining halide; a colour developer then forms positive dye images, and bleach and fix remove the silver. Kodachrome used the far more complex K-14 process, in which dye couplers were introduced during processing; it was discontinued in 2009 and the last processing ended in 2010.

Characteristics

Reversal films generally have finer grain and higher contrast than colour negative films of similar speed, but a narrower exposure latitude, so exposure must be precise. Black-and-white reversal films also exist and were widely used for amateur cinematography in gauges such as 8 mm, Super 8 and 16 mm, since the projected original required no negative-positive printing.

Current status

Digital photography collapsed the market, and manufacturers discontinued most stocks; survivors include Fujifilm's Fujichrome Velvia and Provia and Kodak's revived Ektachrome E100 (reintroduced in 2018). Reversal film retains a niche following for its colour rendering and direct positives.
