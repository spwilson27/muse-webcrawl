Respiratory failure

Respiratory failure
SpecialtyPulmonology, critical care medicine
DefinitionInadequate gas exchange by the respiratory system
Type IHypoxemic — PaO2 < 60 mmHg, normal/low PaCO2
Type IIHypercapnic — PaCO2 > 50 mmHg
TreatmentOxygen therapy, non-invasive or mechanical ventilation, treating the cause

Respiratory failure is a condition in which the respiratory system fails in one or both of its gas-exchange functions: oxygenation of the blood and elimination of carbon dioxide. It is defined by abnormal arterial blood gas values and may develop acutely — over minutes to days — or chronically, and it is a common reason for admission to intensive care.

Classification

Type I (hypoxemic) respiratory failure is a failure of oxygenation: arterial oxygen tension (PaO2) falls below about 60 mmHg while carbon dioxide is normal or low. It results from diseases that flood or collapse the alveoli or impair diffusion — pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and pulmonary fibrosis — producing ventilation–perfusion mismatch and shunt. Type II (hypercapnic) respiratory failure is a failure of ventilation: the lungs cannot move enough air, so carbon dioxide rises above about 50 mmHg. Causes include severe COPD exacerbations, drug overdose suppressing respiratory drive, neuromuscular diseases such as myasthenia gravis and Guillain–Barré syndrome, and chest-wall disorders. Mixed pictures are common.

Signs and symptoms

Hypoxemia causes breathlessness, rapid breathing, restlessness, confusion and cyanosis; carbon dioxide retention causes drowsiness, headache, a flapping tremor (asterixis) and eventually coma. Physical signs may include use of accessory muscles of breathing, paradoxical abdominal movement and exhaustion — an ominous sign heralding respiratory arrest.

Management

Treatment addresses both the gas-exchange failure and its underlying cause. Supplemental oxygen corrects hypoxemia; in type II failure from COPD, oxygen is given carefully to target saturations of 88–92%. Non-invasive ventilation (CPAP or BiPAP) supports many patients with hypercapnic failure or cardiogenic pulmonary oedema and can avert intubation. Patients who deteriorate require endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, with lung-protective strategies in ARDS; refractory cases may be treated with prone positioning or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Antibiotics, bronchodilators, diuretics, steroids or antidotes such as naloxone are given according to the cause.
