Old Chinese
Chinese上古漢語 (Shànggǔ Hànyǔ)
Periodc. 1250 BC – early centuries AD (Shang to Han)
FamilySino-Tibetan → Sinitic
Key sourcesOracle bone inscriptions, bronze inscriptions, Shijing rhymes

Old Chinese, also called Archaic Chinese, is the oldest attested stage of the Chinese language, spoken from the Shang dynasty (c. 1250 BC) through the Han dynasty. It is the ancestor of all modern Chinese varieties, including Mandarin, Cantonese and Min. Unlike its modern descendants, Old Chinese was not tonal and had consonant clusters and a rich derivational morphology.

Evidence and reconstruction

Because Chinese characters do not record pronunciation directly, scholars reconstruct Old Chinese from several lines of evidence: the rhymes of the Shijing (Classic of Poetry), the phonetic components of compound characters, bronze and oracle-bone inscriptions, transcriptions of foreign names, and comparison with Middle Chinese (recorded in the Qieyun of AD 601) and conservative modern dialects such as Min. Influential reconstruction systems include those of Bernard Karlgren, Li Fang-Kuei, and the widely used Baxter–Sagart system of 2014.

Features

Reconstructed Old Chinese had initial consonant clusters (e.g. *kr-, *sn-), voiceless resonants, uvular consonants, and syllable-final consonants such as *-p, *-t, *-k, *-m, *-n, *-ŋ plus *-ʔ and *-s — which later gave rise to the tones of Middle Chinese. Morphological prefixes and suffixes (such as *-s) marked derivation in ways still faintly visible through sound changes. Its grammar, as seen in texts like the Analects and Mencius, already showed the largely analytic, topic-prominent character of later Chinese.
