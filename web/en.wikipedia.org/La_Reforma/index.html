La Reforma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

La Reforma (Spanish for "The Reform") was a period of liberal transformation in Mexico from 1855 to 1876, during which a generation of liberal leaders — most prominently Benito Juárez — sought to modernize Mexico's economy and society by breaking the power of the Roman Catholic Church and the military, abolishing the colonial-era corporate privileges (fueros), and creating a federal republic governed by civil law. The Reform produced a body of liberal laws (the Leyes de Reforma), the Constitution of 1857, and provoked the conservative backlash that led to the Reform War (1858–1861) and, indirectly, to the French intervention and the Second Mexican Empire (1862–1867).

Background

After independence from Spain (1821), Mexico oscillated between liberal federalists and conservative centralists, while the Church retained vast landholdings, its own courts, and control of education and civil registration, and the army enjoyed special legal privileges. An earlier liberal attempt at reform under Valentín Gómez Farías (1833) had been reversed. The Revolution of Ayutla (1854–1855), launched against the dictatorship of Antonio López de Santa Anna, brought the liberals of the "puro" generation to power: Juan Álvarez, Ignacio Comonfort, Benito Juárez, Melchor Ocampo, Guillermo Prieto, and Miguel and Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada.

The Reform Laws

The first measures were enacted before the new constitution:

  • Ley Juárez (1855): abolished the separate legal privileges (fueros) of the clergy and military, making them subject to civil courts.
  • Ley Lerdo (1856): ordered the disentailment of property held by corporate bodies — the Church and indigenous communities (ejidos) — forcing its sale to private owners to create a market in land and a class of small proprietors.
  • Ley Iglesias (1857): regulated (and limited) the fees clergy could charge for sacraments.

Issued later from Veracruz during the Reform War, Juárez's more radical Leyes de Reforma (1859) nationalized church property outright, separated church and state, suppressed monasteries, established civil marriage and a civil registry, and guaranteed freedom of worship.

Constitution of 1857

The constituent congress produced the Federal Constitution of the United Mexican States of 1857, which enshrined the Reform laws, an extensive bill of individual guarantees (including freedom of speech, press, assembly, and education), federalism, a unicameral legislature, and a relatively weak presidency. It abolished slavery and prohibited titles of nobility and monopolies. Conservative opposition to its anticlerical clauses (and to the presidency of Comonfort, who manoeuvred against his own constitution) triggered civil war.

Reform War and French intervention

In the Reform War (1858–1861), conservatives under Félix Zuloaga and Miguel Miramón held Mexico City while Juárez's constitutional government held Veracruz, supported by the United States, which recognized Juárez and signed the (unratified) McLane–Ocampo Treaty. The liberals won by early 1861, and Juárez was elected president. The debt moratorium that followed gave France, Britain, and Spain a pretext to intervene; Napoleon III's attempt to install Maximilian of Habsburg as emperor (1864–1867) united most liberals in resistance. With the end of the American Civil War and U.S. diplomatic pressure, France withdrew, and the republic was restored in 1867; Maximilian was executed at Querétaro. Juárez and then Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada governed the "Restored Republic" until Porfirio Díaz's revolt of 1876, which closed the Reform era and opened the Porfiriato.

Legacy

La Reforma permanently reshaped Mexico: church–state separation, civil marriage and registry, and freedom of worship remain constitutional pillars, and Juárez is revered as a national hero. The Ley Lerdo's disentailment, however, also devastated indigenous communal landholding, concentrating land in haciendas and commercial hands — an unintended consequence that contributed to the grievances behind the Mexican Revolution of 1910. The Constitution of 1917 drew heavily on the reform tradition while adding social rights.
