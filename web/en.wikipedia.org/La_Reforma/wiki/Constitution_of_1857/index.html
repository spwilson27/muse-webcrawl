The Constitution of 1857 (Spanish: Constitución Federal de los Estados Unidos Mexicanos de 1857) was the liberal constitution of Mexico promulgated on 5 February 1857 by a constituent congress convened after the Revolution of Ayutla overthrew the dictator Antonio López de Santa Anna. It remained formally in force, with interruptions, until replaced by the revolutionary Constitution of 1917.

Contents

The constitution established a federal republic with separation of powers, a unicameral congress, and a broad catalogue of individual guarantees (rights): freedom of expression, press, assembly, education and movement; abolition of slavery, imprisonment for civil debt, and hereditary privileges; and the amparo legal remedy, developed by Manuel Crescencio Rejón and Mariano Otero, which became a lasting feature of Mexican law. It incorporated the spirit of the Reform Laws, limiting corporate landholding and curtailing the power of the Catholic Church, though it stopped short of full religious toleration, to the disappointment of radical liberals (puros).

Consequences

Conservative and clerical opposition to the constitution, and President Ignacio Comonfort's vacillation, provoked the conservative coup of the Plan of Tacubaya in December 1857, launching the Reform War (1858–1861) between the constitutional government of Benito Juárez and the conservative regime in Mexico City. The liberal victory in 1861 restored the constitution, which Juárez also upheld against the French Intervention that followed, making its defence a symbol of national sovereignty. Under Porfirio Díaz it was frequently amended and often honoured in the breach.

Legacy

Many principles of 1857 — federalism, individual guarantees, amparo, secular education and limits on church property — passed into the Constitution of 1917, and 5 February remains a Mexican public holiday.
