Second French intervention in Mexico

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The second French intervention in Mexico (Spanish: Segunda Intervención Francesa en México) was an invasion of Mexico by the Second French Empire between 1861 and 1867, undertaken with the support of Mexican conservatives, which led to the establishment of the Second Mexican Empire under Maximilian I.

Origins

In July 1861, after the ruinous Reform War, President Benito Juárez suspended payment of Mexico's foreign debts. France, Britain, and Spain agreed by the Convention of London to intervene and occupied Veracruz. When Britain and Spain withdrew in 1862, realizing France's imperial ambitions, Napoleon III pressed on, seeking a Catholic monarchy in the Americas that would counter United States influence — a policy sometimes called the "Grand Design for the Americas."

War

French forces under General Charles de Lorencez were defeated by General Ignacio Zaragoza at the Battle of Puebla on 5 May 1862 — the victory commemorated as Cinco de Mayo. Reinforced under General Élie Forey, the French took Puebla and Mexico City in 1863. A conservative assembly offered the crown to Archduke Maximilian of Austria, who arrived in 1864 as Emperor Maximilian I, supported by French troops under Marshal François Achille Bazaine.

Collapse

Republican forces under Juárez, who had retreated to the north, waged a persistent guerrilla war. After the American Civil War ended in 1865, the United States invoked the Monroe Doctrine and supplied the republicans. Napoleon III, facing rising Prussian power in Europe, withdrew French troops in 1866–67. Maximilian refused to abdicate, was captured at Querétaro, and was executed by firing squad on 19 June 1867 alongside generals Miramón and Mejía. Juárez restored the republic.
