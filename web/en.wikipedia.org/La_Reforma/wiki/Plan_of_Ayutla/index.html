The Plan of Ayutla was a political proclamation issued on 1 March 1854 in the town of Ayutla, in the present-day state of Guerrero, Mexico. It launched the Revolution of Ayutla, the liberal uprising that ended the final dictatorship of Antonio López de Santa Anna and opened the era of La Reforma.

Contents

Drawn up by Colonel Florencio Villarreal and supported by the southern caudillo Juan Álvarez and the liberal officer Ignacio Comonfort (who modified it at Acapulco), the plan declared that Santa Anna's regime — marked by the sale of the Mesilla (Gadsden Purchase), repression and fiscal abuses — had forfeited legitimacy. It called for Santa Anna's removal, the appointment of an interim president chosen by representatives of the states, and the convocation of a constituent congress to give the nation a liberal republican constitution.

The revolution

The rebellion spread slowly against the government's forces, hampered by Álvarez's regional base and Santa Anna's punitive campaigns in Guerrero, but it attracted liberals nationwide, including exiles returning from New Orleans such as Benito Juárez and Melchor Ocampo. In August 1855, with support collapsing, Santa Anna resigned and fled the country, ending his political career. A junta named Juan Álvarez interim president; he formed a liberal cabinet and summoned the constituent congress that produced the Constitution of 1857.

Significance

The Plan of Ayutla is remembered as the founding act of modern liberal Mexico: it brought to power the generation of Juárez, Lerdo and Ocampo whose Reform Laws reshaped church–state relations, landholding and civil equality in the following decade.
