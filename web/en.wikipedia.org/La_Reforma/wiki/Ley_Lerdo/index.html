The Ley Lerdo (Lerdo Law), formally the Law of Disentailment of the Rural and Urban Properties of the Civil and Religious Corporations of Mexico, was enacted on 25 June 1856, during the presidency of Ignacio Comonfort. Named for its author, finance minister Miguel Lerdo de Tejada, it was one of the foundational Reform Laws of La Reforma, the liberal transformation of Mexico led by Benito Juárez and his allies.

Provisions

The law prohibited civil and ecclesiastical corporations — above all the Roman Catholic Church, then the largest landowner in Mexico — from holding real estate not essential to their direct functions. Corporate lands were to be sold to their tenants, who could purchase them at favourable capitalised values; if tenants declined, the properties could be auctioned to others. The aims were to create a market in land, increase fiscal revenue, weaken the economic power of the Church, and promote a class of independent proprietors.

Consequences

The law's application to civil corporations unintentionally struck indigenous communities, whose communally held ejidos were classed as corporate property and broken up, a long-term blow to communal landholding partly reversed only by the agrarian reform after the Revolution of 1910. Much Church land passed not to smallholders but to speculators and large estate owners, expanding the hacienda system. Church outrage at the Ley Lerdo and the companion Ley Juárez and Constitution of 1857 helped ignite the Reform War of 1858–1861. The principle of disentailment was later enshrined in Article 27 of the Constitution of 1917.
