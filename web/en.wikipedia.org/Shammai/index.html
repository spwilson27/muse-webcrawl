Shammai (c. 50 BCE – c. 30 CE) was a Jewish sage of the late Second Temple period and, with Hillel the Elder, formed the last of the zugot ("pairs") of sages in the Mishnaic tradition. He headed the academy known as Beit Shammai, the House of Shammai, whose disputes with the House of Hillel fill much of early rabbinic literature. In these disputes Shammai's school is generally associated with the stricter interpretation of Jewish law, while Hillel's is associated with the more lenient ruling — the interpretation that usually prevailed in later halakha.

Life and character

Little is known of Shammai's biography; tradition says he worked as a builder. Rabbinic stories contrast his temperament with Hillel's patience: in a famous Talmudic tale (Shabbat 31a), a would-be convert who demanded to be taught the whole Torah while standing on one foot was driven away by Shammai with a builder's measuring rod, but welcomed by Hillel with the principle "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow." Whatever their historical accuracy, such stories encode the schools' reputations — Shammai for exacting standards, Hillel for accessibility.

The houses of Hillel and Shammai

The two houses disputed hundreds of points of ritual and civil law: blessings, Sabbath observance, purity rules, marriage, and festival practice. The Mishnah preserves cases in which the House of Shammai's view is stricter — for example on the order of the Hanukkah lights (Shammai: begin with eight and decrease; Hillel: begin with one and increase) and on grounds for divorce (Shammai: only grave misconduct; Hillel: even a spoiled dish). A few sharp confrontations are also recorded, including a day on which the House of Shammai, then in the majority, forced through a set of restrictive decrees. Under Shammai's disciple generation the school is linked in some sources with the more uncompromising stance toward Rome that fed the revolt of 66 CE, though the connections are debated.

Teachings

In Pirkei Avot (Ethics of the Fathers) Shammai is credited with the maxim: "Make your Torah study a fixed practice; say little and do much; and receive every person with a cheerful countenance." His legacy survived even where his school's rulings were set aside: the Mishnah records minority opinions so that a later court might rely on them, and Jewish tradition frames the Hillel–Shammai disputes as the paradigm of "dispute for the sake of heaven."
