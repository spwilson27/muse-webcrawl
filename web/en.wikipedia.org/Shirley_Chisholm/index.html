Shirley Chisholm (née St. Hill; 30 November 1924 – 1 January 2005) was an American politician who in 1968 became the first Black woman elected to the United States Congress, representing New York's 12th congressional district in Brooklyn for seven terms until 1983. In 1972 she ran for the Democratic nomination for President of the United States — the first Black candidate to seek a major party's nomination and the first woman to run for the Democratic nomination — under the slogan "Unbought and Unbossed". She was a founding member of the Congressional Black Caucus and a prominent advocate for poor people, civil rights, and women's equality.

Early life and education

Chisholm was born in Brooklyn to immigrant parents from Barbados and Guyana; she spent part of her childhood in Barbados with her grandmother, an experience she credited with her disciplined education and distinctive cadence. She graduated from Brooklyn College in 1946, worked as a nursery school teacher and childcare administrator, earned a master's degree in elementary education from Columbia University in 1952, and served in the New York State Assembly from 1965 to 1968.

Congressional career

Elected in 1968 to a newly drawn Brooklyn district, Chisholm famously protested her initial assignment to the Agriculture Committee, converting it into advocacy for food stamps and nutrition programmes — she went on to be instrumental in creating the Special Supplemental Food Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) in 1972. She opposed the Vietnam War, supported the Equal Rights Amendment and legal abortion, and rose to become secretary of the House Democratic Caucus (1977–1981). She survived an assassination attempt during her presidential campaign and retired from Congress in 1983.

The 1972 presidential campaign

Announcing on 25 January 1972 in Brooklyn, Chisholm campaigned as a voice for those excluded from power — "I am not the candidate of Black America, although I am Black and proud; I am not the candidate of the women's movement of this country, although I am a woman, and I am equally proud of that." Barred from televised primary debates and forced to sue for ballot access in several states, she nevertheless won 152 delegates at the Democratic National Convention — a path-breaking showing that later candidates have cited as foundational.

Later life and legacy

After Congress, Chisholm taught at Mount Holyoke College, co-founded the National Political Congress of Black Women, and was nominated by President Clinton as ambassador to Jamaica but withdrew for health reasons. She died in Florida in 2005. President Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom posthumously in 2015, and Shirley Chisholm State Office Building and Shirley Chisholm State Park in Brooklyn bear her name; a statue of her is planned for the US Capitol.
