De Bruijn graph

In graph theory, an n-dimensional de Bruijn graph of m symbols is a directed graph representing overlaps between sequences of symbols. Its vertices are all sequences of length n over the alphabet, and an edge connects two sequences when the last n−1 symbols of the first equal the first n−1 symbols of the second. The graphs are named after the Dutch mathematician Nicolaas Govert de Bruijn, who studied them in connection with de Bruijn sequences.

Properties

The binary de Bruijn graph of dimension n has 2n vertices and is regular: each vertex has indegree and outdegree equal to the alphabet size. De Bruijn graphs are Eulerian and Hamiltonian, and line graphs of smaller de Bruijn graphs — the graph of dimension n is the line graph of the graph of dimension n−1. An Euler tour of the graph corresponds to a de Bruijn sequence, a cyclic sequence in which every length-n string appears exactly once.

Applications

De Bruijn graphs model some network topologies for distributed computing, since they offer good connectivity with small diameter and degree. Their most widespread modern use is in bioinformatics: genome assemblers such as Velvet and SPAdes break DNA sequencing reads into k-mers and assemble them by finding paths through a de Bruijn graph, an approach introduced for assembly by Pevzner and colleagues in 2001.
