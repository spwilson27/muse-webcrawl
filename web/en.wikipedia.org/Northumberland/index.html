Northumberland is a ceremonial county and unitary authority in North East England, bordering Scotland. It is the northernmost county in England, bounded by the North Sea to the east, County Durham and Tyne and Wear to the south, Cumbria to the west, and the Scottish Borders to the north. Despite being England's sixth-largest county by area, it is among the least densely populated.

History

The county's name derives from the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Northumbria, "the land north of the Humber". For centuries Northumberland was a lawless frontier contested between England and Scotland, guarded by castles such as Bamburgh, Alnwick, and Warkworth, and raided by the Border Reivers. Hadrian's Wall, built by the Romans from AD 122, crosses the county's south and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Geography

The landscape ranges from the Cheviot Hills on the Scottish border — the highest ground being The Cheviot at 815 m — through the upland moors of Northumberland National Park to the low coastal plain with its long sandy beaches and offshore islands, including Lindisfarne (Holy Island) and the Farne Islands. Major rivers are the Tyne, Tweed, Coquet, and Wansbeck.

Economy and culture

Once dominated by coal mining and heavy industry in its southeast, the county's modern economy rests on agriculture, tourism, fishing, and pharmaceuticals and renewable energy industries. Northumberland is associated with the Northumbrian smallpipes and a distinct regional culture; the county flag, based on the arms of Bamburgh Castle, is among the oldest in England. The market towns of Alnwick, Berwick-upon-Tweed, Hexham, and Morpeth are its principal settlements.
