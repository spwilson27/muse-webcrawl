Myosin

Myosins are a superfamily of motor proteins best known for their roles in muscle contraction and in a wide variety of other kinds of cell motility in eukaryotes. They are ATP-dependent and responsible for actin-based motility. The first myosin (myosin II) to be described was found in 1864 by Wilhelm Kühne in skeletal muscle; the term derives from the Greek mys ("muscle"). Since the 1990s, dozens of unconventional myosins have been discovered, and more than 30 classes are now recognised, found in nearly all eukaryotic cells.

Structure

Most myosins share a common organisation: one or more head (motor) domains that bind actin and hydrolyse ATP; a neck domain that acts as a lever arm and binds calmodulin or similar light chains; and a tail domain that determines the protein's cargo and localisation. Conventional myosin II molecules dimerise through coiled-coil tails and assemble into thick filaments in muscle, where hundreds of heads interact with actin filaments to produce force.

Mechanism

Myosin converts the chemical energy of ATP hydrolysis into mechanical work through a power stroke: the neck domain swings by several nanometres as the head cycles through states of tight and weak actin binding (the Lymn–Taylor cycle). Single myosin II molecules take steps of about 5–10 nm; ensembles produce the sliding of filaments that underlies muscle contraction, as described by the sliding filament theory proposed in 1954 by Andrew Huxley and Hugh Huxley and their collaborators.

Classes and functions

  • Myosin I — membrane-associated motors involved in endocytosis, membrane tension and the structure of microvilli and stereocilia.
  • Myosin II — the muscle myosins and non-muscle forms driving cytokinesis and cell migration.
  • Myosin V — a processive two-headed motor that walks along actin to transport organelles and vesicles, taking 36 nm steps.
  • Myosin VI — unusual in moving toward the minus end of actin filaments; involved in endocytosis and hearing.
  • Myosin VII and XV — required for normal hearing; mutations cause forms of deafness.

Clinical significance

Mutations in cardiac myosin heavy chain genes cause hypertrophic and dilated cardiomyopathy; mutations in unconventional myosins cause deafness and Usher syndrome. Small-molecule modulators of cardiac myosin, such as mavacamten, have entered clinical use for obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.
