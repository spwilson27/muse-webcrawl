Tiki culture

Tiki culture is an American-originated visual and thematic culture that became popular beginning in the 1930s, drawing loosely on the cultures of Polynesia, Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesian-populated areas of the Pacific. Centred on tiki bars and restaurants, it produced a distinctive style of architecture, decor, music (exotica) and cocktails such as the Mai Tai and the Zombie.

Origins

The movement is generally traced to Donn Beach, whose Hollywood bar Don the Beachcomber opened in 1933–34 and served rum-based "exotic" cocktails in a South Seas setting. Victor Bergeron, founder of Trader Vic's in Oakland, California, became his great rival; Bergeron claimed the invention of the Mai Tai in 1944. Returning servicemen from the Pacific theatre of the Second World War, and Hawaii's admission as a US state in 1959, fuelled the craze.

Features

Tiki venues featured carved tiki figures, bamboo, rattan, pufferfish lamps, waterfalls and tropical murals. The associated music, called exotica, was popularised by Martin Denny, Les Baxter and Yma Sumac, combining jazz with bird calls and Latin and Pacific instrumentation. Popular culture tie-ins included Thor Heyerdahl's Kon-Tiki expedition (1947) and the Enchanted Tiki Room at Disneyland (1963).

Decline and revival

Tiki culture faded in the 1970s and 1980s as tastes changed and the style came to be seen as kitsch or culturally insensitive. From the 1990s a revivalist scene — chronicled by writers such as Sven Kirsten in The Book of Tiki (2000) — restored historic bars, researched original cocktail recipes and opened new craft tiki bars, alongside continuing discussion about its appropriation of Oceanic cultures.
