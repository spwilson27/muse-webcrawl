OLED

An organic light-emitting diode (OLED) is a light-emitting diode in which the emissive layer is a film of organic compounds that emit light in response to an electric current. Because each pixel generates its own light, OLED displays need no backlight, allowing true black, high contrast, thin and even flexible screens. They are used in smartphones, televisions, wearables, and lighting.

Working principle

An OLED stack places organic layers between an anode and a cathode on a substrate. Applied voltage injects electrons and holes, which meet in the emissive layer and form excitons that decay by emitting photons; the colour depends on the organic molecules used. Small-molecule devices are usually made by vacuum evaporation; polymer OLEDs (PLEDs) can be solution-processed or inkjet printed. Phosphorescent materials (PHOLEDs) harvest both singlet and triplet excitons, greatly improving efficiency.

History and applications

The first practical device was reported by Ching W. Tang and Steven Van Slyke at Eastman Kodak in 1987. Commercial displays followed in the late 1990s, and active-matrix OLED (AMOLED) panels became standard in premium smartphones during the 2010s; large OLED televisions followed. Advantages over LCDs include perfect blacks, wide viewing angles and fast response; disadvantages include susceptibility to burn-in and, historically, the shorter lifetime of blue emitters and higher manufacturing cost.

Variants

Related technologies include PMOLED and AMOLED addressing schemes, transparent and foldable displays, white OLED lighting panels, and micro-OLED displays used in headsets.
