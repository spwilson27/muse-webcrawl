Individual voluntary arrangement
JurisdictionEngland and Wales; Northern Ireland
LegislationInsolvency Act 1986, Part VIII
Introduced1986
Typical duration5–6 years

An individual voluntary arrangement (IVA) is a formal, legally binding agreement in England and Wales (with a similar scheme in Northern Ireland) between a debtor and their creditors to repay debts over a fixed period, usually five or six years. It was introduced by the Insolvency Act 1986 as an alternative to bankruptcy, proposed following the recommendations of the Cork Report.

Process

A debtor, assisted by a licensed insolvency practitioner acting as nominee, puts a proposal to creditors—typically offering monthly contributions from income and sometimes a lump sum. At a decision procedure, the arrangement is approved if creditors representing at least 75% by value of those voting agree. Once approved, it binds all unsecured creditors entitled to vote, even those who voted against it or did not vote.

Effects

During the IVA, creditors may not pursue legal action or add certain charges, and interest on included debts is frozen. The supervisor (usually the same insolvency practitioner) distributes payments and monitors compliance. On successful completion, the debtor is released from the balance of the included debts. If the debtor fails to keep up payments, the IVA may fail and bankruptcy can follow.

Comparison with other options

Compared with bankruptcy, an IVA usually allows the debtor to keep their home (though they may be required to release equity) and avoids some occupational restrictions, but it lasts longer and is recorded on the public Individual Insolvency Register and on credit files. Alternatives include debt relief orders, debt management plans, and administration orders.
