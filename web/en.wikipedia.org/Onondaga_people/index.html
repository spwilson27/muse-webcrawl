The Onondaga people (Onondaga: Onöñda'gega', "the people on the hills") are an Indigenous people of the Northeastern Woodlands and one of the five original nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy). Their historic homeland lies in present-day Onondaga County, New York, centered on Onondaga Lake and the Onondaga Creek valley.

Role in the Confederacy

By Haudenosaunee tradition, the Onondaga village was the last to accept the Great Law of Peace brought by the Peacemaker, and the Onondaga became the confederacy's firekeepers: the council fire of the Grand Council burns at Onondaga, the Onondaga keep the confederacy's wampum records, and fourteen Onondaga chiefs sit on the Grand Council, presided over by the Tadodaho. Tradition holds that Hiawatha was an Onondaga (or Mohawk) leader who, with the Peacemaker, overcame the resistance of the Onondaga chief Tadodaho.

History

The Onondaga maintained their central position through the fur-trade era, negotiating with French, Dutch, and English powers. French Jesuits established a mission among them in the 1650s, briefly founding the colony of Sainte Marie de Gannentaha on Onondaga Lake in 1656. During the American Revolution the confederacy split; in 1779 Onondaga villages were attacked by American forces in the campaign ordered by General Sullivan. After the war, state treaties drastically reduced Onondaga land; a portion of the people also relocated to the Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario, while the main body remained on the present territory south of Syracuse.

Culture and language

Onondaga society is matrilineal, organized into clans; the faithkeepers maintain the Longhouse ceremonial cycle, including Midwinter, Green Corn, and Harvest ceremonies. The Onondaga language remains more widely spoken than most Haudenosaunee languages, supported by a language program and immersion efforts. Lacrosse, originated as a medicine game, remains a defining cultural practice.
