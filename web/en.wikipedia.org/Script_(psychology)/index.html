Script (psychology)

In cognitive psychology, a script is a structured mental representation of a stereotyped sequence of events, such as going to a restaurant, visiting a doctor, or attending a birthday party. Scripts are a type of schema that guides expectations and behaviour: knowing the "restaurant script" lets a person predict that they will be seated, given a menu, order, eat and pay.

Origins

The concept was developed in the 1970s by Roger Schank and Robert Abelson in their work on artificial intelligence and story understanding (Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding, 1977). Knowledge of scripts helps people comprehend narratives, fill in unstated events, and remember typical occurrences — though it can also produce false memories of script-consistent details that did not actually happen.

Related ideas

Scripts are related to the wider study of schemas, frames and event knowledge, and have been applied in developmental psychology, social psychology and research on autism, where understanding of social scripts may differ.
