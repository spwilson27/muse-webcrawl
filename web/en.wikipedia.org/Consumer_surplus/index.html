Consumer surplus

Consumer surplus
FieldMicroeconomics, welfare economics
DefinitionWillingness to pay minus price actually paid
Related conceptsProducer surplus, deadweight loss, economic surplus
Developed byJules Dupuit; Alfred Marshall

Consumer surplus is a measure in welfare economics of the difference between what consumers are willing to pay for a good or service and what they actually pay. It arises because consumers would be willing to pay more for the first units of a good than for later units, yet pay a single market price for all units purchased. Consumer surplus for an individual is the difference between the maximum price they would accept and the market price; aggregated over all buyers it is represented graphically by the area under the demand curve and above the price.

History

The concept was first developed by the French engineer-economist Jules Dupuit in the 1840s and was given its modern form and name by Alfred Marshall in his Principles of Economics (1890). Marshall used it to analyze the welfare effects of taxes, subsidies, monopolies, and public works.

Measurement

For a market, total consumer surplus equals the area of the triangle (or, with a curved demand function, the integral) between the demand curve and the market price, up to the quantity traded. When price rises, consumer surplus falls; part is transferred to producers as producer surplus and part may be lost entirely as deadweight loss.

Applications and criticism

Consumer surplus is central to cost–benefit analysis, antitrust economics, and the evaluation of price regulation, trade liberalization, and taxation. Critics note that it depends on assumptions about utility measurement and income effects; modern welfare economics often substitutes compensating and equivalent variation, which adjust for those effects.
