Companion diagnostic

A companion diagnostic (CDx) is a medical device, often an in vitro diagnostic test, that provides information essential for the safe and effective use of a corresponding drug or biological product. The test helps health care professionals determine whether a particular therapeutic product's benefits to patients will outweigh its risks by identifying patients most likely to respond, or those at increased risk of serious side effects.

Regulation

In the United States, companion diagnostics are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which in 2014 issued guidance on their development and co-approval with therapeutics. Ideally, the drug and its companion diagnostic are developed and approved simultaneously, and the test is named in the drug's labeling as required before use. In the European Union, companion diagnostics fall under the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR).

Examples

A landmark early example is the HercepTest, approved in 1998 alongside the breast cancer drug trastuzumab (Herceptin) to detect HER2 protein overexpression. Other examples include tests for EGFR mutations guiding use of gefitinib and osimertinib in lung cancer, KRAS mutation testing before anti-EGFR antibody therapy in colorectal cancer, and PD-L1 expression assays paired with immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab.

Role in personalized medicine

Companion diagnostics are a cornerstone of personalized medicine, particularly in oncology, where targeted therapies work only in molecularly defined subgroups of patients. By selecting likely responders, they improve outcomes and avoid exposing other patients to ineffective treatment and unnecessary toxicity.
