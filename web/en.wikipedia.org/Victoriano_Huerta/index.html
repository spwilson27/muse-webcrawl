José Victoriano Huerta Márquez (22 December 1850 – 13 January 1916) was a Mexican general and politician who seized the presidency of Mexico in a February 1913 military coup — the Decena Trágica ("Ten Tragic Days") — during the Mexican Revolution. His brief, authoritarian regime dissolved Congress, ruled by military force and was held responsible for the murder of his predecessor, Francisco I. Madero.

Early career

Of Huichol (Wixárika) and mestizo descent, Huerta was educated at the Military College of Chapultepec and rose through the federal army under Porfirio Díaz, campaigning against the Yaqui and the Maya of Yucatán and against rebels in Guerrero. Under President Madero he was sent to suppress the revolts of Pascual Orozco in 1912, winning victories in the north and becoming Madero's most trusted commander — and then his betrayer.

Coup and presidency

In February 1913, during the fighting in Mexico City known as the Decena Trágica, Huerta conspired with Félix Díaz and U.S. ambassador Henry Lane Wilson (the "Pact of the Embassy") to overthrow Madero. Madero and Vice President José María Pino Suárez were forced to resign and were murdered days later. Huerta became president through a legal fiction in which Foreign Minister Pedro Lascuráin held the presidency for less than an hour, appointed Huerta to the cabinet, and resigned.

Huerta militarized the government, dissolved Congress in October 1913, and rigged elections, but he never gained recognition from U.S. President Woodrow Wilson, whose administration imposed an arms embargo and occupied Veracruz in 1914. Constitutionalist armies under Venustiano Carranza, Álvaro Obregón, Pancho Villa and Emiliano Zapata steadily defeated his federal forces; after the fall of Zacatecas, Huerta resigned on 15 July 1914 and fled into exile.

Exile and death

Huerta travelled to Spain and then the United States, where in 1915 he was arrested at Newman, New Mexico, on charges of conspiring with German agents to foment a counter-revolution in Mexico. Detained at Fort Bliss, he died in El Paso in January 1916, officially of cirrhosis and Bright's disease. In Mexican memory he remains the archetypal usurper and reactionary of the Revolution.
