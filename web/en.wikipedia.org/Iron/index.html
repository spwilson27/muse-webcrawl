Iron is a chemical element; it has the symbol Fe (from Latin ferrum, 'iron') and atomic number 26. It is a metal that belongs to the first transition series and group 8 of the periodic table. It is, by mass, the most common element on Earth, forming much of Earth's outer and inner core. It is the fourth most common element in the Earth's crust, being mainly deposited by meteorites in its metallic state.

Properties

Pure iron is a lustrous, silvery-grey metal that is relatively soft and malleable, and is strongly ferromagnetic at room temperature — one of only a few elements magnetic at ambient conditions. It readily corrodes in moist air, forming hydrated iron oxides known as rust. Iron has several allotropes: alpha iron (ferrite, body-centred cubic) below 912 °C, gamma iron (austenite, face-centred cubic) between 912 °C and 1394 °C, and delta iron above that. It forms compounds mainly in the +2 (ferrous) and +3 (ferric) oxidation states.

History

Iron artefacts made from meteoric iron date back to about 3500 BCE in Egypt. Smelting of iron ore began in Anatolia and the Caucasus around 2000–1200 BCE, ushering in the Iron Age; mastery of carburised iron (steel) spread widely in the following centuries. Cast iron was produced in China by the 5th century BCE and in Europe much later. The Industrial Revolution was driven by iron and steel: coke smelting (Abraham Darby, 1709), the puddling process, and later the Bessemer (1856) and open-hearth processes made steel cheap and abundant.

Production and uses

Iron is extracted from ores such as hematite (Fe₂O₃) and magnetite (Fe₃O₄) by reduction with carbon in a blast furnace, producing pig iron, which is refined into steel — by far the most common structural metal, used in construction, vehicles, machinery, and tools. Wrought iron and cast iron serve specialised roles. World crude steel production exceeds 1.8 billion tonnes per year.

Biological role

Iron is essential to nearly all living organisms. In humans it is a component of haemoglobin in red blood cells and myoglobin in muscle, and of many enzymes; the body contains about 3–4 grams. Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide and causes anaemia; excess iron is toxic, as in hereditary haemochromatosis.
