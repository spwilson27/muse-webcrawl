The Camino de Santiago (Latin: Peregrinatio Compostellana), known in English as the Way of St James, is a network of pilgrims' ways leading to the shrine of the apostle Saint James in the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela in Galicia, north-western Spain, where tradition holds that the saint's remains are buried.

History

Pilgrimage to Santiago began in the 9th century after the reported discovery of the tomb of St James. By the Middle Ages it was one of the three great pilgrimages of Christendom, alongside Rome and Jerusalem. The 12th-century Codex Calixtinus contains the first pilgrim's guidebook. Numbers revived dramatically in the late 20th century, and hundreds of thousands now walk the routes each year.

Routes and symbols

The most popular route is the Camino Francés from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port in France across the Pyrenees and northern Spain. Pilgrims carry a credential (pilgrim's passport) stamped along the way and traditionally display the scallop shell, the symbol of St James. Those completing at least the last 100 km on foot may receive the Compostela certificate.
