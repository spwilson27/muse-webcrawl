Pilgrimage

A pilgrimage is a journey, often into an unfamiliar place, undertaken for religious or spiritual reasons, in which the traveller—a pilgrim—seeks moral or spiritual significance, often by travelling to a shrine, temple or other location of importance to their faith. Typically the journey itself, with its hardships and encounters, is understood to be as transformative as arrival at the destination.

In the major religions

Pilgrimage is central to many faiths. In Islam, the Hajj to Mecca is one of the Five Pillars, required of every able Muslim once in a lifetime. In Christianity, medieval pilgrims travelled to Jerusalem, Rome and Santiago de Compostela, and shrines such as Lourdes and Fátima still draw millions. Hindu pilgrims visit the Char Dham, the Kumbh Mela festival—the largest gathering on Earth—and rivers such as the Ganges. Buddhist pilgrims travel to Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar, sites of the Buddha's life, while Baháʼí, Sikh, Jewish and Shinto traditions all maintain significant pilgrimage destinations.

History and modern practice

Pilgrimage is among the oldest forms of travel. In medieval Europe it was at once an act of devotion, a penance and sometimes a form of tourism or exile, supported by hospices, guidebooks and badges sold at shrines. The Protestant Reformation attacked the practice in northern Europe, but pilgrimage flourished elsewhere and has revived strongly in the modern era: the Camino de Santiago now attracts hundreds of thousands of walkers each year, many of them motivated by culture, landscape or personal reflection as much as by formal religion. Secular pilgrimages—to battlefields, graves of famous figures or sporting shrines—are also widely recognised.
