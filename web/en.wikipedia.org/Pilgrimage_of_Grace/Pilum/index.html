Pilum

TypeThrowing spear / heavy javelin
Used byRoman Republic and Empire
LengthApproximately 2 m
Weightc. 0.9–2.5 kg

The pilum was the signature heavy javelin of the Roman legionary. About two metres (6 ft 7 in) in overall length, it combined a wooden shaft of roughly 1.2 m with an iron shank of about 60–90 cm ending in a short pyramidal head. A legionary normally carried two into battle.

Tactical role

Pila were thrown at short range—perhaps 15 to 30 metres—as the legion advanced, in one or two volleys immediately before contact. The narrow shank concentrated the weapon's weight behind the point, enabling it to pierce shields and sometimes the man behind them. A pilum lodged in a shield made it unwieldy: the heavy shaft dragged the shield down or had to be cut away, leaving the enemy exposed for the Roman sword charge. Ancient writers such as Vegetius and Caesar describe the disabling effect of pila on enemy shields, and the shank's tendency to bend on impact—which also denied the enemy the chance to throw the weapon back—may have been a recognised, if not wholly intended, feature.

Development

The pilum emerged in the middle Republic, perhaps adapted from Iberian or Samnite throwing spears, and was refined over centuries: the method of joining shank to shaft changed from a tang set in a socket to riveted plates, and heavier and lighter versions coexisted. By the third and fourth centuries AD it was giving way to weapons such as the spiculum and angon within a changing Roman army.
