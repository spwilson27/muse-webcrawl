Scutum (shield)

The scutum (Latin for "shield", plural scuta) was the large, curved rectangular shield carried by Roman legionaries. It was a defining element of Roman heavy infantry equipment from roughly the 4th century BC until the 3rd century AD, when it was gradually replaced by flat oval shields.

Type: Tower shield
Used by: Roman legionaries

Construction

The scutum was made of three layers of wood glued together like plywood, covered with canvas and leather, and bound with metal edging. A central iron or bronze boss (umbo) protected the hand grip and could be used to punch opponents. Typical Imperial scuta measured about 105–125 cm tall and 60 cm wide, curving around the body, and weighed around 10 kg.

Use

The shield's curved shape deflected missiles and enveloped the legionary's body in formation. Overlapping scuta formed the famous testudo (tortoise) formation, protecting a unit from arrows on all sides. Surviving examples, such as the Fayum scutum found in Egypt, confirm descriptions in Polybius and other ancient sources.

Decoration

Scuta were painted with unit insignia, most famously the wings and lightning bolts of Jupiter, aiding identification while presenting a uniform, intimidating front.
