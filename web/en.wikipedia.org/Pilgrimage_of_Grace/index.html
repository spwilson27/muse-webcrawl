Pilgrimage of Grace

The Pilgrimage of Grace was a popular revolt beginning in Yorkshire in October 1536, before spreading to other parts of Northern England including Cumberland, Northumberland, and north Lancashire, in protest against Henry VIII's break with the Catholic Church, the Dissolution of the Monasteries, and the policies of the King's chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, as well as other specific political, social, and economic grievances.

It was the most serious of all Tudor period rebellions, and the greatest revolt against the English monarchy during the 16th century. At its height, the rebels may have numbered some 40,000 people. The rebellion was led by Robert Aske, a lawyer from Yorkshire, who gave the movement its religious name and framed it as a "pilgrimage" rather than a rebellion.

Background

Opposition to Henry VIII's religious changes was strong in the conservative North. The dissolution of the smaller monasteries in 1536 destroyed institutions that provided employment, charity, education, and hospitality. Rumours circulated that church plate would be seized, that taxes would be levied on baptisms and marriages, and that parish churches would be closed. Economic grievances included high rents, enclosure, and poor harvests.

Course of the rising

The rising began after a related revolt in Lincolnshire was suppressed in October 1536. Aske's host entered York on 16 October and then took Pontefract Castle, whose lord, Thomas Darcy, surrendered and joined the rebels. The pilgrims demanded the restoration of dissolved monasteries, the removal of Cromwell and other "low-born" counsellors, and the restoration of the Princess Mary to the succession.

Unable to fight the large rebel army, the Duke of Norfolk negotiated a truce. The pilgrims dispersed after receiving a royal pardon and vague promises that Parliament would consider their grievances. When a smaller rising broke out in early 1537 under Sir Francis Bigod, Henry used it as a pretext to arrest the Pilgrimage's leaders.

Aftermath

Aske, Darcy, and around 200 others were executed in 1537, many by hanging, drawing, and quartering. The failure of the Pilgrimage of Grace removed the last major obstacle to the completion of the Dissolution of the Monasteries and the English Reformation.
