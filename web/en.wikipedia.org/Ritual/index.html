Ritual

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ritual is a sequence of activities involving gestures, words, actions, or objects, performed according to a set sequence. Rituals may be prescribed by the traditions of a community, including a religious community. Ritual is characterised by formalism, traditionalism, invariance, rule-governance, sacral symbolism, and performance. Rituals are a feature of all known human societies, from religious worship and rites of passage to everyday greetings and courtroom procedure.

Categories

Anthropologists classify rituals in many ways: rites of passage (birth, puberty, marriage, death), calendrical and commemorative rites, rites of exchange and communion (sacrifice, feasting), rites of affliction (healing, exorcism), and political rituals that display and legitimize power. Arnold van Gennep's analysis of rites of passage identified three phases — separation, transition (liminality), and incorporation — developed further by Victor Turner, who emphasized the communitas experienced in the liminal phase.

Theories

Émile Durkheim saw ritual as the mechanism by which society worships itself and renews solidarity. Later scholars, such as Catherine Bell, treated ritual not as a fixed category but as a strategic way of acting ("ritualization"). Roy Rappaport argued that the invariance of liturgical orders encodes enduring social information. Cognitive approaches study rituals as evolved responses to perceived hazards and anxiety.

Ritual in everyday life

Secular rituals — graduations, inaugurations, sporting ceremonies, national anthems — perform many of the same social functions as religious ones: marking transitions, reinforcing group identity, and legitimizing authority.
