Sudden stratospheric warming

A sudden stratospheric warming (SSW) is an event in which polar stratospheric temperatures rise by several tens of degrees Celsius (commonly 25 K or more) over the course of a few days. Such warmings, first reported from radiosonde measurements over Berlin in 1952 by Richard Scherhag, occur in the winter hemisphere and are driven by the breaking of planetary waves propagating upward from the troposphere, which decelerate and can reverse the winter polar vortex winds.

Classification

In a major warming, the zonal-mean zonal wind at 60° latitude and 10 hPa reverses from westerly to easterly and the polar vortex is displaced or split. Minor warmings produce strong temperature rises without wind reversal, and a final warming marks the spring transition to the summer circulation. Major Northern Hemisphere warmings occur on average roughly six times per decade; they are much rarer in the Southern Hemisphere, where only one (in 2002) has been observed as major.

Surface weather effects

Major warmings are often followed, over subsequent weeks, by a negative phase of the Arctic/North Atlantic Oscillation, bringing colder-than-average conditions to northern Europe and eastern North America — the February–March 2018 "Beast from the East" cold spell in Europe followed a major SSW. The coupling is probabilistic, not deterministic, and is an active area of seasonal forecasting research.
