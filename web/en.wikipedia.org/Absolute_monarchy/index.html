Absolute monarchy

An absolute monarchy is a form of monarchy in which the monarch holds supreme, unrestricted political power over the state and its people, not bound by a constitution, legislature or laws. Absolute monarchs typically justified their authority through divine right — the doctrine that kings derive their right to rule directly from God — and concentrated legislative, executive and judicial power in their own person, in contrast to constitutional monarchies, where the monarch's powers are limited by law.

History

Absolutism developed in Europe in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as rulers centralised authority at the expense of feudal nobility and representative assemblies, aided by standing armies, professional bureaucracies and new theories of sovereignty from thinkers such as Jean Bodin and Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet. Its archetype is Louis XIV of France (reigned 1643–1715), to whom the phrase "L'État, c'est moi" ("I am the state") is attributed, and who ruled from Versailles with the Estates General unsummoned. Other examples include Philip II of Spain, Peter the Great of Russia, and the monarchies of Prussia and Austria. "Enlightened absolutism" described rulers such as Frederick the Great and Catherine the Great who combined absolute power with reformist policies.

Decline and surviving examples

From the English Civil War and Glorious Revolution of 1688 onward, absolutism was challenged by constitutionalism, and the French Revolution and nineteenth-century liberal movements converted most European monarchies into constitutional ones or republics. Today, countries commonly described as absolute monarchies include Saudi Arabia, Oman, Brunei, Eswatini and the Vatican City, while several other monarchies retain very extensive powers in practice.
