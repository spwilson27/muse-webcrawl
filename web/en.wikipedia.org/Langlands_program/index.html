Langlands program

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the Langlands program is a web of far-reaching and influential conjectures about connections between number theory and geometry, proposed by Robert Langlands in 1967 and 1970. It seeks to relate Galois groups in algebraic number theory to automorphic forms and representations of algebraic groups over local fields and adeles. Widely considered the single biggest project in modern mathematical research, the Langlands program has been described as a "grand unified theory of mathematics". The project has attracted the work of several Fields Medalists and other major prize winners, and some of its central cases have become famous theorems.

Background

The program generalizes class field theory, which describes abelian extensions of number fields using groups associated to the fields themselves (a completed achievement of early 20th-century mathematics, due to Takagi, Artin, and others). Non-abelian class field theory was the goal: Langlands's insight was that the missing objects were automorphic representations. His 1967 letter to André Weil set out the conjectures, introducing the L-group and the principle of functoriality; his work built on Harish-Chandra's theory of representations of real groups and on the emerging theory of automorphic forms over adeles (Gelfand, and the conjectural picture in Tate's thesis).

The conjectures

The two pillars of the program are:

  • Reciprocity: to every suitable (irreducible, admissible) automorphic representation of a reductive group over a number field there should correspond a representation of the Langlands dual group of the Galois group — generalizing Artin reciprocity and matching the two sides' L-functions.
  • Functoriality: homomorphisms between L-groups should induce transfers of automorphic representations between the corresponding groups, generalizing many known constructions (base change, symmetric powers, endoscopy).

Local versions attach to each place of a number field a "local Langlands correspondence" between representations of the local Galois (Weil) group and representations of the local group; the local correspondence for GLn over p-adic fields was proved around 2000 (Harris–Taylor; Henniart).

Fundamental results

Proven cases include Langlands's own base change for GL(2), the work of Tunnell and Langlands on Artin representations of tetrahedral and octahedral type, and the modularity theorem: Andrew Wiles's proof (with Taylor) that semistable elliptic curves over Q are modular, completed for all elliptic curves by Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor (2001) — the result that implies Fermat's Last Theorem. Modularity is the GL(2) case of Langlands reciprocity for elliptic curves. Other landmarks include Drinfeld's proof of the global Langlands correspondence for GL(2) over function fields (Lafforgue extended it to GLn over function fields, earning a Fields Medal in 2002), Arthur's work on endoscopic classification for classical groups, Ngô Bảo Châu's proof of the Fundamental Lemma (Fields Medal 2010), and Peter Scholze's perfectoid methods applied to local Langlands and to the weight-monodromy conjecture. Vincent Lafforgue established functoriality-type results for general reductive groups over function fields.

The conjectures also proved the Sato–Tate conjecture (via potential automorphy results) and have reshaped analytic number theory, representation theory, and arithmetic geometry, including work on the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture through modularity lifting.

Geometric Langlands

A parallel "geometric" version, developed by Drinfeld, Beilinson, and others, replaces number fields by algebraic curves and Galois representations by local systems (flat connections), and automorphic forms by D-modules and Hecke eigensheaves on the moduli stack of bundles. Proposed equivalences between categories in this setting have deep connections to quantum field theory (Kapustin–Witten) and mirror symmetry. In 2024, a team led by Dennis Gaitsgory and Sam Raskin announced a proof of the (categorical, unramified) geometric Langlands conjecture over characteristic-zero curves, in a series of papers totalling hundreds of pages, a result widely hailed as a landmark of the subject.
