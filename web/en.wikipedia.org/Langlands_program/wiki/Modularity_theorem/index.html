Modularity theorem

The modularity theorem (formerly called the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture) states that every elliptic curve over the field of rational numbers is modular. In other words, for every elliptic curve defined over Q there exists a modular form of weight two whose L-series matches the L-series of the curve. It is a special case of the far more general modularity conjectures in the Langlands program.

Statement

An elliptic curve over the rationals can be given by an equation of the form y2 = x3 + ax + b with rational coefficients. The theorem asserts that the number of solutions of the curve modulo a prime p is governed by the Fourier coefficients of a modular form of weight 2 for a congruence subgroup Γ0(N), where N is the conductor of the curve. Equivalently, every rational elliptic curve arises from a modular curve by a rational map.

History

The conjecture was first proposed by Yutaka Taniyama in 1955 and developed by Goro Shimura; André Weil later attached his name to it after working on the precise form involving conductors. For decades it was regarded as deep but likely inaccessible. In 1986, Gerhard Frey suggested, and Jean-Pierre Serre and Ken Ribet proved, that the modularity of a particular semistable elliptic curve would imply Fermat's Last Theorem, because a hypothetical counterexample to Fermat would produce a curve that could not be modular.

In 1994, Andrew Wiles, with assistance from Richard Taylor, proved the semistable case of the conjecture — enough to complete the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. The full modularity theorem for all elliptic curves over Q was proved in 1999–2001 by Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond, and Richard Taylor, who extended Wiles's methods to the remaining cases.

Significance

Beyond Fermat's Last Theorem, the modularity theorem unlocked powerful analytic tools for the study of elliptic curves: properties of their L-functions, such as analytic continuation and functional equation, follow from modularity. It is also a cornerstone example of the Langlands philosophy, in which automorphic forms are matched with Galois representations.
