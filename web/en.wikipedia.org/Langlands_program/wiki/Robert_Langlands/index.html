Robert Phelan Langlands (born October 6, 1936) is a Canadian mathematician best known as the founder of the Langlands program, a vast web of conjectures and theorems connecting representation theory and automorphic forms to algebraic number theory through Galois groups in algebraic geometry and number theory. He was a professor at Princeton University and Yale University, and is professor emeritus at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. In 2018 he received the Abel Prize "for his visionary program connecting representation theory to number theory."

Early life and education

Langlands was born in New Westminster, British Columbia, and grew up in White Rock. He did not initially set out to become a mathematician; he enrolled at the University of British Columbia at age 16 intending to study engineering, but switched to mathematics, earning his bachelor's degree in 1957 and a master's degree in 1958. He went on to Yale University, where he received his PhD in 1960 with a dissertation on the analytic theory of Eisenstein series.

Career

After positions at Princeton University and Yale, Langlands spent a formative year in Turkey in 1967–68, where, in a now-famous 17-page handwritten letter to André Weil, he laid out the first form of what became known as the Langlands conjectures, introducing the concept of the L-group and predicting a correspondence between Galois representations and automorphic forms, as well as functoriality — the idea that automorphic representations of different groups should be related according to maps between their L-groups.

His subsequent work included the Langlands classification of admissible representations of real reductive groups, the foundations of the theory of Eisenstein series, work on the trace formula, percolation theory, and, later, the geometric Langlands program and the "Beyond Endoscopy" proposal. He spent most of his later career at the Institute for Advanced Study, which he joined in 1972, becoming Hermann Weyl Professor.

Awards and honors

Langlands shared the Wolf Prize in Mathematics in 1995–96 with Andrew Wiles. He received the AMS Cole Prize, the Steele Prize for Seminal Contribution to Research (2005), the Shaw Prize (2007, with Richard Taylor), and the Abel Prize in 2018. He is a fellow of the Royal Society and a member of the U.S. National Academy of Sciences.
