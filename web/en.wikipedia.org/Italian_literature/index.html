Italian literature

Italian literature is literature written in the Italian language, particularly within Italy. It may also refer to literature written by Italians in other languages spoken in Italy, frequently languages closely related to modern Italian, including literature in Latin produced in the Italian peninsula.

Origins and the Trecento

Italian vernacular literature emerged in the 13th century, with the Sicilian School of poetry at the court of Frederick II and figures such as Saint Francis of Assisi, whose "Canticle of the Sun" is among the earliest Italian poems. The fourteenth century — the Trecento — produced the three crowns of Italian literature: Dante Alighieri, whose Divine Comedy is regarded as one of the greatest works of world literature; Petrarch, whose Canzoniere shaped European lyric poetry; and Boccaccio, author of the Decameron.

Renaissance to the modern era

The Renaissance produced Ariosto's Orlando Furioso, Tasso's Jerusalem Delivered, and Machiavelli's political writings. Later landmarks include Goldoni's comedies, Manzoni's novel The Betrothed (I promessi sposi), Leopardi's poetry, and Verga's verismo fiction. In the twentieth century, writers such as Pirandello, Svevo, Montale, Pavese, Calvino, Primo Levi, and Umberto Eco achieved international prominence, and six Italians have won the Nobel Prize in Literature.
