In Greek mythology, Penelope (Greek: Πηνελόπη, Pēnelópē) is the wife of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, and the mother of Telemachus. A central figure of Homer's Odyssey, she is celebrated as an emblem of marital fidelity and cleverness, waiting twenty years for her husband's return from the Trojan War.

In the Odyssey

During Odysseus's long absence, Penelope was besieged at her palace by 108 suitors who sought her hand — and with it the kingship of Ithaca — consuming the estate's wealth in feasting. To delay choosing among them, she announced she would remarry only when she finished weaving a burial shroud for her father-in-law Laertes; each night she secretly unravelled the day's work, a ruse that succeeded for three years until a servant betrayed it. When Odysseus returned in disguise, Penelope announced an archery contest: the suitor who could string Odysseus's great bow and shoot through twelve axe heads would win her. Only the disguised Odysseus succeeded; he then slew the suitors.

Recognition and character

Penelope tests the returned stranger by ordering that their marriage bed be moved — knowing it was built around a living olive tree and could not be moved — and Odysseus's indignant response confirms his identity. Critics note that Penelope's mētis (cunning intelligence) matches her husband's, making them a uniquely equal couple in Homer. Later traditions, including the lost Telegony, continued her story.

Legacy

Penelope has inspired works from Ovid's Heroides to Margaret Atwood's novella The Penelopiad (2005), which retells the story from her perspective.
