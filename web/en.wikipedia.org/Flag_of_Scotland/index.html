The flag of Scotland (Scottish Gaelic: bratach na h-Alba) consists of a white saltire — the diagonal cross of Scotland's patron saint, Saint Andrew — on a blue field. It is often known informally as The Saltire or as the St Andrew's Cross. Along with the Royal Standard of Scotland (the Lion Rampant), it is one of the two flags flown from Scottish government buildings.

History and legend

According to legend, in 832 AD Óengus II led an army of Picts and Scots against the Angles near Athelstaneford in East Lothian. On the night before the battle, Saint Andrew appeared to Óengus in a dream promising victory; on the day of the battle a white saltire — the X-shaped cross on which Andrew was said to have been crucified — appeared against the blue sky, encouraging the Scots and demoralizing their foes. Whatever the legend's truth, St Andrew was adopted as Scotland's patron saint, and the saltire appears in Scottish seals and coinage from the 12th and 13th centuries; the Parliament of Scotland ordered the saltire on soldiers' uniforms in 1385.

Design and colour

The shade of blue was long unsettled, with flags produced in hues from sky blue to navy. In 2003 a committee of the Scottish Parliament recommended Pantone 300, a mid-blue, for official use. No ratio is fixed by law; 3:5 and 2:3 are both common. The saltire forms the basis of the Union Flag, in which it is combined with the crosses of St George and St Patrick, and of the flag of Nova Scotia in Canada.

Use

The Saltire flies from Scottish Government buildings daily, alongside the Union Flag and the European flag in earlier years, and is flown widely by the public, particularly on St Andrew's Day (30 November) and at sporting events. In 2018 the Scottish Government's flag-flying guidance, giving the Saltire precedence on designated days, attracted political attention at Westminster.
