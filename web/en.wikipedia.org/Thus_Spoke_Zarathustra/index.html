Thus Spoke Zarathustra: A Book for All and None (German: Also sprach Zarathustra: Ein Buch für Alle und Keinen), also translated as Thus Spake Zarathustra, is a work of philosophical fiction written by German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche; it was published in four volumes between 1883 and 1885. The protagonist is nominally the historical Zoroaster (Zarathustra), founder of Zoroastrianism, whom Nietzsche casts as the first inventor of good-and-evil dualism and therefore the fitting herald of its overcoming.

Summary

After ten years of solitude in the mountains, Zarathustra descends to teach humanity of the Übermensch (Overman) and of the “death of God”. The crowds prefer the comfortable “last man”, and Zarathustra withdraws, gathering disciples and delivering discourses on morality, self-overcoming, and the doctrine of eternal recurrence — the idea that one's life recurs identically forever, which must be affirmed with joy (amor fati). The fourth part, published privately in 1885 and publicly only in 1892, is a satyr-play-like finale in which Zarathustra resists pity for the “higher men” who visit his cave.

Style and themes

The book is written in a poetic, biblical diction modelled partly on Luther's Bible translation, mixing parables, speeches, songs, and aphorisms. Central themes include self-overcoming, the revaluation of all values, the will to power, and eternal recurrence. Nietzsche considered it his most important work, describing it in Ecce Homo as the greatest gift ever made to mankind.

Reception and influence

Initially little read — the first parts sold poorly and the fourth was printed in only forty copies — the book grew in influence in the twentieth century, affecting literature, philosophy, and music. Richard Strauss's tone poem Also sprach Zarathustra (1896), inspired by the work, became famous anew when its opening “Sunrise” fanfare was used in Stanley Kubrick's film 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968).
