Doric order

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Doric order is one of the three orders of ancient Greek and later Roman architecture; the other two canonical orders are the Ionic and the Corinthian. The Doric is most easily recognised by its simple circular capitals at the top of the columns. It represents the Dorian-speaking mainland and western colonies of Greece and is the earliest and plainest of the orders, generally regarded as the most masculine in character by ancient writers such as Vitruvius.

Characteristics

Greek Doric columns stand directly on the stylobate (the top step of the temple platform) with no base. The shaft is cut with 20 shallow vertical grooves, or flutes, meeting at sharp edges (arrises), and tapers upward with a slight convex swelling called entasis. The capital consists of a flaring, cushion-like echinus beneath a square slab, the abacus. Above the columns, the entablature carries a frieze of alternating triglyphs (panels with three vertical bars) and metopes (square panels, often sculpted). Mutules studded with peg-like guttae project beneath the cornice.

The Parthenon in Athens (447–432 BC) is considered the finest expression of the Doric order. Other notable examples include the Temple of Hephaestus in Athens, the temples of Paestum in Italy, and the Temple of Concordia at Agrigento in Sicily.

Roman Doric and later revivals

Roman architects modified the order by adding a base to the column, reducing the flaring of the echinus, and sometimes omitting the fluting; the Theatre of Marcellus in Rome is an early example. The Renaissance theorists, codifying the five orders, placed Doric second after Tuscan in their hierarchy. Its perceived strength and simplicity led to its use for military, civic and funerary buildings, and it was a mainstay of the Greek Revival style of the late 18th and 19th centuries, used for banks, museums, courthouses and universities across Europe and North America.
