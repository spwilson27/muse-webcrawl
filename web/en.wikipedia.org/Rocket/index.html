A rocket is a vehicle that uses jet propulsion to accelerate without using the surrounding air. A rocket engine produces thrust by reaction to exhaust expelled at high speed. Rocket engines work entirely from propellant carried within the vehicle; a rocket can operate in the vacuum of space. Rockets work more efficiently in a vacuum and incur a loss of efficiency in an atmosphere.

Multistage rockets are capable of attaining escape velocity from Earth and therefore can achieve unlimited maximum altitude. Compared with airbreathing engines, rockets are lightweight and powerful and capable of generating large accelerations. Rockets control their attitude using momentum wheels, fins, vernier engines, engine gimbaling, and spin stabilization, among other technologies.

History

Gunpowder rockets were developed in medieval China, where they were used as weapons and fireworks, and rocket artillery was refined by figures such as Hyder Ali and Tipu Sultan in India, inspiring the British Congreve rocket. In the early 20th century, Konstantin Tsiolkovsky derived the rocket equation and theorized spaceflight; Robert H. Goddard launched the first liquid-propellant rocket in 1926; and Hermann Oberth advanced rocket theory in Germany. During World War II Germany developed the V-2, the first long-range ballistic missile, whose engineers later seeded the American and Soviet space programs.

Spaceflight

The Soviet R-7 launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, in 1957, and carried Yuri Gagarin, the first human in space, in 1961. The American Saturn V remains one of the most powerful rockets ever flown, launching the Apollo Moon missions. Modern launch vehicles include Falcon 9 (the first reusable orbital booster), Ariane, Long March, and Starship.

Physics

Rocket performance is described by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation, Δv = ve ln(m₀/mf), relating achievable velocity change to exhaust velocity and the ratio of initial to final mass. This equation explains the dominance of staging and the difficulty of reaching orbit.
