Edmund Gustav Albrecht Husserl (8 April 1859 – 27 April 1938) was a German philosopher who established the school of phenomenology, one of the most influential movements in twentieth-century philosophy. His work laid the groundwork for existentialism, hermeneutics and much of continental philosophy, and influenced thinkers including Martin Heidegger, Jean-Paul Sartre, Maurice Merleau-Ponty and Emmanuel Levinas.

Life

Husserl was born in Proßnitz (now Prostějov, Czech Republic) in Moravia. He studied mathematics, physics and philosophy in Leipzig, Berlin and Vienna, where he attended the lectures of Franz Brentano, whose concept of intentionality shaped his thinking. He taught at Halle, Göttingen and finally Freiburg, where he held the chair of philosophy from 1916 until his retirement in 1928. Because of his Jewish ancestry he was progressively excluded from academic life under the Nazi regime, and he died in Freiburg in 1938. His manuscripts were smuggled to Leuven in Belgium, where the Husserl Archives preserve his extensive Nachlass.

Phenomenology

Husserl sought to make philosophy a rigorous science by returning "to the things themselves" — to the structures of experience as they are lived. Central concepts include intentionality (consciousness is always consciousness of something), the epoché or bracketing of the "natural attitude" (suspending assumptions about the external world in order to describe experience itself), and eidetic reduction, the method of discerning essential structures of phenomena. In his later work he developed the concept of the lifeworld (Lebenswelt), the pre-scientific world of everyday experience.

Major works

His principal published works include the Logical Investigations (1900–1901), which broke with psychologism in logic; Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology (1913); the Cartesian Meditations (lectures delivered in Paris in 1929); and The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology (1936). Much of his research survives in tens of thousands of pages of shorthand manuscripts published posthumously in the Husserliana series.

Legacy

Husserl's phenomenology transformed philosophy in Europe and beyond, and its methods have been taken up in psychology, sociology, cognitive science and the philosophy of mind. His insistence on describing experience without presupposition remains a touchstone for debates about consciousness and first-person method.
