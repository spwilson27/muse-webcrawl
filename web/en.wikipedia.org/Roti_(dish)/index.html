Roti (dish)

Roti (also known as chapati) is a round flatbread native to the Indian subcontinent, made from wholemeal wheat flour (traditionally atta), water, and optionally salt, and cooked on a hot flat griddle called a tawa. It is a staple food in India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, and — carried by the Indian diaspora — in parts of the Caribbean, Southeast Asia, East Africa, and Fiji.

Preparation

The dough is kneaded until soft, rested, divided into balls, and rolled into thin discs. The disc is cooked on a hot tawa until small bubbles form, then flipped; it is often finished directly over an open flame, which causes it to puff up with steam. Finished rotis are commonly brushed with ghee or oil and kept warm wrapped in cloth. A well-made roti is soft, pliable, and lightly blistered with brown spots.

Varieties and related breads

When cooked with oil on the griddle, the result approaches a paratha, a richer, flakier layered flatbread. In the Caribbean, particularly Trinidad and Guyana, layered "paratha roti" (nicknamed "buss up shut" because it resembles a torn shirt) and wrap-style roti filled with curries are staples. In Malaysia and Singapore, roti canai is a related flaky flatbread, while roti prata is its Singaporean counterpart. Other related Indian breads include the deep-fried puri and the leavened naan and kulcha.

Nutrition and use

Made from whole wheat flour, roti provides carbohydrates, dietary fibre, and B vitamins. Rotis are used to scoop up curries, dals, vegetables, and pickles, and form the basis of wraps and rolls in many regional cuisines.

Etymology

The word roti derives from the Sanskrit roṭikā, meaning bread, and is widespread in Indo-Aryan languages. In Pakistan and northern India, roti is also used generically to mean a meal or food itself, as in the phrase "roti kapda aur makaan" (food, clothing, and shelter — the basic necessities of life).
