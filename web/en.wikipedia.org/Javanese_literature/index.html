Javanese literature

Javanese literature is the literature of the Javanese people of Central and East Java, Indonesia, written principally in the Javanese language and its historical form, Old Javanese (Kawi). One of the oldest and richest literary traditions of Southeast Asia, it spans more than a millennium — from ninth-century inscriptions and court epics adapted from Sanskrit models, through the Hindu-Buddhist literature of Majapahit, the Islamic suluk poetry of the coastal kingdoms, the encyclopaedic court literature of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Surakarta, to the modern novel in the twentieth century.

Old Javanese literature

The classical tradition began under the Hindu-Buddhist kingdoms of Central and East Java. The oldest surviving major work is the Ramayana Kakawin (c. 9th–10th century), a court epic in Sanskrit-derived metres that adapts rather than merely translates the Indian Ramayana. Mpu Kanwa's Arjunawiwaha (c. 1030), composed at the court of King Airlangga, celebrates the king through the hero Arjuna. Under the Majapahit empire, Mpu Tantular wrote the Sutasoma, whose phrase Bhinneka Tunggal Ika ("unity in diversity") became Indonesia's national motto, and Mpu Prapanca composed the Nagarakretagama (1365), a eulogy of King Hayam Wuruk that is a principal historical source for the empire. Alongside the courtly kakawin in Indian metres grew the kidung, narrative poems in native metres telling Panji romances and historical tales.

Islamic and early modern period

With the Islamisation of Java's north coast from the fifteenth century, new genres arrived: suluk, allegorical mystical poems associated with the Wali Songo (Nine Saints), adapted Islamic teaching to Javanese sensibilities, while Malay-language romances and Arabic tales were reworked in Javanese. Court chronicles called babad blended history, genealogy, and myth to legitimate dynasties, most famously the Babad Tanah Jawi (Chronicle of Java).

The Surakarta renaissance

After the division of Mataram in 1755, the courts of Surakarta and Yogyakarta sponsored a literary revival. Poets known as pujangga — above all Ranggawarsita (1802–1873), considered the last great court poet — produced vast didactic and encyclopaedic works in macapat verse. The Serat Centhini (compiled early 19th century), sometimes called the encyclopaedia of Javanese culture, runs to thousands of stanzas covering religion, erotica, food, music, and folklore through the wanderings of its protagonists.

Modern literature

Print and colonial education transformed Javanese letters in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The Dutch colonial publisher Balai Pustaka issued Javanese-language novels and periodicals; writers such as Suparto Brata (1932–2015) later sustained a modern Javanese prose tradition with novels like Trem and detective fiction. Wayang shadow theatre — drawing on the Mahabharata and Ramayana as retold by generations of puppeteers — remains a living literary performance tradition, recognised by UNESCO. Today Javanese literature survives in publishing niches, school curricula in Central and East Java, and periodicals, even as Indonesian dominates print culture.
