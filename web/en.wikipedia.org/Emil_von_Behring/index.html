Emil Adolf von Behring (15 March 1854 – 31 March 1917) was a German physiologist who received the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901 for his work on serum therapy, particularly its application against diphtheria. He is often called the "saviour of children", since diphtheria was a major cause of child mortality before his antitoxin became available.

Emil von Behring, pioneer of immunology

Early life and career

Behring was born in Hansdorf (now Ławice, Poland), the eldest of twelve children of a schoolmaster. He studied medicine at the Army Medical College in Berlin, graduating in 1878, and served as a military surgeon. In 1889 he joined the Institute for Infectious Diseases in Berlin, directed by Robert Koch, where he worked alongside Paul Ehrlich and the visiting Japanese scientist Shibasaburō Kitasato.

Serum therapy

In 1890, Behring and Kitasato published their landmark finding that animals immunised with weakened tetanus or diphtheria toxins produced blood serum containing substances — antitoxins — that could protect other animals against the diseases. This demonstrated that immunity could be transferred passively through serum, founding the field of humoral immunology and the practice of serum therapy. The diphtheria antitoxin was first used successfully on a child on Christmas night 1891, and industrial production with the Hoechst company followed from 1894, dramatically reducing diphtheria mortality.

Later work

Behring became professor at the University of Marburg in 1895, where he remained for the rest of his life. He continued research into tuberculosis, attempting to develop a bovine vaccine, and founded the Behringwerke company in 1904 to produce sera and vaccines. He was ennobled in 1901, adding "von" to his name.

Legacy

Behring's discovery of antitoxins established passive immunisation as a cornerstone of medicine and opened the way to modern immunology. The Emil von Behring Prize of the University of Marburg commemorates his name.
