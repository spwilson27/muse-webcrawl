Philosophes
Era18th century (Age of Enlightenment)
CenterParis, France
Key figuresVoltaire, Montesquieu, Diderot, Rousseau, d'Alembert, Condorcet
Key workEncyclopédie (1751–1772)

The philosophes were the intellectuals of the 18th-century French Enlightenment. Neither a school nor a unified movement, they were writers, philosophers, scientists, and critics united by a commitment to reason, empirical inquiry, religious toleration, and reform of what they saw as the irrational institutions of the Old Regime. Their ideas profoundly shaped the American and French Revolutions and the modern world.

Ideas

Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) developed the separation of powers. Voltaire campaigned against religious intolerance and championed freedom of expression. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's The Social Contract (1762) advanced popular sovereignty and the general will, though his views frequently clashed with those of his fellow philosophes. Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d'Alembert edited the monumental Encyclopédie, intended to gather and disseminate all useful knowledge and to challenge authority by doing so.

Salons and the Republic of Letters

The philosophes spread their ideas through salons hosted by women such as Madame Geoffrin, through pamphlets, plays, and clandestine books, and through the international "Republic of Letters." Many worked under censorship and risked imprisonment or exile; Diderot was jailed at Vincennes, and Voltaire repeatedly fled or was exiled from France.

Legacy

Their critique of throne and altar supplied the intellectual framework for modern concepts of human rights, constitutionalism, and the separation of church and state. Historians debate the degree of their direct responsibility for the French Revolution, but their influence on modern liberal thought is widely acknowledged.
