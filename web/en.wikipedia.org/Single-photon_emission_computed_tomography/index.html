Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT, or less commonly SPET) is a nuclear medicine tomographic imaging technique using gamma rays. A radioactive tracer is injected into the patient, and a gamma camera rotates around the body recording two-dimensional projections from many angles; computer reconstruction then produces three-dimensional images of tracer distribution, showing blood flow and functional activity in organs.

Technique

Unlike positron emission tomography (PET), SPECT detects single gamma photons emitted isotopically labelled compounds, most often based on technetium-99m, whose six-hour half-life and 140 keV gamma emission suit gamma cameras. Collimators — lead plates with thousands of parallel holes — restrict detected photons to known directions, trading sensitivity for spatial information.

Applications

SPECT is widely used for myocardial perfusion imaging to assess coronary artery disease, for bone scans locating metastases and fractures, for brain perfusion studies in dementia and epilepsy, and for imaging of tumours and infections. Hybrid SPECT/CT scanners fuse the functional images with anatomical CT for precise localization.

Comparison with PET

SPECT tracers are cheaper and more widely available, with longer half-lives that ease logistics, but SPECT images generally have lower spatial resolution and sensitivity than PET. Both are forms of molecular imaging that reveal physiology rather than anatomy alone.
