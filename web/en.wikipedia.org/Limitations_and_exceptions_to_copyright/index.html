Limitations and exceptions to copyright


Limitations and exceptions to copyright are provisions in copyright law that permit certain uses of protected works without the right holder's permission.

Limitations and exceptions to copyright are a number of provisions in local copyright laws that permit the use of copyrighted works without the need to acquire permission from the rights holder, balancing the author's rights against the public interest.

Forms

Common-law jurisdictions such as the United States use the open-ended doctrine of fair use, assessed by four factors including the purpose of the use and its effect on the market. Most civil-law systems, and UK-style "fair dealing", instead list specific permitted acts, such as quotation, private copying, parody, research and private study, and use in education and libraries.

International framework

The Berne Convention's three-step test constrains exceptions to certain special cases that do not conflict with normal exploitation of the work and do not unreasonably prejudice the author's legitimate interests. The Marrakesh Treaty (2013) mandates exceptions for accessible copies for people who are blind or print-disabled.
