Income inequality in the United States is the extent to which income is distributed unevenly among the American population. The United States has the highest level of income inequality among the large advanced economies of the OECD. After a period of relative equality from the 1940s to the 1970s — the "Great Compression" — inequality rose sharply from the late 1970s onward, returning by the 2010s to levels last seen in the 1920s.

Trends

Using tax-return data, economists Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez showed that the top 1% of households received about 10% of national income in the 1970s but around 20% by the 2000s–2010s, while the top 0.1% captured a disproportionate share of the gains. Census Bureau figures show the official Gini coefficient rising from roughly 0.40 in the late 1960s to about 0.49 by the late 2010s. Incomes of the top quintile grew much faster than those of the middle and bottom; median wages stagnated for long stretches even as productivity rose — the "productivity–pay gap".

Causes

Economists debate the relative weight of several drivers: skill-biased technological change raising returns to education; globalisation, which exposed manufacturing workers to foreign competition; the decline of unions and of the real minimum wage; tax policy changes lowering top marginal rates; financialisation and soaring executive compensation; and "superstar" and winner-take-all markets in which small differences in talent or reach produce enormous pay differences. Assortative mating and rising returns to capital (Piketty's r > g) are also cited.

Consequences and debate

Research associates high inequality with slower social mobility — the "Great Gatsby curve" — and, more contestably, with worse health outcomes, political polarisation and slower growth, arguments popularised by works such as The Spirit Level and Piketty's Capital in the Twenty-First Century. Others argue that absolute living standards, consumption inequality and poverty trends tell a more favourable story, and that some rise in inequality reflects benign forces such as technology and consumer gains. Disagreements also turn on measurement: whether to count taxes and transfers, capital gains, and employer health benefits can substantially change the picture. Income inequality was a central theme of the Occupy movement of 2011 and of subsequent US electoral politics.
