A terrestrial planet, also called a telluric or rocky planet, is a planet composed primarily of silicate rocks or metals, with a solid surface. The term derives from the Latin Terra (Earth). Within the Solar System, the terrestrial planets are the four inner planets — Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars — in contrast to the gas and ice giants of the outer system.

Structure and formation

Terrestrial planets have a differentiated internal structure: a metallic (mostly iron–nickel) core, a silicate mantle, and a crust. They formed inside the frost line of the protoplanetary disk, where temperatures were too high for volatile compounds to condense, so they accreted from rocky and metallic planetesimals and ended up small and dense, with few or no rings and few moons. Geological activity varies: Earth and (to a degree) Venus and Mars show evidence of volcanism and tectonics.

Beyond the Solar System

Thousands of exoplanets have been found with sizes and densities consistent with rocky compositions, including the planets of TRAPPIST-1 and Proxima Centauri b. "Super-Earths" are terrestrial planets more massive than Earth but lighter than ice giants. The search for Earth-sized rocky planets in habitable zones is a central goal of exoplanet science, because such worlds are considered the most plausible abodes for life.
