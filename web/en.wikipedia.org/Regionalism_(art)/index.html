Regionalism (art)

Regionalism was an American realist modern art movement that flourished during the 1930s. Reacting against the avant-garde abstraction of European modernism and the dislocations of the Great Depression, Regionalist artists depicted realistic scenes of rural and small-town life in the American Midwest, often with an idealised or nostalgic tone.

Principal artists

The movement's leading figures — sometimes dubbed the "Regionalist Triumvirate" — were Grant Wood of Iowa, Thomas Hart Benton of Missouri, and John Steuart Curry of Kansas. Wood's American Gothic (1930) became one of the most recognisable images in American art, while Benton produced vigorous murals of American industry and history and Curry painted dramatic scenes of Kansas farm life, tornados and religious revivalism.

Decline

Regionalism was celebrated in the 1930s — the three artists appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1934 — but fell from favour during the Second World War, criticised as parochial or propagandistic, and was displaced after 1945 by Abstract expressionism, whose centre, ironically, was New York. Later scholarship has reassessed the movement's engagement with race, industry and national identity.
