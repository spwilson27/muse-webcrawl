Volcanology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Volcanology (also spelled vulcanology) is the study of volcanoes, lava, magma and related geological, geophysical and geochemical phenomena. A volcanologist is a geologist who studies the eruptive activity and formation of volcanoes and their current and historic eruptions. Volcanologists frequently visit volcanoes to collect samples, monitor activity and observe eruptions, and they work at observatories that issue hazard assessments and forecasts.

Scope

The field covers the processes that transport melt from the Earth's mantle and crust to the surface: magma generation and storage, eruption dynamics, lava flows, pyroclastic deposits, volcanic gas emissions, and the landforms volcanoes create. It draws on petrology, geochemistry, geophysics (seismology, geodesy, gravimetry) and remote sensing, and includes planetary volcanology — the study of volcanoes on the Moon, Mars, Io and other bodies.

Monitoring and hazards

Volcano observatories such as the USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (founded 1912), the Alaska and Cascades observatories, and their counterparts in Iceland, Italy, Japan and Indonesia monitor seismicity, ground deformation (by GPS and InSAR), gas composition and thermal anomalies to forecast eruptions. Famous successes and failures shape the discipline: the evacuation before the 1991 Pinatubo eruption saved thousands of lives, while the 1985 Nevado del Ruiz eruption in Colombia killed about 23,000 people despite warnings.

History

Classical writers described Vesuvius; Pliny the Younger's letters on the AD 79 eruption are the first detailed account of an eruption, and "Plinian" eruptions bear his name. Modern volcanology coalesced in the 20th century with observatories on Vesuvius (1841, the world's first), Kīlauea, and later the global network built after the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens.
