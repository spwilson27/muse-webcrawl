Saigō Takamori (Japanese: 西郷 隆盛, 23 January 1828 – 24 September 1877) was a Japanese samurai and politician who was one of the most influential figures of the Meiji Restoration. A low-ranking samurai of the Satsuma Domain, he rose to become a leader of the movement that overthrew the Tokugawa shogunate. He later broke with the Meiji government he had helped create and led the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877, the last major armed uprising against the new order. He is often romanticised as the "last true samurai".

Early life

Saigō was born in Kagoshima, the castle town of Satsuma, into a family of modest samurai rank. He served the daimyō Shimazu Nariakira, whose reformist policies shaped his outlook. After Nariakira's death he fell into political exile on the islands of Amami Ōshima and Okinoerabu, experiences that deepened his reputation for incorruptibility and loyalty.

Role in the Restoration

Recalled to Satsuma, Saigō became the domain's leading military figure. He helped forge the Satsuma–Chōshū Alliance of 1866 and commanded imperial forces during the Boshin War (1868–1869). His negotiations with Katsu Kaishū secured the bloodless surrender of Edo Castle, sparing the city from destruction.

Meiji government and the Korea debate

In the new government Saigō served as a councillor and commander of the Imperial Guard. In 1873 he argued for a mission to Korea (the Seikanron debate) and offered to go himself; when the council decided against him, he resigned and returned to Kagoshima, where he founded a network of private military schools (shigakkō).

Satsuma Rebellion and death

In February 1877 discontented Satsuma samurai, fearing government moves against the schools, rose in revolt and acclaimed Saigō as their leader. The rebellion was defeated after months of fighting. At the final Battle of Shiroyama on 24 September 1877, the remnants of Saigō's army were annihilated; Saigō, gravely wounded, died — according to legend, aided by a loyal retainer so that his body would not fall into enemy hands.

Legacy

Despite dying a rebel, Saigō was posthumously pardoned in 1889 and restored to court rank. A famous bronze statue of him stands in Ueno Park, Tokyo. His life has been retold in countless novels, films and dramas, and he remains a symbol of samurai honour, sincerity and resistance to corrupt authority.
