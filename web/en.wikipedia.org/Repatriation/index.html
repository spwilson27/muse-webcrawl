Repatriation

Voluntary repatriation is one of the three durable solutions for refugees.

Repatriation is the return of a person to their place of origin or citizenship. The term applies to many contexts: the return of refugees and migrants to their home countries, the release and return of prisoners of war under the Geneva Conventions, the return of human remains and cultural property to indigenous peoples and countries of origin, and, in finance, the bringing back of earnings held abroad.

Refugees

Under international refugee law, voluntary repatriation in safety and dignity is regarded as the preferred durable solution to refugee situations, alongside local integration and resettlement in a third country. The principle of non-refoulement prohibits returning refugees to territories where they face serious threats to life or freedom, so repatriation programmes are in principle voluntary; large-scale returns have followed the end of conflicts in countries such as Mozambique, Afghanistan, and Liberia, often assisted by the UNHCR.

Cultural property and remains

Repatriation also describes the return of museum objects, artworks, and ancestral human remains to their communities or countries of origin — a central issue in debates over colonial-era collecting. Legislation such as the United States' Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (1990) established procedures for the return of remains and sacred objects to Native American tribes, and claims concerning objects such as the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles continue to reshape museum practice.
