Sadducees

PeriodSecond Temple period (c. 2nd century BCE – 70 CE)
BaseJerusalem; the Temple priesthood
RivalsPharisees
FateDisappeared after the destruction of the Temple, 70 CE

The Sadducees (Hebrew: Tzedukim) were a Jewish religious and political party during the Second Temple period, from roughly the second century BCE until the destruction of the Temple in 70 CE. Drawn largely from the wealthy, aristocratic priestly families who controlled the Jerusalem Temple and held seats on the Sanhedrin, they were the chief rivals of the Pharisees. The name is often connected with Zadok, the high priest of Solomon's time, and hence with the priestly line.

Beliefs

The ancient sources — chiefly Josephus, the New Testament and later rabbinic literature — portray the Sadducees as accepting only the written Torah as authoritative and rejecting the Pharisaic oral law. Accordingly they denied the resurrection of the dead, the existence of angels and spirits, an afterlife of rewards and punishments, and predestination, emphasising human free will. In Acts 23:8 the contrast is stated directly: "The Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, and no angel or spirit, but the Pharisees acknowledge them all."

Role in society

As the priestly aristocracy, the Sadducees administered the Temple cult, cooperated pragmatically with the Hasmonean rulers and later with the Roman authorities, and dominated the high priesthood under Roman rule. Their legal rulings are remembered in rabbinic sources as stricter in some matters (for example, the actual carrying out of "eye for eye" penalties) and more lenient in others than Pharisaic practice.

Disappearance

Because their authority rested on the Temple, the Sadducees vanished as an organised movement after the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in 70 CE; the Judaism that survived and developed was that of their Pharisaic rivals, the rabbis. Almost nothing written by Sadducees survives, so their views are known only through opponents' accounts.
