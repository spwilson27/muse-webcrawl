Distress signal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A distress signal, also known as a distress call, is an internationally recognized means for obtaining help. Distress signals are communicated by transmitting radio signals, displaying a visually detectable item or illumination, or making a sound audible from a distance. A distress signal indicates that a person, group of people, ship, aircraft or other vehicle is threatened by serious or imminent danger and requires immediate assistance.

Maritime and aviation signals

The best-known distress signal is SOS in Morse code (· · · – – – · · ·), adopted internationally in 1906. The spoken equivalent over voice radio is "Mayday", from the French m'aider ("help me"). Maritime distress may also be signalled by flying the national ensign upside down, firing flares, displaying the International Code of Signals flags N over C, waving arms slowly, or activating an Emergency Position-Indicating Radio Beacon (EPIRB). Modern vessels and aircraft use the Global Maritime Distress and Safety System (GMDSS) and 406 MHz beacons monitored by the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system.

Mountain and land signals

In the mountains, the Alpine distress signal consists of six blasts (whistle, shout or light flashes) per minute, answered by three blasts per minute from rescuers. The ground-to-air signal "Y" means assistance is required, while a displayed square orange panel with a black circle is a recognised distress marker. Misuse of distress signals is an offence in many jurisdictions because false alarms divert rescue resources.
