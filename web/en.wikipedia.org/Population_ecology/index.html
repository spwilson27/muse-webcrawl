Population ecology

Population ecology is a sub-field of ecology that deals with the dynamics of species populations and how these populations interact with the environment, such as birth and death rates, and by immigration and emigration. A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in the same geographical area at the same time, and population ecology studies how and why its numbers change over time.

Concepts

Key concepts include population size and density, dispersion patterns (clumped, uniform, or random), age structure, and the growth of populations. In an environment with unlimited resources, populations can grow exponentially, following a J-shaped curve; in reality, growth is limited by resources and follows logistic growth toward a carrying capacity (K), producing an S-shaped curve. Populations are also shaped by density-dependent factors such as competition, predation, and disease, and by density-independent factors such as weather and natural disasters.

Models and dynamics

Population ecologists use mathematical models including the exponential and logistic growth equations, life tables, survivorship curves, and predator–prey models such as the Lotka–Volterra equations. Metapopulation theory, developed by Richard Levins, describes populations as networks of local subpopulations connected by dispersal, and is central to conservation biology and landscape ecology.

Applications

Population ecology underlies wildlife and fisheries management, pest control, epidemiology, and the conservation of endangered species, where concepts such as minimum viable population and population viability analysis guide interventions.
