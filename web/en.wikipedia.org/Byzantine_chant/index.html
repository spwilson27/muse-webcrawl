Byzantine chant is the sacred chant of the churches following the Byzantine Rite, centred on Constantinople and developed during the Byzantine Empire. It is monophonic, sung without instrumental accompaniment, and organised according to the eight-mode system known as the Octoechos. Byzantine chant is still the liturgical music of the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Byzantine-rite Catholic churches.

History

The chant tradition took shape in late antiquity from the psalmody of the early church, enriched by hymnographers such as Romanos the Melodist (6th century), creator of the kontakion, and John of Damascus, credited with systematising the Octoechos. Kassia, a 9th-century abbess, is among the earliest named composers whose works survive. In the 10th–13th centuries a highly ornamented "kalophonic" (beautiful-sounding) style flourished, associated with John Koukouzelis.

Notation and practice

Chant is written in Byzantine neumatic notation, which indicates melodic intervals relative to a starting note rather than absolute pitches. In performance, a choir or single cantor (psaltis) sings the melody while other voices may hold a sustained drone, the ison. Chrysanthos of Madytos's reform of 1814 simplified the notation into the "New Method" still in use. In 2019 UNESCO inscribed Byzantine chant on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
