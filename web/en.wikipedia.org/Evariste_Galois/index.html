Évariste Galois (25 October 1811 – 31 May 1832) was a French mathematician and political activist who, while still a teenager, founded the theory now called Galois theory, determining when a polynomial equation can be solved by radicals. He died at twenty from wounds suffered in a duel, leaving manuscripts that reshaped algebra.

Life

A brilliant but frustrated student, Galois twice failed the entrance examination of the École Polytechnique and saw his memoirs lost or rejected by the Academy of Sciences (one was in Cauchy's hands, another was lost after Fourier's death). An ardent republican during the July Monarchy, he was imprisoned for political agitation. In May 1832 he was drawn into a duel — possibly over an affair of honour, possibly a political trap — and was shot in the abdomen, dying the next day.

Work

On the night before the duel, Galois wrote a testamentary letter to Auguste Chevalier sketching his discoveries. His ideas associated to each polynomial a group of permutations of its roots (the Galois group): the equation is solvable by radicals exactly when that group is solvable. This explained why no general formula exists for quintic equations, complementing the Abel–Ruffini theorem. His notion of a group, normal subgroup, and finite fields ("Galois fields") were foundational — though only published by Liouville in 1846, fourteen years after his death.

Legacy

Galois theory is a cornerstone of modern algebra, linking field theory and group theory, and the romance of his short life made him mathematics' archetypal tragic genius.
