Elimination (pharmacology)


Drugs are eliminated mainly by renal excretion and hepatic metabolism

In pharmacology, elimination (or excretion) is the removal of a drug or its metabolites from the body. Together with absorption, distribution and metabolism it forms the ADME framework of pharmacokinetics. Elimination is quantified by clearance — the volume of plasma cleared of a substance per unit time — and by the elimination half-life, the time required for the plasma concentration to fall by half.

Routes

The kidneys are the principal organs of excretion: drugs are filtered at the glomerulus, may be actively secreted into the tubules, and may be reabsorbed depending on their lipid solubility and ionisation. The liver excretes some drugs and metabolites into bile, whence they leave in the faeces or are reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation). Minor routes include the lungs (volatile anaesthetics), sweat, saliva, tears and breast milk.

Kinetics

Most drugs are eliminated by first-order kinetics: a constant fraction is removed per unit time, so concentration falls exponentially with a constant half-life. A few substances, notably ethanol and phenytoin at high doses, show zero-order kinetics, where a constant amount is eliminated per unit time regardless of concentration because the eliminating enzymes are saturated. After about four to five half-lives, a drug is effectively eliminated, or reaches steady state under repeated dosing.

Clinical significance

Impaired kidney or liver function slows elimination, risking accumulation and toxicity, so doses of many drugs are adjusted in renal or hepatic impairment. Elimination rates also determine dosing intervals and the duration of drug interactions when one drug inhibits or induces the enzymes or transporters that clear another.
