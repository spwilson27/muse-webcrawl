Nucleus accumbens

Part ofBasal ganglia, ventral striatum
SystemLimbic system, mesolimbic pathway
ArteryAnterior cerebral artery branches
IdentifiersMeSH D009714; NeuroLex birnlex_727

The nucleus accumbens (NAc or NAcc), also known as the accumbens nucleus, is a region in the basal forebrain rostral to the preoptic area of the hypothalamus. It is a key component of the ventral striatum and of the brain's reward system, playing a central role in motivation, pleasure, reward-seeking behaviour, and reinforcement learning.

Structure

The nucleus accumbens is divided into two main subregions: the core and the shell. The core surrounds the anterior commissure and is associated with motor functions related to reward, while the shell is involved in the emotional and motivational aspects of reward processing. The principal cell type is the medium spiny neuron, which is GABAergic and inhibitory. The nucleus accumbens receives dense dopaminergic input from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) via the mesolimbic pathway, as well as glutamatergic input from the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus. Its output projects mainly to the ventral pallidum and, through it, to thalamic and cortical targets.

Function

The nucleus accumbens is critically involved in reward anticipation, incentive salience, and the reinforcing effects of food, sex, social interaction, and drugs of abuse. Dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens signals reward prediction and prediction error, driving learning about cues associated with rewards. Dysfunction of the nucleus accumbens has been implicated in addiction, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, schizophrenia, and other neuropsychiatric conditions. It is also a target for deep brain stimulation in severe treatment-resistant cases of addiction and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

History

The nucleus accumbens was first described in the late 19th century. Its name derives from Latin, meaning "nucleus leaning against" (the septum). Its role in reward was established in the mid-20th century through intracranial self-stimulation experiments by James Olds and Peter Milner, in which rats would repeatedly press a lever to stimulate electrodes placed in this and related regions.
