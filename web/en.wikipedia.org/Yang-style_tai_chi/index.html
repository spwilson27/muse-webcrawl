Yang-style tai chi (Chinese: 楊氏太極拳; pinyin: Yángshì tàijíquán) is one of the five major styles of tai chi, and the most widely practised style in the world today. It is named after the Yang family, principally its founder Yang Luchan (1799–1872), who learned the art in Chenjiagou from Chen Changxing of the Chen style.

History

Yang Luchan taught tai chi in Beijing, where he was nicknamed "Yang the Invincible" (Yang Wudi) for his martial skill. His sons Yang Banhou and Yang Jianhou carried on the teaching, and his grandson Yang Chengfu (1883–1936) standardised the long solo form into the large-frame, flowing version most commonly practised today. Yang Chengfu's ten essential points of tai chi remain a foundational teaching text of the style.

Characteristics

Yang style is characterised by large, open, graceful movements performed at a slow, even pace, in contrast to the alternating fast and slow, explosive (fa jin) movements retained in Chen style. Postures are upright and natural, steps are wide and steady, and the energy is expressed evenly throughout the form. The standard bare-hand form taught by Yang Chengfu's lineage contains 85 or 108 postures depending on how movements are counted.

Forms

The traditional curriculum includes the long bare-hand form, the Yang-style sword (jian) form, sabre (dao) form, spear and staff forms, and pushing hands (tui shou) practice. A simplified 24-posture form created in 1956 by the Chinese Sports Committee is drawn primarily from Yang style and is the most widely taught tai chi routine in the world.
