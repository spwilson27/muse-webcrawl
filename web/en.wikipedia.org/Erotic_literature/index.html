Erotic literature

Works of erotic literature range from ancient poetry to the modern erotic novel; many were banned for long periods.

Erotic literature comprises fictional and factual stories and accounts intended to sexually arouse the reader. It includes erotic novels, short stories, poetry, drama and memoirs. Erotic literature has a long history in many cultures, and has frequently been subject to censorship, obscenity prosecution and moral campaigning, while particular works have also been recognised as serious contributions to literature.

History

Ancient examples include the love poetry of Sappho, Ovid's Ars Amatoria, the Sanskrit Kama Sutra (part erotic manual, part treatise), and erotic episodes in Petronius and Apuleius. Medieval and early modern Europe produced the fabliaux, Boccaccio's Decameron, and Aretino's works. The invention of printing made possible works such as the anonymous Fanny Hill (1748) by John Cleland, a landmark of the English erotic novel that remained legally contested into the 20th century. The 18th and 19th centuries added the writings of the Marquis de Sade and clandestine Victorian erotica such as My Secret Life.

Twentieth century and later

D. H. Lawrence's Lady Chatterley's Lover (1928), Henry Miller's Tropic of Cancer (1934) and Anaïs Nin's Delta of Venus were central to obscenity trials that liberalised publishing law in the United States, Britain and elsewhere. Pauline Réage's Story of O (1954) and the works of the Marquis de Sade influenced later literary erotica, and Anne Rice published erotic novels under the pen name A. N. Roquelaure. In the 21st century, commercial romance and erotica expanded greatly with e-books and self-publishing; E. L. James's Fifty Shades of Grey (2011) became a global bestseller. Scholars distinguish erotica (literarily ambitious depictions intended to arouse) from pornography, though the boundary is disputed.
