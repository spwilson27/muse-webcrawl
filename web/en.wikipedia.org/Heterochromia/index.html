Heterochromia

Heterochromia is a difference in coloration, usually of the iris but also of hair or skin. Heterochromia is determined by the production, delivery, and concentration of melanin. It may be inherited, or caused by genetic mosaicism, chimerism, disease, or injury. It occurs in humans and certain breeds of domesticated animals, and is especially familiar in cats with one blue eye and one eye of another color.

Classification

Complete heterochromia

In complete heterochromia, one iris is a different color from the other — for example, one blue eye and one brown eye. It is common in white or partly white cats (the "odd-eyed cat") and in dog breeds such as the Siberian Husky and Australian Shepherd.

Sectoral heterochromia

In sectoral (partial) heterochromia, part of one iris is a different color from the rest of the same iris, giving a wedge- or segment-like appearance.

Central heterochromia

In central heterochromia, the iris has a ring of one color around the pupil differing from the outer portion of the iris.

Causes

Congenital heterochromia is usually a benign genetic trait, sometimes associated with syndromes such as Waardenburg syndrome, which can also involve sensorineural hearing loss and pigmentary changes of the hair and skin. Acquired heterochromia may result from injury, inflammation (such as Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis), tumors, or certain medications including some glaucoma eye drops; a change in eye color later in life therefore merits medical evaluation.

In animals

Beyond cats and dogs, heterochromia occurs in horses, cattle, and water buffalo, frequently in animals with white or pinto coat patterns, reflecting the shared developmental origin of pigment cells in the skin and iris.
