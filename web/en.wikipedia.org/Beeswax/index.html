Beeswax (Latin: cera alba) is a natural wax produced by honey bees of the genus Apis. Worker bees secrete wax from glands on their abdomens and use it to build the honeycomb in which honey and pollen are stored and young are raised. Chemically, beeswax consists mainly of esters of fatty acids and long-chain alcohols.

Production

Young worker bees between about 12 and 20 days old produce wax scales, which are chewed and moulded into comb. Producing one kilogram of wax requires bees to consume several kilograms of honey. Beekeepers harvest surplus wax when extracting honey, melt and filter it, and sell it as blocks.

Uses

Beeswax has been used since prehistory in candles, cosmetics, pharmaceutical ointments, food coatings and polishes. It is the traditional material for high-quality church candles because it burns cleanly with a pleasant scent. In food it is used as a glazing agent (E901) and in lip balms, lotions and encaustic painting.

History

Archaeological evidence of beeswax use dates back thousands of years; it was used in ancient Egypt for embalming and shipbuilding, by the Romans in lost-wax casting, and in medieval Europe as a valuable trade good and form of payment.
