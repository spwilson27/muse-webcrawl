Strabismus

Strabismus is a vision disorder in which the eyes do not properly align with each other when looking at an object. The eye that is focused on an object can alternate, and the condition may be present occasionally or constantly. If present during a large part of childhood, it may result in amblyopia, or lazy eye, and loss of depth perception.

Types

Strabismus is classified by the direction of the misalignment: esotropia (inward turning), exotropia (outward turning), hypertropia (upward), and hypotropia (downward). It may be comitant, where the angle of deviation is the same in all directions of gaze, or incomitant, where it varies, often due to dysfunction of the extraocular muscles or their nerves.

Causes and diagnosis

Causes include refractive errors, problems with the muscles or nerves controlling the eyes, cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, and family history. Diagnosis is made by observing corneal light reflexes and cover tests during an eye examination. Strabismus affects roughly 2–4% of children.

Treatment

Treatment aims to realign the eyes and preserve binocular vision. Options include glasses (sometimes with prisms), vision therapy, patching of the stronger eye to treat amblyopia in children, botulinum toxin injections, and strabismus surgery to adjust the length or position of the extraocular muscles. Early treatment in childhood gives the best chance of developing normal depth perception.
