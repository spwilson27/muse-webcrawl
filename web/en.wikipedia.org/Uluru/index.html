Uluru, also known as Ayers Rock, is a large sandstone inselberg in the southern part of the Northern Territory, Australia. It is located within the Uluṙu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park, about 335 kilometres southwest of Alice Springs. Uluru is sacred in Tjukurpa, the law and moral system of the local Aṉangu Aboriginal people, and is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Geology

Uluru is composed of arkose, a coarse-grained sandstone rich in feldspar, deposited about 550 million years ago and later tilted to an 85-degree angle by tectonic movements. The rock appears to change colour at different times of day, glowing red at dawn and sunset, owing to the iron oxide coating of its surface. It is 3.6 km long and rises 348 m above the plain, with most of its bulk lying underground.

History and tourism

The first European to sight Uluru was surveyor William Gosse in 1873, who named it Ayers Rock after Sir Henry Ayers, then Chief Secretary of South Australia. In 1985 the title to the land was handed back to the Aṉangu, who lease it to Parks Australia. Out of respect for Aṉangu wishes — and because of safety and environmental concerns — climbing Uluru was permanently closed on 26 October 2019, the 34th anniversary of the handback. The park attracts several hundred thousand visitors per year, who walk the 10.6 km base walk passing waterholes, caves and rock art.
