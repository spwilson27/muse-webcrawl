Activity (chemistry)

In chemical thermodynamics, activity (symbol a) is a dimensionless measure of the "effective concentration" of a species in a mixture. It corrects the ideal concentration for non-ideal behaviour caused by interactions between molecules or ions, so that equilibrium laws written for ideal systems remain valid. The concept was introduced by G. N. Lewis in 1907.

Definition

The activity of a species is defined through its chemical potential: μ = μ° + RT ln a, where μ° is the chemical potential in a chosen standard state. For a solute, activity is written as a = γ · (c/c°), where γ is the activity coefficient, c the concentration, and c° the standard concentration (usually 1 mol/L or 1 mol/kg). For an ideal solution γ = 1 and activity equals the (relative) concentration; for real systems γ deviates from unity.

Activity coefficients

In electrolyte solutions, electrostatic interactions make activity coefficients fall well below 1 at moderate concentrations. The Debye–Hückel theory (and its limiting law) predicts these deviations at low ionic strength, while empirical models such as the Davies equation and Pitzer equations are used for concentrated solutions. For gases, the analogous correction is fugacity; for pure solids and liquids, activity is taken as 1 under standard conditions.

Applications

Equilibrium constants expressed in activities are true constants, independent of composition: for example, pH is defined as the negative logarithm of the hydrogen-ion activity, not its concentration. Activities are essential in geochemistry, oceanography, electrochemistry, and the prediction of solubilities and reaction equilibria in real (non-ideal) systems.
