Propensity score matching

Matching balances treated and control groups on observed covariates.

In the statistical analysis of observational data, propensity score matching (PSM) is a technique that attempts to estimate the effect of a treatment by accounting for the covariates that predict receiving the treatment. The propensity score, introduced by Paul Rosenbaum and Donald Rubin in 1983, is the conditional probability of assignment to treatment given observed covariates, usually estimated with logistic regression.

Method

Treated and untreated units with similar propensity scores are matched (for example by nearest-neighbour matching, with or without a caliper), and the outcomes of the matched groups are compared. Conditioning on the scalar score balances the distribution of all observed covariates used in the model, mimicking some features of a randomized experiment.

Assumptions and limitations

PSM relies on strong ignorability: treatment assignment must be independent of potential outcomes given the observed covariates, and every unit must have a positive probability of treatment (common support). Unlike randomization, matching cannot balance unobserved confounders, so results are sensitive to hidden bias; sensitivity analyses are often used to assess robustness.
