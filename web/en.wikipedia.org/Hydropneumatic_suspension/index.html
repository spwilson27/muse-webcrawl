Hydropneumatic suspension

Hydropneumatic suspension is a type of motor vehicle suspension system, designed by Paul Magès, invented by Citroën, and fitted to Citroën cars, as well as being used under licence by other car manufacturers, notably Rolls-Royce, Maserati and Peugeot. Similar systems are also widely used on modern tanks and other large military vehicles. The suspension was introduced on the rear axle of the 1954 Citroën Traction Avant and in full form on the revolutionary 1955 Citroën DS.

The system uses a belt-driven pump from the engine to pressurise a hydraulic accumulator, and uses a combination of hydraulic fluid and compressed nitrogen in spheres to provide both springing and damping. Because the gas is compressible while the oil is not, the characteristics of a conventional steel spring and damper are combined in a compact unit. The system also provides self-levelling: the ride height of the vehicle is maintained constant regardless of load by height correctors that add or release fluid.

Advantages

Hydropneumatic suspension offers a very soft, comfortable ride with excellent road holding, because the wheels can follow road undulations closely. The constant ride height keeps the geometry of the suspension and the headlamp aim consistent, and the system absorbs load changes that would make a conventional car sag. On the Citroën DS, CX, GS, SM, BX, Xantia and XM the system also powered the brakes and steering.

Working principle

Each wheel is connected to a suspension sphere containing nitrogen separated from the hydraulic fluid by a flexible diaphragm. As the wheel rises, fluid is displaced into the sphere, compressing the gas. A central accumulator and pump maintain system pressure, and height corrector valves sense the position of the anti-roll bar to restore the set ride height.

Later developments included the computer-controlled Hydractive system, which could switch between soft and firm damping states, and Hydractive III with electronically selected ride heights.
