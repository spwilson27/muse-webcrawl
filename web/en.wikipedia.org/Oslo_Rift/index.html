Oslo Rift

The Oslo Rift (or Oslo Graben) is a geological rift system in south-eastern Norway and south-western Sweden, formed in the late Carboniferous and Permian periods, roughly 300–240 million years ago. It extends from the Skagerrak coast near Langesund northward through Oslo to the Mjøsa region, a length of about 200 km. It is the northernmost part of the Rotliegend rift system of north-western Europe.

Geology

Rifting began with basaltic lavas and proceeded through stages of explosive volcanism that produced distinctive rhomb-porphyry lavas found almost nowhere else in such volume, followed by large caldera eruptions and the emplacement of syenite and granite plutons, including the larvikite quarried around Larvik. Subsidence preserved sedimentary rocks—sandstones and limestones—of Carboniferous and Permian age that are otherwise rare in Norway's predominantly Precambrian shield terrain. The rift's faults outline a classic graben structure.

Significance

The rift failed before splitting the continent, making it an aulacogen. Its rocks underlie much of the Oslo region, influence the course of the Oslofjord, and host deposits of contact-metamorphosed minerals prized by collectors. The Permian igneous province is associated with the broader magmatism preceding the breakup events that eventually formed the North Sea basin.
