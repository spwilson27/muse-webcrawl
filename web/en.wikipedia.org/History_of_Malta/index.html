History of Malta

The history of Malta spans several millennia. The islands of Malta and Gozo, lying in the central Mediterranean between Sicily and North Africa, have been inhabited since approximately 5900 BC, when farmers from Sicily arrived during the Neolithic period. The islands are famous for some of the oldest free-standing structures in the world, the megalithic temples built by a temple-building culture between about 3600 and 2500 BC.

Prehistory

Archaeological evidence, including pottery similar to that of Stentinello culture in Sicily, indicates settlement by around 5900 BC. The most elaborate phase of prehistory was the Temple Period (c. 3600–2500 BC), when complexes such as Ġgantija on Gozo and Ħaġar Qim, Mnajdra and Tarxien on Malta were erected. The Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum, an underground necropolis, dates from roughly the same era. The temple culture ended abruptly around 2500 BC for reasons that remain uncertain.

Phoenicians, Carthaginians and Romans

Phoenician traders colonised the islands from about 800 BC, using them as an outpost on the route between the Levant and the western Mediterranean. Malta later came under Carthaginian control. During the Second Punic War the islands were captured by Rome in 218 BC, and Malta flourished as a municipium known for its textiles and honey. According to tradition, the Apostle Paul was shipwrecked on Malta around AD 60, an event commemorated in the Acts of the Apostles.

Byzantine and Arab periods

After the division of the Roman Empire, Malta was governed from Constantinople. In 870 the islands were conquered by Aghlabid Arabs from North Africa; the siege of the Byzantine capital Melite is said to have been followed by a near-depopulation, and Malta was resettled by Muslims from Sicily around the year 1048–49, when Mdina was founded on the site of Melite. Arabic influence profoundly shaped the Maltese language, which descends from Siculo-Arabic.

Norman and medieval rule

The Norman Count Roger I of Sicily attacked Malta in 1091, and the islands were fully integrated into the Kingdom of Sicily by the early 12th century. Malta was subsequently ruled as a Sicilian dependency under Swabian, Angevin and Aragonese dynasties. In 1283 the Battle of Malta, fought in Grand Harbour during the War of the Sicilian Vespers, was a notable Aragonese naval victory. By the late Middle Ages Malta was a fief granted to various nobles.

The Knights of St John

In 1530 Emperor Charles V granted Malta and Tripoli in perpetual fief to the Order of St John (the Knights Hospitaller), who had been expelled from Rhodes by the Ottomans. The Order made the islands their home for 268 years. In 1565 the Knights, under Grand Master Jean Parisot de Valette, withstood the Great Siege of Malta by a much larger Ottoman force — one of the defining events of the islands' history. The fortified capital Valletta was founded in 1566 and named after Valette. The Knights transformed Malta into a baroque stronghold and naval base until their rule ended in 1798.

French interlude and British rule

En route to Egypt, Napoleon Bonaparte captured Malta in June 1798 and expelled the Order. French rule was unpopular, and the Maltese rose in revolt; with British assistance the French garrison surrendered in 1800. Malta formally became a British Crown Colony in 1813. The islands' harbours became vital to the Royal Navy, especially after the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, and Malta served as headquarters of the Mediterranean Fleet.

World War II

During the Second World War Malta, lying close to Axis shipping lanes to North Africa, endured a prolonged siege from 1940 to 1942, suffering some of the heaviest bombing of the war. On 15 April 1942 King George VI awarded the George Cross to the island — the first collective award of the decoration — "to bear witness to a heroism and devotion that will long be famous in history". The George Cross remains on Malta's flag.

Independence

Malta became independent from the United Kingdom on 21 September 1964, with Elizabeth II as Queen of Malta. It became a republic on 13 December 1974, with Sir Anthony Mamo as its first president. Malta joined the European Union in 2004 and adopted the euro in 2008.
