Quasimodo is the fictional protagonist of Victor Hugo's 1831 novel The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (Notre-Dame de Paris). The deaf, hunchbacked bell-ringer of Notre-Dame Cathedral in fifteenth-century Paris, he is one of the most famous characters in French literature and a symbol of the outcast redeemed by inner devotion.

In the novel

Abandoned as a deformed foundling on the cathedral steps on Quasimodo Sunday — the first Sunday after Easter, from the Latin introit Quasi modo geniti infantes ("as newborn babes") — the child is adopted by the archdeacon Claude Frollo, who names him for the day. Years of ringing the great bells leave Quasimodo deaf; shunned by the Parisians for his appearance, he lives in near-solitary communion with the cathedral and its bells, which Hugo presents as his only companions and language.

Quasimodo's devotion to Frollo is tested when both men become fixated on the Romani dancer Esmeralda — the only person who shows him kindness, offering him water while he is being flogged in the pillory. After Esmeralda is executed, Quasimodo kills Frollo in despair and disappears; his skeleton is later found in the charnel house of Montfaucon, clasped around Esmeralda's corpse, crumbling to dust when separated from it.

Interpretation and adaptations

Hugo contrasts Quasimodo's grotesque exterior with a capacity for love and loyalty, and Frollo's respectable exterior with inner corruption — a reversal central to the novel's critique of appearances. The character has been portrayed in numerous adaptations, notably by Lon Chaney (1923) and Charles Laughton (1939) on film, and in Disney's 1996 animated version, in which he is the sympathetic hero.
