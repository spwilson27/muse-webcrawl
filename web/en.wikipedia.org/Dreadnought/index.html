Dreadnought

The dreadnought was the predominant type of battleship in the early 20th century. The first of the kind, the Royal Navy's HMS Dreadnought, launched in 1906, had such an impact that earlier battleships became known as "pre-dreadnoughts". Her design had two revolutionary features: an "all-big-gun" armament of ten 12-inch guns, and propulsion by steam turbines, making her faster than any existing battleship.

Origins

Naval theorists including Vittorio Cuniberti had argued that long-range gunnery, controlled by salvo fire, favoured a uniform main battery over the mixed calibres of earlier ships. Under First Sea Lord Admiral Sir John "Jacky" Fisher, a committee designed the ship in 1905; she was built at Portsmouth in just over a year.

Arms race

Dreadnought rendered existing fleets obsolete and triggered a naval arms race, above all between Britain and Imperial Germany, whose naval laws under Admiral Tirpitz funded a rival dreadnought fleet. Successive classes grew into "super-dreadnoughts" with 13.5-inch and 15-inch guns. Dreadnought fleets fought once at scale, at the Battle of Jutland (1916). The Washington Naval Treaty of 1922 halted construction and scrapped many ships; survivors served into the Second World War.
