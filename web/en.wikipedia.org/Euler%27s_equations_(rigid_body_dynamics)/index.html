Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)

In classical mechanics, Euler's equations describe the rotation of a rigid body using a rotating reference frame whose axes are fixed in the body and aligned with the body's principal axes of inertia. They were derived by Leonhard Euler from Newton's second law of motion.

Equations

For torques τ1, τ2, τ3, angular velocities ω1, ω2, ω3 and principal moments of inertia I1, I2, I3:

I1ω̇1 + (I3 − I2)ω2ω3 = τ1, and cyclically for the other two components.

Torque-free motion

With zero applied torque the equations yield celebrated qualitative results: a body spins stably about its axes of greatest and least inertia, but rotation about the intermediate axis is unstable — the "tennis racket theorem" or Dzhanibekov effect, dramatically demonstrated with a tumbling wing nut aboard a space station. For symmetric bodies the equations also describe free precession (nutation) of the rotation axis about the symmetry axis.
