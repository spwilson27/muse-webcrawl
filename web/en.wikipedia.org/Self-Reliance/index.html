Self-Reliance

Ralph Waldo Emerson's 1841 essay "Self-Reliance" is a foundational text of American individualism and Transcendentalism.

"Self-Reliance" is an essay written by American essayist and philosopher Ralph Waldo Emerson. First published in his collection Essays: First Series in 1841, it is the fullest statement of his doctrine of individualism: the individual should trust his or her own intuition and judgment rather than conform to the expectations of society, tradition or institutions. It is among the most influential works of Transcendentalism and of American thought generally.

Themes

The essay opens with the injunctions "Trust thyself" and contains the famous lines "Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist" and "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines." Emerson argues that genius consists in believing that what is true in one's own heart is true for all, that society conspires against individuality by rewarding imitation and consistency, and that envy and conformity waste human potential. He criticises reliance on property, charity given without thought, and foreign travel undertaken to escape oneself, while praising self-trust, spontaneity and the sanctity of one's own perception.

Composition and sources

Emerson developed the essay from lectures and journal entries written through the 1830s, drawing on his break with organised religion (announced in his Divinity School Address of 1838), on classical and Oriental philosophy, and on his reading of Montaigne, Plutarch and Swedenborg. The motto prefixed to the essay — "Ne te quaesiveris extra" ("Do not seek yourself outside yourself") — is adapted from Persius.

Influence

"Self-Reliance" shaped later American writers including Walt Whitman, Henry David Thoreau and Emily Dickinson, and its ideas about nonconformity and intuition recur throughout American culture, from nineteenth-century reform movements to modern motivational literature. Nietzsche admired Emerson's essays and carried them with him; the essay is frequently assigned in American schools and quoted in popular culture.
