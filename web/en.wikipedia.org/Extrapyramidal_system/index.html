Extrapyramidal system

In anatomy, the extrapyramidal system is a part of the motor system of the nervous system involved in the coordination of movement. The term distinguishes the indirect motor pathways — centred on the basal ganglia and brainstem nuclei such as the red nucleus, substantia nigra, reticular formation, and vestibular nuclei — from the direct pyramidal (corticospinal and corticobulbar) tracts that pass through the medullary pyramids. The extrapyramidal system regulates posture, muscle tone, and automatic, involuntary movements, and modulates the activity of the pyramidal system through its influence on the motor cortex and spinal interneurons.

Components and pathways

The principal extrapyramidal circuits loop between the cerebral cortex, basal ganglia (striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra), and thalamus, and descend to the spinal cord via the rubrospinal, reticulospinal, vestibulospinal, and tectospinal tracts. Dopaminergic projections from the substantia nigra pars compacta to the striatum are central to normal function.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the extrapyramidal system produce movement abnormalities without paralysis: Parkinson's disease (from loss of dopaminergic neurons), Huntington's disease, dystonia, chorea, athetosis, and hemiballismus. Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) are also well-known side effects of dopamine-receptor-blocking antipsychotic drugs, and include acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism, and the potentially irreversible tardive dyskinesia. Modern neurology increasingly treats the pyramidal/extrapyramidal distinction as a heuristic, since the systems interact extensively, but the terminology remains embedded in clinical practice.
