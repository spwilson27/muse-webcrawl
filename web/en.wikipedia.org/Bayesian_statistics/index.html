Bayesian statistics

Bayesian statistics is a theory in the field of statistics based on the Bayesian interpretation of probability, where probability expresses a degree of belief in an event. The degree of belief may be based on prior knowledge about the event, such as the results of previous experiments, or on personal beliefs. This differs from the frequentist interpretation, which views probability as the long-run frequency of events.

Method

Bayesian methods use Bayes theorem to update beliefs as data arrive: a prior distribution over unknown parameters is combined with the likelihood of the observed data to give a posterior distribution, from which estimates, credible intervals and predictions are derived. Model checking and prior sensitivity analysis are integral parts of the workflow.

Computation

For most realistic models the posterior cannot be computed analytically. Modern Bayesian practice was transformed from the 1990s by Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods — such as Gibbs sampling, the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo — and by software including BUGS, JAGS, Stan and PyMC.

History

Named for Thomas Bayes and developed by Laplace, Bayesian statistics was eclipsed in the early 20th century by frequentist methods associated with Fisher, Neyman and Pearson. Work by Jeffreys, de Finetti, Savage and others kept the Bayesian programme alive until cheap computation made it broadly practical; it is now widely used in machine learning, epidemiology, ecology, clinical trials and many other fields.
