Peyer's patch

Peyer's patches are organized lymphoid follicles found in the wall of the small intestine, principally in the distal ileum. They are an important part of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) and play a central role in immune surveillance of the intestinal lumen.

Structure

Each patch consists of an aggregation of lymphoid follicles beneath a specialized follicle-associated epithelium containing M cells (microfold cells). M cells sample antigens and microorganisms from the gut lumen and deliver them to underlying immune cells, including dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells, and T cells.

Function

Peyer's patches initiate mucosal immune responses, notably the production of IgA antibodies. They are also a known portal of entry for certain pathogens, including Salmonella typhi, poliovirus, and prions, which exploit the M-cell transport pathway.

History

The patches are named after the Swiss anatomist Johann Conrad Peyer, who described them in 1677.
