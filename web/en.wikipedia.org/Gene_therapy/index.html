Gene therapy

Gene therapy is a medical treatment that modifies a person's genes to treat or cure disease. It works by replacing a disease-causing gene with a healthy copy, inactivating a malfunctioning gene, or introducing a new or modified gene into the body. Gene therapies may be delivered in vivo, using vectors administered directly to the patient, or ex vivo, by modifying cells removed from the body and returning them.

Approaches and vectors

Most gene therapies use engineered viruses — adeno-associated viruses (AAV), adenoviruses, lentiviruses and retroviruses — stripped of their ability to cause disease and loaded with therapeutic DNA. Non-viral methods include lipid nanoparticles and direct injection of nucleic acids. Since the 2010s, gene editing tools such as CRISPR–Cas9 have enabled therapies that correct mutations in place rather than merely adding a gene.

History

The first approved gene therapy trial took place in 1990, treating a child with ADA-SCID ("bubble boy disease"). The field suffered a major setback with the death of Jesse Gelsinger in a 1999 trial, which prompted tighter oversight. Milestones since include Glybera (2012, the first approval in Europe), Luxturna for inherited blindness (2017), Zolgensma for spinal muscular atrophy (2019), and Casgevy (2023), the first CRISPR-based therapy, approved for sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia. CAR-T cell therapies apply the same ex vivo principles to cancer.

Risks and regulation

Risks include immune reactions to vectors, insertional mutagenesis (a cause of leukaemias in some early trials), and off-target editing. Regulators such as the FDA and EMA review gene therapies as biological medicines; many approved products are among the most expensive drugs in the world, with list prices exceeding US$2 million per patient, reflecting one-time administration and small patient populations.
