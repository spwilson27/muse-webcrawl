Polio vaccine

TypesIPV (injected), OPV (oral)
DevelopersJonas Salk (IPV); Albert Sabin (OPV)
First licensed1955 (Salk)
TargetPoliovirus types 1, 2, 3

Polio vaccines are vaccines used to prevent poliomyelitis (polio). Two types are used: an inactivated poliovirus given by injection (IPV), developed by Jonas Salk and first used in 1955, and a weakened poliovirus given by mouth (OPV), developed by Albert Sabin and first used in 1961. They are on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

History

Salk's injected vaccine was tested in the massive 1954 Francis field trial involving over a million children and declared safe and effective on April 12, 1955. Sabin's oral vaccine, using attenuated virus strains, was easier to administer and produced stronger intestinal immunity, and became the mainstay of global immunization campaigns. The Cutter incident of 1955, in which improperly inactivated vaccine caused polio cases, led to stricter vaccine safety regulation.

Eradication efforts

The Global Polio Eradication Initiative, launched in 1988 by the WHO, UNICEF, Rotary International, and the CDC, reduced annual cases from an estimated 350,000 to a handful. Wild poliovirus type 2 was declared eradicated in 2015 and type 3 in 2019. Wild type 1 transmission persists only in Afghanistan and Pakistan, while circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus — a rare consequence of the oral vaccine — is addressed by switching to IPV and newer, more stable OPV strains.

How the vaccines work

IPV contains formalin-inactivated virus of all three serotypes and induces serum antibodies that prevent the virus from reaching the nervous system. OPV contains live attenuated virus that replicates in the gut, inducing mucosal immunity and interrupting transmission, and can secondarily immunize contacts.
