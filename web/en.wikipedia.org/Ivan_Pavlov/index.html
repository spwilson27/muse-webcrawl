Ivan Petrovich Pavlov (Russian: Иван Петрович Павлов; 26 September 1849 – 27 February 1936) was a Russian and Soviet physiologist famous for his discovery of classical conditioning. His research on the digestive system won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1904, and his later studies of conditioned reflexes in dogs profoundly influenced psychology and neuroscience.

Life and work

Pavlov, the son of a village priest, trained in science and medicine at St. Petersburg, studied in Germany under Carl Ludwig and Rudolf Heidenhain, and from 1890 directed physiology at the Institute of Experimental Medicine. Working with chronic fistulas that allowed him to observe digestion in conscious animals, he demonstrated the nervous (cephalic) phase of gastric secretion — "sham feeding" dogs secreted gastric juice though food never reached the stomach — founding modern physiology of digestion.

Conditioned reflexes

Pursuing the "psychic secretions" he observed, Pavlov systematically studied how a neutral stimulus (a bell or metronome) paired with food comes to elicit salivation by itself. His laboratory mapped acquisition, extinction, spontaneous recovery, generalisation and discrimination, and he built a theory of cortical excitation and inhibition intended to explain temperament, sleep and neurosis. The English-language fame of his work grew from the 1927 translation of Conditioned Reflexes.

Legacy

Pavlov remained a sharp critic of the Soviet regime even as it lavishly funded his laboratories; he died in Leningrad in 1936. "Pavlov's dog" became a universal metaphor, and his methods and concepts underlie behaviourism, learning theory and modern research on associative learning.
