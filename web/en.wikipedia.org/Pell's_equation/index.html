Pell's equation

Pell's equation, also called the Pell–Fermat equation, is any Diophantine equation of the form

x² − n y² = 1

where n is a given positive nonsquare integer, and integer solutions are sought for x and y. The equation is named after the 17th-century English mathematician John Pell, though he had little to do with it; the name results from a mistaken attribution by Leonhard Euler. The equation was studied much earlier by Indian mathematicians such as Brahmagupta and Bhāskara II, and in connection with Archimedes' cattle problem.

Solutions

Besides the trivial solution x = 1, y = 0, Pell's equation always has infinitely many integer solutions whenever n is a positive nonsquare. All solutions can be generated from the fundamental solution—the one with smallest positive values—by raising the quantity x + y√n to successive powers. Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the first rigorous proof that solutions always exist, using the theory of continued fractions: the fundamental solution is obtained from the continued fraction expansion of √n.

Examples

For n = 2, the fundamental solution is x = 3, y = 2, since 3² − 2·2² = 9 − 8 = 1. Further solutions include (17, 12), (99, 70) and (577, 408), generated by successive powers of 3 + 2√2.

For some values of n the fundamental solution is very large: for n = 61, Bhāskara II found x = 1,766,319,049 and y = 226,153,980, a result Fermat later used as a challenge problem in 1657.

History

Brahmagupta (7th century) developed methods for solving the equation, and Bhāskara II (12th century) gave the cyclic cakravāla method. In Europe, Fermat challenged mathematicians to solve it in 1657; Brouncker and Wallis found methods, and Lagrange completed the theory in the 18th century. Euler mistakenly credited Pell, and the name stuck.
