Four temperaments

The four temperaments are a proto-psychological theory which suggests that there are four fundamental personality types: sanguine, choleric, melancholic, and phlegmatic. Most formulations include the possibility of mixtures among the types where an individual's personality types overlap and they share two or more temperaments.

The theory derives from the ancient medical concept of humourism, systematised by Hippocrates and developed by Galen, in which the balance of four bodily fluids — blood, yellow bile, black bile, and phlegm — was held to determine health and disposition.

History and influence

Galen's account in De temperamentis tied the temperaments to the "qualities" of hot, cold, wet, and dry. The scheme dominated Western medicine for over a thousand years and passed into literature and art: Shakespeare's and Chaucer's characters are frequently typed by humour. Immanuel Kant gave a purely psychological account of the four temperaments in 1798, and the classification continued to influence later personality theorists, including Hans Eysenck, who mapped the temperaments onto his dimensions of extraversion and neuroticism.

Modern scientific psychology does not regard the four temperaments as a valid model of personality, having superseded it with trait models such as the Big Five, but the terms survive in everyday usage and in some popular personality tests.
