Wang Hui (Chinese: 王翬; 1632–1717) was a Chinese landscape painter of the early Qing dynasty and the most famous of the "Four Wangs", the orthodox masters who dominated officially sanctioned painting in the late 17th and early 18th centuries. A child prodigy from Changshu, he studied under Wang Jian and Wang Shimin, the other senior Wangs, and became renowned for his ability to synthesize the styles of many ancient masters into monumental landscape compositions.

The Southern Inspection Tour

In 1691 the Kangxi Emperor commissioned Wang Hui to direct the creation of the Southern Inspection Tour scrolls, a series of twelve enormous handscrolls documenting the emperor's 1689 tour of southern China. The project, completed with teams of court assistants, employed traditional ink and colour techniques on an unprecedented narrative scale and cemented Wang's standing at court. The emperor inscribed praise on his work, calling his brushwork unsurpassed.

Style and legacy

Wang Hui's mature landscapes combine meticulous brushwork with grand, integrated compositions, reworking the manners of Song and Yuan masters such as Zhao Mengfu and Huang Gongwang. Though later critics of the individualist school sometimes faulted the orthodox masters as derivative, Wang Hui's technical command and his codification of the orthodox landscape tradition shaped Chinese painting for generations.
