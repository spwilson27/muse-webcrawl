Digital rights

Digital rights are those human rights and legal rights that allow individuals to access, use, create and publish digital media or to access and use computers, other electronic devices, and telecommunications networks. The concept is particularly related to the protection and realisation of existing rights, such as the right to privacy and freedom of expression, in the context of digital technologies, especially the Internet. The laws of several countries recognise a right to Internet access.

History

A number of human rights groups and technology organisations have articulated principles for digital rights. The Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF), founded in 1990, became a leading advocate in litigation and policy over online civil liberties. In 1996 John Perry Barlow published "A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace", a libertarian manifesto asserting the autonomy of the online world from government control. The Association for Progressive Communications developed an Internet Rights Charter in the early 2000s. In 2012 the United Nations Human Rights Council affirmed that the same rights people have offline must also be protected online, and subsequent UN resolutions have addressed privacy in the digital age.

Key areas

  • Access — universal, affordable access to the Internet and digital devices, and net neutrality in the treatment of traffic.
  • Privacy — protection of personal data against surveillance and misuse, reflected in regimes such as the EU General Data Protection Regulation.
  • Freedom of expression — protection of online speech, and debate over platform content moderation.
  • Consumer and ownership rights — the rights of purchasers of digital goods, affected by licensing terms and digital rights management (DRM), including the right to repair devices.
  • Intellectual property balance — exceptions such as fair use, open access, and free and open-source software licensing.
  • Anonymity and encryption — the ability to communicate privately and anonymously.

Relation to DRM terminology

The phrase "digital rights" is distinct from "digital rights management", a set of access-control technologies that restrict the use of proprietary hardware and copyrighted works. Critics within the digital rights movement sometimes expand DRM as "digital restrictions management". Landmark disputes include anti-circumvention provisions of the US Digital Millennium Copyright Act (1998), debates over e-book lending and resale, and "right to repair" legislation in the EU and US states.

Organisations

Notable digital rights organisations include the Electronic Frontier Foundation, Access Now, Article 19, Privacy International, the Open Rights Group, La Quadrature du Net and Bits of Freedom, alongside free-software organisations such as the Free Software Foundation.
