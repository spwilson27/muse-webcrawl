Scientific nameMonodon monoceros

The narwhal (Monodon monoceros) is a species of toothed whale native to the Arctic. It is the only member of the genus Monodon and one of two living species in the whale family Monodontidae, along with the beluga whale. It is known for its large "tusk" — a protruding upper left canine tooth that grows into a long, spiralled horn-like appendage, found primarily in males.

Description

Narwhals are medium-sized whales, about the size of belugas, with a mottled pigmentation that lightens with age. Adults lack a dorsal fin, an adaptation thought to help them swim under ice. The tusk is actually an elongated left canine, unique among teeth in its left-handed helical spiral; it is richly innervated and is now understood to function as a sensory organ as well as a secondary sexual characteristic used in dominance displays. In rare cases two tusks occur, and females occasionally grow a smaller tusk.

Behaviour and diet

Narwhals winter in the dense pack ice of Baffin Bay and Davis Strait, making deep dives of up to 1,500 m (occasionally deeper) to feed on Greenland halibut, shrimp and squid. In summer they move into ice-free coastal fjords. Predators include killer whales, polar bears and Greenland sharks; they are also hunted by Inuit communities, for whom the skin (muktuk) is a traditional source of vitamin C.

Culture and trade

In medieval Europe, narwhal tusks were traded as "unicorn horns" (alicorn), prized as antidotes to poison and as symbols of power; tusks were made into ceremonial cups and staffs and appear in royal treasuries. International trade in narwhal ivory is now regulated under CITES.
