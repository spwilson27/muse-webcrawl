Iconoclasm

Iconoclasm
[Image: A defaced Byzantine icon illustrating iconoclast destruction]
DefinitionDeliberate destruction of religious icons and images
Major episodesByzantine Iconoclasm (8th–9th c.); Protestant Reformation (16th c.)

Iconoclasm is the social belief in the importance of destroying icons — religious images — and other images or monuments, usually for religious or political reasons. People who engage in or support iconoclasm are called iconoclasts, a term also applied metaphorically to anyone who challenges cherished beliefs or institutions. Opponents of iconoclasm, who venerate images, are called iconodules or iconophiles.

Byzantine Iconoclasm

The most famous episode is Byzantine Iconoclasm. Beginning under Emperor Leo III, who ordered the removal of icons around 726–730, and intensified under Constantine V, the Byzantine state persecuted the veneration of religious images for over a century. A first period ended in 787 when the Second Council of Nicaea restored icon veneration under Empress Irene; a second period began in 814 and lasted until 843, when Empress Theodora definitively restored the icons — an event still celebrated in Orthodoxy as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Defenders of icons such as John of Damascus and Theodore the Studite argued that because God had become visible flesh in Christ, depicting him was legitimate.

Reformation and other episodes

During the Protestant Reformation, iconoclastic riots swept parts of Europe — most famously the Beeldenstorm of 1566 in the Low Countries — as reformers stripped churches of statues, paintings, and stained glass they regarded as idolatrous. Islam generally prohibits images of God and prophets in religious contexts, and episodes such as the Taliban's destruction of the Buddhas of Bamiyan in 2001 were widely described as iconoclasm. The term is also used for political destructions of monuments, from revolutionary France to the toppling of statues of fallen rulers.
