Kalinga War

The Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE) was fought between the Mauryan Empire under the emperor Ashoka and the state of Kalinga, an independent region on the east coast of present-day Odisha, India. It is remembered as one of the largest and bloodiest battles of ancient India, and as the turning point in Ashoka's life: the carnage of the conquest led him to embrace Buddhism and to rule thereafter by dhamma (righteous conduct) rather than by force.

Background and course

Kalinga controlled rich ports and trade routes along the Bay of Bengal and had once been part of the Nanda Empire before regaining independence. Around 261 BCE, in the eighth year of his reign, Ashoka invaded with the full strength of the Mauryan army. Kalinga resisted fiercely; some accounts place the main fighting near the Daya River, close to the later temple city of Bhubaneswar.

Casualties and aftermath

According to Ashoka's own Major Rock Edict XIII, 100,000 people were killed, 150,000 were deported, and many more died of disease and other causes. The edict records the emperor's remorse: after witnessing the slaughter, Ashoka embraced Buddhism, renounced aggressive conquest (digvijaya) in favour of "conquest by dhamma," and issued edicts promoting tolerance, welfare works and non-violence across his empire. Kalinga was annexed and administered as a Mauryan province from Tosali.
