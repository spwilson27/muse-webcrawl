Degrowth

Degrowth
French termDécroissance
Key thinkersSerge Latouche, Giorgos Kallis, Jason Hickel, Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen
Core ideaPlanned reduction of energy and resource throughput in wealthy economies

Degrowth (French: décroissance) is a political, economic and social movement and body of theory arguing that wealthy societies should deliberately reduce energy and resource consumption in order to operate within ecological limits, while improving human well-being through redistribution, public services and reduced working time.

Origins

The term emerged in France in the early 2000s, drawing on the work of Romanian-American economist Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen, whose 1971 book The Entropy Law and the Economic Process argued that economic activity is bounded by thermodynamics, and on the ecological economics tradition. Serge Latouche popularised décroissance as a "provocative slogan" intended to challenge the centrality of GDP growth.

Arguments

Proponents argue that continued aggregate growth in high-income countries is ecologically unsustainable and has not reliably delivered well-being, that efficiency gains are offset by rebound effects, and that absolute decoupling of GDP from emissions and material use has not occurred at the speed required. Policies proposed include work-sharing, universal basic services, caps on resource use, advertising restrictions and wealth taxes.

Criticism

Critics argue that degrowth would harm living standards, especially in developing countries; that recessions already show the social costs of shrinking output; and that technological innovation and green growth can deliver sufficient decoupling. Degrowth scholars respond that their proposals apply to rich countries and differ from involuntary recession because they are planned and redistributive.
