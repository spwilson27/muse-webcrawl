Heat engine

In thermodynamics and engineering, a heat engine is a system that converts heat to usable energy, particularly mechanical energy, which can then be used to do mechanical work. It does this by bringing a working substance from a higher state temperature to a lower state temperature. A heat source generates thermal energy that brings the working substance to the higher temperature state. The working substance generates work in the working body of the engine while transferring heat to the colder sink until it reaches a lower temperature state.

Efficiency

The efficiency of a heat engine is the ratio of useful work output to heat input. In 1824 Sadi Carnot showed that no engine operating between two reservoirs can exceed the efficiency of a reversible Carnot engine: η = 1 − TC/TH, where temperatures are absolute. This result became a foundation of the second law of thermodynamics. Real engines fall short because of friction, turbulence, and irreversible heat transfer.

Types

Heat engines are classified by their thermodynamic cycle and construction. Examples include steam engines and steam turbines (Rankine cycle), internal combustion engines (Otto and Diesel cycles), gas turbines (Brayton cycle), and Stirling engines. Refrigerators and heat pumps are heat engines run in reverse, using work to move heat from cold to hot.

History

Heat engines powered the Industrial Revolution: Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine (1712) and James Watt's improved steam engine (1776) drove factories, pumps, and locomotives. In the 19th and 20th centuries, internal combustion engines displaced steam in transport, while steam turbines remained dominant in electricity generation.
