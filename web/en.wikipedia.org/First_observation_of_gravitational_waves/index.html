First observation of gravitational waves

First observation of gravitational waves (GW150914)
EventGW150914
Detected14 September 2015, 09:50:45 UTC
InstrumentLIGO Hanford and Livingston interferometers
SourceMerger of two black holes, ~29 and ~36 solar masses
Distance~410 megaparsecs (1.3 billion light-years)
Announced11 February 2016

The first observation of gravitational waves was made on 14 September 2015 and announced by the LIGO and Virgo collaborations on 11 February 2016. The detected signal, designated GW150914, was generated by the inspiral and merger of two black holes, of about 36 and 29 solar masses, into a single black hole of about 62 solar masses, roughly 1.3 billion light-years away.

The observation confirmed a prediction made 100 years earlier by Albert Einstein's general theory of relativity — that accelerating masses radiate ripples in spacetime — and opened an entirely new window on the universe, founding the field of gravitational-wave astronomy. In 2017 the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish, and Kip Thorne for their contributions to the LIGO detector and the observation.

The signal

GW150914 arrived first at the Livingston, Louisiana detector and 6.9 milliseconds later at Hanford, Washington. The signal swept upward in frequency from 35 to 250 Hz over about 0.2 seconds — the characteristic "chirp" of two orbiting black holes spiralling together — reached a peak strain of about 1.0 × 10−21, and matched numerical-relativity templates with a statistical significance exceeding 5.1 sigma. Roughly three solar masses of energy were radiated as gravitational waves, with a peak power exceeding the combined light output of all stars in the observable universe.

Significance

The event was the first direct detection of gravitational waves and the first observation of a binary black hole merger. It demonstrated that stellar-mass black holes of ~30 solar masses exist and can merge within the age of the universe, and it tested general relativity in the strong-field, highly dynamical regime, with results consistent with the theory. Later detections, including GW170817 — the merger of two neutron stars observed also in light across the electromagnetic spectrum — inaugurated multi-messenger astronomy with gravitational waves.

Background

Gravitational waves were predicted by Einstein in 1916. Indirect evidence came from the Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar, whose orbital decay matched the theory and won the 1993 Nobel Prize. LIGO was proposed in the 1980s, funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation, and reached the required sensitivity with its Advanced LIGO upgrade in 2015 — just days before GW150914 arrived during its first observing run.
