Drainage basin

A drainage basin, showing tributaries converging on a main river

A drainage basin (also known as a river basin, catchment or, in North America, a watershed) is an area of land where all flowing surface water converges to a single point, such as a river mouth, or flows into another body of water, such as a lake or ocean. Basins are separated from neighbouring basins by a drainage divide — a ridge of high ground such as a mountain range. A sub-basin or tributary basin feeds a larger river within the main basin.

Characteristics

The size, shape, relief, geology, soils, vegetation and climate of a basin control how precipitation becomes streamflow: how quickly runoff responds to storms, how much sediment and dissolved material the river carries, and how flood waves propagate. Drainage density (total stream length per unit area) and drainage patterns (dendritic, trellis, radial, rectangular) reflect the underlying geology.

Major basins

The world's largest drainage basin is that of the Amazon River (about 7 million km²), followed by the Congo and the Mississippi–Missouri systems. Endorheic basins, such as those of the Caspian Sea, the Tarim Basin and Australia's Lake Eyre basin, drain inland with no outlet to the ocean.

Management

Because water ignores administrative boundaries, river basin management coordinates water supply, flood control, irrigation, hydropower and pollution control across whole catchments; bodies such as river basin authorities and international commissions (for example on the Rhine and Danube) embody this approach. Basin boundaries also define freshwater ecoregions and the distribution of freshwater species.
