Leibniz's law

Leibniz's law is the name given to two related principles associated with the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz concerning identity: the identity of indiscernibles and the indiscernibility of identicals. Together they underpin the standard logic of identity. The second principle — that if x and y are identical, then every property of x is a property of y and vice versa — is often called Leibniz's law proper, and its failure in intentional contexts is a classic puzzle in the philosophy of language.

The two principles

The indiscernibility of identicals states that if x is identical to y, then anything true of x is true of y. The identity of indiscernibles states the converse: if x and y share every property, they are numerically identical. Leibniz asserted the latter in his metaphysics, arguing that no two substances in nature are perfectly alike — there cannot be two leaves or two drops of water exactly the same.

Discussion

The identity of indiscernibles is the more controversial direction. Max Black's 1952 counterexample imagines a universe containing only two perfectly identical spheres; if coherent, such a world would contain two things sharing all (purely qualitative) properties. Defenders respond by including spatial or relational properties in the count. The principle also faces questions from quantum mechanics, where elementary particles of the same kind appear to be indiscernible yet many.

The indiscernibility of identicals is a cornerstone of formal logic, but substitution of identicals can fail in opaque contexts — for example, "Lois Lane believes Superman can fly" does not license substituting "Clark Kent", even though Superman is Clark Kent. Frege's distinction between sense and reference was designed in part to explain such cases.
