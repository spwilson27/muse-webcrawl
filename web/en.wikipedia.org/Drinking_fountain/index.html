Drinking fountain

A drinking fountain, also called a water fountain or bubbler in some regions, is a fountain designed to provide drinking water. It usually consists of a basin with either continuously running water or a tap, and a stream of water arched so that a person can drink by bending down to it without a cup.

History

Public drinking fountains spread in the nineteenth century as part of public-health and temperance movements: the Metropolitan Free Drinking Fountains and Cattle Troughs Association, founded in London in 1859, erected fountains to give people and animals an alternative to the water sold by vendors or the beer of public houses. In the United States, temperance organizations likewise promoted fountains, and the Woman's Christian Temperance Union funded many of them.

Design

Modern fountains commonly use a push-button or sensor to release an arcing stream from a bubbler head guarded against contact with the mouth. Refrigerated units chill the water, and many public buildings install bottle-filling stations alongside. In the United States, plumbing codes and the Americans with Disabilities Act specify mounting heights and clearances so wheelchair users can drink from a forward-approach spout.

Terminology

In parts of Wisconsin and New England, drinking fountains are commonly called "bubblers," a term traced to an early Kohler design that produced a bubbling vertical stream.
