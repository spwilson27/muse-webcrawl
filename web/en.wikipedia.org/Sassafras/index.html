Sassafras

Sassafras is a genus of three extant species of deciduous trees in the laurel family, Lauraceae, native to eastern North America and eastern Asia. The best-known species, Sassafras albidum, is notable for its aromatic bark, roots, and leaves, and for its variable leaves, which may be unlobed, mitten-shaped, or three-lobed on the same tree.

Uses

Sassafras has a long history of use by Native American peoples and European settlers. Root bark oil, rich in safrole, was used to flavour root beer and teas, but safrole was banned as a food additive by the US FDA in 1960 after being shown to be carcinogenic in animal studies. Dried, ground sassafras leaves — filé powder — remain a key ingredient and thickener in some Creole gumbo recipes. The wood is durable and has been used for furniture, barrels, and fence posts.

Ecology

Sassafras is a pioneer species that colonises old fields and forest edges. Its fruits are eaten by birds, and it is a host plant for the spicebush swallowtail butterfly. In autumn its foliage turns vivid orange and red.
