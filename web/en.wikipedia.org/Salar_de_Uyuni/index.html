Salar de Uyuni
The salt flat under a thin layer of water
LocationPotosí Department, Bolivia
Area10,582 km²
Elevation3,656 m above sea level
TypeSalt flat

Salar de Uyuni is the world's largest salt flat, covering 10,582 km² on the Altiplano of southwestern Bolivia near the crest of the Andes. Formed by the evaporation of prehistoric lakes, its brilliant white surface of hexagonal salt crust extends to the horizon.

Formation and composition

The salar was created as ancient lakes, notably Lake Minchin, dried up over thousands of years, leaving a crust of sodium chloride — in places several metres thick — over rich brine. Beneath it lies one of the world's largest reserves of lithium, a resource of growing strategic importance for batteries. The flat also yields salt harvested in small mounds by local workers.

Features

Islands within the salar, such as Incahuasi, are remnant volcanic outcrops covered in giant cacti and fossil coral. After rain, a thin film of water turns the surface into the world's largest natural mirror, reflecting the sky so perfectly that the horizon disappears; this flatness and reflectivity are used to calibrate satellite altimeters. Three species of South American flamingos breed in the area.

Tourism

Reached from the town of Uyuni, the salar is Bolivia's most famous attraction. Landmarks include a train cemetery of rusting locomotives, hotels built from salt blocks, and the Dakar Rally routes once staged across the flats.
