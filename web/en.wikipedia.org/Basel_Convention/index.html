The Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal, usually known simply as the Basel Convention, is an international treaty that was designed to reduce the movements of hazardous waste between nations, and specifically to prevent transfer of hazardous waste from developed to less developed countries. It does not, however, address the movement of radioactive waste. The convention is also intended to minimize the amount and toxicity of wastes generated, to ensure their environmentally sound management as closely as possible to the source of generation, and to assist less developed countries in the environmentally sound management of their own hazardous and other wastes.

History

The Convention was opened for signature on 21 March 1989, and entered into force on 5 May 1992. A series of notorious incidents of hazardous waste dumping in the late 1980s — including the voyage of the cargo ship Khian Sea, which dumped incinerator ash in Haiti and around the Atlantic and Indian Oceans — helped galvanize negotiators to complete the treaty. Its obligations are grouped around two principal themes: a regulatory system for the transboundary movement of hazardous wastes based on prior written notification and consent, and obligations for the environmentally sound management of such wastes.

Key provisions

Under the Convention, parties must not permit hazardous wastes to be exported to a party that has banned their import, or to any non-party. Exporting parties must notify the importing state in writing and obtain its written consent before shipment. Parties are also required to ensure that hazardous wastes are managed in an environmentally sound manner and to cooperate in reducing waste generation at source.

In 1995, parties adopted the Ban Amendment, which prohibits the export of hazardous wastes from OECD and EU countries to all other countries for final disposal, and for recycling as well. The amendment entered into force on 5 December 2019. In 2019, parties also adopted amendments bringing most plastic waste under the Convention's prior informed consent procedure, effective 1 January 2021.

Parties

As of recent counts, 191 states and the European Union are parties to the Convention. The United States signed the Convention in 1990 but has not ratified it, and is therefore an observer that may not participate in decision-making, although it may trade hazardous waste with parties only under separate bilateral or multilateral agreements consistent with the Convention.
