Sfumato (Italian: [sfuˈmaːto], from sfumare, "to tone down" or "to vanish like smoke") is a painting technique in which colours and tones are blended so gradually that there are no sharp transitions, lines, or borders — creating the soft, hazy effect famously seen in the face of the Mona Lisa. It is one of the four canonical painting modes of Renaissance art, alongside chiaroscuro, cangiante, and unione.

The Mona Lisa's smile: sfumato blurs the corners of the mouth and eyes. (Image placeholder)

Technique

Sfumato is achieved with very thin, translucent glazes — often dozens of layers, some only micrometres thick — laid over a tonal underpainting so that tones melt into one another. Leonardo da Vinci, who called for transitions "without lines or borders, in the manner of smoke", developed the technique most fully in works such as the Virgin of the Rocks, the Mona Lisa, and St John the Baptist. X-ray studies of Leonardo's panels confirm the extraordinary thinness and gradual build-up of his glazes.

History and influence

The effect had precedents in Flemish oil painting and in Leonardo's master Verrocchio's workshop, but Leonardo codified it, applying optical observation of atmosphere — objects lose contrast and definition with distance — to the modelling of flesh. Giorgione, Correggio, and Raphael adopted the method, and it became a hallmark of High Renaissance naturalism.
