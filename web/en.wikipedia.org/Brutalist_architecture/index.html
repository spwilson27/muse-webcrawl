Brutalist architecture is an architectural style that emerged during the 1950s in the United Kingdom, among the reconstruction projects of the post-war era. Brutalist buildings are characterised by minimalist construction showcasing the bare building materials and structural elements over decorative design. The style commonly makes use of exposed, unpainted concrete or brick, angular geometric shapes and a predominantly monochrome palette; other materials, such as steel, timber and glass, are also featured.

Origins of the term

The term derives from the French béton brut ("raw concrete"), used by Le Corbusier for the board-marked concrete of his Unité d'Habitation (1952) and other postwar work. The critic Reyner Banham popularised "the New Brutalism", associating it with the British architects Alison and Peter Smithson; the name's resemblance to the English word "brutal" — though a coincidence of etymology — thereafter coloured public perception.

Characteristics and spread

Descending from modernism, Brutalism answered postwar needs for cheap, rapid, monumental civic construction. It was widely used for government buildings, universities, libraries, housing estates and cultural centres around the world: examples include the Barbican Estate and National Theatre in London, Boston City Hall, the University of Massachusetts Dartmouth campus, and the vast concrete housing and ministries of Eastern Europe, Latin America and Asia.

Reception

Brutalism has always polarised opinion. Admirers praise its honesty of materials, sculptural power and social ambition; critics call it cold, inhuman and ill-suited to damp climates, as the concrete streaks and weathers. From the 1980s the style fell from favour, and many Brutalist buildings have been demolished. In the 21st century a reappraisal — fuelled by photography, social media and preservation campaigns — has seen key examples listed and restored, even as prominent losses continue.
