J. Erik Jorpes

Born: 15 July 1894, Kökar, Åland, Finland
Died: 10 July 1973, Stockholm, Sweden
Fields: Biochemistry
Known for: Purification and clinical introduction of heparin

Johan Erik Jorpes (1894–1973) was a Finnish-Swedish biochemist at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm whose purification and chemical characterisation of the anticoagulant heparin made its routine clinical use possible.

Life and career

Jorpes was born in Kökar in the Åland Islands, then part of the Russian Empire's Grand Duchy of Finland. He studied medicine and chemistry, and after early work in Finland moved to Sweden, where he joined the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm and eventually became professor of physiological (medical) chemistry. He built a research school devoted to the chemistry of biologically active carbohydrates and anticoagulants.

Heparin

Heparin had been discovered in 1916 by Jay McLean and William Howell, but early preparations were impure, toxic and variable. In the 1930s Jorpes developed methods to purify heparin from bovine lung and intestinal mucosa, showed that it is a highly sulphated polysaccharide (a glycosaminoglycan) built from uronic acid and glucosamine residues, and established its strong negative charge as central to its activity. Working with the surgeon Clarence Crafoord, he then led the clinical introduction of heparin at the Karolinska Hospital for the prevention and treatment of thrombosis and embolism, including its use in surgery — among the first systematic clinical trials of an anticoagulant. His monograph Heparin: Its Chemistry, Physiology and Application in Medicine (1939; second edition 1946) became the standard work on the subject.

Other work and legacy

Jorpes also studied chondroitin sulphates, blood coagulation and the chemistry of insulin-era carbohydrate metabolism, and he wrote biographical and historical works on Swedish chemistry, including studies of Jöns Jacob Berzelius. The reliable supply of standardised heparin that followed his work transformed vascular surgery, dialysis and thrombosis prophylaxis, and remains foundational to anticoagulant therapy.
