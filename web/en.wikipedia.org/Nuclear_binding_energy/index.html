Nuclear binding energy

Nuclear binding energy
DefinitionEnergy required to split a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons
Maximum per nucleonNear iron-56 and nickel-62 (~8.8 MeV)
UnitMeV per nucleus or per nucleon

Nuclear binding energy in experimental physics is the minimum energy required to disassemble the nucleus of an atom into its constituent protons and neutrons. It equals the energy that would be released if the nucleus were assembled from free nucleons, and it manifests as a mass defect: a bound nucleus always weighs slightly less than the sum of its parts, the difference given by Einstein's relation E = mc².

Binding energy curve

Plotted per nucleon against mass number, binding energy rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon near iron-56 and nickel-62 — the most tightly bound nuclei — and declines slowly for heavier nuclei. The shape of the curve explains the two great energy-releasing nuclear processes:

  • Fusion: combining light nuclei (below iron) produces nuclei higher on the curve, releasing energy — the process that powers the Sun and stars.
  • Fission: splitting very heavy nuclei such as uranium-235 yields fragments nearer the peak, also releasing energy — the basis of nuclear power and atomic bombs.

Origins

The binding arises from the strong nuclear force acting between nucleons, counteracted at larger sizes by the electrostatic repulsion of the protons. The semi-empirical mass formula models the total binding as a sum of volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry and pairing terms.
