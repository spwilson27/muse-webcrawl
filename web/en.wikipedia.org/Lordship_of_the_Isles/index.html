Lordship of the Isles

Lordship of the Isles
Native name: Triath nan Eilean / Rìgh Innse Gall
Flourished: 12th century – 1493
Territory: Hebrides, Kintyre, parts of the west Highland coast
Ruling house: Clann Somhairle (Clan Donald)
Centre: Finlaggan, Islay
Lords: Somerled's descendants, incl. John of Islay, Donald of Harlaw, Alexander of Islay

The Lordship of the Isles (Scottish Gaelic: Triath nan Eilean or Rìgh Innse Gall) was a semi-autonomous Gaelic-Norse lordship in western Scotland, centred on the Hebrides and ruled by Clan Donald from the 12th century until its forfeiture to the Scottish crown in 1493. At its height under the MacDonald Lords, it was a maritime power of consequence — effectively an independent kingdom that treated with England against the Scottish crown and sustained a distinctive Gaelic court culture of bards, harpists, and hereditary learned families.

Finlaggan, Islay — council seat of the Lords of the Isles

Origins

The lordship grew from the realm of Somerled (d. 1164), the Norse-Gaelic kinglet who wrested the southern Hebrides and Kintyre from Norse and Manx control. His descendants, Clann Somhairle, divided his inheritance: the MacDougalls of Argyll, the MacRorys, and the MacDonalds, whose chief Angus Óg supported Robert the Bruce. Angus Óg's son John of Islay was the first to style himself Dominus Insularum — Lord of the Isles — from about 1336, uniting the MacDonald and MacRory inheritances by marriage.

Heyday and conflict with the crown

Donald's son Donald of Islay invaded the mainland and fought the indecisive Battle of Harlaw (1411) in pursuit of the earldom of Ross. His son Alexander, as Earl of Ross, extended the lordship's power, but it provoked royal retaliation. In 1462 John II MacDonald signed the Treaty of Westminster-Ardtornish, agreeing with Edward IV of England to conquer and partition Scotland — a treason exposed in 1475, costing him Ross, Kintyre and Knapdale. After further rebellion by his son Angus Óg (victor at Bloody Bay, c. 1481), James IV finally forfeited the lordship in 1493; John II died a crown pensioner in 1503.

Culture and legacy

The Lords governed from Finlaggan on Islay, where a council of leading men (the Council of the Isles) advised them. They patronised Iona Abbey, commissioned carved grave slabs of the West Highland school, and maintained hereditary bardic families such as the MacMhuirichs. The forfeiture of 1493 is often taken as the beginning of the end of the old Gaelic order in Scotland, foreshadowing the crown's later campaigns against the Highlands.
