Bureaucracy

Bureaucracy is a system of organisation in which decisions and administration are carried out by appointed officials following fixed rules and procedures, within a hierarchy of offices. The term combines the French bureau (desk, office) with the Greek kratos (rule, power). Bureaucracies characterise modern governments, large corporations, militaries and other complex organisations.

The Weberian model

The sociologist Max Weber described bureaucracy as the most rational and efficient form of administration. Its ideal-type features include a clear hierarchy of offices, a division of labour with defined spheres of competence, written rules and records (the files), appointment on the basis of technical qualifications, full-time salaried officials who do not own their office, and impersonal treatment of cases according to rule rather than personal favour.

History

Bureaucratic administration is ancient: the civil services of imperial China (with examinations for office), Egypt and Rome all displayed bureaucratic traits. Modern bureaucracy expanded with the growth of the nation-state, taxation, standing armies and, in the 19th and 20th centuries, the welfare state and the large managerial corporation.

Criticism

"Bureaucracy" often carries a pejorative sense of red tape, delay, inflexibility and unaccountable power. Critics from Kafka's fiction to Parkinson's law have satirised its tendencies toward self-perpetuation and rule-fetishism, while reformers have repeatedly sought to make public administration more responsive without losing its legality and impartiality.
