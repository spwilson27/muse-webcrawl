Sergei Mikhailovich Eisenstein (22 January 1898 – 11 February 1948) was a Soviet film director and film theorist, widely regarded as one of the most influential filmmakers in the history of cinema. He pioneered the theory and practice of montage — the editing of film fragments to create meaning and emotional impact beyond what the individual shots contain.

Early career and montage

Trained as an engineer, Eisenstein worked in theatre before making his first feature, Strike (1925). His Battleship Potemkin (1925), dramatising the 1905 mutiny, contains the celebrated "Odessa Steps" sequence, repeatedly cited as one of the greatest in film history. October: Ten Days That Shook the World (1928) developed his "intellectual montage," juxtaposing images to generate abstract ideas.

Later work

After projects in the United States and Mexico (the unfinished ¡Que viva México!), Eisenstein returned to the USSR. He made the patriotic epic Alexander Nevsky (1938), with a score by Prokofiev, and the two-part Ivan the Terrible (1944–46); Part II was suppressed by Stalin and released only after Eisenstein's death. His theoretical writings, including Film Form and The Film Sense, remain foundational texts of film studies.

Legacy

Eisenstein's montage theories shaped generations of directors and editors, and his films consistently rank among the greatest ever made in critics' polls.
