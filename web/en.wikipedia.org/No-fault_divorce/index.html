No-fault divorce

No-fault divorce is a legal system under which a court may dissolve a marriage without requiring either spouse to prove wrongdoing — such as adultery, cruelty, or desertion — by the other. Typically, one spouse need only state that the marriage has irretrievably broken down, that the couple have irreconcilable differences, or that they have lived apart for a specified period.

History

Before no-fault reforms, divorcing couples often had to stage evidence of fault, fuelling perjury and acrimony. California became the first US state to adopt pure no-fault divorce with the Family Law Act of 1969, signed by Governor Ronald Reagan and effective in 1970; every US state had adopted some form of no-fault divorce by 2010, when New York became the last to do so. Similar reforms followed in many countries: Australia introduced no-fault divorce in 1975 with the Family Law Act, Canada in 1968 and 1985, and England and Wales with the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020, in force from April 2022.

Effects and debate

Studies associate no-fault laws with a temporary rise in divorce rates followed by long-run stabilisation, declines in domestic violence and in female suicide. Critics argue that unilateral no-fault divorce can disadvantage dependent spouses in property and support settlements, while supporters emphasise reduced conflict and court costs.
