Quasar

TypeActive galactic nucleus
Power sourceAccretion onto a supermassive black hole
LuminosityUp to ~1014 times the Sun
RedshiftsUp to z ≈ 7.6 and beyond

A quasar (from quasi-stellar radio source) is an extremely luminous active galactic nucleus powered by a supermassive black hole accreting matter from its host galaxy. Quasars are among the most luminous and most distant objects known, outshining their entire host galaxies despite emitting their energy from a region smaller than the Solar System. Unlike the black hole at the center of the Milky Way, which is currently quiet, a quasar's black hole is fed by large quantities of infalling gas.

Quasars were identified in the early 1960s as star-like radio sources; in 1963 Maarten Schmidt recognized that the spectrum of 3C 273 showed a large redshift, implying an enormous distance and energy output. More than a million quasars are now catalogued, most in wide-field surveys.

Properties

Gas falling toward the black hole forms an accretion disk heated to extreme temperatures, emitting across the electromagnetic spectrum. Some quasars launch relativistic jets; in the unified model of active galaxies, quasars, blazars, and radio galaxies are the same phenomenon viewed at different angles. Quasars were far more common in the early universe, peaking when the universe was about 3 billion years old, and their study illuminates galaxy evolution and the growth of supermassive black holes.
