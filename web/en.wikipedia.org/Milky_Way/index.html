The Milky Way is the galaxy that includes the Solar System, with the name describing the galaxy's appearance from Earth: a hazy band of light seen in the night sky formed from stars in the galactic plane that cannot be individually distinguished by the naked eye.

The Milky Way as seen from a dark-sky site on Earth.

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy with a diameter of about 100,000 light-years and an estimated visible mass of 100–400 billion stars. It is part of the Local Group of galaxies and contains at least 100 billion planets. The Solar System is located about 26,000 light-years from the Galactic Center, on the inner edge of the Orion Arm, one of the galaxy's spiral arms.

Structure

The galaxy consists of a central bar-shaped core surrounded by a disk of gas, dust, and stars arranged in spiral arms, all enclosed in a roughly spherical halo of older stars and globular clusters. At the centre lies Sagittarius A*, a supermassive black hole of about 4.1 million solar masses. The Milky Way rotates differentially; the Sun completes one orbit around the centre in roughly 230 million years.

History of understanding

Galileo Galilei first resolved the band of light into individual stars with his telescope in 1610. In the 1920s, Edwin Hubble's observations of other galaxies ended the Great Debate over whether the Milky Way comprised the whole universe. Modern surveys such as the European Space Agency's Gaia mission have mapped the positions and motions of over a billion stars.

Future

The Milky Way and the Andromeda Galaxy are approaching each other and are expected to collide and merge in about 4–5 billion years, eventually forming a single elliptical galaxy.
