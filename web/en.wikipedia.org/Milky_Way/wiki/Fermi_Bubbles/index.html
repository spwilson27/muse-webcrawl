Fermi Bubbles

The Fermi Bubbles are two enormous structures of gamma-ray and X-ray emitting plasma extending roughly 25,000 light-years (about 8 kiloparsecs) above and below the centre of the Milky Way galaxy — each bubble is about half the diameter of the galaxy's visible disc. They were discovered in 2010 by Douglas Finkbeiner, Meng Su and Tracy Slatyer while analysing data from the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope, work that earned the 2014 Bruno Rossi Prize.

Properties

The bubbles have sharply defined edges, a surprisingly uniform surface brightness, and a hard gamma-ray spectrum consistent with inverse-Compton scattering by very high-energy electrons or with proton interactions. A corresponding microwave "haze" was seen by the WMAP and Planck satellites, and in 2020 the eROSITA telescope found even larger X-ray bubbles enveloping the Fermi Bubbles, suggesting a total energy release of order 1056 erg.

Origin

Two main explanations are debated: an outburst of star formation near the galactic centre driving a wind over tens of millions of years, or a short, violent outburst a few million years ago from Sagittarius A*, the Milky Way's central supermassive black hole, when it accreted matter as an active galactic nucleus. Features such as the ultraviolet "Fermi arc" and ionised gas streamers favour a recent Seyfert-like flare from the black hole around 3–4 million years ago — within the lifetime of early hominins.
