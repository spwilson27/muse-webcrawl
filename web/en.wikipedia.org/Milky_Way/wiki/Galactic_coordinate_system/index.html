Galactic coordinate system

The galactic coordinate system is a celestial coordinate system in spherical coordinates, with the Sun as its centre, the primary direction aligned with the approximate centre of the Milky Way galaxy, and the fundamental plane parallel to the galactic plane. It uses the right-handed convention: galactic longitude l measured eastward along the galactic equator from the galactic centre direction, and galactic latitude b measured north from the plane.

Definition

William Herschel first used an equatorial analogue in 1785; Lund Observatory established an early standard in 1932. The modern system was fixed by the International Astronomical Union in 1958: the north galactic pole is defined at (J2000) right ascension 12h 51.4m, declination +27.13°, and galactic longitude zero points to the galactic centre in Sagittarius, at position angle 123° from the north celestial pole. Longitude increases toward Cygnus (l = 90°), where the Sun's orbital motion around the galaxy is directed, and the anticentre in Auriga/Gemini lies at l = 180°.

Use

Galactic coordinates are the natural frame for studying the structure of the Milky Way — spiral arms, the galactic halo, interstellar gas and cosmic-ray sources are all plotted in (l, b). All-sky survey images from radio to gamma rays are conventionally shown in galactic coordinates, usually with the galactic centre in the middle of the map.
