The Andromeda Galaxy is a barred spiral galaxy and the nearest major galaxy to the Milky Way. It is catalogued as Messier 31 (M31) and NGC 224. Located about 2.5 million light-years from Earth in the constellation Andromeda, it is the largest galaxy in the Local Group, which also contains the Milky Way, the Triangulum Galaxy and roughly 80 smaller galaxies. Under dark skies it is visible to the naked eye as a faint, fuzzy patch — the most distant object ordinarily visible without a telescope.

Observation history

The Persian astronomer Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi described the galaxy as a "little cloud" in his Book of Fixed Stars around 964 CE. Simon Marius gave the first telescopic description in 1612. For centuries it was classed as a nebula; in 1864 William Huggins noted its star-like spectrum. In the "Great Debate" of 1920, Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis argued over whether such "spiral nebulae" were inside or outside the Milky Way. Edwin Hubble settled the question in 1923–24 by identifying Cepheid variable stars in Andromeda, proving it lay far beyond the Milky Way's bounds and vastly expanding the known scale of the universe.

Structure and properties

Andromeda spans roughly 220,000 light-years across its extended disk, making it larger than the Milky Way in diameter, though studies suggest the two galaxies have comparable total mass (around 1.5 trillion solar masses including dark matter). It contains roughly one trillion stars — at least twice the Milky Way's count. Its nucleus is double-peaked, centred on a supermassive black hole of about 100 million solar masses. Prominent dust lanes and a ring of star formation about 10 kiloparsecs from the centre are visible in infrared images from the Spitzer and Herschel space telescopes. Andromeda has at least 35 known satellite galaxies, including the compact elliptical M32 and the elliptical M110.

Future collision with the Milky Way

Andromeda is approaching the Milky Way at about 110 km/s (its spectrum is blueshifted), and the two galaxies are expected to begin colliding in roughly 4–4.5 billion years, eventually merging into a large elliptical or lenticular galaxy sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda". Simulations show the Solar System would likely be displaced outward but not destroyed; stellar collisions are improbable because of the vast distances between individual stars.
