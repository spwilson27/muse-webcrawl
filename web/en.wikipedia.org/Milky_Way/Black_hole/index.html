The Milky Way's Black Hole (Sagittarius A*)

Sagittarius A*
Object: Supermassive black hole
Location: Galactic Center, ~26,000 light-years away
Mass: about 4.3 million solar masses
Imaged: 12 May 2022 (Event Horizon Telescope)

At the centre of the Milky Way lies a supermassive black hole known as Sagittarius A* (pronounced "Sagittarius A-star"). Radio astronomers detected a compact, intense radio source in the constellation Sagittarius in 1974; decades of observations of stars whipping around an invisible point — notably by teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez — proved that the object is a black hole of roughly 4.3 million solar masses, work recognised by the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Observation

The star S2 (S0-2) orbits the black hole in about 16 years, reaching speeds of several percent of the speed of light at closest approach, and its orbit shows the gravitational redshift and precession predicted by general relativity. In May 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first direct image of Sagittarius A*: a glowing orange ring of superheated gas surrounding the black hole's shadow, remarkably similar in structure to the earlier image of the black hole in M87 but a thousand times smaller and far more rapidly variable.

Behaviour

Sagittarius A* is an unusually quiet black hole, accreting matter at a very low rate and producing only modest flares in X-ray and infrared light — evidence that the Milky Way's central engine is in a long, dim phase compared with active galactic nuclei in other galaxies. Giant gamma-ray structures called the Fermi bubbles suggest it was considerably more active in the geologically recent past.
