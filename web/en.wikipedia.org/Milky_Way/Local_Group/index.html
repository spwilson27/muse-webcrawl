The Local Group is the galaxy group that includes the Milky Way, the Andromeda Galaxy, and the Triangulum Galaxy, together with more than 80 smaller galaxies, most of them dwarf galaxies. It spans a region roughly 10 million light-years across and forms part of the larger Virgo Supercluster.

The two most massive members, the Milky Way and Andromeda, are large barred spiral galaxies of roughly comparable mass, each orbited by a system of satellite dwarf galaxies including the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds around the Milky Way, and M32 and M110 around Andromeda. The Milky Way and Andromeda are approaching each other and are expected to collide and merge in about 4–5 billion years.

Members and dynamics

The third-largest member, the Triangulum Galaxy, is a spiral that may be a satellite of Andromeda. Most other Local Group galaxies are dwarf spheroidals and dwarf irregulars gravitationally bound to one of the two giant spirals. The group's total mass is estimated at around 2 trillion solar masses, most of it dark matter.

The term "Local Group" was used by Edwin Hubble in the 1930s as astronomers established that these galaxies form a distinct nearby grouping, separate from the more distant Virgo Cluster.
