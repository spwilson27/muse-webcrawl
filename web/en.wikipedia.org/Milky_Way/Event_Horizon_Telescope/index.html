The Event Horizon Telescope and the Milky Way

Event Horizon Telescope (EHT)
Type: Global very-long-baseline interferometry array
Targets: M87* (imaged 2019), Sagittarius A* (imaged 2022)
Observing wavelength: 1.3 mm

The Event Horizon Telescope is a worldwide network of radio observatories synchronised by atomic clocks to act as a single Earth-sized telescope. Using very-long-baseline interferometry at a wavelength of 1.3 millimetres, it achieves the angular resolution needed to image the immediate surroundings of supermassive black holes — including the one at the centre of our own galaxy, the Milky Way.

Imaging Sagittarius A*

Observing the Milky Way's black hole, Sagittarius A*, proved harder than imaging the more distant M87*. Gas orbits Sagittarius A* in minutes rather than days, so the source changes while the Earth rotates, and interstellar scattering blurs the view toward the galactic centre. After observations in April 2017 and years of analysis, the collaboration released the image on 12 May 2022: a bright ring about 52 microarcseconds across, consistent with a black hole of about four million solar masses and confirming predictions of Einstein's general relativity in a new regime.

Significance

The two black-hole images together let astronomers test how black holes feed and launch jets across very different mass scales and environments. Upgraded campaigns with more stations and higher frequencies aim to produce sharper images and even movies of gas swirling around the Milky Way's dark centre.
