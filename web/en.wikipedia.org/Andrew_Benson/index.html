Andrew Benson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Andrew Alm Benson (24 September 1917 – 16 January 2015) was an American biologist and plant physiologist best known for his work, with Melvin Calvin and James Bassham at the University of California, Berkeley, elucidating the carbon-assimilation cycle of photosynthesis — the cycle commonly called the Calvin cycle, and more fully the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle.

Biography

Benson was born in Modesto, California, and studied chemistry at UC Berkeley, taking his PhD there in 1942. During the Second World War he worked on explosives research and briefly taught, before returning to Berkeley in 1946 to join Calvin's group at the Lawrence Radiation Laboratory.

The photosynthesis work

The Berkeley group exploited the newly available radioactive isotope carbon-14 to trace the path of carbon in photosynthesis. Benson developed the two-dimensional paper chromatography techniques that separated the labelled compounds produced when algae were exposed to radioactive carbon dioxide for seconds at a time. This work identified 3-phosphoglyceric acid as the first stable product of carbon fixation and mapped the regenerative cycle of reactions (1946–1953) by which plants build sugars from CO2. Calvin alone received the 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this research, an outcome long debated; Benson's foundational role has been increasingly recognised, including by the naming of the cycle with his name.

Later career

After periods at Penn State and UCLA, Benson joined the Scripps Institution of Oceanography in 1962, where he studied plant and membrane lipids, including the sulfolipid chemistry of chloroplasts, until his retirement. He received numerous honours late in life, including the Stephen Hales Prize of the American Society of Plant Physiologists. He died in 2015, aged 97.
