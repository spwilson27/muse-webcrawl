Spike fiddle

A spike fiddle is a bowed string instrument whose neck passes through — and protrudes from — the body of the instrument, forming a spike. The spike typically passes through a resonator made of wood, gourd, coconut shell or another material, often covered with a skin soundboard. Spike fiddles are found across Asia, the Middle East and parts of Africa and Europe, and are among the oldest types of bowed instrument, spreading along the Silk Road.

Construction and playing

The player usually holds the instrument vertically, resting the spike on the knee, thigh or ground, and stops the strings with the fingers of the left hand — often touching the side of the string rather than pressing it to a fingerboard, since many spike fiddles have no fingerboard. The number of strings ranges from one to four.

Examples

  • Erhu (China) — two strings, snakeskin (traditionally python) soundboard, the most widely known spike fiddle.
  • Jinghu (China) — higher-pitched companion used in Peking opera.
  • Kokyū (Japan) — the only traditional Japanese string instrument played with a bow.
  • Rebab (Middle East and Southeast Asia) — with a small round or heart-shaped body.
  • Morin khuur (Mongolia) — the horsehead fiddle.
  • Kemenche and related instruments of the Black Sea and Eastern Mediterranean.
  • Masenqo (Ethiopia and Eritrea) — a single-stringed spike fiddle.

History

Bowed spike fiddles appear to have developed in Central Asia around the 10th century and diffused eastward into China and westward into the Islamic world and Europe, where they contributed to the ancestry of the European violin family via instruments such as the rebec. In many cultures the spike fiddle is the characteristic instrument of bards, storytellers and court musicians.
