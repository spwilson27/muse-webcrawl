Exocrine gland

Exocrine glands are glands that secrete substances onto an epithelial surface by way of a duct. They contrast with endocrine glands, which secrete their products directly into the bloodstream. Examples of exocrine glands include the sweat glands, salivary glands, mammary glands, and glands of the gastrointestinal tract such as the liver and pancreas — though the pancreas and liver also have endocrine functions and are sometimes called mixed glands.

Structure and classification

An exocrine gland consists of a secretory portion (the glandular epithelium that produces the secretion) and a duct system that carries the product to its destination. Glands are classified by structure as simple (single unbranched duct) or compound (branched duct), and by the shape of the secretory units as tubular, acinar (alveolar), or tubuloacinar. They are also classified by the nature of their product as serous, mucous, or mixed (seromucous).

Modes of secretion

Three mechanisms of secretion are recognized. In merocrine (eccrine) secretion, products are released by exocytosis, as in most sweat glands and the pancreas. In apocrine secretion, part of the apical cytoplasm is pinched off with the product, classically attributed to mammary glands. In holocrine secretion, the entire cell disintegrates to release its contents, as in sebaceous glands of the skin.

Examples and functions

Exocrine secretions serve digestion (saliva, gastric juice, bile, pancreatic juice), lubrication and protection (mucus, sebum, tears), thermoregulation (sweat), and nutrition of offspring (milk). Disorders of exocrine glands include cystic fibrosis, which impairs chloride transport in exocrine epithelia throughout the body, and Sjögren's syndrome, an autoimmune attack on salivary and lacrimal glands.
