Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968

The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968 (ICRA), enacted as Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1968 (Pub. L. 90-284), imposes on tribal governments in the United States many of the same constitutional restrictions that the Bill of Rights and the Fourteenth Amendment impose on federal and state governments. It also authorized a model code for tribal courts and required tribal consent for states to assume civil and criminal jurisdiction under Public Law 280.

Provisions

ICRA guarantees, against tribal governments, rights including free speech and press, freedom of religion, protection against unreasonable search and seizure and self-incrimination, due process and equal protection, a speedy and public trial, assistance of counsel (at the defendant's own expense), and protection against cruel and unusual punishment and excessive bail. It omits some federal guarantees — notably an establishment clause and a right to appointed counsel for indigents — and originally limited tribal court sentences to one year imprisonment and a $5,000 fine.

Enforcement and case law

In Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978), the Supreme Court held that ICRA's only federal remedy is habeas corpus; other claims must be pursued in tribal forums, preserving tribal sovereignty. The Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 allowed tribes meeting certain conditions to impose sentences of up to three years per offense, and the Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act of 2013 restored limited tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians in domestic-violence cases.
