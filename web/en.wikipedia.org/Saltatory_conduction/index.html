Saltatory conduction

Saltatory conduction
The action potential "leaps" from node to node
SystemNervous system
StructuresMyelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier
Speedup to about 120 m/s in myelinated axons

Saltatory conduction (from Latin saltare, "to leap") is the propagation of action potentials along myelinated axons, in which the electrical signal appears to jump from one node of Ranvier to the next. Because the myelin sheath insulates the axon between nodes, regenerating the signal only at the exposed nodes greatly increases conduction speed and reduces energy cost compared with unmyelinated fibres.

Mechanism

In a myelinated axon, voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels are concentrated at the nodes of Ranvier, the small gaps between Schwann cells (in the peripheral nervous system) or oligodendrocyte segments (in the central nervous system). An action potential at one node generates local currents that rapidly depolarise the next node to threshold, typically about a millimetre away. Conduction velocities reach roughly 70–120 m/s, versus about 1 m/s in thin unmyelinated fibres.

Discovery

The jumping behaviour was proposed by Ralph Lillie in the 1920s as a theoretical model and demonstrated experimentally by Ichiji Tasaki and by Andrew Huxley and Robert Stämpfli in the late 1930s and 1940s.

Clinical significance

Demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis destroy the myelin of central axons, slowing or blocking saltatory conduction and producing symptoms including weakness, numbness and visual loss. Peripheral demyelination occurs in Guillain–Barré syndrome.
