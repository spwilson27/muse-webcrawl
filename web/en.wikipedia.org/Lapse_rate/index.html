Lapse rate

The lapse rate is the rate at which an atmospheric variable, normally temperature in Earth's atmosphere, falls with altitude. Formally, it is the negative of the rate of temperature change with altitude change: the lapse rate is positive when the temperature decreases with elevation. Though this concept is most often applied to Earth's troposphere, it can be extended to any gravitationally supported parcel of gas, and is central to meteorology, aviation, and climate science.

Types of lapse rates

Environmental lapse rate

The environmental lapse rate is the actual rate of decrease of temperature with altitude at a given time and place, measured for example by a radiosonde. In the standard atmosphere it averages about 6.5 °C per kilometre. It varies widely: on calm clear nights a temperature inversion — a negative lapse rate, with temperature increasing with height — may form near the ground.

Dry adiabatic lapse rate

The dry adiabatic lapse rate is the rate at which a rising parcel of unsaturated air cools purely by expansion, about 9.8 °C per kilometre. It follows from the first law of thermodynamics and the hydrostatic equation and depends only on gravity and the specific heat of air.

Moist adiabatic lapse rate

When a rising parcel is saturated, condensation of water vapor releases latent heat and partially offsets the expansion cooling. The moist (saturated) adiabatic lapse rate is therefore smaller — typically between about 4 and 7 °C per kilometre, varying with temperature and pressure.

Stability

Comparing the environmental lapse rate with the adiabatic rates determines atmospheric stability. If the environmental lapse rate lies between the moist and dry adiabatic rates, the atmosphere is conditionally unstable: unsaturated air is stable, but saturated air rises buoyantly once lifted to saturation — the usual setup for thunderstorms. An atmosphere steeper than the dry adiabatic rate is absolutely unstable; one shallower than the moist rate is absolutely stable.

Effects and relevance

Lapse rates govern cloud formation, the height of the tropopause, mountain weather, pollutant dispersion, and the greenhouse effect: in climate theory, changes in the lapse rate with warming constitute the lapse rate feedback, which is negative in the tropics because the moist adiabat flattens as the climate warms.
