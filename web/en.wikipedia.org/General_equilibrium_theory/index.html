General equilibrium theory

In economics, general equilibrium theory attempts to explain the behaviour of supply, demand, and prices in a whole economy with several or many interacting markets, by seeking to prove that the interaction of demand and supply will result in an overall general equilibrium. It contrasts with partial equilibrium theory, which analyses only single markets.

History

The theory dates to Léon Walras's Elements of Pure Economics (1874), the first attempt at a rigorous mathematical model of an entire economy. Walras proposed a system of simultaneous equations whose unknowns were the prices of all goods, and a process of tâtonnement by which an auctioneer adjusts prices until all markets clear. The modern existence proof was given in the 1950s by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu, who used fixed-point theorems from topology to show conditions under which a competitive equilibrium exists.

Fundamental theorems

The first fundamental theorem of welfare economics states that, under certain conditions, any competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. The second states that any Pareto-efficient allocation can be sustained as a competitive equilibrium after suitable lump-sum transfers of initial endowments. These results underpin the theoretical case for market allocation, while their assumptions — complete markets, no externalities, perfect information — also frame the study of market failure.

Limitations

The Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu theorem showed that aggregate excess demand functions can take almost any shape consistent with Walras's law, so the basic model places few restrictions on equilibrium prices and cannot guarantee uniqueness or stability. Applied general equilibrium models nevertheless remain widely used in trade, tax, and climate-policy analysis.
