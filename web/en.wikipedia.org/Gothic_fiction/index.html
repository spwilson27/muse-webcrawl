Gothic fiction

Gothic fiction, sometimes called Gothic horror, is a loose literary aesthetic of fear and haunting. The name refers to the (pseudo)-medieval buildings in which many of these stories are set, but early examples are sometimes set in other places and periods. Gothic fiction is characterised by an environment of fear, the threat of supernatural events, and the intrusion of the past upon the present. The genre gave rise to modern horror fiction and influenced romance, science fiction, and the Southern Gothic tradition.

History

The genre began with Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto (1764), subtitled in its second edition "A Gothic Story". Ann Radcliffe refined the form in the 1790s with novels such as The Mysteries of Udolpho (1794), favouring explained supernaturalism and picturesque terror, while Matthew Lewis's The Monk (1796) embraced outright horror and the demonic. Nineteenth-century Gothic produced many of literature's most durable creations: Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818), the works of Edgar Allan Poe, Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde (1886), Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890), and Bram Stoker's Dracula (1897).

Themes and conventions

Recurrent features include castles, monasteries, dungeons, and secret passages; omens, curses, and ancestral sins; fainting heroines and brooding, Byronic villains; doubles and doppelgängers; and confrontations between modernity and a repressed past. Gothic writing often channels contemporary anxieties — about religion, gender, science, empire, and class — through its supernatural machinery.

Legacy

Gothic conventions persist across media: horror cinema, gothic rock and the wider goth subculture, neo-Gothic novels by authors such as Daphne du Maurier and Shirley Jackson, and the "female Gothic" tradition running from Radcliffe through the Brontës' Jane Eyre and Wuthering Heights.
