Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa) is a flowering plant in the amaranth family, grown as a grain crop primarily for its edible seeds, which are rich in protein, dietary fibre, B vitamins, and minerals. Although treated as a grain in cooking, it is a pseudocereal rather than a true cereal grass.

History

Quinoa was domesticated in the Andean region of South America around Lake Titicaca some 3,000 to 4,000 years ago, and was a staple of the Inca Empire, which revered it as the "mother of all grains". Spanish colonists suppressed its cultivation in favour of wheat, but it remained essential to Andean farmers in Peru and Bolivia.

Nutrition and preparation

Quinoa provides all nine essential amino acids, making it a complete protein source, and is naturally gluten-free. The seeds are coated with bitter saponins, which are removed by rinsing or polishing before cooking; cooked quinoa has a fluffy texture and mildly nutty flavour.

Global popularity

International demand rose sharply in the 2000s and 2010s, and the United Nations declared 2013 the International Year of Quinoa. Rising export prices raised concern for affordability among Andean consumers, though subsequent research suggested the welfare effects on producing households were generally positive.
