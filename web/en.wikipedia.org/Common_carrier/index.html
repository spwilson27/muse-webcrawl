Common carrier

A common carrier in common law countries is a person or company that transports goods or people for any person or company and is responsible for any possible loss of the goods during transport. A common carrier offers its services to the general public under a licence or authority provided by a regulator, as opposed to a contract carrier, which serves specific clients under individual agreements, or a private carrier.

Duties and liability

Historically, common carriers were held to a high standard of care and were treated almost as insurers of the goods they carried, liable for loss or damage except where caused by an act of God, a public enemy, the shipper's own fault, or the inherent vice of the goods. In return, they were obliged to serve all customers without unreasonable discrimination and to charge only reasonable rates.

Regulation

Because of their importance to commerce and their tendency toward monopoly, common carriers have long been subject to special regulation. In the United States, railroads were regulated by the Interstate Commerce Commission under the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887. Airlines, trucking firms, pipelines, and telecommunications providers have all been treated as common carriers at various times. In telecommunications law, common carrier status under Title II of the Communications Act of 1934 became central to debates over network neutrality, since it obliges providers to carry traffic without discrimination.
