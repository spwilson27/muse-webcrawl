Competitive strategy

Competitive strategy concerns a firm's position within its industry

Competitive strategy is the long-term plan by which a business seeks to gain a defensible advantage over its rivals in an industry. The field was shaped decisively by Michael E. Porter, whose 1980 book Competitive Strategy: Techniques for Analyzing Industries and Competitors introduced the five forces framework and the concept of generic strategies.

Generic strategies

Porter argued that a firm can pursue above-average performance through one of three generic strategies:

  • Cost leadership — becoming the lowest-cost producer in the industry, allowing the firm to underprice rivals or earn higher margins at prevailing prices.
  • Differentiation — offering a product or service perceived as unique, commanding a premium price.
  • Focus — concentrating on a narrow market segment, serving it with either cost leadership or differentiation within that niche.

Porter warned that firms failing to commit to one strategy risk becoming "stuck in the middle", with no clear advantage.

Industry analysis

The five forces — rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, bargaining power of buyers, and bargaining power of suppliers — determine the profit potential of an industry. Competitive strategy also draws on game theory, the resource-based view of the firm, and concepts such as the value chain, which decomposes a firm's activities to locate sources of cost or differentiation advantage.

Later developments

Subsequent frameworks include the resource-based view (Barney), core competence (Prahalad and Hamel), disruptive innovation (Christensen), and blue ocean strategy (Kim and Mauborgne), which proposes creating uncontested market space rather than competing in existing markets.
