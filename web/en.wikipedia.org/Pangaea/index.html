Pangaea or Pangea was a supercontinent that existed during the late Paleozoic and early Mesozoic eras, assembling from earlier continental units about 335 million years ago and beginning to break apart about 175 million years ago. The name, proposed by Alfred Wegener in 1912, is Greek for "all lands."

Formation

Pangaea formed through a series of continental collisions: the closure of the Iapetus and Rheic oceans brought together Laurentia, Baltica, Avalonia, and Gondwana, raising the Appalachian, Caledonian, and Variscan mountain belts. By the Permian period, the Siberian craton and the North and South China blocks had also joined, so that almost all continental crust formed one landmass centered near the equator and stretching nearly pole to pole.

Climate and life

The vast interior of Pangaea experienced extreme continental climates with hot, arid conditions, while monsoonal circulation affected the coasts around the Tethys Ocean. Terrestrial life was unified across the supercontinent: the same fossil flora (such as Glossopteris) and fauna (such as Lystrosaurus and Cynognathus) are found on now widely separated continents — evidence Wegener used for continental drift. The end-Permian mass extinction, about 252 million years ago, occurred while Pangaea was intact, and the supercontinent was also the setting for the early evolution of dinosaurs.

Breakup

Rifting began in the Jurassic period, first splitting Pangaea into Laurasia in the north and Gondwana in the south, and opening the Atlantic Ocean. Through the Jurassic and Cretaceous, the fragments separated further into the modern continents, in a process that continues today as the Atlantic widens and the Pacific shrinks. Pangaea is the most recent of several supercontinents in Earth's history, preceded by Rodinia and Pannotia.
