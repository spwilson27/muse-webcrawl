Death in Venice (German: Der Tod in Venedig) is a novella by the German author Thomas Mann, published in 1912. The work presents a celebrated writer, Gustav von Aschenbach, who travels to Venice and is liberated — and ultimately destroyed — by his obsession with the beauty of a young Polish boy, Tadzio. The novella is a meditation on art, beauty, discipline and decay, and on the tension between Apollonian restraint and Dionysian passion.

Plot

Aschenbach, an aging and widowed Munich writer famed for his disciplined mastery, suffers writer's block and yearns to travel. In Venice he settles at the Grand Hotel des Bains on the Lido, where he notices Tadzio, a beautiful Polish adolescent holidaying with his family. Aschenbach becomes infatuated, contriving encounters while never speaking to the boy. Meanwhile a cholera epidemic, originating in India, spreads through the city; Venetian authorities conceal it to protect tourism. Aschenbach learns the truth but does not warn Tadzio's family, unwilling to lose him. Increasingly dissolute — he has his hair dyed and his face rouged — he follows the family through the plague-stricken city. Weakened by illness, he dies on the beach, watching Tadzio wade in the sea.

Origins and themes

Mann drew on a real trip to Venice in 1911, his own fascination with a Polish boy, and the death of composer Gustav Mahler, whose first name and appearance he gave Aschenbach. The novella weaves in Plato's Phaedrus and Symposium on beauty and desire, Nietzsche's Apollo–Dionysus dichotomy, and the figure of the psychopomp Hermes, echoed in the strangers who steer Aschenbach toward Venice and death.

Adaptations

Luchino Visconti's 1971 film Death in Venice, starring Dirk Bogarde with music by Mahler, is the most famous adaptation; Benjamin Britten's opera Death in Venice (1973) was his last. The novella remains one of Mann's most read and analysed works.
