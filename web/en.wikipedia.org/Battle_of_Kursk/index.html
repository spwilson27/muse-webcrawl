The Battle of Kursk was a major engagement between German and Soviet forces on the Eastern Front of the Second World War, fought near Kursk in the summer of 1943. The German offensive, codenamed Operation Citadel, aimed to pinch off the Kursk salient; its failure gave the strategic initiative on the Eastern Front permanently to the Red Army. The battle included the clash at Prokhorovka, often described as the largest tank battle in history.

Background

After the German catastrophe at Stalingrad and Manstein's counterstroke at Kharkov, the front line bulged westward around Kursk in a salient roughly 250 km wide. German planners proposed converging attacks from Orel in the north and Belgorod in the south to encircle the Soviet forces inside. Soviet intelligence, aided by the "Lucy" spy ring and British Ultra decrypts, learned the plan in detail; the Red Army prepared defence in depth—belt after belt of minefields, anti-tank strongpoints and trenches—while holding powerful reserves in the Steppe Front.

Operation Citadel

The attack opened on 5 July 1943. In the north, Model's Ninth Army ground forward only about 15 km before being halted. In the south, Hoth's Fourth Panzer Army and Army Detachment Kempf made deeper progress, reaching Prokhorovka by 12 July, where the 5th Guards Tank Army met the II SS Panzer Corps in a massive armoured engagement. On 13 July Hitler called off Citadel, citing the Allied landing in Sicily and Soviet counteroffensives already under way.

Soviet counteroffensives

Operation Kutuzov (from 12 July) liberated Orel, and Operation Rumyantsev (from 3 August) took Belgorod and Kharkov, ending the battle on 23 August. Soviet losses were far greater than German, but the Red Army could replace them; Germany's irreplaceable panzer strength was squandered against prepared defences.

Significance

Kursk was the last major German offensive on the Eastern Front. From the summer of 1943 the Wehrmacht was in near-continuous retreat toward Berlin.
