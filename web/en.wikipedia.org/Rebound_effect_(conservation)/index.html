Rebound effect (conservation)

In energy economics, the rebound effect (or take-back effect) is the reduction in expected gains from new technologies that increase the efficiency of resource use, because of behavioural or other systemic responses. These responses usually tend to offset the beneficial effects of the new technology or other measures taken. A classic example: more fuel-efficient cars make driving cheaper per kilometre, so people drive more, offsetting part of the fuel saving.

Definitions

The direct rebound effect is increased consumption of the same good or service whose efficiency improved. Indirect rebound effects arise when money saved is spent on other goods and services that themselves consume resources. Economy-wide effects include changes in prices, output and growth. When rebound exceeds 100% — so that efficiency improvements actually increase total resource use — the result is called backfire, a modern statement of the Jevons paradox, named after William Stanley Jevons, who in 1865 observed that more efficient steam engines increased total coal consumption.

Evidence

Empirical estimates for household energy services and personal transport commonly find direct rebound effects in the range of roughly 10–30%, though estimates vary widely by sector, income level and country. Rebound tends to be larger in developing economies and for energy services that are far from saturated. Economists debate the size of economy-wide effects and their implications for energy-efficiency policy and climate mitigation.
