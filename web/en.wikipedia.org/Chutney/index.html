Chutney

A chutney is a family of condiments originating in the Indian subcontinent, made from mixtures of fruits, vegetables, herbs, spices, and aromatics. The word derives from the Hindi chaṭnī, from chāṭnā, "to lick" or "to eat with appetite". Chutneys range from fresh, coarse pastes eaten the day they are made to sweet, vinegar-preserved relishes that keep for months.

Indian varieties

Fresh chutneys are ground — traditionally on a stone — from ingredients such as coconut, coriander and mint, green chilli, ginger, and lime, and are served with dosas, idlis, pakoras, and chaat. Cooked and preserved types include tamarind chutney, tomato chutney, and fiery garlic and peanut chutneys of Maharashtra. Nearly every region and household has its own repertoire, and chutneys provide the sour, hot, and sweet accents of an Indian meal.

Anglo-Indian and global chutneys

British colonials carried the concept home, transforming it into sweet, jam-like preserves of fruit cooked with vinegar, sugar, and spices; the most famous, Major Grey's mango chutney, dates to the 19th century. Chutney became a standard accompaniment to cheese, cold meats, and the ploughman's lunch, and inspired related preserves worldwide, including South African blatjang and Caribbean pepper sauces.
