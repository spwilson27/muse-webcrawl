The House of Valois-Burgundy was a cadet branch of the French House of Valois that ruled the Duchy of Burgundy and a growing collection of territories in France and the Low Countries from 1363 to 1482. Its four dukes — Philip the Bold, John the Fearless, Philip the Good and Charles the Bold — transformed an appanage into one of the most powerful and culturally brilliant states of late medieval Europe.

History

In 1363 King John II of France granted the Duchy of Burgundy to his youngest son, Philip, who had earned the epithet "the Bold" fighting beside his father at the Battle of Poitiers in 1356. Through his marriage to Margaret III, Countess of Flanders, Philip acquired Flanders, Artois, Nevers, Rethel and Franche-Comté, laying the foundation of the Burgundian state.

John the Fearless (r. 1404–1419) plunged Burgundy into the Armagnac–Burgundian civil war; his assassination at Montereau drove his son Philip the Good (r. 1419–1467) into alliance with England. Philip expanded the state to include Namur, Holland, Zeeland, Hainaut, Brabant, Limburg and Luxembourg, and founded the Order of the Golden Fleece in 1430. Charles the Bold (r. 1467–1477) sought a royal crown but was killed at the Battle of Nancy. His daughter Mary, the last of the line, married Maximilian of Habsburg, and on her death in 1482 the Burgundian inheritance passed to the Habsburgs, while the duchy proper reverted to France.

Culture

The Valois-Burgundian court at Dijon and Bruges was a centre of Franco-Flemish polyphony, illuminated manuscripts and Early Netherlandish painting, patronising artists such as Claus Sluter, Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden.
