Real presence of Christ in the Eucharist

The real presence of Christ in the Eucharist is the Christian doctrine that Jesus Christ is truly present in the Eucharist, not merely symbolically or metaphorically. It is affirmed in differing forms by the Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, Oriental Orthodox churches, the Assyrian Church of the East, and many Lutherans and Anglicans, though the mode of presence is explained differently across traditions.

Terminology across traditions

The Catholic Church teaches transubstantiation: the substance of bread and wine is converted into Christ's body and blood while the accidents (appearances) remain; the doctrine was defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and reaffirmed at the Council of Trent. Eastern Orthodoxy likewise confesses a true change (metousiosis) while generally avoiding scholastic definition, treating the change as a mystery. Lutheran theology speaks of Christ's body and blood being present "in, with, and under" the forms of bread and wine — popularly called consubstantiation, a term Lutherans often reject. Anglican formularies affirm a real, spiritual presence while rejecting transubstantiation's philosophical apparatus.

Reformed and memorialist views

Reformed theology, following Calvin, teaches a spiritual presence: believers truly feed on Christ by the Holy Spirit, though Christ's body remains in heaven. Zwinglian and many Baptist traditions hold a memorialist view in which the Supper commemorates Christ without his presence being located in the elements. These disagreements were among the defining controversies of the Reformation and remain ecumenically significant.

History

Early Church Fathers such as Ignatius of Antioch, Justin Martyr and Irenaeus used strongly realist language about the Eucharist. Medieval theologians developed accounts using Aristotelian categories of substance and accident, and popular devotion produced practices like eucharistic adoration, Benediction and the feast of Corpus Christi.
