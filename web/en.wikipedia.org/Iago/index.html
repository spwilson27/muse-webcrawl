Iago

Iago is a fictional character in William Shakespeare's tragedy Othello (c. 1603). Iago is the play's main antagonist and Othello's standard-bearer (ensign). He is married to Emilia, who is a maid (attendant) to Othello's wife Desdemona. Iago, passed over for promotion in favour of Cassio and harbouring a complex of resentments, plots to manipulate Othello into believing Desdemona is unfaithful, driving Othello to murder her and himself. Iago has the largest speaking part of the play — some 1,097 lines — and is one of literature's most studied villains.

Role in the play

Feigning loyalty ("I am not what I am"), Iago exploits Roderigo's love for Desdemona, arranges Cassio's disgrace in a drunken brawl, steals Desdemona's handkerchief via Emilia and plants it in Cassio's lodging, and stage-manages a conversation that Othello misreads as confirmation of adultery. In the final scene Emilia exposes his scheme; Iago kills her, is captured, and refuses to explain himself: "From this time forth I never will speak word." Othello wounds him and Villiers/Lodovico orders his torture and imprisonment.

Motives and criticism

Iago offers shifting motives — being passed over for promotion, suspicion that Othello has slept with Emilia, and resentment of Cassio — leading Samuel Taylor Coleridge famously to describe his soliloquies as "the motive-hunting of motiveless malignity". Critics have read Iago variously as a personification of evil, a Machiavel stage-villain, a product of racism and class resentment, or a dramatization of jealousy turned outward. A. C. Bradley and later scholars emphasised his intellectual superiority over the other characters and his intimacy with the audience through asides and soliloquies.

Performance history

The role has attracted major actors, including Edwin Booth, Laurence Olivier, Ian McKellen, Christopher Walken, Mark Rylance and Rory Kinnear. In Shakespeare's source, a tale by Cinthio, the ensign character is unnamed; Shakespeare gave him the Spanish name Iago — the Spanish form of James — associated in the period with Spain, England's adversary. Pronunciation varies between "ee-AH-go" and "YAH-go".
