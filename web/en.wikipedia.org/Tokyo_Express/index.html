Tokyo Express


A Japanese destroyer on a night supply run

The Tokyo Express was the name given by Allied forces to the use of Imperial Japanese Navy ships at night to deliver personnel, supplies and equipment to Japanese forces operating in and around New Guinea and the Solomon Islands during the Pacific theatre of World War II.

The tactic was adopted because Allied air superiority made daylight transport runs suicidal. Fast warships — usually destroyers, whose loads were limited — would race down "the Slot" (New Georgia Sound) from Rabaul or the Shortland Islands after dark, unload at their destination, and be back under friendly air cover by dawn. Supplies were often loaded in drums that were pushed overboard to be picked up by barges or float ashore, since destroyers could not linger.

The runs began during the Guadalcanal campaign in August 1942 and continued through the Solomon Islands campaign. American forces, which called the runs the "Tokyo Express" (Japanese troops called them "rat transportation"), repeatedly attempted to intercept them, producing a series of night battles including Cape Esperance, Tassafaronga, Kula Gulf, Kolombangara and Vella Lavella.

Although the Express kept Japanese garrisons supplied far longer than daylight transport could have, destroyers could not deliver heavy equipment or adequate food, and the strategy contributed to the starvation of Japanese forces. The last runs were made in the autumn of 1943 as Allied control of the seas and air tightened.
