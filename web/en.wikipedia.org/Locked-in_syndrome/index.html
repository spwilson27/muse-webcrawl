Locked-in syndrome (LIS), also known as pseudocoma, is a neurological condition in which a patient is fully conscious and aware but almost completely paralysed, unable to speak or move except— in the classic form—by vertical eye movements and blinking. It is usually caused by damage to the ventral part of the pons in the brainstem, which severs the descending motor pathways while leaving the reticular activating system, and thus consciousness, intact.

Causes

The most common cause is an ischaemic stroke of the basilar artery or a pontine haemorrhage. Other causes include traumatic brain injury, central pontine myelinolysis (often after overly rapid correction of low blood sodium), tumours, encephalitis, and advanced stages of diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, which can produce a comparable state. The term was coined in 1966 by neurologists Fred Plum and Jerome Posner.

Clinical features and classification

A widely used classification distinguishes classic LIS (quadriplegia and anarthria with preserved vertical eye movement and blinking), incomplete LIS (some additional voluntary movement), and total LIS (complete immobility including the eyes, with consciousness demonstrable only by brain imaging or electrophysiology). Because patients appear unresponsive, the condition must be distinguished from coma, persistent vegetative state, and akinetic mutism; misdiagnosis is common, and surveys suggest many patients are first recognized as conscious by family members noticing purposeful eye movements.

Communication and prognosis

Communication is typically established through eye-blink codes—looking up or blinking to answer yes/no questions or to select letters from an alphabet board read aloud. Modern assistive technology adds eye-tracking computers and, experimentally, brain–computer interfaces. The most famous account is The Diving Bell and the Butterfly (1997), dictated letter by letter through blinking by Jean-Dominique Bauby, the French journalist who developed LIS after a massive stroke; he died shortly after its publication. With modern intensive care, many patients survive for years or decades. Recovery of some motor function occurs in a proportion of cases, particularly when the cause is not a completed stroke, and studies of self-reported quality of life in chronic LIS find that a majority of respondents describe themselves as happy—a finding often cited against assumptions that the condition is invariably intolerable.
