Brocade

Brocade is a class of richly decorative shuttle-woven fabrics, often made in coloured silks and sometimes with gold and silver threads. The name comes from the Italian broccato, "embossed cloth", from brocco, "small nail", from Latin broccus, "projecting, pointed". In brocade the pattern is woven into the fabric during production using a supplementary weft, producing a raised, embroidered appearance; traditionally this required a drawloom and considerable skill.

History

Figured silk weaving originated in China, where brocades (such as the jin silks of the Han dynasty) were prized luxury goods traded along the Silk Road. Techniques spread to Persia and the Byzantine Empire, where imperial workshops produced silks for court dress and diplomacy. Medieval and Renaissance Italy — especially Lucca, Florence, Venice, and Genoa — became famous for sumptuous silk brocades worn by the aristocracy and used for ecclesiastical vestments. In India, Banarasi brocade woven with gold and silver zari thread remains central to bridal saris. Japanese nishiki brocade is used for kimono and obi.

Production

Originally woven on drawlooms operated by a weaver and a drawboy who lifted pattern threads, brocade production was revolutionised by the Jacquard loom in the early nineteenth century, whose punched-card control automated pattern selection — a mechanism often cited as a precursor of computing. Modern brocades are woven on computer-controlled looms in silk, synthetic fibres, or blends.

Uses

Brocade has been used for court and ceremonial dress, religious vestments, upholstery, drapery, and formal evening wear. It remains associated with luxury and formality, and appears in wedding gowns, historical costume, and high-fashion collections.
