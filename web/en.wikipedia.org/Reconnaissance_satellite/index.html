Reconnaissance satellite

A reconnaissance satellite or intelligence satellite (commonly, though unofficially, referred to as a spy satellite) is an Earth observation satellite or communications satellite deployed for military or intelligence applications. Essentially space telescopes pointed toward the Earth rather than the stars, they gather imagery and signals intelligence, provide early warning of missile launches, and support nuclear test monitoring and electronic intelligence.

History

The first generation, such as the American CORONA programme (publicly known as Discoverer, 1959–1972) and the Soviet Zenit satellites, photographed territory on film that was returned to Earth in re-entry capsules snatched in mid-air. CORONA's first successful recovery in August 1960 provided more imagery of the USSR than all previous U-2 flights combined. Later systems transmitted images electronically, beginning with the KH-11 KENNEN in 1976, whose descendants use large mirrors comparable to the Hubble Space Telescope and are believed to resolve objects on the order of 10–15 cm.

Types

Reconnaissance satellites are commonly grouped by mission: optical imaging; synthetic-aperture radar imaging (able to see through cloud and at night); signals intelligence (SIGINT) and communications eavesdropping; missile early warning using infrared sensors; and ocean surveillance tracking ships. Major operators include the United States (National Reconnaissance Office), Russia, China, France, Germany, Israel, India and Japan.

Secrecy and disclosure

Such programmes are usually highly classified; the United States did not acknowledge the NRO's existence publicly until 1992, and declassified the CORONA programme in 1995. Modern commercial imagery firms now sell sub-metre resolution imagery to the public, blurring the line between civilian and military capabilities.
