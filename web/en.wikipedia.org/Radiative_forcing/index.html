Radiative forcing quantifies disturbances to Earth's energy balance.

Radiative forcing (or climate forcing) is a measure of how a factor alters the balance of incoming and outgoing energy in the Earth–atmosphere system. It is expressed in watts per square metre (W/m²) and is conventionally defined as the change in net irradiance at the tropopause after allowing stratospheric temperatures to readjust, with surface and tropospheric conditions held fixed. A positive forcing tends to warm the surface; a negative forcing tends to cool it.

Forcing agents

Long-lived greenhouse gases — carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and halogenated gases — produce positive forcing by absorbing outgoing infrared radiation. Aerosols exert a net negative forcing by scattering sunlight and modifying clouds, partly masking greenhouse warming. Natural forcings include changes in solar output and volcanic eruptions, whose sulfate aerosols cool the climate for a few years. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report estimated total human-caused forcing from 1750 to 2019 at about +2.72 W/m².

Use in climate science

Radiative forcing provides a simple first-order predictor of global temperature change through the climate sensitivity parameter: a doubling of atmospheric CO2 gives a forcing of roughly 3.7 W/m². The related concept of effective radiative forcing accounts for rapid adjustments in the troposphere, clouds and land surface, and is now preferred by the IPCC for comparing agents.
