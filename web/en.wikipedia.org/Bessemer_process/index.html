The Bessemer process was the first inexpensive industrial process for the mass production of steel from molten pig iron before the development of the open-hearth furnace. The key principle is the removal of impurities from the iron by oxidation with air blown through the molten metal. The oxidation also raises the temperature of the iron mass and keeps it molten. Named after its inventor, the Englishman Henry Bessemer, who took out a patent in 1856, the process revolutionised steelmaking and the industries that depended on cheap steel.

Operation

The conversion takes place in a large pear-shaped vessel, the Bessemer converter, lined with silica refractory and mounted on trunnions so it can be tilted. A charge of molten pig iron is poured in, the vessel is raised upright, and air is blasted through tuyères in the base. During the "blow", lasting roughly 20 minutes, silicon and manganese oxidise first and are removed as slag; then carbon burns off with a brilliant flame at the converter mouth. When the flame drops, the blow is complete, and measured amounts of carbon and alloys (often as spiegeleisen, a manganese-rich iron) are added to give the desired grade of steel.

History and importance

Before Bessemer, steel was an expensive specialty material made in small batches; afterwards it fell in price dramatically, enabling steel rails that outlasted iron, steel ships, skyscraper frames, and large bridges. The process spread rapidly through Britain, continental Europe, and the United States, where Andrew Carnegie built his steel empire on it. A major limitation was its inability to remove phosphorus; the problem was solved in 1879 by Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and Percy Gilchrist with a basic (dolomite) lining, enabling the use of phosphoric ores. The Bessemer process was progressively displaced in the twentieth century by the open-hearth furnace and, after the 1950s, by basic oxygen steelmaking, which uses pure oxygen rather than air, avoiding nitrogen embrittlement of the steel.
