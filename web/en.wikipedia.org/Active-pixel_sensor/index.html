Active-pixel sensor

Active-pixel sensor (APS)
Type: image sensor
Technology: CMOS
Invented: concept 1960s; practical CMOS form developed at NASA JPL in the 1990s
Applications: phone cameras, webcams, digital cameras, medical and scientific imaging

An active-pixel sensor (APS) is an image sensor, most commonly made in CMOS technology, in which each pixel contains a photodetector and an active amplifier. Unlike passive-pixel sensors, which relied on amplifiers at the edge of the array, the active pixel amplifies the signal within the pixel itself, improving noise performance and readout speed.

History and operation

The concept dates to work by Peter Noble in 1968, but practical devices awaited the development of intra-pixel charge transfer and, later, CMOS fabrication. A team at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory led by Eric Fossum developed the modern CMOS active-pixel sensor in the early 1990s, seeking smaller, lower-power cameras for spacecraft. A typical pixel uses three or four transistors: a reset transistor, a source-follower amplifier, a row-select switch and, in four-transistor designs, a transfer gate enabling correlated double sampling with a pinned photodiode.

Comparison with CCDs

CMOS active-pixel sensors consume far less power than charge-coupled devices (CCDs), can integrate processing electronics on the same chip, allow random access to regions of the array and are cheaper to manufacture in volume. Early CMOS sensors suffered from higher noise and rolling-shutter artefacts, but backside illumination and stacked designs closed the quality gap. By the 2010s CMOS APS had displaced CCDs in almost all consumer imaging, and it dominates scientific imaging as well.
