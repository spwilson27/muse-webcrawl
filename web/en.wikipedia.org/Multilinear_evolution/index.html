Multilinear evolution

Multilinear evolution
FieldAnthropology, archaeology
Key figuresJulian Steward, Leslie White, Marshall Sahlins, Elman Service

Multilinear evolution is a theory of social evolution in anthropology that holds that different societies evolve along different, parallel trajectories rather than through a single universal sequence of stages. It was developed in the mid-20th century, principally by the American anthropologist Julian Steward (1902–1972), in reaction against 19th-century unilinear evolutionism (which ranked all societies on one ladder from "savagery" to "civilization") and the anti-evolutionary particularism of Franz Boas.

Steward argued that similar environments and technologies tend to produce similar cultural adaptations — an approach he called "cultural ecology". Societies in arid river valleys, for example, might independently develop irrigation agriculture, centralised management and state institutions, while societies elsewhere follow different lines adapted to their own conditions. His comparative work examined the parallel "levels of sociocultural integration" reached by Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, Mesoamerica and Peru.

Related schools

Multilinear evolution is one branch of neo-evolutionism, alongside Leslie White's universal evolution (which tracked overall energy use) and the "general" versus "specific" evolution distinguished by Marshall Sahlins and Elman Service. The approach influenced later work on state formation and world-systems, though it was later criticised for environmental determinism and for treating cultures as bounded units.
