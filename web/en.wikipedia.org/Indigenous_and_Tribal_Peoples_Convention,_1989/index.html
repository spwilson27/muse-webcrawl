Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989

ILO Convention No. 169
Adopted27 June 1989
Into force5 September 1991
BodyInternational Labour Organization
SubjectRights of indigenous and tribal peoples

The Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention, 1989 is an International Labour Organization (ILO) convention, numbered C169, and the principal binding international instrument concerning the rights of indigenous and tribal peoples. Adopted in Geneva on 27 June 1989, it revised the earlier Indigenous and Tribal Populations Convention of 1957 (C107), whose integrationist approach had come to be seen as outdated.

Content

The convention requires ratifying states to recognize indigenous peoples' rights to their traditional lands and natural resources, to respect their customs and institutions, and to consult them through representative institutions whenever legislative or administrative measures may affect them directly. It covers land rights, recruitment and employment, vocational training, social security and health, education, and cross-border contacts.

Ratification

About two dozen countries, most of them in Latin America, have ratified the convention, including Mexico, Bolivia, Peru, Colombia, Norway, Denmark, Spain, Nepal, and Fiji. Norway was the first country to ratify. Several states with large indigenous populations, including the United States, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, have not ratified it, though they supported the non-binding UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), for which C169 was an important precursor.

Significance

The convention's concepts of self-identification, consultation, and—interpreted in light of later instruments—free, prior and informed consent have influenced national constitutions, court decisions, and development-bank safeguards across Latin America and beyond.
