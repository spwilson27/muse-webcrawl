Inverter

InputDirect current (DC)
OutputAlternating current (AC)
Key componentsTransistors (IGBTs, MOSFETs), control circuitry
WaveformsSquare, modified sine, pure sine
UsesSolar power, UPS, motor drives, EVs

An inverter (or power inverter) is an electronic device or circuitry that changes direct current (DC) to alternating current (AC). The resulting AC frequency depends on the device employed. Inverters do the opposite of rectifiers, which convert AC to DC.

Operation

Modern inverters use semiconductor switches — typically IGBTs or MOSFETs arranged in an H-bridge — that are switched on and off at high frequency to synthesize an AC waveform from a DC source. Pulse-width modulation (PWM) shapes the output so that, after filtering, it closely approximates a sine wave.

Waveform types

The simplest inverters produce a square wave, suitable only for tolerant loads. Modified sine wave inverters add steps that approximate the RMS value of a sine wave and run most appliances adequately. Pure sine wave inverters reproduce utility-quality power and are required for sensitive electronics and many motor loads.

Applications

Inverters are essential in photovoltaic systems, converting DC from solar panels to grid-compatible AC; grid-tie inverters synchronize with the utility and include anti-islanding protection. They are also used in uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), variable-frequency drives for motors, induction heating, electric vehicle traction systems, and portable power from vehicle batteries.

Efficiency and ratings

Quality inverters achieve 90–97% conversion efficiency. Ratings specify continuous and surge power in watts, input voltage (commonly 12, 24 or 48 V DC for battery systems), and output voltage and frequency (e.g. 120 V at 60 Hz in North America, 230 V at 50 Hz in much of the rest of the world).
