Apoptosis (from Ancient Greek apóptōsis, "falling off" — as of leaves) is a form of programmed cell death that occurs in multicellular organisms. It is a highly regulated process in which a cell activates an intracellular programme that dismantles it in an orderly fashion, so that its contents can be removed by phagocytes without provoking inflammation. Between 50 and 70 billion cells die each day by apoptosis in the average adult human.

Mechanism

Apoptosis is executed by a family of cysteine proteases called caspases. The intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway is triggered by internal stresses such as DNA damage: pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins (Bax, Bak) permeabilise the mitochondrial outer membrane, releasing cytochrome c, which assembles the apoptosome and activates caspase-9. The extrinsic pathway begins when extracellular ligands such as FasL or TNF bind death receptors, activating caspase-8. Both converge on executioner caspases (3, 6, 7), which cleave cellular substrates, fragment DNA, and produce the characteristic morphology: cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and the formation of apoptotic bodies.

Biological significance

Apoptosis sculpts tissues during development — separating the digits, shaping the nervous system, and deleting self-reactive lymphocytes — and maintains tissue homeostasis in adults. The genetic pathway was elucidated in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans by Sydney Brenner, H. Robert Horvitz, and John Sulston (Nobel Prize, 2002). Defective apoptosis contributes to disease: too little permits cancer and autoimmunity (e.g. mutations in the tumour suppressor p53, which normally promotes apoptosis of damaged cells), while excessive apoptosis contributes to neurodegeneration and ischaemic damage.
