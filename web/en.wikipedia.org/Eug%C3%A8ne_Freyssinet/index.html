Eugène Freyssinet (13 July 1879 – 8 June 1962) was a French structural engineer and pioneer of prestressed concrete. After early work on reinforced-concrete arch bridges — including the Pont du Veurdre and the great Plougastel bridge (1930), whose three 186-metre arches were then records — he turned to understanding why concrete loses stress over time.

Prestressed concrete

Freyssinet's insight was that creep and shrinkage could be overcome by pre-compressing concrete with high-strength steel tendons tensioned beyond the elastic limit, so that the concrete remains in compression under load. His 1928 patent established the basis of modern prestressing, and his company developed post-tensioning systems, including the Freyssinet anchorage, used in bridges, hangars, dams and nuclear containment vessels worldwide.

Among his notable works are the Orly airport hangars' parabolic thin shells and the Luzancy bridge. His methods made long-span concrete construction economical and transformed twentieth-century civil engineering.
