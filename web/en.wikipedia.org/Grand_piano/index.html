A grand piano is a type of piano in which the frame and strings are horizontal, with the strings extending away from the keyboard. The lid can be propped open so that sound projects outward, and the long strings and large soundboard give the grand a richer, more powerful tone than the upright (vertical) piano. Grand pianos are the standard instrument of the concert hall.

Construction

Inside the wing-shaped case, more than 200 steel strings — wound with copper in the bass — are stretched under a total tension of some 20 tonnes over a cast-iron plate. Hammers strike the strings and fall back by gravity, allowing very rapid repetition of notes; the escapement action perfected by Sébastien Érard in 1821 made the modern action possible. Three pedals provide sustain (damper), soft (una corda), and sostenuto functions.

History and types

The piano was invented by Bartolomeo Cristofori in Florence around 1700; grand-shaped instruments were built throughout the 18th century by makers such as Gottfried Silbermann and the Viennese school, while firms including Steinway & Sons, Bösendorfer, Bechstein, and later Yamaha and Kawai defined the modern concert grand in the 19th and 20th centuries. Grands are described by length: baby grand, medium grand, parlor grand, semi-concert, and concert grand (about 9 feet).
