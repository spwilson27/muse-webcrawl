Dal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dal (also spelled daal or dhal; from Sanskrit दल्, "to split") is a term used in the Indian subcontinent for dried, split pulses (lentils, peas, and beans) that do not require pre-soaking, and for the thick stew or soup prepared from them. Dal is a staple across India, Pakistan, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, providing a principal source of protein in largely vegetarian diets.

Varieties

Common dals include toor dal (split pigeon peas, yellow), chana dal (split chickpeas), masoor dal (red lentils), moong dal (split mung beans, yellow or green), and urad dal (split black gram), the latter used whole-black in dal makhani and split in idli and dosa batters. Whole (unsplit) pulses are distinguished in naming, though usage varies by region.

Preparation

Pulses are boiled — often in a pressure cooker — with water, turmeric, and salt until soft, then finished with a tadka (chaunk): whole and ground spices such as cumin, mustard seed, asafoetida, dried red chillies, garlic, and curry leaves fried in ghee or oil and poured over the dal. Regional styles range from the thin, sweet-sour Gujarati dal and tangy Bengali preparations to rich, slow-cooked Punjabi dal makhani with butter and cream.

Nutrition and accompaniment

Dal is rich in protein, complex carbohydrates, folate, and iron, and is traditionally eaten with rice (dal bhat), roti, or naan, often alongside vegetables, pickles, and yogurt. The rice-and-dal combination provides complementary amino acids.
