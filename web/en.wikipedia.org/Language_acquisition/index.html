Language acquisition

Language acquisition is the process by which humans acquire the capacity to perceive and comprehend language, as well as to produce and use words and sentences to communicate. It is studied in linguistics, psychology, cognitive science and neuroscience. The term usually refers to first-language acquisition — infants' acquisition of their native language — as distinct from second-language acquisition by older children and adults.

Stages

Infants typically move through cooing and babbling in the first year, a one-word (holophrastic) stage, two-word combinations, and then telegraphic speech, gaining vocabulary explosively and mastering grammar over the preschool years. Children typically produce their first words around 12 months and combine words by about two years.

Theories

Behaviourist accounts (Skinner) emphasised imitation and reinforcement; Noam Chomsky argued that children are born with an innate capacity — often described as a universal grammar or language acquisition device — because the input is too impoverished to explain the speed and uniformity of development (the "poverty of the stimulus" argument). Usage-based and social-interactionist theories emphasise statistical learning, joint attention and communication with caregivers.

Critical period

Evidence from cases of extreme deprivation, and from second-language learning, suggests a critical or sensitive period after which native-like acquisition becomes much harder.
