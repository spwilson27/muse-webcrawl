Avalanche

A powder avalanche: large avalanches can reach speeds of 300 km/h.

An avalanche (also called a snowslide) is a rapid flow of snow down a slope, such as a hill or mountain. Avalanches can be triggered naturally, by increases in the load on a snowpack from new snowfall or warming, or by external forces including skiers, snowmobiles, animals and controlled explosives. They are among the most serious hazards in mountain terrain, killing on average about 150 people a year worldwide.

Types

Slab avalanches, the most deadly type, occur when a cohesive layer of snow breaks away as a single block along a weak layer within the snowpack. Loose-snow (point-release) avalanches fan out from a single point and usually involve less snow. A powder avalanche develops a turbulent cloud of suspended snow that can outrun the denser core. Wet avalanches, common in spring, move more slowly but with great force, and glide avalanches involve the entire snowpack sliding on the ground.

Formation and terrain

Most large avalanches release on slopes of 30–45 degrees. The structure of the snowpack — layers of differing hardness and crystal type, such as depth hoar or surface hoar — determines its stability, and forecasters assess it from weather history, snow pits and tests. Warning signs include recent snowfall or wind loading, cracking and collapsing sounds in the snowpack, and recent avalanche activity on similar slopes.

Safety and control

Backcountry travellers manage risk with avalanche forecasts, route choice, and rescue equipment: transceivers, shovels, probes and increasingly airbag packs; survival depends heavily on rescue within about 15 minutes. Mountain communities and ski areas mitigate the hazard with land-use zoning, defensive structures such as snow fences, sheds and dams, reforestation, and preventive release using explosives delivered by hand, artillery or fixed gas systems.

Notable avalanches

The Winter of Terror of 1950–51 brought some 650 avalanches in the Alps and 265 deaths. In 1970 an avalanche triggered by the Ancash earthquake buried the Peruvian town of Yungay, killing around 20,000 people in combination with the landslide. The 1910 Wellington avalanche in Washington State, which swept two trains into a canyon and killed 96, remains the deadliest in United States history.
