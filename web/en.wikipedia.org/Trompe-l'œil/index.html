Trompe-l'œil

Trompe-l'œil aims to deceive the eye into mistaking a painting for reality.

Trompe-l'œil (French: [tʀõp lœj]; from trompe "to deceive" and l'œil "the eye") is an artistic term for the highly realistic optical illusion of three-dimensional space and objects on a two-dimensional surface. Trompe-l'œil, which is most often associated with painting, seeks to deceive the viewer into mistaking painted objects for real ones.

History

The illusionistic tradition is ancient: Pliny the Elder tells of the Greek painters Zeuxis, who painted grapes so convincingly that birds pecked at them, and Parrhasius, whose painted curtain fooled Zeuxis himself. Renaissance artists, armed with linear perspective, produced celebrated illusions such as Andrea Mantegna's ceiling di sotto in sù in Mantua and Andrea Pozzo's ceiling of Sant'Ignazio in Rome, where painted architecture extends the real building into a heavenly vista.

Later developments

In 17th-century Dutch painting, artists such as Cornelius Norbertus Gijsbrechts and Samuel Dirksz van Hoogstraten painted letter racks, cupboard doors and the backs of paintings with playful literal deception; Pere Borrell del Caso's Escaping Criticism (1874) shows a boy climbing out of the frame. Nineteenth-century American painters including William Harnett made trompe-l'œil a specialty. The technique also survives in architecture (painted facades and false windows), theatre scenery, pavement art, and forced perspective in theme parks and film.
