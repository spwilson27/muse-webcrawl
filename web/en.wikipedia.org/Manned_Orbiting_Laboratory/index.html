Manned Orbiting Laboratory

The Manned Orbiting Laboratory (MOL) was part of the United States Air Force's manned spaceflight programme, a follow-on to the unfulfilled military space station concept of the X-20 Dyna-Soar. The project was developed from early USAF concepts of crewed reconnaissance satellites and was publicly announced in December 1963. MOL was designed as a small space station in polar orbit, crewed by two astronauts launched in a modified Gemini capsule (Gemini B) atop the laboratory on a Titan IIIM rocket. Its primary mission, classified at the time, was photographic reconnaissance of the Soviet Union using a large KH-10 Dorian telescope camera.

Development

Seventeen astronauts were selected in three groups for the programme, including Robert Crippen, Richard Truly and a young Karol Bobko; plans called for 30-day missions in which the crew would operate the telescope and return exposed film in the Gemini B capsule. An uncrewed test flight (OPS 0855) on 3 November 1966 successfully flew a refurbished Gemini capsule with a mock-up laboratory built from a Titan II tank. The programme's launch complex, SLC-6 at Vandenberg Air Force Base, was built for polar MOL launches.

Cancellation and legacy

Escalating costs, delays and the improving capability of uncrewed reconnaissance satellites such as the KH-8 Gambit undermined the case for MOL. The programme was cancelled in June 1969, having spent about $1.56 billion without a crewed flight. Seven MOL astronauts transferred to NASA and all flew on the Space Shuttle; Truly later became NASA Administrator. SLC-6 was eventually rebuilt for Shuttle launches from the West Coast, which never occurred, and later for Delta IV rockets. Declassified in 2015, MOL documents confirmed the reconnaissance role long hidden behind the public description of the station as a laboratory for military experiments.
