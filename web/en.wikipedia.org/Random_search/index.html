Random search

Random search is a family of numerical optimisation methods that do not require the gradient of the problem to be optimised, and can hence be used on nonlinear, non-convex or discontinuous functions. Introduced by Anderssen and others and popularised in control engineering by Rastrigin and in computing contexts by Brooks (1958) and Karnopp, random search samples candidate solutions from a probability distribution over the search space (or a neighbourhood of the current best point) and moves to improved candidates.

Basic method

In its simplest form, random search samples points uniformly (or from another distribution) over the feasible region, evaluates the objective, and keeps the best found. Local variants sample within a hypersphere around the current iterate; if a better point is found the centre moves there, and the radius may be adapted — increased after successes, decreased after failures. Luus and Jaakola (1973) proposed a widely cited version in which the search region shrinks over iterations.

Properties and variants

Under mild conditions, pure random search converges to the global optimum with probability one as iterations go to infinity, though it may be slow in high dimensions. Random search is simple, parallelises easily, and serves as a baseline for derivative-free optimisation. Related methods include stochastic hill climbing, simulated annealing and stochastic approximation. Random search has also attracted attention in machine learning for hyperparameter optimisation, where it often outperforms grid search when only a few hyperparameters strongly affect performance.

Applications

Applications include engineering design, process control, training of neural networks, and optimisation of functions available only through simulation or experiment.
