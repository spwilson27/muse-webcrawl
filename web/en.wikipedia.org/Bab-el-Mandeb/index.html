The Bab-el-Mandeb (Arabic: باب المندب, "Gate of Tears") is the strait connecting the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden and the Indian Ocean. It separates Yemen on the Arabian Peninsula from Djibouti and Eritrea on the Horn of Africa. Together with the Suez Canal at the Red Sea's northern end, it forms one of the world's most important shipping chokepoints: a large share of trade between Europe and Asia, including several million barrels of oil per day, passes through it.

Geography

The strait is about 26 kilometres wide at its narrowest. The island of Perim divides it into two channels: the eastern channel, Bab Iskender, about 3 km wide, and the western channel, Dact-el-Mayun, about 25 km wide, which carries most shipping. Traffic separation schemes route vessels in designated lanes.

History

The strait's name is linked to the dangers of navigation there — strong currents, reefs and, in legend, an earthquake that separated Asia from Africa. In prehistory, fluctuating sea levels made the strait a plausible crossing point for early humans out of Africa. Britain occupied Perim from 1857 and Aden from 1839, guarding the route to India; after the opening of the Suez Canal (1869) the strait became a vital imperial artery.

Strategic importance

Because alternative routes require the long voyage around the Cape of Good Hope, the Bab-el-Mandeb is watched closely in every regional conflict. Piracy from Somalia in the 2000s, the Yemen civil war, and attacks on commercial shipping in 2023–24 — which forced many carriers to reroute around Africa — have repeatedly demonstrated its significance to global trade. Foreign navies, including those of the United States, France, China and Japan, maintain facilities in Djibouti near the strait.
