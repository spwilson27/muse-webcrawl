Cobra effect

The cobra effect is the phenomenon in which an attempted solution to a problem makes the problem worse—a type of unintended consequence that occurs when incentives reward the wrong behavior. The term comes from an anecdote set in colonial India and is closely associated with the concept of perverse incentives.

Origin of the term

According to the story, the British government, concerned about the number of venomous cobras in Delhi, offered a bounty for every dead cobra. Initially the scheme reduced cobra numbers, but enterprising locals began breeding cobras in order to collect the reward. When the authorities discovered this and ended the program, the breeders released their now-worthless snakes, and the wild cobra population ended up larger than before. A similar story is told of a rat bounty in French colonial Hanoi, where bounty hunters clipped rats' tails and released the animals to breed more.

Usage

The term was popularized by the German economist Horst Siebert, who used it in discussing perverse incentives in policy and economics. It is invoked whenever a rule or reward predictably encourages behavior opposite to its intent—for example, paying for a metric that can be gamed, or banning something in a way that increases its production. The cobra effect is a standard cautionary example in discussions of mechanism design and performance measurement, related to Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.
