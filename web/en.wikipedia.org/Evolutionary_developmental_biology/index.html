Evolutionary developmental biology

Evolutionary developmental biology, informally evo-devo, is a field of biological research that compares the developmental processes of different organisms to infer how developmental processes evolved, and how changes in development generate the morphological diversity of life. It addresses how embryonic development is controlled by genes, and how modifications of development — in timing, spatial pattern, or gene regulation — produce new body plans, novelties, and adaptations.

Key discoveries

The field crystallised in the 1980s with the discovery of the homeobox — a conserved DNA sequence in genes that pattern the body axis — first in the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster and then across virtually all animals. The Hox genes that specify segment identity are arranged on the chromosome in the same order as the body regions they control, and are strikingly conserved between flies, mice, and humans. Equally surprising was the discovery of "deep homology": the same toolkit genes, such as Pax6 in eye development, pattern structures long considered independently evolved in distant lineages.

Concepts

Evo-devo emphasises that evolution largely tinkers with a conserved genetic toolkit — transcription factors and signalling pathways — by changing cis-regulatory elements that switch genes on and off in particular places and times. Other central ideas include modularity of body parts, heterochrony (shifts in developmental timing), developmental plasticity, and the structure of gene regulatory networks, including "kernels" that pattern body plans and are refractory to change. These ideas extend the modern synthesis by explaining not just the sorting of variation but how variation itself is generated.
