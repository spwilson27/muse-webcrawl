Decisive moment

The decisive moment (French: images à la sauvette, "images on the run") is a concept in photography popularised by Henri Cartier-Bresson in his 1952 book The Decisive Moment. It describes the instant when the visual and psychological elements of a scene come together spontaneously and briefly, and the photographer captures that fleeting alignment.

Origin

Cartier-Bresson took the phrase from a line by Cardinal de Retz: "There is nothing in this world that does not have a decisive moment." His book, published in French as Images à la sauvette, collected photographs made over two decades of travel and established the idea as a foundation of street photography and photojournalism.

Technique

Practising the decisive moment requires anticipation, patience and quick reflexes: the photographer pre-visualises a composition and waits for the subject to complete it, rather than directing the scene. Cartier-Bresson characteristically shot with a 35 mm Leica rangefinder and a 50 mm lens, and he opposed cropping his images afterwards.

Influence and criticism

The concept shaped generations of documentary photographers associated with Magnum Photos, which Cartier-Bresson co-founded. Later critics have questioned whether spontaneity is always achievable or desirable, and staged and conceptual photography movements partly defined themselves against it.
