Glycosidic bond


The β(1→4) glycosidic bond of lactose, joining galactose to glucose.

In molecular biology and biochemistry, a glycosidic bond or glycosidic linkage is a type of covalent bond that joins a carbohydrate (sugar) molecule to another group, which may or may not be another carbohydrate. A glycosidic bond is formed between the hemiacetal or hemiketal group of a saccharide (or a molecule derived from a saccharide) and the hydroxyl group of some compound such as an alcohol. A substance containing a glycosidic bond is a glycoside.

Nomenclature

Glycosidic bonds are labelled according to the identities of the atoms involved and the numbers of the carbons they connect. An O-glycosidic bond links through an oxygen, as in disaccharides and polysaccharides; N-glycosidic bonds link sugars to nitrogen atoms, as between ribose and the bases in nucleosides; S- and C-glycosidic bonds use sulfur and carbon respectively. The bond is also described by the anomeric configuration — α or β — describing the relative stereochemistry at the anomeric and reference carbons, and by the carbon numbers, e.g. β(1→4).

Types and examples

Maltose consists of two glucose units joined by an α(1→4) bond; lactose joins galactose and glucose by a β(1→4) bond; sucrose joins glucose and fructose through an α,β(1↔2) bond involving both anomeric carbons. The polysaccharides starch and glycogen are mostly α(1→4) glucans (with α(1→6) branch points in amylopectin and glycogen), while cellulose is a β(1→4) glucan whose straight chains hydrogen-bond into strong fibres. Humans digest the α-linkages of starch with amylases but lack cellulases for the β-linkages of cellulose, which is the chemical basis of dietary fibre.

Formation and cleavage

In living systems glycosidic bonds are formed by glycosyltransferases using activated sugar donors such as UDP-glucose, with loss of water formally a condensation. They are broken by hydrolysis catalysed by glycoside hydrolases (glycosidases) such as amylase, lactase, and lysozyme — lysozyme's cleavage of the β(1→4) bonds in bacterial peptidoglycan is a classic enzyme mechanism taught in biochemistry. Acid can also hydrolyse glycosidic bonds non-enzymatically.

Biological significance

Glycosidic linkages define the structure and digestibility of carbohydrates, the N-glycosidic bonds of DNA and RNA (whose spontaneous hydrolysis — depurination — is a constant source of DNA damage repaired by base-excision repair), and the sugar modifications of proteins (N- and O-linked glycosylation) and lipids that govern cell recognition, immunity, and signalling.
