Clinker (boat building)

Clinker built (also known as lapstrake) is a method of boat building in which the edges of hull planks overlap each other and are fastened together along the overlap. Where the planking is joined along the laps with iron rivets over roves, the technique is sometimes called clench building. It produces a light, strong, flexible hull whose stepped surface is a distinctive visual feature.

History

The technique developed in northern Europe and is famously associated with Scandinavian boatbuilding: the great Viking-age ships, including the Gokstad and Oseberg ships and the knarr and longship types, were clinker built, as were medieval cogs in modified form. In Britain the method became standard for traditional working craft and lifeboats; the Royal National Lifeboat Institution's pulling lifeboats were typically clinker built in mahogany.

Technique

The builder sets up the keel and stems, then fastens successive strakes working upward, each overlapping the one below by about an inch and riveted to it as well as to the timbers (frames) fitted inside afterward. The overlapping lands add longitudinal stiffness, allowing thinner planking than the smooth, flush-laid carvel method, which suited lightly built rowing and sailing craft. Modern lapstrake construction often substitutes marine plywood and epoxy bonding for riveted solid timber, and the term is used loosely even for simulated lapstrake in fibreglass hulls.
