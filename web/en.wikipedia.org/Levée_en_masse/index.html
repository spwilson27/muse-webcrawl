Levée en masse (French for "mass levy" or "mass uprising") is a term for the mass conscription of all able-bodied citizens for military service, most famously decreed by the French National Convention on 23 August 1793 during the French Revolutionary Wars. It is regarded as a foundational moment in the development of modern conscription and of "total war," linking military service to citizenship.

The decree of 1793

Facing invasion by the First Coalition and internal revolt in the Vendée, the Convention, on the report of Bertrand Barère, declared that "from this moment until that in which the enemy shall have been driven from the soil of the Republic, all Frenchmen are in permanent requisition for the service of the armies." Young men unmarried between 18 and 25 were called first; married men were to forge arms and transport provisions, women to make tents and clothing, children to shred linen for bandages, and old men to exhort the soldiers in public squares.

Effects

The decree raised an army of unprecedented size — by 1794 France had perhaps 750,000 to 800,000 men under arms, organised in part through the amalgame of regulars and volunteers. The enlarged armies, administered by representatives on mission and the Committee of Public Safety under Lazare Carnot — the "Organiser of Victory" — won the victories that saved the Republic, including Fleurus in June 1794.

Legacy

The levée en masse transformed warfare from the limited contests of professional armies to conflicts between nations in arms, and it inspired later systems of universal conscription, including the Jourdan Law of 1798 and the mass armies of the 19th and 20th centuries.
