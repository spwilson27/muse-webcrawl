Siegfried Line

The Siegfried Line, known in German as the Westwall, was a German defensive line built opposite the French Maginot Line during the late 1930s. It stretched more than 630 km (390 mi) — from Kleve on the Dutch border, along the western frontier of the German Reich, to Weil am Rhein on the Swiss border — and featured more than 18,000 bunkers, tunnels, and tank traps.

Construction

Named by the Allies after the Siegfried position of the First World War (part of the Hindenburg Line), the Westwall was built in phases between 1936 and 1940 on Hitler's orders, employing at its peak nearly half a million workers of the Organisation Todt. Its characteristic "dragon's teeth" (Höckerhindernisse) rows of concrete pyramids were designed to impede tanks.

World War II

Neglected and partly stripped after the fall of France in 1940, the line was hurriedly re-armed in 1944 as the Western Allies approached Germany. Between September 1944 and early 1945 American forces fought costly battles to breach it — around Aachen, in the Hürtgen Forest, and along the Roer — before operations such as Veritable, Grenade, and Lumberjack broke through into the Rhineland in February–March 1945. The popular Allied song "We're Going to Hang out the Washing on the Siegfried Line" dated from the "Phoney War" of 1939–40.

Aftermath

Most fortifications were demolished after the war, though some bunkers survive, and dragon's-teeth sections remain as wildlife refuges, including parts of a "green belt" of protected habitat along the former line.
