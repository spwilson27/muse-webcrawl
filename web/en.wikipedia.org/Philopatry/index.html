Philopatry

Philopatry is the tendency of an organism to stay in or return to its home area, typically the place of its birth. In ecology it is studied as natal philopatry — the return of breeding adults to their natal site — and as breeding philopatry, the reuse of a previous breeding site. The word derives from Greek roots meaning "love of the fatherland."

Occurrence

Philopatry is famously exhibited by female sea turtles, which cross oceans to nest on the beaches where they hatched, probably guided by geomagnetic imprinting, and by salmon, which return to their natal streams to spawn. Many migratory birds return to the same breeding territories each year, and marine mammals such as elephant seals and fur seals return to natal colonies.

Causes and consequences

Returning to a known site offers advantages: the animal knows local food resources, refuges, and conditions, and the natal habitat has demonstrably permitted successful reproduction. The costs include inbreeding and reduced colonization of new habitat. Strong philopatry produces fine-scale genetic structure within species — female lineages in particular may diverge between nearby colonies — and it complicates conservation, because a lost breeding site may not be recolonized even if suitable habitat exists elsewhere.

Related concepts

Philopatry is the opposite pole of dispersal in population ecology; most species show a mixture, with one sex often more philopatric than the other. In primates and many mammals, males disperse while females remain in their natal groups — female philopatry — shaping kinship and social structure.
