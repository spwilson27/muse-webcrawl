Treaty of Fort Jackson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Treaty of Fort Jackson (also known as the Treaty with the Creeks, 1814) was signed on 9 August 1814 at Fort Jackson in present-day Alabama, near the confluence of the Coosa and Tallapoosa rivers. It concluded the Creek War (1813–1814), a conflict within the Muscogee (Creek) Nation and part of the wider War of 1812, in which forces under Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Stick faction at the Battle of Horseshoe Bend.

Terms

By the treaty, the Creek Nation — including chiefs who had fought alongside the United States — ceded some 23 million acres (about half of Creek territory) in present-day Alabama and Georgia to the United States. The boundary line was drawn by Jackson's negotiators; the cession opened large areas to white settlement and compensation claims by the U.S. government for the costs of the war.

Consequences

The treaty embittered many Creeks, including U.S. allies, who received no exemption from the cession. It paved the way for the removal of the southeastern nations in the 1830s and for Alabama's rapid cotton-driven settlement. Jackson's role bolstered his national fame and subsequent political career.
