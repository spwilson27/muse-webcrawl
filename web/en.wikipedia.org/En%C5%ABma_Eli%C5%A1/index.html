Enūma Eliš

The Enūma Eliš (Akkadian Cuneiform: e nūma e liš, "when on high") is the Babylonian creation myth, named from its opening words. It was recovered from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh in the 19th century and is recorded on seven clay tablets in Akkadian, probably composed in the second millennium BC.

Content

The poem describes the primordial waters Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water), from whom the generations of gods descend. When the younger gods disturb Apsu, a conflict ensues; eventually the young god Marduk, champion of the gods, defeats Tiamat, splits her body to form heaven and earth, orders the cosmos, and creates humankind from the blood of the rebel god Kingu to serve the gods. The text culminates in the founding of Babylon and the recitation of Marduk's fifty names.

Religious function

The epic legitimised Marduk's supremacy within the Babylonian pantheon and Babylon's political ascendancy; it was recited during the Akitu (New Year) festival. Some versions from Assyria substitute the god Ashur for Marduk.

Parallels

Scholars have long debated parallels between Enūma Eliš and the creation account in Genesis — especially the primordial waters, the division of the waters, and the sequence of creation — as part of a broader ancient Near Eastern tradition, though direct borrowing remains contested.
