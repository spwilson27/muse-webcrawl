Comparative politics

Comparative politics is a field of political science that studies political systems, institutions, and behaviour by comparing them across countries. Rather than a distinct subject matter, it is defined by its comparative method: scholars ask why similar countries differ (or different countries resemble each other) in areas such as democratisation, party systems, voting behaviour, welfare states, and revolutions.

Approaches

Classic approaches include structural functionalism, systems theory, and the study of political culture (Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba's The Civic Culture, 1963). Later schools include rational choice institutionalism, historical institutionalism, and political economy. Research designs range from large-N statistical studies and regime indices (Polity, Freedom House, the Varieties of Democracy project) to paired case studies and "most similar" and "most different" systems designs.

Subfields

Major topics include democratisation and authoritarianism, electoral systems and parties, legislatures and executives, federalism, nationalism and ethnic politics, contentious politics and social movements, and the political economy of development and welfare.
