Mycobacterium

Domain: Bacteria
Phylum: Actinomycetota
Family: Mycobacteriaceae
Type species: Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Notable species: M. tuberculosis, M. leprae, M. ulcerans, M. avium

Mycobacterium is a genus of over 190 species of aerobic, mostly non-motile bacteria in the family Mycobacteriaceae. They are characterised by exceptionally lipid-rich cell walls containing mycolic acids, which make them acid-fast — retaining carbol fuchsin stain despite decolourisation with acid-alcohol in the Ziehl–Neelsen method — and resistant to many disinfectants and antibiotics. The genus includes some of humanity's oldest scourges.

Important pathogens

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex — causes tuberculosis, one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide, spread by airborne droplets and capable of lifelong latency.
  • M. leprae and M. lepromatosis — cause leprosy (Hansen's disease); M. leprae has never been grown in artificial culture and grows extremely slowly in the mouse footpad and armadillos.
  • M. ulcerans — causes Buruli ulcer, a necrotising skin disease, via the toxin mycolactone.
  • Nontuberculous mycobacteria such as the M. avium complex and M. abscessus — environmental organisms causing lung disease, especially in people with structural lung disease or immune suppression, and notoriously drug-resistant infections.

Characteristics and classification

Mycobacteria are slender, slightly curved rods. They grow slowly — M. tuberculosis divides roughly once a day, versus twenty minutes for many bacteria — owing partly to their impermeable wall and single rRNA operon; historically they are grouped as rapid growers (visible colonies in under seven days) and slow growers. Their waxy cell envelope, built of mycolic acids linked to arabinogalactan and peptidoglycan, underlies both acid-fastness and intrinsic drug resistance, and is the target of key anti-tuberculosis drugs such as isoniazid and ethambutol. Most species are harmless environmental saprophytes found in soil and water.
