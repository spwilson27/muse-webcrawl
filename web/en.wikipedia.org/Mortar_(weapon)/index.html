A mortar is a short-barrelled artillery weapon that fires explosive projectiles (mortar bombs) at low velocities, short ranges, and high, arcing trajectories. It is typically muzzle-loading and consists of a smooth-bore metal tube on a baseplate, supported by a bipod. Because of its simplicity, light weight, and ability to fire from defilade behind cover, the mortar is the primary indirect-fire support weapon of infantry units.

Design and operation

The classic infantry mortar comprises three parts: the barrel (tube), the baseplate that absorbs recoil, and the bipod that supports and aims the tube. A bomb is dropped down the muzzle; a firing pin at the base ignites its propellant charge, launching it in a high arc so that it descends steeply onto the target. This steep angle of fall lets mortars strike targets behind hills, buildings, and trench lines that flat-trajectory guns cannot reach. Range is adjusted by changing elevation and by adding or removing incremental propellant charges.

History

Weapons called mortars — short, wide siege pieces firing shells in high arcs — existed from the 15th century. The modern portable mortar was developed during World War I for trench warfare, notably the British Stokes mortar of 1915, whose design principles (smooth bore, fin-stabilised bomb, drop-firing) became the template for nearly all subsequent infantry mortars. Mortars were used extensively in World War II, Korea, Vietnam, and every major conflict since.

Types

Light mortars (typically 60 mm) are carried and operated by small infantry teams. Medium mortars (81 mm or 82 mm) serve at battalion level, while heavy mortars (120 mm) may be towed or mounted in armoured vehicles and fire bombs of roughly 16 kg to ranges exceeding 7 km. Ammunition includes high-explosive, smoke, and illumination rounds.
