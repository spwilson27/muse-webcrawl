The quadrivium (Latin: "four ways") is the upper division of the seven liberal arts of medieval education, comprising the four mathematical disciplines: arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. It followed the elementary trivium — grammar, logic and rhetoric — and formed, with it, the curriculum of the medieval university arts faculty, preliminary to the higher studies of medicine, law and theology.

Origins

The grouping descends from Plato's Republic, which prescribed arithmetic, geometry, astronomy and harmonics for the education of philosopher-rulers, and from Pythagorean ideas of number as the key to the cosmos. The term itself is attributed to Boethius (c. 480–524), whose textbooks on arithmetic and music, drawing on Nicomachus and Euclid, transmitted the scheme to the Latin West; Cassiodorus and Isidore of Seville further codified it.

The four arts

  • Arithmetic — number in itself (number theory, ratios and proportions)
  • Geometry — magnitude at rest, following Euclid's Elements, including geography and surveying
  • Music — number in time: harmonic theory (musica mundana, humana, instrumentalis) rather than performance
  • Astronomy — magnitude in motion: celestial spheres and computation, using Ptolemy and calendar reckoning (computus)

In the medieval scheme, the quadrivium taught students to see mathematical order as the structure of God's creation, and its framework shaped European education into the early modern period; its legacy survives in the modern concept of STEM-like mathematical curricula and in liberal-arts education.
