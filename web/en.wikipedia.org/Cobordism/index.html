Cobordism

In mathematics, cobordism is an equivalence relation on the class of compact manifolds of the same dimension, defined by the possibility of joining two manifolds together through a higher-dimensional one whose boundary is their disjoint union. Two manifolds are said to be cobordant if together they bound such a manifold; cobordism classes can be added by disjoint union, forming cobordism groups that have become central objects in algebraic topology.

Definition and examples

Formally, an (n+1)-dimensional manifold W is a cobordism between n-manifolds M and N if the boundary of W is the disjoint union of M and N (with orientation conventions where appropriate). The relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. A pair of pants—a sphere with three boundary circles—is the classic cobordism between one circle and two circles. Any closed manifold that is itself the boundary of another manifold is called null-cobordant.

Cobordism groups

René Thom's celebrated work in the 1950s showed how to compute the unoriented and complex cobordism groups using homotopy theory, work that earned him the Fields Medal. The oriented cobordism ring was partly computed by Thom and later by John Milnor and Sergei Novikov. Cobordism invariants, such as characteristic numbers, determine when two manifolds are cobordant in many cases.

Applications

Cobordism theory underlies the classification of manifolds, the proof of the generalized Poincaré conjecture in high dimensions via the h-cobordism theorem, and, in physics, the classification of topological phases of matter and the structure of topological quantum field theories, which can be described as functors out of a cobordism category.
