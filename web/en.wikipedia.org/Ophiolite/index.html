Ophiolite

An ophiolite sequence exposes a slice of oceanic crust and upper mantle thrust onto continental crust.

An ophiolite is a section of Earth's oceanic crust and the underlying upper mantle that has been uplifted and exposed above sea level and often emplaced onto continental crustal rocks. The term derives from the Greek ophio (snake) and lithos (stone), referring to the green, snake-like appearance of some of its rocks.

Ideal sequence

A complete ophiolite sequence, from bottom to top, consists of mantle peridotites (often serpentinized), layered gabbros, massive gabbros, a sheeted dyke complex, and pillow basalts, typically capped by deep-sea sediments such as chert. This layering mirrors the structure of oceanic lithosphere produced at mid-ocean ridges.

Significance

Ophiolites provided crucial evidence for the theory of plate tectonics and seafloor spreading, because they preserve fragments of ancient ocean floor on land where they can be studied directly. Famous examples include the Troodos Ophiolite in Cyprus, the Semail Ophiolite in Oman (one of the largest and best-preserved in the world), and the Bay of Islands Ophiolite in Newfoundland.

Emplacement

The process by which dense oceanic lithosphere comes to rest on top of continental crust — rather than sinking beneath it — is called obduction, and remains a subject of geological research.
