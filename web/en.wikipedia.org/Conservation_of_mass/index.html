Conservation of mass

The law of conservation of mass states that for any closed system, the mass of the system must remain constant over time: mass can neither be created nor destroyed, only rearranged. In chemical reactions, the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products. The principle is fundamental to classical physics and chemistry, where it underlies the balancing of chemical equations and the continuity equations of fluid mechanics.

History

The idea that matter is not created or destroyed appears in ancient Greek philosophy, but its modern form emerged from quantitative experiments. Mikhail Lomonosov demonstrated the principle in sealed-vessel experiments in 1756, and Antoine Lavoisier established it decisively in the 1770s–1780s through careful weighing of reactants and products, showing that combustion and calcination involve the uptake of a component of air rather than the loss of a mysterious substance. Lavoisier's Traité Élémentaire de Chimie (1789) made mass balance the foundation of chemistry. Landolt's precision experiments in the early 20th century verified the law to extraordinary accuracy.

Limits in modern physics

With Einstein's mass–energy equivalence (E = mc²), mass and energy are understood as aspects of a single conserved quantity: in nuclear reactions and particle processes, rest mass alone is not conserved, though total mass-energy is. Because the mass changes involved in ordinary chemical reactions are far below measurable thresholds, conservation of mass remains an excellent approximation in chemistry. In relativity and cosmology the law is subsumed into the conservation of energy-momentum.
