Amphetamine

Amphetamine (contracted from alpha-methylphenethylamine) is a central nervous system stimulant marketed under several brand names, including Adderall and Dexedrine. It is used in the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), narcolepsy, and obesity. Chemically, it is a substituted phenethylamine with the formula C9H13N, and it exists as a pair of enantiomers: levoamphetamine and dextroamphetamine.

Mechanism of action

Amphetamine increases the activity of the monoamine neurotransmitters dopamine and norepinephrine in the brain, primarily by promoting their release from presynaptic terminals and by blocking their reuptake. These actions produce increased wakefulness, alertness, and concentration, along with euphoria at higher doses.

History

Amphetamine was first synthesized in 1887 by Romanian chemist Lazăr Edeleanu, but its stimulant properties were not recognized until the late 1920s, when Gordon Alles resynthesized and tested it. It was marketed in the 1930s as Benzedrine, initially as an inhaler for nasal congestion, and was widely used by military forces during the Second World War to combat fatigue.

Medical use and risks

At therapeutic doses, amphetamine improves attention and reduces impulsivity in people with ADHD and promotes wakefulness in narcolepsy. Side effects include insomnia, appetite suppression, dry mouth, and increased heart rate and blood pressure. Long-term recreational use at high doses can lead to addiction, cardiovascular harm, and stimulant psychosis. Amphetamine is a controlled substance in most jurisdictions.
