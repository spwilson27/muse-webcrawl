Model organisms
[Image: images/model-organism.png]
DefinitionA non-human species studied intensively to understand biological processes, on the expectation that discoveries generalise to other species, including humans
Classic examplesE. coli, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila melanogaster, mouse, zebrafish, Arabidopsis thaliana, Xenopus
Desirable traitsShort generation time, cheap and easy culture, small genome or sequenced genome, tractable genetics, similarity to target organisms

A model organism (often shortened to model) is a non-human species that is extensively studied to understand particular biological phenomena, with the expectation that discoveries made in the model will provide insight into the workings of other organisms. Model organisms are widely used to explore potential causes and treatments for human diseases when experimentation on humans would be unfeasible or unethical. This strategy is made possible by the common descent of all living organisms, and the conservation of metabolic and developmental pathways and genetic material over the course of evolution.

Selection criteria

Researchers choose model organisms for practical and biological reasons: short life cycles and rapid reproduction so that many generations can be studied; ease of maintenance in the laboratory; small size; a genome that is compact, sequenced and easy to manipulate; and the ability to generate mutations and transgenic lines. Equally important is relevance — the degree to which the organism shares the process under study, whether yeast cell division, fly development or mouse immunity.

Major model organisms

The bacterium Escherichia coli founded molecular genetics (gene regulation, the genetic code, recombinant DNA). The yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe underpin studies of the cell cycle and eukaryotic gene function. The nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, with its completely mapped cell lineage and nervous system, illuminated programmed cell death and RNA interference. The fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, in use since Thomas Hunt Morgan's work of the 1910s, established the chromosome theory of heredity and the genetics of development. Among vertebrates, the house mouse (Mus musculus) dominates biomedical research; the zebrafish (Danio rerio) offers transparent embryos for developmental studies; the frog Xenopus laevis provides large eggs and oocytes; and the thale cress Arabidopsis thaliana serves plant biology.

Contributions

Work on model organisms has produced many Nobel Prizes — for the operon, the cell-division cycle, homeotic genes, apoptosis, telomeres, green fluorescent protein and CRISPR biology among them — and nearly every drug in clinical use was first characterised in animal models.

Limitations and ethics

Model systems can mislead: mice differ from humans in immunity, metabolism and lifespan, and many candidate drugs that succeed in animals fail in human trials. Ethical frameworks such as the "three Rs" (replacement, reduction, refinement) govern animal use, and there is growing interest in alternatives including organoids, computer models and additional species chosen for phylogenetic relevance ("Krogh's principle": for every problem there is an organism ideally suited to its study).
