The western honey bee (Apis mellifera) is the most common of the honey bee species worldwide and the principal species kept in beekeeping (apiculture). Native to Europe, Africa and western Asia, it has been introduced to every continent except Antarctica. It is valued both for its honey, beeswax and other products, and — economically far more important — for pollination services to crops; a large share of the world's food crops benefit from insect pollination dominated by this species.

Biology and society

Honey bees are eusocial. A colony consists of a single fertile queen, tens of thousands of sterile female workers and, seasonally, a few hundred male drones. Workers progress through tasks — cleaning, nursing larvae, building comb, guarding, foraging — as they age. Foragers communicate the location of food sources with the waggle dance, decoded by Karl von Frisch. Colonies reproduce by swarming: the old queen leaves with a fraction of the workers to found a new nest, while a new queen emerges in the original hive. Workers are haplodiploid offspring: females develop from fertilised eggs, males from unfertilised ones. The species has numerous subspecies, such as the Italian bee (A. m. ligustica), the Carniolan (A. m. carnica) and the African (A. m. scutellata), the latter ancestor of the defensive "Africanised" hybrids in the Americas.

Threats

Managed and feral colonies face combined pressures: the parasitic mite Varroa destructor and the viruses it transmits, other pathogens and pests, pesticide exposure (notably neonicotinoids), habitat and forage loss, and climate stress. From 2006, reports of colony collapse disorder — the sudden disappearance of worker bees — heightened public concern, though annual colony losses have multiple interacting causes and managed hive numbers globally have continued to grow overall.
