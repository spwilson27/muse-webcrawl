The Pipidae are a family of primitive, tongueless frogs. The thirty or so living species are found in two widely separated regions — South America (the Surinam toads, genus Pipa) and sub-Saharan Africa (the clawed frogs, Xenopus, and the dwarf clawed frogs, Hymenochirus) — a distribution reflecting the ancient breakup of the supercontinent Gondwana.

Characteristics

Pipids are highly aquatic frogs, spending nearly their entire lives in water. They share distinctive anatomical features: they lack tongues (hence the group name Agossa sometimes applied to them), have flattened bodies, fully webbed hind feet with (in Xenopus) clawed toes, and a lateral line system retained into adulthood that detects vibrations in the water. Their eyes are small and positioned on top of the head. Unusually among tetrapods, pipids lack ribs in most genera, and their vertebrae are opisthocoelous. They feed by suction and by shovelling prey into the mouth with their forelimbs.

Notable species

The African clawed frog Xenopus laevis is a major laboratory model organism in developmental biology and was historically used in human pregnancy tests; its escape and release from laboratories established feral populations worldwide, and it has been implicated in spreading the amphibian disease chytridiomycosis. The common Surinam toad (Pipa pipa) is famous for its extraordinary reproduction: the male presses fertilised eggs into the female's back, where they develop in individual pockets of skin until fully formed froglets emerge.
