Chytridiomycosis

Chytridiomycosis has driven catastrophic declines in amphibian populations worldwide

Chytridiomycosis is an infectious disease of amphibians caused by the chytrid fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd) and Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal). It is considered one of the most destructive infectious diseases ever described in vertebrates: a 2019 global assessment attributed declines in at least 501 amphibian species to chytridiomycosis, including around 90 presumed extinctions, representing the greatest loss of biodiversity attributable to a single pathogen.

Biology

The fungi infect the keratinised skin of amphibians — in adults, the skin through which frogs breathe and regulate water and electrolytes. The fungus grows within skin cells, forming flask-shaped sporangia that release swimming zoospores. Infection causes thickening of the skin (hyperkeratosis) and disrupts its transport functions; death typically follows from electrolyte imbalance and cardiac arrest, as sodium and potassium levels in the blood collapse. Tadpoles, whose keratin is confined to the mouthparts, usually survive infection but can carry the fungus.

History and spread

The disease was first identified in 1998 in dying frogs from Australia and Central America, following waves of unexplained declines that had swept through pristine rainforest habitats in the 1970s–1990s, extinguishing species such as the golden toad of Costa Rica. Bd has since been detected on every continent where amphibians live. Its global spread is attributed largely to the international trade in amphibians, including the African clawed frog Xenopus laevis (a member of the family Pipidae), exported worldwide for pregnancy testing and laboratory use in the twentieth century. Bsal, described in 2013, emerged in Europe where it has devastated fire salamander populations, and is of Asian origin.

Conservation

There is no widely deployable cure for wild populations. Responses include captive assurance colonies of threatened species, antifungal treatment of individuals, probiotic skin bacteria, habitat management, and reintroduction attempts. Some species have shown partial recovery or evolved resistance, but chytridiomycosis remains a primary driver of the global amphibian decline crisis, interacting with habitat loss and climate change.
