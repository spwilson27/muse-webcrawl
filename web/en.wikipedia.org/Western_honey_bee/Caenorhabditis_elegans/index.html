Caenorhabditis elegans is a free-living, transparent nematode (roundworm) about 1 mm in length that lives in temperate soil environments, feeding on bacteria. Since 1963, when Sydney Brenner began developing it as a model organism, it has become one of the most important experimental animals in biology (see Animal testing), used to study development, neurobiology, ageing and genetics.

Biology

C. elegans exists as self-fertilising hermaphrodites and as males. An adult hermaphrodite has exactly 959 somatic cells, whose entire lineage — from fertilised egg to adult — has been completely mapped by John Sulston and colleagues, an achievement unique among animals. Its nervous system has exactly 302 neurons, whose connections (the connectome) have also been fully mapped. The worm has a rapid life cycle of about three days at 20 °C and a lifespan of two to three weeks.

Research significance

C. elegans was the first multicellular organism to have its genome completely sequenced, in 1998 — about 100 million base pairs encoding roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes, a surprisingly large fraction of which have human counterparts. Research on the worm led to the discovery of programmed cell death (apoptosis) genes, of RNA interference (for which Andrew Fire and Craig Mello received the 2006 Nobel Prize), and of green fluorescent protein as a biological marker. Worms have even been flown on space missions, and some survived the Space Shuttle Columbia disaster in their experiment canisters.
