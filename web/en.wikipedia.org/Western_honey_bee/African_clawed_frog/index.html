The African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis) is a species of African aquatic frog of the genus Xenopus. Its name is derived from the three short claws on each hind foot, which it uses to tear apart its food. The body is flattened and streamlined, the eyes sit on top of the head, and the frog lacks a tongue and external ears; a lateral-line system detects movement in the water. Entirely aquatic as an adult, it tolerates a wide range of water conditions, including stagnant and even brackish ponds, and can survive drought by burrowing into mud.

Biology

X. laevis grows to about 12 cm (5 in) and lives 5–15 years in the wild (over 20 in captivity). It is a voracious opportunistic feeder, eating living, dying and dead prey — fish, snails, worms, crustaceans and even its own tadpoles — shovelling food into its mouth with its small forelimbs. The species is unusual in being a functional tetraploid, carrying a duplicated genome from an ancient hybridisation event. Mating typically occurs at night; the female lays hundreds to thousands of small eggs that are fertilised externally by the clasping male.

Use in research and medicine

The species is one of the most important model organisms in developmental biology: its large, robust eggs and externally developing embryos made classical experiments on induction and cell fate possible, and its oocytes are a standard system for expressing and studying ion channels and other proteins. From the 1930s to the 1960s the frog was exported worldwide for the "Hogben test", a pregnancy test in which a female frog injected with a pregnant woman's urine would lay eggs within hours in response to the hormone hCG.

Invasion and disease

Released or escaped frogs have established invasive populations in California, Arizona, Mexico, Chile, France, Italy and elsewhere, where they prey on native wildlife including endangered fish and amphibians. The global trade in Xenopus is also implicated in spreading the chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, a driver of amphibian declines: the frogs carry the fungus without obvious harm, a hypothesis supported by infections detected in museum specimens dating to the 1930s.
