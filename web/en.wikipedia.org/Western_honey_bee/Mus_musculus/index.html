Mus musculus is a small rodent known as the house mouse. It is one of the most abundant and widespread mammals in the world, having spread with human agriculture and settlement from its origins in Asia to every continent. The house mouse is a commensal species that lives in close association with humans, as well as a serious agricultural and household pest — and, in its laboratory form, it is the most widely used mammalian model organism in biomedical research (see Animal testing).

Description and ecology

Adults weigh 12–30 g, with a body length of 7.5–10 cm and a nearly hairless tail of similar length. Colour ranges from light brown to dark grey, with a lighter belly. House mice are omnivorous, nocturnal, and highly adaptable, with excellent senses of smell and hearing. In the wild they live in burrows and family groups called demes; in buildings they nest in wall cavities and stored goods. Females reach sexual maturity at about six weeks and can produce litters of 3–14 pups every three weeks under favourable conditions, allowing explosive population growth.

Laboratory mouse

Laboratory mice are bred from domesticated stocks of M. musculus, including inbred strains such as C57BL/6, BALB/c and DBA/2 in which individuals are genetically almost identical. Because mice share the great majority of their genes with humans (~99% of mouse genes have a human homologue), breed rapidly, and can be genetically engineered — including gene knockouts and knock-ins — they are the primary subjects for studying mammalian genetics, immunology, cancer, neuroscience and drug development. The mouse genome, about 2.7 billion base pairs, was sequenced in 2002. Millions of mice are used in research annually worldwide, and genetically modified mice underpin major advances such as monoclonal antibody production and transgenic disease models.

As a pest and invasive species

House mice contaminate stored food, damage structures and wiring, and can transmit diseases such as leptospirosis and salmonellosis. On islands where they were introduced by ships, mice devastate seabird colonies — famously on Gough Island, where oversized mice prey on Tristan albatross chicks — prompting major eradication programmes.
