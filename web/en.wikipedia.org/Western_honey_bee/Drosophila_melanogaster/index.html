Drosophila melanogaster is a species of fly (an insect of the order Diptera) in the family Drosophilidae, commonly known as the common fruit fly, vinegar fly or pomace fly. The species, about 3 mm long, is attracted to fermenting fruit and is a familiar pest of kitchens and breweries. Since Thomas Hunt Morgan began experimenting with it around 1910, it has become the pre-eminent model organism of classical and molecular genetics (see Animal testing).

Genetics

D. melanogaster has only four pairs of chromosomes (three autosomes and the X/Y sex chromosomes), a short generation time of about ten days at 25 °C, high fecundity, and easily scored visible mutations. Morgan's group at Columbia University used the fly to demonstrate that genes reside on chromosomes, discovering sex-linked inheritance through the white-eyed mutation, and constructing the first genetic maps from recombination frequencies. Work on Drosophila established the chromosomal theory of heredity and later elucidated the homeotic (Hox) genes controlling body-plan development.

Its genome, about 180 million base pairs with roughly 13,600 protein-coding genes, was sequenced in 2000. Around 75% of known human disease genes have a recognisable counterpart in the fly, making it valuable for studying neurodegeneration, cancer, immunity, metabolism and behaviour. Six Nobel Prizes have been awarded for research using Drosophila, including those for the discovery of the Toll pathway in immunity and of the molecular mechanisms controlling circadian rhythms (2017).

Life cycle

Development proceeds from egg through three larval instars and a pupal stage to the adult fly, in about 8.5 days at 25 °C. Females lay up to 100 eggs per day on fermenting material, on whose yeasts and micro-organisms the larvae feed. The giant polytene chromosomes of the larval salivary glands, with their distinctive banding patterns, were historically crucial for mapping genes and chromosomal rearrangements.
