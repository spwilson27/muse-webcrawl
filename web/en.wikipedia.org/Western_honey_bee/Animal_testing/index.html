Animal testing

Mice account for the great majority of animals used in research

Animal testing, also known as animal experimentation, animal research and in vivo testing, is the use of non-human animals in experiments that seek to control the variables affecting behaviour or biological systems. An estimated 50 to 200 million vertebrate animals worldwide are used annually — the great majority being mice and rats bred specifically for research — in addition to vast numbers of invertebrates such as the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans.

Purposes

Animal research is used in basic biological research, in the development and safety testing of medicines and vaccines, in toxicology and cosmetics testing, in psychology and education. Animal models have contributed to most major medical advances, including insulin for diabetes, antibiotics, vaccines for polio and other diseases, organ transplantation and many cancer treatments. Animals are also used in applied research for veterinary and agricultural purposes, and in defence research.

Regulation and ethics

The practice is regulated to varying degrees around the world. In the United Kingdom, the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 licenses projects, premises and personnel; the European Union's Directive 2010/63/EU harmonises standards across member states; the United States Animal Welfare Act excludes purpose-bred rats, mice and birds, which make up about 95% of laboratory animals there.

The "three Rs" — Replacement, Reduction and Refinement — first proposed by W. M. S. Russell and R. L. Burch in 1959, guide efforts to replace animal experiments with alternatives such as cell cultures and computer models, to reduce the numbers used, and to refine procedures to minimise suffering. Animal testing is ethically contested: supporters emphasise medical benefits and the absence of alternatives for whole-organism questions, while opponents — animal rights and animal welfare campaigners — argue that the suffering involved is not justified and that results often translate poorly to humans. Testing of cosmetics on animals has been banned in the EU and several other jurisdictions.
