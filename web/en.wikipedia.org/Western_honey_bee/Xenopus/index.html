Xenopus (commonly known as the clawed frog) is a genus of highly aquatic frogs native to sub-Saharan Africa. The twenty or so species in the genus are characterised by flattened bodies, slippery skin coated in protective mucus, eyes positioned on top of the head, and three short horny claws on each hind foot used to shred food. They lack tongues and visible eardrums, instead detecting vibrations through a lateral-line system and hearing with an underwater ear adapted to their fully aquatic life.

Description and behaviour

Xenopus species range from about 4 cm to 13 cm in length. They are strong swimmers with fully webbed hind feet, but poorly adapted to land, moving on shore with an awkward crawl. In the water they feed on invertebrates, small fish and carrion, sweeping food into the mouth with their unwebbed fingers. When ponds dry up, they burrow into the mud and aestivate until the rains return, and they can also migrate overland on wet nights. Most species are polyploid — having arisen through hybridisation and genome duplication — with chromosome counts ranging from diploid (2n = 20 in X. tropicalis) to dodecaploid.

Use in science

Xenopus laevis is one of the most widely used vertebrate model organisms. Its large eggs (about 1.2 mm) and embryos that develop outside the mother supported landmark discoveries in embryology, including John Gurdon's 1962 demonstration that the nucleus of a differentiated cell retains the full genome — the first cloning of an animal from a somatic cell, recognised by a Nobel Prize in 2012. Xenopus oocytes, injected with foreign messenger RNA, serve as a living test-tube for studying proteins such as ion channels. Because X. laevis is tetraploid, geneticists increasingly favour the smaller, true-diploid X. tropicalis, whose genome was sequenced in 2010. From the 1930s the genus was also exported worldwide for use in the Hogben pregnancy test.

Ecology and invasion

Escaped and released X. laevis have become invasive in parts of North and South America and Europe, preying on native fauna. The worldwide trade is also thought to have spread the amphibian chytrid fungus Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis, which Xenopus carries asymptomatically.
