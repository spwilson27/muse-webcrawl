Batavia was the capital of the Dutch East Indies, on the site of modern Jakarta, Indonesia. Founded in 1619 by Jan Pieterszoon Coen of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) on the ruins of the Bantenese port of Jayakarta, it became the hub of the Dutch trading empire in Asia — the headquarters of the VOC and later of the colonial government of the Netherlands East Indies — until its fall to Japan in 1942.

Foundation and the VOC era

After expelling the Bantenese and English, Coen rebuilt the captured town as a Dutch fortified city on the model of Amsterdam, with canals, a castle, warehouses and a town hall. From Batavia the VOC directed trade in pepper, nutmeg, cloves, textiles, tea and coffee across Asia. The city drew a cosmopolitan population of Dutch officials and soldiers, Chinese merchants and artisans, Mardijker freedmen, Javanese and slaves from across the archipelago. In 1740, amid economic downturn and anti-Chinese tension, Dutch troops massacred thousands of Chinese residents in the Batavia massacre.

The colonial capital

As the canal district grew unhealthy with malaria, elites moved south to higher suburbs such as Weltevreden. The VOC was dissolved in 1800 and the colony passed to the Dutch state; briefly seized by Britain under Stamford Raffles (1811–1816), Batavia was returned by the Congress of Vienna. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries it expanded with railways, the port of Tanjung Priok, municipal government (from 1905) and a population surpassing half a million by 1930.

End of Batavia

Japanese forces occupied the city in March 1942 and renamed it Jakarta (Djakarta) to encourage Indonesian nationalism. After Japan's defeat, Indonesian nationalists proclaimed independence (17 August 1945) with Jakarta as capital; the Dutch briefly reoccupied the city but recognised Indonesian sovereignty in 1949. The old town survives today as Kota Tua, Jakarta's heritage district, with the former town hall now the Jakarta History Museum.
