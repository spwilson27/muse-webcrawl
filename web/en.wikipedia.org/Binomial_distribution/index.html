Binomial distribution

In probability theory and statistics, the binomial distribution with parameters n and p is the discrete probability distribution of the number of successes in a sequence of n independent experiments, each asking a yes–no question, and each with its own Boolean-valued outcome: success (with probability p) or failure (with probability 1 − p). Such experiments are called Bernoulli trials.

Definition

The probability of obtaining exactly k successes in n trials is given by the probability mass function:

P(X = k) = C(n, k) · pk · (1 − p)n−k

where C(n, k) is the binomial coefficient, "n choose k". For example, the probability of exactly two heads in four fair coin tosses is C(4,2)(1/2)4 = 6/16 = 0.375.

Properties

The expected value of a binomially distributed random variable is np, and its variance is np(1−p). The distribution is the sum of n independent Bernoulli(p) random variables. When n is large, the binomial distribution is well approximated by the normal distribution with the same mean and variance (the de Moivre–Laplace theorem, a special case of the central limit theorem); when p is small and n large, it is approximated by the Poisson distribution with mean np.

Applications

The binomial distribution is a foundation of the binomial test of statistical significance and is used wherever outcomes are counted in fixed numbers of independent trials: quality control, election polling and survey sampling, clinical trials, and genetics. The binomial coefficients that define it appear in the expansion of (x + y)n, the binomial theorem.
