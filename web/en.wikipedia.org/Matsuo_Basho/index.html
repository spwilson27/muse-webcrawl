Matsuo Bashō

Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694), born Matsuo Kinsaku and later known as Matsuo Munefusa, was the most famous poet of the Edo period in Japan. He is recognised as the greatest master of haiku (then called hokku), transforming the form from comic linked verse into serious poetry marked by simplicity, nature imagery and the aesthetics of sabi — quiet loneliness and impermanence.

Life

Bashō was born near Ueno in Iga Province, of a low-ranking samurai family, and served the local lord's household before moving to Edo (Tokyo) in the 1670s, where he taught poetry and gathered disciples. He took his pen name from a banana plant (bashō) given to him by a pupil, beside which his hut stood. In his last decade he undertook long journeys on foot through Japan, recording them in travel diaries that mix prose and verse.

Works

His masterpiece is Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Interior, published posthumously in 1702), an account of a 2,400-kilometre journey through northern Honshu in 1689. Other travel records include Nozarashi Kikō and Sarashina Kikō. Famous verses include the frog haiku — "old pond / a frog jumps in / the sound of water" — and poems on the summer grasses at Hiraizumi and the cicadas at Yamadera. Bashō died in Osaka in 1694 while on another journey.

Legacy

Bashō's influence on Japanese poetry is immense, and through translation he became a central figure in the worldwide reception of haiku in the 20th century.
