Paris Peace Conference, 1919

Paris Peace Conference Dates: 18 January 1919 – 21 January 1920
Venue: Paris and Versailles, France
Participants: 32 states
Principal figures: Wilson, Clemenceau, Lloyd George, Orlando
Outcome: Five treaties; League of Nations founded

The Paris Peace Conference was a set of formal and informal diplomatic meetings in 1919 and 1920, following the end of World War I, in which the victorious Allies set the peace terms for the defeated Central Powers. Dominated by the leaders of Britain, France, Italy, and the United States — David Lloyd George, Georges Clemenceau, Vittorio Emanuele Orlando, and Woodrow Wilson, the "Big Four" — the conference produced five major treaties and created the League of Nations.

Proceedings

The conference opened on 18 January 1919 at the French foreign ministry on the Quai d'Orsay. Some 32 states sent delegations, but the defeated powers were excluded from the negotiations until the terms were presented. Decisions were made first by a Council of Ten, then by the Council of Four after Orlando's delegation briefly withdrew in April 1919 over the disposition of Fiume. Wilson pressed his Fourteen Points, above all the League of Nations; Clemenceau sought security against Germany through reparations, demilitarisation, and territorial buffers; Lloyd George steered a middle course; Japan obtained recognition of its gains in Shandong, prompting China's delegation to refuse to sign the Treaty of Versailles.

Treaties

The conference produced a separate treaty with each defeated power: the Treaty of Versailles with Germany (signed 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors), the Treaty of Saint-Germain with Austria, the Treaty of Trianon with Hungary, the Treaty of Neuilly with Bulgaria, and the Treaty of Sèvres with the Ottoman Empire — the last later superseded by the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) after the Turkish War of Independence. The treaties redrew the maps of Europe and the Middle East, dissolved the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman imperial orders, and created a system of League of Nations mandates for former colonies.

Legacy

The settlement was criticised from the start — John Maynard Keynes's The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919) attacked reparations as vindictive and unworkable — and the United States Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, keeping the US out of the League its president had championed. Historians continue to debate how far the Paris settlement sowed the grievances exploited by the Axis powers two decades later.
