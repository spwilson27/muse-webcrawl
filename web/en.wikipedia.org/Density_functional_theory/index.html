Density functional theory (DFT) is a computational quantum mechanical modelling method used in physics, chemistry and materials science to investigate the electronic structure (or nuclear structure) of many-body systems, in particular atoms, molecules and condensed phases.

Overview

Unlike methods based on the many-electron wavefunction, DFT uses the electron density — a function of only three spatial variables — as the fundamental quantity. The Hohenberg–Kohn theorems (1964) proved that the ground-state properties of a system are a unique functional of its ground-state density. The Kohn–Sham scheme (1965) made the theory practical by mapping the interacting system onto a fictitious non-interacting system with the same density, folding all unknown many-body effects into an exchange–correlation functional.

Approximations

Because the exact exchange–correlation functional is unknown, approximations form a hierarchy sometimes called "Jacob's ladder": the local density approximation (LDA), generalised gradient approximations (GGA) such as PBE, meta-GGAs, and hybrid functionals such as B3LYP that mix in exact Hartree–Fock exchange. DFT is known to struggle with strongly correlated systems, dispersion (van der Waals) forces and band-gap underestimation.

Applications

DFT is among the most widely used methods in computational materials science and quantum chemistry, applied to crystal structures, catalysis, semiconductors, batteries and drug design. Walter Kohn shared the 1998 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for its development.
