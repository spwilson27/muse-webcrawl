Obelisk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Luxor obelisk, Place de la Concorde, Paris

An obelisk (from Ancient Greek ὀβελίσκος, "small spit") is a tall, four-sided, narrow tapering monument which ends in a pyramid-like shape called a pyramidion at the top. Originally called tekhenu by their builders, the ancient Egyptians, they were carved from a single piece of stone, usually red granite from the quarries at Aswan.

Ancient Egyptian obelisks

Obelisks symbolised the sun god Ra and the petrified rays of the sun; they were typically erected in pairs at the entrances of temples. Most pharaonic obelisks were made during the New Kingdom. Surviving examples include those of Hatshepsut and Thutmose III at Karnak. Many were later transported abroad: Rome alone holds thirteen ancient obelisks, more than remain in Egypt, including the Lateran Obelisk, the largest known. Others stand in London and New York (the "Cleopatra's Needles") and in Paris, where the Luxor obelisk was erected in 1836.

Transport and erection

Moving obelisks weighing hundreds of tonnes was a major feat of ancient engineering, involving barges on the Nile, sledges, and earthen ramps. The unfinished obelisk at Aswan, abandoned because of a crack, reveals the quarrying technique of pounding and undercutting the granite.

Modern obelisks

The form was revived in later architecture; the Washington Monument in Washington, D.C. (169 m) is the world's tallest obelisk, completed in 1884. Obelisks are also common as funerary and commemorative monuments.
