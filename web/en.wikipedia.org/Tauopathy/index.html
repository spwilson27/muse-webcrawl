Tauopathy

Tauopathies are a class of neurodegenerative diseases characterised by the pathological aggregation of the microtubule-associated protein tau in the brain. In healthy neurons, tau stabilises microtubules, especially in axons. In tauopathies, tau becomes abnormally hyperphosphorylated, detaches from microtubules, and assembles into insoluble filaments — neurofibrillary tangles and related inclusions — accompanied by neuronal dysfunction and death.

Classification

Tauopathies are divided into primary tauopathies, in which tau pathology predominates, and secondary tauopathies, in which it accompanies another pathology. Alzheimer's disease, the most common, is a secondary tauopathy in which tangles accompany amyloid-beta plaques. Primary tauopathies include progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal degeneration (CBD), Pick's disease, several frontotemporal dementias, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), associated with repetitive head impacts. Mutations in the MAPT gene (chromosome 17), which encodes tau, cause inherited cases of frontotemporal dementia with parkinsonism (FTDP-17), proving that tau dysfunction alone can drive neurodegeneration.

Isoforms and strains

Alternative splicing of MAPT produces tau isoforms with three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule-binding repeats; diseases are classed as 3R, 4R, or mixed (3R+4R, as in Alzheimer's). Cryo-electron microscopy has shown that tau filaments adopt distinct, disease-specific folds — "strains" — which may explain the differing cell types and brain regions affected in each disorder and supports the hypothesis that tau spreads through the brain in a prion-like manner.

Diagnosis and treatment

Tau PET imaging and blood and cerebrospinal-fluid phosphorylated-tau biomarkers have transformed diagnosis, particularly in Alzheimer's disease. No disease-modifying therapy directed at tau is yet approved; approaches under investigation include anti-tau antibodies, aggregation inhibitors, antisense oligonucleotides lowering tau production, and kinase inhibitors.
