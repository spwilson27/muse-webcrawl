Disciplinary institution

Bentham's panopticon, the emblematic design of a disciplinary institution.

A disciplinary institution is an organization or enclosed setting — such as a prison, barracks, school, hospital, asylum, workshop, or monastery — in which the behaviour, time, and bodies of individuals are systematically observed, ordered, and trained. The concept is most closely associated with the French philosopher Michel Foucault, especially his 1975 work Discipline and Punish.

Foucault's analysis

Foucault argued that from the 17th and 18th centuries, European societies increasingly replaced spectacular sovereign punishment (public torture and execution) with pervasive disciplinary power. Disciplinary institutions operate through techniques including enclosure and partitioning of space; timetables and drills regulating activity; hierarchical observation; normalizing judgement against standards; and the examination, which combines observation with documentation of each individual. Jeremy Bentham's panopticon — a prison design in which inmates are always potentially visible to a central, unseen watcher — served Foucault as the ideal-type of this power, producing self-regulating, "docile bodies."

Related concepts

The sociologist Erving Goffman analysed similar settings as "total institutions" in Asylums (1961): places where large groups of similarly situated people live enclosed lives administered by staff. Gilles Deleuze later proposed that disciplinary societies were being succeeded by "societies of control," in which continuous modulation by codes and data replaces enclosed moulds — a thesis widely debated in contemporary social theory.
