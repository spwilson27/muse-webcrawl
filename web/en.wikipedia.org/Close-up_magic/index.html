Close-up magic

Close-up magic (also known as table magic or micromagic) is magic performed in an intimate setting, usually no more than a few feet from the audience, and usually performed while sitting at a table. It is one of the most demanding forms of conjuring because spectators watch from very close range, and the performer relies on sleight of hand rather than large apparatus or stage machinery.

Techniques and props

Close-up magicians work with everyday objects — playing cards, coins, sponge balls, cups and balls, pens and borrowed items such as rings and banknotes. Core techniques include palming, false shuffles and cuts, switches, loads and controlled forces, delivered under cover of misdirection and natural-looking handling. Dai Vernon, "The Professor", revolutionised the field in the 20th century by insisting on naturalness and psychological subtlety; later masters such as Ed Marlo, Slydini and Juan Tamariz further developed card and coin work.

Performance contexts

Close-up magic is performed at restaurant tables, cocktail parties, corporate events and trade shows ("walk-around" magic), and in formal close-up shows for small audiences. The annual FISM world championships and organisations such as the Magic Castle in Hollywood have helped establish close-up as a recognised art form.

Related forms

It is distinguished from parlour magic (for somewhat larger audiences at moderate distance) and stage magic (large-scale illusions), though many effects migrate between categories.
