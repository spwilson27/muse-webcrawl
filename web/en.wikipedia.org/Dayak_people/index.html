Dayak people

The Dayak or Dyak are the diverse indigenous peoples of the island of Borneo, divided between the Indonesian provinces of Kalimantan and the Malaysian states of Sarawak and Sabah, with a small population in Brunei. The term is an exonym covering more than 200 riverine and hill-dwelling ethnic subgroups — among them the Iban, Ngaju, Kayan, Kenyah, Bidayuh and Penan — each with its own language and customs.

Culture and society

Traditionally, many Dayak groups lived in communal longhouses along rivers, practising swidden (shifting) rice cultivation, hunting and gathering of forest products. Tattooing, elaborate beadwork and carved hornbill-feather regalia marked ritual life. Some groups, notably the Iban, were famous as headhunters — a practice tied to ritual and prestige, suppressed under the Brooke Rajahs in Sarawak and Dutch colonial rule in the 19th–20th centuries — and the Iban also gained a reputation as seafarers and, later, as renowned jungle trackers in British service during the Malayan Emergency and Konfrontasi. The Ngaju religion, Kaharingan, was officially recognised in Indonesia under the umbrella of Hinduism.

Modern history

Dayaks served prominently in politics on both sides of the border; Stephen Kalong Ningkan, an Iban, became Sarawak's first chief minister in 1963. Logging, oil-palm plantations and mining have brought deforestation and land disputes with customary (adat) tenure, while transmigration programmes sparked tensions that erupted in the Sambas and Sampit conflicts of 1997–2001 between Dayak and Madurese communities. Dayak identity has since become a political rallying point in Kalimantan, and Dayak governors now lead several Indonesian provinces.
