Bovine spongiform encephalopathy

Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE), commonly known as mad cow disease, is a fatal neurodegenerative disease of cattle caused by misfolded proteins called prions. It belongs to the family of transmissible spongiform encephalopathies that also includes scrapie in sheep and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease in humans.

Course of the disease

Infected cattle develop spongy holes in the brain tissue, leading to behavioral changes, tremors, loss of coordination, and eventually death. The incubation period is long, typically four to five years. BSE was first identified in the United Kingdom in 1986 and reached epidemic proportions there in the early 1990s; it is believed to have spread through cattle feed containing meat-and-bone meal derived from infected carcasses.

Human health and control

In 1996 the UK government announced a link between BSE and a new human prion disease, variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD), which has caused more than 170 deaths in the UK. Control measures — including bans on feeding ruminant protein to ruminants, removal of specified risk materials such as brain and spinal cord from the food chain, and mass culling of affected herds — have reduced cases to near zero, and the epidemic is considered under control.
