Constitutional Convention (United States)

The Constitutional Convention took place in Philadelphia from May 25 to September 17, 1787. Although the convention was intended to revise the Articles of Confederation, its delegates instead created a new, more powerful framework of government: the Constitution of the United States. George Washington presided over the convention; 55 delegates attended from twelve states (Rhode Island sent none), and 39 ultimately signed the document.

Background

Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress lacked powers to tax, regulate commerce, or enforce its decisions. Economic disorder, interstate trade disputes and Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts convinced many leaders that reform was urgent. The Annapolis Convention of 1786 called for a broader meeting in Philadelphia; Congress endorsed it "for the sole and express purpose of revising the Articles".

Debates and compromises

The Virginia and New Jersey Plans

Edmund Randolph presented the Virginia Plan, drafted largely by James Madison, proposing a bicameral legislature with representation proportional to population. Small states countered with William Paterson's New Jersey Plan, preserving equal state representation. The deadlock was broken by the Connecticut Compromise (Great Compromise): a House of Representatives apportioned by population and a Senate with two members per state.

Slavery

The Three-fifths Compromise counted three-fifths of the enslaved population toward representation and taxation. Delegates also agreed that Congress could not ban the international slave trade before 1808, and included a fugitive slave clause. The convention otherwise created a federal government with separated powers — legislative, executive and judicial — and checks and balances among them, influenced by Montesquieu and by state constitutions.

Ratification

The convention proposed that the Constitution take effect upon ratification by conventions in nine of the thirteen states, bypassing the Articles' unanimity requirement. The ensuing debate between Federalists and Anti-Federalists produced The Federalist Papers; Delaware ratified first (7 December 1787), New Hampshire ninth (21 June 1788), and the new government began in 1789. A promise to add a Bill of Rights helped secure support.
