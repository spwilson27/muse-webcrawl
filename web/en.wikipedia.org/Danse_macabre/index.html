Danse macabre

Danse macabre (from the French phrase), also called the Dance of Death, is an artistic genre of allegory of the Late Middle Ages on the universality of death. The danse macabre consists of the dead, or a personification of death, summoning representatives from all walks of life—a pope, an emperor, a king, a child, a labourer—to dance along to the grave, typically with a lively and ragged musical accompaniment.

Origins

The motif emerged in the aftermath of the Black Death and the Hundred Years' War, reflecting a culture preoccupied with mortality and the fragility of earthly glory. The earliest recorded visual example is the fresco formerly in the Cemetery of the Holy Innocents in Paris (1424–25), accompanied by verses in which Death addresses each victim in turn. Printed books, notably the woodcut series by Hans Holbein the Younger (published 1538), spread the imagery across Europe.

Themes

The dance is a memento mori: it emphasises that death unites all regardless of rank or wealth, and it often has a satirical edge, pairing the mighty with their decaying doubles. Related traditions include the German Totentanz and the Spanish Danza de la Muerte.

In later culture

The theme inspired music such as Franz Liszt's Totentanz and Camille Saint-Saëns's tone poem Danse macabre (1874), and continues to appear in literature, film and popular culture.
