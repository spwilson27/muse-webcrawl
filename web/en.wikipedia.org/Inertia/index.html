Inertia

Inertia is the property of a physical body by which it resists any change in its state of motion — including changes to its speed and direction. A body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion continues moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a net external force. The principle is stated as Newton's first law of motion and is foundational to classical mechanics. The inertia of a body in translational motion is measured by its mass; for rotating bodies the corresponding quantity is the moment of inertia.

History of the concept

Aristotelian physics held that motion required a continuously acting mover, so a projectile's flight was a puzzle explained by the surrounding medium. Medieval scholars such as John Philoponus and later Jean Buridan developed the theory of impetus, an internal quality imparted to a moving body. Galileo, from his inclined-plane experiments, approached the modern idea: a ball rolling on a frictionless horizontal plane would continue indefinitely. Descartes stated the principle of rectilinear persistence, and Isaac Newton gave it canonical form in the Principia (1687) as his first law, defining a body's vis insita ("innate force") of resisting changes to its state. Johannes Kepler had earlier used the word inertia (from Latin iners, idle or sluggish), though for him it meant resistance to motion rather than persistence in it.

In classical mechanics

Newton's first law defines the class of inertial frames of reference in which the law holds; within them, the second law, F = ma, quantifies how a force overcomes inertia to produce acceleration. Everyday manifestations include passengers lurching forward when a vehicle brakes, the difficulty of stopping a heavy or fast-moving object, and the behaviour of objects in free fall or orbit, where continuous "falling" around a planet is simply inertial motion bent by gravity.

In relativity and modern physics

In general relativity, free bodies follow geodesics in curved spacetime, and inertia is intimately connected with the geometry of spacetime and the distribution of mass-energy — an idea anticipated by Mach's principle, Ernst Mach's suggestion that local inertial frames are determined by the mass of the universe as a whole, which influenced Einstein. In special relativity, inertia increases with energy: the relativistic mass/energy relation E = mc2 means that adding energy to a body increases its resistance to acceleration. The origin of inertial mass remains a deep question; in the Standard Model, elementary particles acquire mass through the Higgs mechanism, though most of the mass of ordinary matter comes from the binding energy of quarks inside protons and neutrons.
