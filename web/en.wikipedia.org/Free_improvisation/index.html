Free improvisation or free music is improvised music played without predetermined rules, structures, harmonies, or idioms. Performers create music spontaneously in the moment, often exploring extended techniques and unconventional timbres. Although it emerged partly from free jazz and experimental classical music, free improvisation is usually distinguished from both by its refusal of any genre framework at all.

History

The practice took shape in the mid-1960s. In Britain, groups such as AMM and the Spontaneous Music Ensemble, and guitarist Derek Bailey, developed an approach Bailey described as "non-idiomatic improvisation". In continental Europe, musicians including Peter Brötzmann and Evan Parker built parallel scenes; in the United States, free jazz players such as Ornette Coleman and Cecil Taylor had already loosened improvisation from chord changes, and later artists like John Zorn pushed further into collage and game pieces.

Characteristics

Free improvisation prizes listening and interaction over display. Musicians may use their instruments unconventionally — preparing pianos, bowing cymbals, or using electronics and found objects. Performances range from near-silence to dense noise. Because nothing is rehearsed in the conventional sense, long-standing groups develop a collective vocabulary through familiarity rather than repertoire.

Reception and influence

Often challenging for audiences, free improvisation has remained a small but durable international network sustained by specialist labels (such as Incus and FMP), festivals, and venues. Its ethos — real-time collective creation without a score or leader — has influenced experimental rock, sound art, and contemporary composition.
