Asterism (astronomy)

An asterism is an observed pattern or group of stars in the sky. Asterisms can be any identified pattern or group of stars, and therefore are a general concept rather than a formally listed designation. They can be part of a constellation, part of more than one constellation, or standalone patterns made of stars from several constellations.

Background

Humans have grouped stars into patterns since antiquity, and asterisms appear in the astronomical traditions of many cultures. The modern system of 88 constellations was formalized by the International Astronomical Union, but asterisms have no official boundaries and are recognized purely by tradition and usefulness.

Examples

Among the best-known asterisms are the Big Dipper (the Plough), formed by the seven brightest stars of Ursa Major; the Great Square of Pegasus; the Summer Triangle, formed by Vega, Deneb, and Altair; the Winter Triangle; the Teapot in Sagittarius; and the Keystone in Hercules. The three stars of Orion's Belt are another widely recognized asterism.

Asterisms versus constellations

Constellations are officially defined regions of the celestial sphere with fixed boundaries, whereas asterisms are simply recognizable shapes. An asterism may be smaller than a constellation, span several constellations, or coincide with a constellation's most recognizable stars.
