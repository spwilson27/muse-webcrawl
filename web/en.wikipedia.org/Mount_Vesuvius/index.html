Mount Vesuvius is a volcano on the Gulf of Naples in Campania, Italy, about 9 km east of the city of Naples. It is one of the most famous and dangerous volcanoes in the world, best known for the eruption of AD 79 that destroyed and buried the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum.

The eruption of AD 79

On 24 August AD 79 (some scholars argue for October), after centuries of quiet during which the mountain was not even generally recognised as a volcano, Vesuvius exploded in a Plinian eruption. A column of ash and pumice buried Pompeii under metres of debris while pyroclastic surges and flows destroyed Herculaneum. The younger Pliny's letters to Tacitus give the classic eyewitness account; his uncle, Pliny the Elder, died during a rescue mission. The buried cities were rediscovered in the 18th century and their excavations transformed archaeology and European taste.

Later eruptions and monitoring

Vesuvius has erupted many times since — notably in 1631, which killed thousands, and repeatedly through the 18th–20th centuries. Its last eruption, in March 1944 during the Second World War, destroyed villages and dozens of USAAF bombers at a nearby airfield. The volcano has been quiet since, but its flanks and the surrounding "red zone" are home to some 3 million people in the Naples metropolitan area, making it the most densely populated volcanic risk area in the world. The Vesuvius Observatory, founded in 1841, is the oldest volcanological observatory and monitors the mountain continuously; Italian authorities maintain evacuation plans for a future eruption.
