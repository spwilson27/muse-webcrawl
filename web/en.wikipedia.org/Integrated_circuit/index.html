An integrated circuit (IC), also known as a microchip, is a set of electronic circuits on one small flat piece (or "chip") of semiconductor material, usually silicon. Large numbers of miniaturized transistors and other electronic components are integrated onto the chip, connected by thin wires of metal deposited on its surface. Integrated circuits are the foundation of modern electronics, found in computers, phones, vehicles, and countless embedded systems.

Invention

The idea was developed independently in 1958–1959 by Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments, who demonstrated a working germanium circuit in September 1958, and Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor, whose silicon device using the planar process Jean Hoerni had developed was more practical to manufacture. Kilby received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2000 for his part in the invention.

Manufacturing

ICs are fabricated by photolithography: patterns are projected onto a silicon wafer coated with photoresist, and layers of circuitry are built up through deposition, etching, and ion implantation. Modern chips may contain tens of billions of transistors with features only a few nanometres across, fabricated in facilities ("fabs") costing billions of dollars. The steady miniaturization was summarized by Moore's law, the observation that transistor counts per chip roughly double every two years.

Types

Integrated circuits include digital logic chips and microprocessors, memory (such as DRAM and flash), analog devices such as amplifiers and voltage regulators, and mixed-signal devices combining both. Scales of integration progressed from small-scale integration (SSI) through large-scale (LSI) to very-large-scale integration (VLSI), with millions or billions of transistors on a single die.

Packaging

The silicon die is enclosed in a protective package of plastic or ceramic, with pins, leads, or solder balls (as in ball grid arrays) connecting the chip to a printed circuit board.
