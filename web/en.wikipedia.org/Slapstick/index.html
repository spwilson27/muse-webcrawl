Slapstick

Slapstick is a style of humour involving exaggerated physical activity that exceeds the boundaries of normal physical comedy — pratfalls, chases, collisions and mock violence presented so broadly that the audience laughs rather than winces. The name comes from the battacio, a pair of wooden slats used in Italian commedia dell'arte to create a loud smacking noise when one actor struck another.

Slapstick

Type: Physical comedy
Origins: Commedia dell'arte; vaudeville; silent film
Notable performers: Chaplin, Keaton, the Three Stooges, Mr. Bean

History

Slapstick flourished in music hall and vaudeville before becoming the dominant mode of early film comedy. Mack Sennett's Keystone Cops institutionalised the chase finale, while Charlie Chaplin, Buster Keaton and Harold Lloyd elevated the form in the silent era. Later masters include the Three Stooges, Lucille Ball, Jacques Tati and Rowan Atkinson's Mr. Bean.

Characteristics

Slapstick depends on precise timing and choreography: pain is cartoonish and consequence-free, props misbehave, dignity is punctured, and authority figures are humiliated. Modern scholars treat it as a physical language intelligible across cultures and languages.
