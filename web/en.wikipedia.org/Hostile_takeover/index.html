Hostile takeover

In a hostile takeover, an acquiring company attempts to buy a target company against the wishes of its management.

In business, a hostile takeover is the acquisition of one company (the target) by another (the acquirer) that is accomplished by going directly to the company's shareholders or fighting to replace management, rather than by negotiating with the target's board of directors. A takeover is considered hostile if the target's board rejects the offer but the bidder continues to pursue it.

Strategies

  • Tender offer — the acquirer offers to buy shareholders' shares directly, usually at a premium to the market price, bypassing management.
  • Proxy fight — the acquirer persuades shareholders to vote out existing management and elect a board willing to approve the deal.
  • Creeping acquisition — gradually purchasing shares on the open market up to the limit that triggers disclosure or control rules.

Defenses

Target companies use a variety of takeover defenses, including the poison pill (shareholder rights plan), staggered boards, golden parachutes, white knight arrangements in which a friendlier buyer is sought, and litigation. The "Pac-Man defense" turns the tables: the target attempts to acquire the raider.

History

Hostile takeovers became prominent in the United States in the 1980s, when corporate raiders and leveraged buyout firms used high-yield "junk" bonds to finance bids for large public companies. Famous contests included the battle for RJR Nabisco in 1988. Later waves included unsolicited bids in technology and pharmaceuticals, such as Oracle's acquisition of PeopleSoft in 2004.

Regulation

In the United States, hostile bids are regulated by the Williams Act of 1968, which requires disclosure of large share accumulations and regulates tender offers. In the United Kingdom, the Takeover Code administered by the Takeover Panel restricts defensive tactics by boards.
