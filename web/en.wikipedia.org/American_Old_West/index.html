American frontier

The American frontier, also known as the Old West or the Wild West, comprises the geography, history, folklore and culture associated with the forward wave of American expansion in mainland North America that began with European colonial settlements in the early 17th century and ended with the admission of Arizona and New Mexico as states in 1912. The archetypal "Wild West" period — cowboys, cattle drives, gunfighters, gold rushes and frontier towns — is centred on the decades after the American Civil War.

History

Westward migration accelerated after the Louisiana Purchase (1803) and the Lewis and Clark Expedition, drawing settlers along trails such as the Oregon and Santa Fe Trails. The Mexican–American War and the California Gold Rush of 1849 swelled the movement; the Homestead Act of 1862 granted land to settlers. Expansion displaced Native American peoples through war, treaties and forced relocation, culminating in conflicts such as the Sioux Wars. Railroads, barbed wire, mining booms and the cattle industry reshaped the region, while the Census Bureau declared the frontier "closed" in 1890.

Culture and myth

Historian Frederick Jackson Turner's 1893 "Frontier Thesis" argued the frontier forged American individualism and democracy. Dime novels, Buffalo Bill's Wild West shows, and later the Western film and television genres turned figures like Wild Bill Hickok, Billy the Kid and Wyatt Earp into legends, often romanticising a violent and diverse reality that also included Mexican vaqueros, Black and Chinese cowboys, and women homesteaders.
