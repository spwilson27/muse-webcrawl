Snellen chart

A typical Snellen chart, read from 6 metres (20 feet)

A Snellen chart is an eye chart used by eye care professionals to measure visual acuity. It is named after the Dutch ophthalmologist Herman Snellen, who developed the chart in 1862. The chart displays rows of block letters (optotypes), carefully designed so that at the designated distance each letter subtends an angle of five minutes of arc, with its strokes one minute wide. The letters decrease in size from the top of the chart to the bottom.

Snellen fractions

Results are expressed as a fraction such as 6/6 (in metres) or 20/20 (in feet). The numerator is the testing distance; the denominator is the distance at which a person with standard vision could read the same line. Thus 6/12 (20/40) means that what the patient reads at 6 metres could be read by a normally sighted person at 12 metres. "Normal" vision is conventionally 6/6, although many healthy eyes achieve 6/5 or better. Legal blindness in many jurisdictions corresponds to acuity of 6/60 (20/200) or worse in the better eye with correction.

Variants and limitations

For children and non-readers, charts using pictures or the tumbling E (where the patient indicates the direction the letter E faces) are used. The Snellen chart is criticised for irregular progression of letter sizes between lines and differing numbers of letters per line; the LogMAR chart, designed with uniform steps, is preferred in research and increasingly in clinical practice.
