Teratology

Teratology
FieldMedicine, developmental biology
SubjectCongenital abnormalities and their causes
Key agentsTeratogens (drugs, infections, radiation, chemicals)
EtymologyGreek teras ("monster") + logos ("study")

Teratology is the study of congenital abnormalities and abnormal development—their causes, mechanisms and patterns. Agents that disturb embryonic or fetal development are called teratogens. Although the word derives from the Greek for "monster", modern teratology is a scientific discipline spanning embryology, genetics, epidemiology and toxicology.

Principles

In 1959 James G. Wilson formulated six principles of teratology: susceptibility depends on the genotype of the conceptus and its interaction with the environment; it varies with the developmental stage at exposure; teratogens act through specific mechanisms on developing cells; the adverse outcomes are death, malformation, growth retardation and functional disorder; access of the agent to tissues depends on its nature; and effects increase with dose. The embryonic period (roughly weeks 3–8 of human gestation), when organs form, is the time of greatest vulnerability to structural malformations.

Major teratogens and history

Recognised teratogens include the rubella virus (identified by Norman Gregg in 1941 as a cause of congenital cataracts and deafness), alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorders), ionizing radiation, and drugs such as thalidomide—whose prescription to pregnant women in the late 1950s caused thousands of limb malformations and transformed drug regulation worldwide—and isotretinoin. Folate deficiency, causing neural tube defects, is preventable by periconceptional folic acid supplementation.
