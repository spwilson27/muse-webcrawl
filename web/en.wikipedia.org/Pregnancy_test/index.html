A pregnancy test is a diagnostic test used to determine whether a person is pregnant. Modern tests detect the hormone human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which is produced by the placenta shortly after implantation of a fertilised egg and rises rapidly in early pregnancy.

How tests work

Home urine tests use antibody-based lateral-flow strips: if hCG is present above a threshold (typically around 20–25 mIU/mL), it triggers a visible line or symbol. Blood tests performed in clinics are more sensitive and can measure the exact hCG level, allowing earlier detection and monitoring of the pregnancy's progress.

Accuracy and timing

Used correctly on or after the first day of a missed period, home tests are highly accurate. Testing too early, diluted urine, certain medications and rare medical conditions can cause false negatives or, less commonly, false positives. A positive result is normally confirmed clinically, and ultrasound can verify the location and viability of the pregnancy.

History

Earlier 20th-century tests were bioassays: the Aschheim–Zondek test (1928) injected urine into immature mice, and the later "rabbit test" and frog tests worked on similar principles. Immunoassays replaced animal tests in the 1960s, and the first home pregnancy test kits reached consumers in the late 1970s.
