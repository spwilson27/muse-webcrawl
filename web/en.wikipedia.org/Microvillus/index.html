Microvillus

Microvilli (singular: microvillus) are microscopic, finger-like projections of the cell membrane found on the surface of many epithelial cells. They greatly increase a cell's surface area and are specialized for absorption and secretion. Densely packed microvilli form a structure visible under the light microscope known as a brush border.

Structure

Each microvillus is about 0.5–1.0 micrometres long and roughly 0.1 micrometres thick, supported by a core bundle of parallel actin filaments cross-linked by proteins such as fimbrin and villin, and anchored to the cell's terminal web. The membrane over the microvilli is coated with a glycocalyx containing digestive enzymes.

Locations and functions

Microvilli are most prominent in the small intestine, where they cover the villi of enterocytes and complete the sequence of surface amplification (folds, villi, microvilli) that gives the gut an absorptive surface of hundreds of square metres. Membrane enzymes embedded in the brush border — lactase, sucrase-isomaltase, peptidases — perform the final steps of digestion ("brush border digestion"). Microvilli also occur in the kidney proximal tubule, the placenta, and on white blood cells, and are involved in mechanosensation in the ear via related stereocilia.

Clinical significance

Damage to microvilli features in coeliac disease, where villous atrophy reduces absorption, and in microvillus inclusion disease, a rare congenital disorder in which enterocytes fail to form proper microvilli, causing life-threatening diarrhea in newborns.
