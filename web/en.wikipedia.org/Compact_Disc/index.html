Compact disc

The compact disc (CD) is a digital optical disc data storage format that was co-developed by Philips and Sony to store and play digital audio recordings. Released in 1982, it was the first commercial optical disc format and revolutionized the music industry before being adapted for general data storage as the CD-ROM.

Compact disc
Media type: Optical disc
Capacity: typically 650–700 MB (74–80 minutes of audio)
Developed by: Philips, Sony
Released: 1982
Standard: Red Book (CD-DA)

History

Engineers at Philips and Sony collaborated in the late 1970s to define the Compact Disc Digital Audio standard, published as the "Red Book" in 1980. The first commercial discs and players reached the market in Japan and Europe in 1982. Legend holds that the 74-minute playing time was chosen so that Beethoven's Ninth Symphony could fit on a single disc. By the late 1980s CDs had overtaken vinyl records in sales, and in the 1990s they eclipsed cassette tapes as well.

Technology

A CD is a 120 mm polycarbonate disc coated with a thin reflective aluminium layer. Data is stored as a spiral track of microscopic pits and lands read by a 780 nm infrared laser. Audio is encoded as 16-bit stereo PCM sampled at 44.1 kHz, with Cross-Interleaved Reed–Solomon coding providing error correction that can conceal scratches and dust.

Formats

The "Rainbow Books" define related formats: CD-ROM for data, CD-R for recordable discs, CD-RW for rewritable media, Video CD, and Super Audio CD. The CD was succeeded as a video medium by the DVD and Blu-ray, and as a music medium by downloads and streaming, though it remains in use for niche releases and data distribution.
