History of mathematical notation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of mathematical notation traces the development of the symbols and conventions by which mathematics is written, from counting marks and rhetorical prose through the compressed symbolic language of modern mathematics. Mathematical notation includes numerals and number systems, symbols for operations (+, −, ×, ÷), relations (=, <, >), variables, and the specialised notations of algebra, calculus, set theory, logic, and beyond. The standard history of the subject is Florian Cajori's two-volume A History of Mathematical Notations (1928–29).

Early number systems

Counting is prehistoric—tally marks such as those on the Ishango bone date back tens of thousands of years. The Babylonians developed a sexagesimal (base-60) positional system with cuneiform numerals, while Egyptian mathematics used hieroglyphic and hieratic numerals in base 10. Greek mathematics used alphabetic numerals and expressed results geometrically in prose; Roman numerals, additive and later subtractive, served commerce for over a millennium. Two transformative innovations came from India: the decimal positional system and the symbol for zero, developed by the early centuries AD and described by Brahmagupta (628), transmitted to Europe via Arabic mathematics as the "Hindu–Arabic numerals".

Rhetorical and syncopated algebra

For most of history, algebra was "rhetorical": problems and solutions were written out in words. Diophantus (3rd century AD) introduced abbreviations—"syncopated" algebra—for the unknown and its powers. Indian and Islamic mathematicians, notably al-Khwarizmi (whose Algebra, c. 820, named the subject), developed systematic methods but still largely in prose. In Renaissance Europe, the German Cossists and Italian masters introduced symbols: Johannes Widmann printed + and − as commercial marks in 1489; Michael Stifel and others developed exponent notation; and Robert Recorde invented the equals sign = in 1557, choosing "a pair of parallels" because "no two things can be more equal".

The symbolic breakthrough

François Viète (1540–1603) made the decisive step of using letters systematically—vowels for unknowns, consonants for known quantities—turning algebra into a calculus of symbols. René Descartes's La Géométrie (1637) fixed much of the modern convention: x, y, z for unknowns, a, b, c for parameters, and superscript exponents. William Oughtred introduced × for multiplication; the obelus ÷, parentheses, and the radical sign √ (from Christoff Rudolff, 1525) spread through the 16th and 17th centuries. Thomas Harriot's posthumous work introduced < and > inequality signs (1631). Ludolph van Ceulen's constant was given the symbol π by William Jones in 1706 and popularised by Euler.

Calculus and the 18th–19th centuries

The invention of calculus produced rival notations: Newton's dots (ẋ) for fluxions versus Leibniz's differentials (dy/dx) and the long integral sign ∫ (1675) and the letter d—Leibniz's notation, being far more suggestive and manipulable, ultimately prevailed. Leonhard Euler standardised an enormous amount of notation: e for the base of natural logarithms, i for the imaginary unit, Σ for summation, f(x) for functions, and much trigonometric notation. The 19th century added vector and matrix notations, the nabla ∇, set theory's symbols (∈, ∪, ∩ under Cantor and Peano), and formal logic's quantifiers (∀, ∃ from Frege, Peano, and Russell).

Modern notation

The 20th century saw notation standardised through international typesetting and, from the 1930s, the Bourbaki group's conventions; through Donald Knuth's TeX system (1978), mathematical typesetting was standardised digitally. Notation continues to evolve with new fields—category theory's diagrams, Dirac's bra–ket notation in physics, and the symbolic languages of computer algebra—while remaining, at core, the system assembled between Viète and Euler.
