Freeport Doctrine

The Freeport Doctrine was a position articulated by Stephen A. Douglas during the second of the Lincoln–Douglas debates, held at Freeport, Illinois, on 27 August 1858. It held that the people of a United States territory could effectively exclude slavery from their borders, notwithstanding the Supreme Court's decision in Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857), which had held that Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories.

The question and the answer

Abraham Lincoln, Douglas's Republican opponent for the Senate, pressed him with a pointed question: could the people of a territory lawfully exclude slavery before statehood, despite Dred Scott? Douglas answered that they could, because slavery could not exist anywhere without supportive local legislation and police enforcement: a territorial legislature could simply refuse to enact slave codes, or pass "unfriendly legislation", and slavery would be excluded in practice regardless of the abstract constitutional right asserted by slaveholders.

Consequences

The answer preserved Douglas's doctrine of popular sovereignty and helped him win re-election to the Senate, but it alienated Southern Democrats, who demanded federal protection of slavery in the territories. The resulting split in the Democratic Party in 1860 — Northern Democrats nominating Douglas, Southern Democrats nominating John C. Breckinridge — helped enable Lincoln's election as president. Lincoln's question is often remembered as a politically astute trap: either answer cost Douglas support somewhere, and Douglas's reply at Freeport became a lasting point of contention in the sectional crisis that led to the American Civil War.
