Three-age system

The three-age system is a periodization of human prehistory into three time periods, according to the main material used in tools and weapons: the Stone Age, the Bronze Age, and the Iron Age. The system was most fully articulated by the Danish antiquarian Christian Jürgensen Thomsen in the 1830s, and it remains the basic framework for organising the archaeology of the Old World, with refinements such as the subdivision of the Stone Age into Palaeolithic, Mesolithic and Neolithic periods.

Origins

The idea that humanity had passed through ages of stone, bronze and iron is ancient: the Roman poet Lucretius described such a sequence in De rerum natura, and similar schemes appear in Hesiod's five ages of man. In 1734 the French scholar Nicolas Mahudel presented a paper arguing from grave goods that stone, bronze and iron tools had been used in succession. The Danish historian Vedel Simonsen proposed a similar scheme in 1813.

Thomsen's formulation

Christian Jürgensen Thomsen, curator of the Danish National Museum, arranged the museum's collections by material and in his Guide to Northern Antiquity (1836) set out the three-age system as an empirical, artefact-based chronology: closed finds such as burials showed that stone tools occurred without metal, bronze with little iron, and iron latest of all. His student Jens Jacob Asmussen Worsaae confirmed the sequence through stratigraphy. In 1865 John Lubbock split the Stone Age into the Palaeolithic ("Old Stone Age") and Neolithic ("New Stone Age"); the Mesolithic was added later for the transitional period after the last Ice Age.

Subdivisions and regional variants

In parts of Africa, where iron working followed stone working without a general bronze phase, archaeologists often speak instead of the Early, Middle and Later Stone Age followed directly by an Iron Age. In the Near East a Chalcolithic ("Copper Age") is commonly inserted between the Neolithic and Bronze Age. The system does not apply to the pre-Columbian Americas, where metallurgy developed late and independently, and regional chronologies are used instead.

Criticism

Critics note that the system is technological and Eurocentric in origin, that the "ages" began and ended at very different times in different regions, and that materials overlapped for long periods — iron, for instance, coexisted with bronze and stone for centuries. With the advent of radiocarbon dating, the sequence ceased to be a primary dating tool, but it remains a convenient shorthand for technological stages of prehistory.
