Nabataeans

The Nabataeans (Nabataean Aramaic: NBṬW) were an ancient Arab people who inhabited northern Arabia and the southern Levant. Their loosely controlled trading network, which centred on strings of oases they controlled, stretched from their capital at Petra in modern Jordan southward into the Hejaz and north to Damascus.

History

Originally nomadic Arabs, the Nabataeans grew wealthy taxing and protecting the incense and spice trade — frankincense and myrrh from southern Arabia, silk and spices from the East — carried by caravans across the desert. By 85 BCE King Aretas III ruled Damascus. The Nabataean Kingdom retained independence between the Seleucid, Roman, and Parthian spheres until Emperor Trajan annexed it in 106 CE as the province of Arabia Petraea.

Culture and achievements

The Nabataeans were master hydraulic engineers, harvesting scarce rainwater with dams, cisterns, and ceramic pipes that made Petra flourish in the desert. They carved monumental rock-cut tombs and temples at Petra and Hegra (Madain Salih), developed a distinctive fine painted pottery, and wrote in a late form of Aramaic whose cursive script is an ancestor of the Arabic alphabet.
