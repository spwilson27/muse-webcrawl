Henri Desgrange (31 January 1865 – 16 August 1940) was a French bicycle racer, sports journalist, and the founder and first organiser of the Tour de France. As a rider he set a dozen world track records, including the first recognised hour record in 1893 (35.325 km). As editor of the newspaper L'Auto, he conceived the Tour in 1903 to boost circulation against the rival paper Le Vélo.

The Tour de France

The idea for a multi-stage race around France was proposed by Desgrange's young colleague Géo Lefèvre and enthusiastically adopted by Desgrange, who directed the race from its first edition until 1939. He ruled the Tour with famous severity, designing ever more gruelling routes — introducing the high Pyrenees in 1910 and the Alps in 1911 — and in 1919 he introduced the yellow jersey (maillot jaune) so the race leader could be easily identified. His initials appear on the yellow jersey to this day, and the Souvenir Henri Desgrange prize is awarded to the first rider over the Col du Galibier or the race's highest point.

Other work

Desgrange was a tireless advocate of physical education, helped develop the brevet system of audax riding, and wrote extensively on cycling training. His authoritarian style shaped the Tour's identity for decades.
