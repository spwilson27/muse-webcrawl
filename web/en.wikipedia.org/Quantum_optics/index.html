Quantum optics

Quantum optics is a branch of atomic, molecular, and optical physics dealing with how individual quanta of light, known as photons, interact with atoms and molecules. It includes the study of the particle-like properties of photons. Photons have been used to test many of the counter-intuitive predictions of quantum mechanics, such as entanglement and teleportation, and are a useful resource in quantum information processing.

History

The field developed from the quantisation of the electromagnetic field, beginning with Max Planck's explanation of black-body radiation in 1900 and Albert Einstein's 1905 account of the photoelectric effect. The term photon was introduced by Gilbert N. Lewis in 1926. The invention of the laser in 1960 transformed the discipline, and Roy J. Glauber's quantum theory of optical coherence, published in 1963, supplied much of its modern mathematical foundation; Glauber shared the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

Key concepts

Quantum optics studies nonclassical states of light, including coherent states, squeezed states, Fock states and entangled photon pairs generated by spontaneous parametric down-conversion. Central phenomena include photon antibunching, the Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect, cavity quantum electrodynamics, electromagnetically induced transparency and Hong–Ou–Mandel interference. Measurement techniques include homodyne detection and photon counting with single-photon detectors.

Applications

Quantum optical methods underpin quantum key distribution, optical atomic clocks, quantum-enhanced metrology and interferometers such as those used in gravitational-wave observatories, where squeezed light improves sensitivity beyond the standard quantum limit.
