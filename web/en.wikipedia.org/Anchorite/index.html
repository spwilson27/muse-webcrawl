Anchorite

An anchorite or anchoret (female: anchoress; from Ancient Greek anachōrētḗs, "one who has withdrawn") is someone who, for religious reasons, withdraws from secular society to lead an intensely prayerful, ascetic life. Anchorites are frequently considered a type of hermit, but unlike hermits, anchorites were required to take a vow of stability of place, opting for lifelong confinement to a single cell, known as an anchorhold, which was often attached to a church.

History

The anchoritic life became widespread in the early and high Middle Ages, especially in England, where anchorholds were attached to parish churches in villages and towns. The anchorite was enclosed in a ceremony that included elements of the office of the dead, symbolizing death to the world. The cell typically had small openings: one into the church, allowing the anchorite to hear Mass and receive communion, and one to the outside world, through which food was delivered and visitors could seek counsel.

Notable anchorites

The best-known English anchoress is Julian of Norwich, whose Revelations of Divine Love — based on visions she experienced in 1373 — is the earliest surviving book in English known to have been written by a woman. The 13th-century guide Ancrene Wisse ("Guide for Anchoresses") was written for three sisters living as anchoresses and remains an important work of Middle English prose.

Distinction from hermits

While hermits (eremites) lived in solitude but were generally free to move, and often lived in the wilderness, anchorites were permanently enclosed. The tradition has roots in the Desert Fathers and Mothers of Egypt. Anchoritic life largely declined in England with the Dissolution of the Monasteries in the 16th century, though solitary religious vocations continue in various forms.
