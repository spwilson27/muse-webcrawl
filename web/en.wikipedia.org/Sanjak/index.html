A sanjak (Ottoman Turkish: سنجاق, sancak, lit. "banner" or "flag") was an administrative division of the Ottoman Empire. Sanjaks were subdivisions of provinces (eyalets, later vilayets), and each was governed by a sanjak-bey, who was responsible for military mobilization, taxation and order within his district. The term reflects the practice by which a governor received a banner from the sultan as the symbol of his authority.

Organization

Within the classical Ottoman timar system, a sanjak was composed of fiefs (timars, zeamets and hasses) whose holders owed cavalry service to the sanjak-bey, who in turn served under the beylerbey of the eyalet. Sanjaks were further divided into kazas (districts under a kadi, or judge). Some sanjaks were attached directly to the imperial center, and a few privileged districts — later called mutasarrifates, such as Mount Lebanon and Jerusalem — reported directly to Constantinople.

Later usage

The Tanzimat reforms of the 19th century reorganized sanjaks within the vilayet system, where they were also called liva and administered by a mutasarrif. After the Ottoman defeat in the First World War, several former sanjaks became objects of international dispute — most famously the Sanjak of Alexandretta, transferred from Syria to Turkey in 1939 as Hatay Province. The name survives geographically in the region of Sandžak, straddling Serbia and Montenegro, named for the former Sanjak of Novi Pazar.
