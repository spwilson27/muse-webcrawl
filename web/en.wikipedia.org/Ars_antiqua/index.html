Ars antiqua

Ars antiqua ("old art"), also called ars veterum or ars vetus, is a term of music history used by modern scholars for the polyphonic music of the high medieval period, roughly from 1170 to 1310, centred on Paris and the Notre Dame school. The term was coined retrospectively by musicians of the following era, who contrasted the "old art" with their own ars nova.

Characteristics

Ars antiqua music is characterised by the rhythmic modes — patterned long–short rhythms derived from poetic metres — and by genres such as organum, conductus, clausula and the early motet. The Notre Dame composers Léonin and Pérotin expanded organum to three and four voices, while theorists such as Franco of Cologne (c. 1260) systematised mensural notation, in which note shapes themselves indicate duration, freeing polyphony from the modal patterns.

Legacy

The motet, the period's most innovative genre, layered new texts over chant-derived tenor lines and became the principal vehicle of compositional experimentation. Around 1310–1320 the innovations described in Philippe de Vitry's treatise Ars nova — especially greater rhythmic flexibility and the use of isorhythm — led contemporaries to label the earlier style "antique", though ars antiqua techniques continued in use for decades.
