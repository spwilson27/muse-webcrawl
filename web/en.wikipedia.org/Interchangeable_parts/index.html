Interchangeable parts

Interchangeable parts are parts (components) that are, for practical purposes, identical. They are made to specifications that ensure that they are so nearly identical that they will fit into any assembly of the same type. One such part can freely replace another, without any custom fitting, such as filing. This interchangeability allows easy assembly of new devices, and easier repair of existing devices, while minimizing the time and skill required of the assembly or repair worker.

History

The French gunsmith Honoré Blanc demonstrated interchangeable musket lock parts in the 1780s, influencing Thomas Jefferson and American armory practice. In the United States, Eli Whitney is popularly credited with the idea, though historians credit the federal armories — especially John Hall and Simeon North — with achieving true interchangeability in the early nineteenth century. The system, known in Europe as the "American system of manufactures", spread through precision machine tools, gauges, and inspection, and underpinned mass production in industries from sewing machines to automobiles.

Significance

Interchangeability decoupled production from individual craft fitting, enabling the division of labor, spare-parts logistics, and the maintenance of complex machines far from their factories.
