Critique of Practical Reason

Critique of Practical Reason
Author: Immanuel Kant
Original title: Kritik der praktischen Vernunft
Published: 1788, Riga
Subject: Moral philosophy
Preceded by: Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785)
Followed by: Critique of Judgment (1790)

The Critique of Practical Reason (German: Kritik der praktischen Vernunft) is the second of Immanuel Kant's three critiques, published in 1788. It follows on from Kant's first Critique, the Critique of Pure Reason, and deals with his moral philosophy. The third, the Critique of Judgment, followed in 1790. The second Critique is commonly abbreviated as KpV.

Overview

Where the first Critique examines theoretical reason and the conditions of knowledge, the second investigates practical reason—reason as a faculty that determines the will. Kant argues that the moral law is given to us as a “fact of reason” and that its supreme principle is the categorical imperative: act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it become a universal law. An action has moral worth only when done from duty, not merely in conformity with duty.

Autonomy and freedom

Kant locates the foundation of morality in the autonomy of the rational will, which gives the moral law to itself, in contrast to heteronomy, in which the will is determined by inclination or external authority. Although freedom of the will cannot be proven theoretically, Kant argues it must be postulated from the standpoint of practical reason: “freedom is the ratio essendi of the moral law”.

Postulates and the highest good

The Dialectic of the second Critique introduces the postulates of practical reason—freedom, the immortality of the soul, and the existence of God—needed to make coherent the pursuit of the highest good (summum bonum), the union of virtue and happiness. Kant's famous conclusion contrasts “the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me”.
