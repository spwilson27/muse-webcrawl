Extraterritorial jurisdiction

States sometimes apply their law beyond their territory.

Extraterritorial jurisdiction (ETJ) is the legal ability of a government to exercise authority beyond its normal territorial boundaries. Under international law, jurisdiction is primarily territorial, but states may legislate with extraterritorial effect on several recognized bases.

Bases of jurisdiction

The classic principles include the nationality principle (over a state's own nationals abroad), the protective principle (over acts threatening the state's security, such as counterfeiting its currency), passive personality (where the victim is a national), and universality, under which any state may prosecute certain grave crimes such as piracy, genocide, and crimes against humanity regardless of where they occurred. The "effects doctrine" in antitrust law asserts jurisdiction over foreign conduct with substantial domestic effects.

Applications

Examples include anti-corruption statutes such as the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act and the UK Bribery Act, sanctions legislation, tax rules reaching citizens abroad, and data-protection regimes such as the EU's GDPR, which applies to organizations processing data of people in the Union. Historically, "extraterritoriality" also referred to capitulation regimes, such as those once imposed in China and the Ottoman Empire, under which foreign nationals were exempt from local law—an arrangement abolished in the 20th century.

Tensions

Extraterritorial assertions can conflict with other states' sovereignty and produce blocking statutes, diplomatic disputes, and compliance dilemmas for multinational entities subject to contradictory legal commands.
