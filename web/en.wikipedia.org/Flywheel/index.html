Flywheel

A flywheel on a stationary steam engine.

A flywheel is a mechanical device which uses the conservation of angular momentum to store rotational energy; a form of kinetic energy proportional to the product of its moment of inertia about its axis and the square of its rotational speed. In particular, assuming the flywheel's moment of inertia is constant (i.e., a flywheel with fixed mass and second moment of area revolving about some fixed axis), then the stored rotational energy is directly associated with the square of its rotational speed.

Since a flywheel serves to store mechanical energy for later use, it is natural to consider it as a kinetic energy analogue of an electrical inductor. Once suitably abstracted, this shared principle of energy storage is described in the generalised concept of an accumulator. As with other types of accumulators, a flywheel smooths sufficiently small deviations in the power output of a system, thereby effectively playing the role of a low-pass filter with respect to the mechanical velocity (angular, or otherwise) of the system.

Applications

  • Engines — on a reciprocating engine, the flywheel smooths the pulses of torque from the pistons, stores energy between power strokes, and carries the crankshaft through the non-power strokes.
  • Machinery — punches, shears and riveting machines use flywheels to deliver large forces from a modest continuous power supply. The potter's wheel is an ancient flywheel.
  • Energy storage — flywheel energy storage systems (FESS) use high-speed composite rotors on magnetic bearings in vacuum enclosures for grid frequency regulation and uninterruptible power supplies.
  • Vehicles — flywheel hybrid systems have been used in buses and in motorsport (e.g., KERS) to recover braking energy.
  • Attitude control — reaction wheels and momentum wheels stabilise spacecraft.

Physics

The kinetic energy stored in a rotating flywheel is E = ½Iω², where I is the moment of inertia and ω the angular velocity. For a solid disc, I = ½mr²; concentrating mass at the rim increases I for a given mass. Maximum practical energy is limited by the tensile strength of the rotor material — if the rim speed exceeds the material's capacity, the wheel bursts — which is why modern high-speed flywheels use carbon-fibre composites.

History

The principle is ancient: spindle whorls and potter's wheels exploited rotational inertia in prehistory. Flywheels became essential components of the steam engines of the Industrial Revolution, and James Watt's engines are closely associated with large spoked flywheels that also carried the drive belt.
