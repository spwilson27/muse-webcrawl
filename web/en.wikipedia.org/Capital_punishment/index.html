Capital punishment, also known as the death penalty, is the state-sanctioned killing of a person as punishment for a crime. It has been practised by most societies historically, but since the mid-twentieth century a global trend toward abolition has seen a majority of the world's countries abolish it in law or in practice. A minority of states — including China, Iran, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and the United States — continue to carry out executions.

History

Death penalties are codified in the earliest surviving law codes, such as the Code of Hammurabi, and for offenses ranging from murder to theft. In Europe, execution methods included hanging, beheading, and burning. The Enlightenment philosopher Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764) argued against the penalty's justice and utility. Venezuela became the first country to abolish it for all crimes (1863), and abolition spread widely after 1945, accelerating with the adoption of human-rights instruments such as the European Convention on Human Rights' protocols.

Methods

Methods in current or recent use include hanging, shooting, lethal injection, electrocution, beheading, and stoning. Lethal injection predominates in the United States; hanging remains common in parts of Asia and the Middle East.

Debate

Supporters cite deterrence, retribution, and incapacitation; opponents cite the risk of executing the innocent (well over a hundred death-row exonerations have occurred in the US since 1973), racial and economic disparities in application, doubtful deterrent effect in comparative studies, and moral objections. International bodies including the UN General Assembly have repeatedly called for a global moratorium.
