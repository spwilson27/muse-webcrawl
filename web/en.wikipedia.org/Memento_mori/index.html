Memento mori

Memento mori (Latin for "remember [that you have] to die") is an artistic or symbolic trope acting as a reminder of the inevitability of death. The concept has its roots in the philosophers of classical antiquity and Christianity, and appeared in funerary art and architecture from the medieval period onwards.

History

In ancient Rome, tradition held that a servant accompanied a victorious general in his triumph, whispering that he was mortal. Christian art and literature adopted the theme strongly: skulls, hourglasses, extinguished candles, and wilting flowers in paintings; the danse macabre; and tombs decorated with decaying cadavers (transi tombs) all reminded viewers of life's brevity. The vanitas still lifes of the 17th century are the best-known painted form.

Expressions

Beyond art, memento mori took the form of mourning jewellery and rings bearing skulls, inscriptions on clocks and gravestones ("hodie mihi, cras tibi" — "today me, tomorrow you"), and devotional practices and philosophical reflections on death in Stoicism and Buddhism alike.

Modern use

The motif persists in contemporary art, literature, and popular culture, and in revived interest in Stoic practice, where reflecting on mortality is used to focus on living well.
