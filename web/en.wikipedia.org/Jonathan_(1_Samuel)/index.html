Jonathan (1 Samuel)

House: House of Saul
Father: Saul, king of Israel
Children: Merib-Baal (Mephibosheth)
Died: Mount Gilboa (battle against the Philistines)

Jonathan (Hebrew: יְהוֹנָתָן Yəhōnāṯān, "Yahweh has given") is a hero of the Kingdom of Israel, appearing in the Book of Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. He was the eldest son of King Saul and a military commander of distinction, and is best remembered for his covenant friendship with David, Saul's eventual successor — a bond that led him to defend David against his own father's attempts to kill him.

Biblical narrative

Jonathan first appears leading a garrison that defeated the Philistines at Geba, provoking a full-scale war. In a celebrated episode (1 Samuel 14), Jonathan and his armour-bearer alone climbed to a Philistine outpost at Michmash and routed it, throwing the enemy camp into confusion and enabling an Israelite victory. His father's rash oath had decreed death to anyone who ate before evening; Jonathan, unaware, tasted honey, and was nearly executed before the soldiers ransomed him.

After David's victory over Goliath, the text says "the soul of Jonathan was knit to the soul of David, and Jonathan loved him as his own soul". Jonathan gave David his robe, sword, bow and belt, and made a covenant with him. When Saul pursued David, Jonathan warned him, interceded with Saul, and at their final meeting in the wilderness of Ziph renewed the covenant, acknowledging that David would be king and he himself would stand second to him. Jonathan died with his father and two brothers at the Battle of Mount Gilboa, fighting the Philistines; David lamented them in the "Song of the Bow", declaring of Jonathan, "thy love to me was wonderful, passing the love of women." David later honoured his covenant by sheltering Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth at the royal table.

Interpretation

Jonathan's friendship with David is one of the Bible's most studied relationships, widely cited as a paradigm of covenant loyalty and selfless friendship; its precise nature — political alliance, sworn brotherhood, or romantic attachment — has been debated by scholars and commentators for centuries.
