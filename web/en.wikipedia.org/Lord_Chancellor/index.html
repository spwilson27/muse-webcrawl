Lord Chancellor

The Lord Chancellor, formally the Lord High Chancellor of Great Britain, is one of the most senior officials in the government of the United Kingdom and, historically, the highest-ranking Great Officer of State. The Lord Chancellor is appointed by the Sovereign on the advice of the Prime Minister and is a member of the Cabinet.

Functions

The office combines roles in all three branches of government: the holder sits in Cabinet as a minister (since 2007 also holding the title Secretary of State for Justice and heading the Ministry of Justice), is responsible for the efficient functioning and independence of the courts, and formerly presided over the House of Lords and served as head of the judiciary of England and Wales. The Constitutional Reform Act 2005 transferred the judicial and Lords functions to the Lord Chief Justice and the Lord Speaker respectively.

History

The chancellorship dates to the Anglo-Saxon period; the Chancellor was keeper of the Great Seal and of the monarch's conscience, presiding over the Court of Chancery from which the law of equity developed. Notable holders include Thomas More, Francis Bacon, Thomas Wolsey and, in modern times, Lord Irvine of Lairg. Since 2007 the office has commonly been held by an MP rather than a peer.
