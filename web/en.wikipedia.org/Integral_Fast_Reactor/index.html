The Integral Fast Reactor (IFR) was a design project for a nuclear reactor with a fast neutron spectrum and an on-site, closed fuel cycle, developed by Argonne National Laboratory in the United States from 1984 until the programme was terminated by Congress in 1994. The word "integral" refers to the integration of the reactor with electrorefining-based fuel recycling facilities on the same site, so that spent fuel would never leave the plant as separated plutonium.

Design

The reference design was a pool-type, liquid-sodium-cooled fast reactor using metallic fuel — an alloy of uranium, plutonium and zirconium developed from the fuel of the Experimental Breeder Reactor II (EBR-II). Metallic fuel conducts heat well and swells predictably, permitting a high burnup. Spent fuel was to be recycled on site by pyroprocessing: electrorefining in molten salt separates uranium and transuranic elements together, a mixture unsuitable for weapons, and the actinides are cast into new fuel pins. In principle the cycle extracts far more energy from uranium than once-through thermal reactors and reduces the long-lived component of nuclear waste, since actinides that would otherwise dominate waste radiotoxicity for tens of thousands of years are fissioned instead.

Safety demonstrations

EBR-II, the programme's test reactor, demonstrated passive safety in April 1986 experiments widely cited by advocates: with coolant pumps stopped and control rods not inserted, reactor power fell naturally to safe levels because thermal expansion of the metallic fuel and core increased neutron leakage. A simultaneous loss-of-heat-sink test showed similar benign behaviour.

Cancellation and influence

The IFR programme was cancelled in 1994 by the Clinton administration and Congress on grounds including cost and nonproliferation policy. Its technologies influenced later Generation IV International Forum work on sodium-cooled fast reactors and pyroprocessing research in the United States, South Korea and elsewhere, and EBR-II operated until 1994. Proponents continue to cite the design as a solution to nuclear waste accumulation and uranium resource limits, while critics note that commercial deployment of fast reactors and pyroprocessing remains unrealised.
