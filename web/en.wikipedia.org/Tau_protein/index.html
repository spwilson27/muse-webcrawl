Tau protein

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The tau proteins (abbreviated from tubulin associated unit) form a group of six highly soluble protein isoforms produced by alternative splicing from the gene MAPT (microtubule-associated protein tau). They have roles primarily in maintaining the stability of microtubules in axons and are abundant in the central nervous system, mainly in neurons.

Function

Tau proteins interact with tubulin to stabilize microtubules and promote tubulin assembly into microtubules. In humans, mutations in the MAPT gene and abnormal aggregation of hyperphosphorylated tau are associated with a class of neurodegenerative diseases known as tauopathies, including Alzheimer's disease, frontotemporal dementia, progressive supranuclear palsy and chronic traumatic encephalopathy. In Alzheimer's disease, tau becomes abnormally phosphorylated and forms neurofibrillary tangles inside neurons, one of the two hallmark lesions of the disease alongside amyloid plaques.

Structure and isoforms

Alternative splicing of exon 10 yields tau isoforms with either three (3R) or four (4R) microtubule-binding repeats; healthy adult human brain contains roughly equal amounts of each, and imbalance between them is characteristic of several tauopathies. Tau is an intrinsically disordered protein whose binding to microtubules is regulated by phosphorylation at many sites by kinases such as GSK-3β and CDK5.

Clinical significance

Tau is measured in cerebrospinal fluid and, increasingly, in blood plasma (phospho-tau species such as p-tau181 and p-tau217) as a biomarker for Alzheimer's disease, and tau aggregation is imaged in the living brain by PET ligands.
