Moss

KingdomPlantae
DivisionBryophyta
Classes8 (incl. Sphagnopsida, Polytrichopsida, Bryopsida)
Species~12,000

Mosses are small, non-vascular flowerless plants in the botanical division Bryophyta. They typically form dense green clumps or mats, often in damp or shady locations. The individual plants are usually composed of simple leaves that are generally only one cell thick, attached to a stem that may be branched or unbranched and has only a limited role in conducting water and nutrients. Although some species have conducting tissues, these are poorly developed and structurally different from those of vascular plants.

Life cycle

Mosses have a life cycle dominated by the haploid gametophyte generation: the familiar leafy plant is haploid, producing gametes in antheridia and archegonia. After fertilisation, a diploid sporophyte grows out of the archegonium, typically consisting of a stalk (seta) topped by a capsule (sporangium) in which spores are produced by meiosis. Mosses lack true roots, anchoring themselves instead with multicellular rhizoids, and they absorb water and nutrients largely through their leaves. They reproduce asexually by fragmentation as well.

Ecology and uses

Mosses are important pioneers on bare rock and soil, retain moisture, and in the case of peat mosses (Sphagnum) build up vast peatlands that store enormous amounts of carbon. Historically, mosses were used for bedding, wound dressings, and insulation, and today in horticulture and floristry. Like other bryophytes, they are sensitive bioindicators of air pollution.
