Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament

Formation: 1957
Headquarters: London, United Kingdom
Aim: Unilateral nuclear disarmament by the United Kingdom

The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) is a British organisation that advocates unilateral nuclear disarmament by the United Kingdom, international nuclear disarmament, and tighter regulation of nuclear weapons through treaties such as the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty. Founded in November 1957, it became one of the largest peace movements in British history and its symbol — designed by Gerald Holtom in 1958 from the semaphore letters N and D — became an internationally recognised peace sign.

History

CND arose amid public anxiety over British hydrogen bomb tests. Its founding figures included Canon John Collins (its first chair), Bertrand Russell (its first president), J. B. Priestley, and Michael Foot. Its first great action was the 1958 Easter march from London to the Atomic Weapons Research Establishment at Aldermaston, which became an annual event and drew tens of thousands. In 1960 the Labour Party conference voted for unilateral disarmament, a decision reversed the following year.

Revival and later campaigns

The movement surged again in the early 1980s in response to the deployment of U.S. cruise missiles in Europe and the stationing of ground-launched cruise missiles at RAF Greenham Common, where a women's peace camp maintained a long vigil. CND membership and demonstrations reached levels rivalling the 1960s. In later decades CND campaigned against the replacement of the Trident submarine system, against the Iraq War (it was a founding member of the Stop the War Coalition), and in favour of the UN Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons.

Organisation and influence

CND operates through local groups and affiliated national campaigns in Scotland and Wales (CND Cymru), with specialist sections and an annual conference setting policy. Although it has never achieved its central aim, it has shaped British public debate on nuclear weapons and influenced generations of activists in other social movements.
