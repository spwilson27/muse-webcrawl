Jim Bridger

James Felix "Jim" Bridger (17 March 1804 – 17 July 1881) was an American mountain man, fur trapper, and frontier guide, widely regarded as one of the greatest explorers of the American West. Beginning in 1822 with the Ashley-Henry fur expedition, Bridger ranged across the Rocky Mountains for more than forty years. He was among the first non-Native Americans to see the geysers of the Yellowstone region and is often credited as the first to report the Great Salt Lake (1824–25). He co-founded Fort Bridger in Wyoming (1843), a vital way station on the Oregon, California, and Mormon trails, and served as a scout and guide for the US Army and countless emigrant parties, blazing routes such as Bridger Pass and the Bridger Trail.

Early life and the fur trade

Bridger was born in Virginia and raised near St. Louis, Missouri, orphaned by his early teens and apprenticed to a blacksmith. In 1822, at eighteen, he answered William Ashley's famous advertisement for "enterprising young men" to ascend the Missouri River, joining the expedition that founded the Rocky Mountain fur trade. He spent nearly two decades trapping beaver, attending the annual rendezvous, and surviving the trade's dangers — including, famously (and controversially), the 1823 mauling of Hugh Glass by a grizzly bear: the young Bridger was one of two men who stayed with the grievously wounded Glass and then left him, an episode later romanticised in the novel and film The Revenant, though details and Bridger's culpability are debated by historians.

Exploration

Bridger's knowledge of the northern Rockies was unrivalled. In the winter of 1824–25 he floated down the Bear River to the Great Salt Lake, which — tasting its salinity — he reported as an arm of the Pacific Ocean; he is generally considered the first European-American to see it (Étienne Provost reached it at about the same time). He traversed the South Pass region, explored the Yellowstone plateau's geysers and boiling springs (his tall tales of a "petrified forest" and a mountain of glass made listeners discount his true reports), and ranged from the Great Salt Lake to the Yellowstone and from the Green River to the Three Forks of the Missouri. In 1850 he found Bridger Pass in present Wyoming, later used by the Overland Stage and the Union Pacific Railroad.

Fort Bridger and guide service

In 1843 Bridger and Louis Vasquez built Fort Bridger on Blacks Fork of the Green River (in present-day Wyoming), supplying and repairing the wagons of emigrants on the Oregon and California trails and trading with the Shoshone, with whom Bridger had close ties — his third wife was a daughter of Chief Washakie. Mormon settlers later purchased and contested control of the fort; Bridger claimed he was driven out by Mormon authorities in 1853, a dispute that fed the tensions of the Utah War. From the 1850s he served repeatedly as guide and scout for the US Army — with Albert Sidney Johnston's Utah expedition, in the Powder River campaigns of the 1860s, and at Fort Laramie and Fort Phil Kearny — and in 1864 blazed the Bridger Trail to the Montana goldfields as a safer alternative to the Bozeman Trail. His counsel against engaging Red Cloud's forces preceded the Fetterman Fight disaster of 1866.

Later life and legacy

Failing eyesight ended Bridger's scouting career by 1868. He settled on a farm near Westport (Kansas City), Missouri, where he died in 1881. He was reburied in 1904 in Mount Washington Cemetery, Kansas City, beneath a monument erected through the efforts of Major General Grenville Dodge. His name is preserved across the West — the Bridger Mountains, Bridger National Forest, Bridger Pass, and Fort Bridger — and in literature he stands as the archetypal mountain man, illiterate in books yet master of an encyclopaedic geography of the Rockies.
