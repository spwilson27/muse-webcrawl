The Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (Pub. L. 107-204), commonly called Sarbanes–Oxley or SOX, is a United States federal law that mandates certain practices in financial record keeping and reporting for public corporations. It was enacted in response to the accounting scandals at Enron, WorldCom and other firms, which cost investors billions when the companies collapsed. The act passed with overwhelming bipartisan support and is widely regarded as the most significant reform of U.S. securities law since the 1930s.

Major provisions

The act's eleven titles established the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board to regulate auditors; required chief executives and chief financial officers to certify financial reports personally (Sections 302 and 906); mandated management assessment and auditor attestation of internal controls over financial reporting (Section 404); strengthened audit committee independence; restricted auditors from providing many non-audit services; lengthened prison sentences for securities fraud; and protected corporate whistleblowers. Section 404's internal-control requirements have been both the most consequential and the most criticized for their compliance costs, particularly for smaller companies, some of which were later exempted.

Effects and debate

Supporters credit SOX with restoring investor confidence and improving the reliability of financial statements; critics argue it raised the cost of going public in the United States and drove listings abroad. Some reporting requirements were eased by the JOBS Act of 2012 for emerging growth companies, but the act's core framework remains in force.
