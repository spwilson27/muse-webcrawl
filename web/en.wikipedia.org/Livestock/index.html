Livestock

Livestock are domesticated animals raised in an agricultural setting to provide labour and produce commodities such as meat, eggs, milk, fur, leather, and wool. The term is sometimes used to refer solely to animals raised for consumption, and sometimes solely to farmed ruminants such as cattle, sheep, and goats.

History

The domestication of animals began in the Neolithic period, with sheep and goats domesticated in Southwest Asia around 10,000 years ago, followed by cattle and pigs. Animal husbandry spread with farming across the world and was transformed by selective breeding from the 18th century onwards, notably in Britain under pioneers such as Robert Bakewell.

Types

Globally the most important livestock species are cattle, sheep, goats, pigs and chickens, followed by buffalo, horses, donkeys, ducks, turkeys, rabbits and bees. Livestock supply roughly a third of humanity's protein intake and support the livelihoods of around a billion people, while also accounting for a significant share of agricultural land use and greenhouse gas emissions.

Husbandry

Livestock are kept under systems ranging from nomadic pastoralism and mixed smallholder farming to intensive, industrialised production. Management covers feeding, breeding, housing and veterinary care; animal welfare standards, disease control and environmental impact are major subjects of modern policy debate.
