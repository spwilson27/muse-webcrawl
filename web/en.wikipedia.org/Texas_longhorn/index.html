Texas Longhorn

The Texas Longhorn is an American breed of beef cattle, characterized by its long horns, which can span more than 8 feet (2.4 m) from tip to tip. It descends from cattle brought by Spanish colonists to the Americas beginning in the late fifteenth century, which interbred in Texas with Anglo-American stock. The breed is known for its hardiness, lean beef, longevity, and ability to thrive on marginal grazing land.

History

Iberian cattle, of Andalusian origin, reached the Caribbean with Columbus's second voyage in 1493 and spread into Mexico and then Texas with Spanish missions and ranching. Over centuries the cattle adapted to the brush country, developing long legs, resistance to disease, and the breed's signature horns. After the American Civil War, millions of Longhorns were driven north on trails such as the Chisholm Trail to railheads in Kansas, supplying beef to eastern markets.

By the early twentieth century the breed was nearly displaced by faster-fattening British breeds and by crossbreeding. Preservation efforts began in 1927, when the U.S. Forest Service assembled a herd at the Wichita Mountains Wildlife Refuge in Oklahoma; private ranchers such as those of the "seven families" also maintained pure lines. The breed has since recovered and is kept for beef, for its historic and cultural value, and as a symbol of Texas — it is the official large mammal of the state and the mascot of the University of Texas at Austin (whose live mascot is named Bevo).

Characteristics

  • Horns extend sideways and upward, often with a slight twist; recorded spans exceed 10 feet (3 m) in exceptional steers.
  • Coat colour is highly variable — red, black, brown, white, and spotted or speckled patterns.
  • Cows weigh roughly 800–1,100 lb (360–500 kg); bulls 1,000–1,600 lb (450–730 kg).
  • Famed for calving ease, fertility, and longevity, with cows producing into their late teens or twenties.
