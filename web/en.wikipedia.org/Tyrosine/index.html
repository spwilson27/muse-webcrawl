Tyrosine (symbol Tyr or Y) is an α-amino acid used in the biosynthesis of proteins. It contains an amino group, a carboxylic acid group and a side chain consisting of a benzyl group bearing a hydroxyl — a phenol — which makes it a polar, non-essential amino acid for humans, who can synthesise it from phenylalanine. Its name derives from the Greek tyros, meaning cheese, from whose protein casein it was first isolated by Justus von Liebig in 1846.

Biological roles

Tyrosine is the precursor of several important signalling molecules: the catecholamine neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine and epinephrine; the thyroid hormones thyroxine and triiodothyronine; and the pigment melanin. In proteins, tyrosine residues are frequent sites of phosphorylation by protein kinases — a central mechanism of cellular signal transduction — and of other modifications such as sulfation.

Metabolism and disorders

Because tyrosine is made from phenylalanine, people with phenylketonuria, who cannot metabolise phenylalanine properly, must monitor both amino acids carefully. Defects in tyrosine catabolism cause the tyrosinaemias; excess urinary excretion of related metabolites is also seen in alkaptonuria, a disorder of a later step in the same pathway. Dietary sources include cheese, soy products, meat, fish, nuts and oats.
