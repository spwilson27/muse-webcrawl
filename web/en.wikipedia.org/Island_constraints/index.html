Island constraints

In generative linguistics, island constraints are restrictions on syntactic movement: certain structures, called islands, block the extraction of an element from within them, so that a constituent inside an island cannot be moved out of it by processes such as question formation or topicalisation. The concept was introduced by John Robert Ross in his 1967 MIT dissertation Constraints on Variables in Syntax, a foundational work of generative grammar.

Classic islands

Ross identified several island types. A complex noun phrase blocks extraction from inside a relative clause or noun complement clause:

*What did you hear the rumour that John bought __?

The wh-island (or interrogative island) blocks movement out of an embedded question:

*What did you wonder whether John bought __?

Other islands include adjunct phrases, subject phrases (the subject condition), coordinated structures (the coordinate structure constraint), and sentential subjects. Extraction is typically grammatical from ordinary object clauses, a contrast that has driven decades of theorising.

Theoretical accounts

Proposed explanations range from structural principles — such as Chomsky's subjacency condition, the Barriers framework, phase impenetrability, and the CED (Condition on Extraction Domain) — to processing-based and semantic accounts arguing that islands reflect discourse-backgroundedness or the limits of working memory. Island effects are found in many languages, though their exact strength varies, making them a central testing ground for theories of syntax.
