Motor protein

Motor proteins are a class of molecular motors that can move along the cytoskeleton of cells. They convert chemical energy stored in ATP into mechanical work, and are essential for intracellular transport, cell division, muscle contraction, and the beating of cilia and flagella.

Families

The three major superfamilies of cytoskeletal motor proteins are myosins, which move along actin filaments; kinesins, which move (mostly) toward the plus end of microtubules; and dyneins, which move toward the minus end of microtubules. All share a motor domain that binds ATP and the filament track, coupled to a "cargo-binding" region.

A kinesin dimer "walks" hand-over-hand along a microtubule, hauling membrane cargo.

Myosin

Myosins power muscle contraction: in skeletal muscle, myosin II filaments slide actin filaments past one another (the sliding filament model). Other myosins transport vesicles and organelles, tension the cell cortex, and drive cytokinesis.

Kinesin

Conventional kinesin (kinesin-1) is a two-headed dimer that takes 8-nanometre steps along a microtubule, one ATP consumed per step, delivering vesicles and organelles toward the cell periphery. Other kinesin families position the mitotic spindle and depolymerise microtubule ends.

Dynein

Cytoplasmic dynein is a huge complex that carries cargo toward microtubule minus ends — transport from the cell periphery toward the nucleus, essential in neurons for retrograde axonal transport. Axonemal dyneins power the bending of cilia and flagella. Defects in motor proteins are linked to neurodegenerative diseases and ciliopathies.
