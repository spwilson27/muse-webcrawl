Sanitation

Sanitation refers to public health conditions related to the provision of clean drinking water and the adequate treatment and disposal of human excreta and sewage. It encompasses toilets and latrines, sewerage systems, wastewater treatment, solid waste management, handwashing with soap, and the hygienic handling of food and water.

Health importance

Preventing human contact with faeces is central to sanitation, since faecal–oral transmission spreads diseases such as cholera, typhoid, hepatitis, polio and diarrhoeal diseases, which together kill large numbers of children under five in low-income countries. Historically, improved sanitation — together with clean water — produced some of the largest gains in life expectancy of the industrial era, as in 19th-century London, where engineers such as Joseph Bazalgette built a modern sewer system after the Great Stink of 1858.

Global access

Under the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 6, the world aims for adequate and equitable sanitation for all by 2030. The WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme tracks a "sanitation ladder" from open defecation through unimproved and shared facilities to safely managed services. As of the early 2020s, billions of people still lacked safely managed sanitation, and hundreds of millions practised open defecation, though the numbers have steadily declined.

Approaches

Sanitation systems range from centralised sewer networks with treatment plants, through septic tanks and on-site dry systems such as pit latrines and container-based sanitation, to ecological sanitation that recovers nutrients from excreta for agriculture. Community-led total sanitation programs focus on behaviour change to end open defecation.
