Georgism, also called geoism, is an economic philosophy holding that the economic value derived from land — including natural resources and locations — should belong equally to all members of society, while individuals should own the value they create themselves. It takes its name from the American economist and journalist Henry George.

Progress and Poverty

In Progress and Poverty (1879), one of the best-selling economics books of the nineteenth century, George asked why increasing wealth and technological progress coexisted with deepening poverty. His answer was land rent: as populations grow and land appreciates, landowners capture the gains of progress in rent, while wages and interest are pressed down. Private appropriation of ground rent, he argued, was the fundamental cause of poverty amid progress.

The single tax

George's remedy was a tax on the unimproved value of land — the "single tax" — high enough to capture most economic rent, replacing taxes on labour, buildings, trade, and capital, which he viewed as penalties on production. Because land is fixed in supply, taxing its value does not discourage its production; later economists, including Milton Friedman, called the land value tax "the least bad tax," and the Henry George theorem in public economics formalises conditions under which land rents suffice to fund public goods.

Influence

Georgist ideas influenced land reforms and tax systems from Denmark to Taiwan and Australia, the design of Hong Kong's leasehold system, and movements for land value taxation that persist today.
