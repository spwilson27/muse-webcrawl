Muhammad Ali Jinnah (born Mahomedali Jinnahbhai; 25 December 1876 – 11 September 1948) was a barrister, politician and the founder of Pakistan. Jinnah served as the leader of the All-India Muslim League from 1913 until the inception of Pakistan on 14 August 1947, and then as the Dominion of Pakistan's first governor-general until his death a year later.

Born in Karachi and trained as a barrister at Lincoln's Inn in London, England, Jinnah rose to prominence in the Indian National Congress in the first two decades of the 20th century. In these early years of his political career, Jinnah advocated Hindu–Muslim unity, helping to shape the 1916 Lucknow Pact between the Congress and the All-India Muslim League, in which Jinnah had also become prominent. Jinnah became a key leader in the All-India Home Rule League, and proposed a fourteen-point constitutional reform plan to safeguard Muslim political rights.

Road to Pakistan

Disillusioned by the Congress's turn to mass civil disobedience under Gandhi, and after the failure of negotiations over power-sharing, Jinnah by 1940 had come to the conclusion that Muslims and Hindus in British India should have separate states — a view expressed in the Muslim League's Lahore Resolution of that year, based on the "two-nation theory". During the Second World War the League grew in strength while Congress leaders were imprisoned. In the 1946 provincial elections the League won most Muslim seats, and Jinnah pressed the demand for Pakistan through the Cabinet Mission negotiations and, after their breakdown, "Direct Action Day" in August 1946, which triggered deadly rioting.

Founder of Pakistan

With partition agreed, the Dominion of Pakistan came into being on 14 August 1947, with Jinnah as governor-general and Karachi as its capital. In his address to the Constituent Assembly on 11 August 1947 he set out a vision of a state with equal citizenship regardless of religion. He died of tuberculosis on 11 September 1948, a little over a year after independence. In Pakistan he is revered as the Quaid-e-Azam ("Great Leader") and Baba-e-Qaum ("Father of the Nation"); his mausoleum, the Mazar-e-Quaid in Karachi, is a national symbol, and his image appears on Pakistani currency.
