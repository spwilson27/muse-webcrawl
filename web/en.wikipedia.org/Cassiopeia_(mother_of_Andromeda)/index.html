Cassiopeia (mother of Andromeda)

Cassiopeia (Ancient Greek: Κασσιόπεια, Kassiópeia) is a figure in Greek mythology, queen of Aethiopia, wife of King Cepheus and mother of Andromeda. Her hubris — boasting of her beauty — provoked the wrath of Poseidon and set in motion the famous myth of Perseus and Andromeda.

Myth

Cassiopeia boasted that she (or, in some versions, her daughter Andromeda) surpassed the Nereids, the sea nymphs, in beauty. Offended, the Nereids appealed to Poseidon, who sent floods and a sea monster, Cetus, to devastate Aethiopia. An oracle declared that the land would be spared only if Andromeda were sacrificed to the monster.

Andromeda was chained to a rock by the sea but was rescued by the hero Perseus, who had just slain the Gorgon Medusa. Perseus killed Cetus, freed Andromeda and married her. At the wedding feast, Andromeda's former suitor Phineus attacked Perseus, who defeated him by showing the attackers the Gorgon's head, turning them to stone.

In the heavens

As punishment for her vanity, Cassiopeia was placed among the stars as the constellation Cassiopeia, seated on her throne by the northern celestial pole. Because of the position of the constellation, she is depicted spending half of each night hanging upside down — a humiliation said to be Poseidon's lasting rebuke to her pride.

Sources and legacy

The myth is told in Apollodorus's Library and in Ovid's Metamorphoses. Cassiopeia appears frequently in later art and literature as a type of maternal pride punished, and her story frames one of the best-known rescue myths of the ancient world.
