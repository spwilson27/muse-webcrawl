The United States Department of the Treasury is the national treasury and a cabinet department of the US federal government. Established by Congress on 2 September 1789, it is responsible for managing federal finances: collecting taxes, paying government bills, issuing currency and bonds, managing the public debt, and advising the president on economic and fiscal policy. The department is headed by the Secretary of the Treasury, who is fifth in the presidential line of succession.

History

The department's first secretary, Alexander Hamilton, laid the foundations of the American financial system: federal assumption of state debts, a national bank, a mint, and a system of tariffs and excise taxes. The department grew with the nation, financing wars through bond sales, creating the national banking system during the Civil War under Salmon P. Chase, and issuing the first federal paper currency ("greenbacks"). In the 20th century, Treasury officials shaped Bretton Woods, managed wartime finance, and later administered economic sanctions and responses to financial crises, including the Troubled Asset Relief Program of 2008.

Organisation

Major components include the Internal Revenue Service (tax administration), the Bureau of the Fiscal Service (payments and debt), the United States Mint, the Bureau of Engraving and Printing (banknotes), the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC, which administers sanctions), and the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau. The Treasurer of the United States, whose signature appears on currency along with the Secretary's, is also part of the department. Functions now housed elsewhere include the Secret Service (transferred to Homeland Security in 2003) and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency remains within Treasury, supervising national banks.
