The Greenland ice sheet (Greenlandic: Sermersuaq) is a vast body of ice covering 1,710,000 square kilometres, roughly 81% of the surface of Greenland. It is one of only two permanent ice sheets on Earth, the other being the Antarctic ice sheet. The ice sheet is almost 2,900 kilometres long in a north–south direction at its greatest extent; its average thickness is about 2 km and more than 3 km at its thickest point. The weight of the ice has depressed the central land surface to below sea level in places. If the entire ice sheet melted, global sea level would rise by about 7.4 metres.

Formation and history

Glaciation of Greenland developed as the climate cooled over the last several million years, with a large permanent ice sheet established by about 2.5–3 million years ago; the oldest ice recovered from its base may be over a million years old. Ice cores drilled at Summit (GRIP, GISP2) and at NorthGRIP and NEEM have produced climate records spanning more than 100,000 years, including the abrupt warmings of Dansgaard–Oeschger events and the Eemian interglacial. During past warm periods the ice sheet was considerably smaller: the Eemian and the Pliocene saw reduced ice cover and higher seas.

Melting and sea-level rise

Satellite gravimetry (GRACE) and altimetry show the ice sheet losing mass at an accelerating rate — on the order of 250–280 billion tonnes per year in recent decades — contributing roughly 0.7 mm per year to global sea-level rise. Losses come from surface meltwater runoff, increasing discharge of outlet glaciers such as Jakobshavn Isbræ (Sermeq Kujalleq), Helheim and Kangerlussuaq glaciers, and calving at marine margins. Extreme melt seasons, notably 2010, 2012 (when surface melt extended over nearly the whole ice sheet), and 2019, produced record losses. Meltwater darkens the surface (albedo feedback), forms supraglacial lakes that can drain rapidly to the bed, and in some places lubricates ice flow. Studies suggest a threshold of sustained warming — possibly between 1.5 °C and 3 °C globally — beyond which the ice sheet is committed to long-term near-total loss ("tipping point"), though over millennia rather than centuries.

Research

Key research stations include Summit Station at the ice sheet's apex (3,216 m elevation), and the former Camp Century, a Cold War U.S. Army base under the ice whose abandoned waste has raised environmental questions as melt progresses. Drill cores and radar sounding have revealed subglacial lakes, a giant canyon system beneath the ice, and meteorite impact structures such as the Hiawatha crater in the northwest.
