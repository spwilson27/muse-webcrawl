Psychedelic rock

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Psychedelic rock is a rock music genre that is inspired, influenced, or representative of psychedelic culture, which is centred around perception-altering hallucinogenic drugs such as LSD. The music incorporated new electronic sound effects and recording techniques, extended instrumental solos, and improvisation. Many psychedelic groups differ in style, and the label is often applied spuriously.

Originating in the mid-1960s among British and American musicians, the sounds of psychedelic rock were often imitative of the effects of such drugs. The genre bridged the transition from early blues and folk-based rock to progressive rock and heavy metal, and as a result influenced the development of subgenres such as glam rock. Since the 1970s, revivals have included neo-psychedelia and stoner rock.

Characteristics

Psychedelic rock typically features electric guitars with effects such as fuzz, phasing, flanging, and feedback; elaborate studio production including tape loops, reversed sounds, and musique concrète elements; incorporation of instruments such as the sitar and tabla from Indian music, and the Mellotron and harpsichord; surreal, whimsical, or esoteric lyrics often referencing dreams, literature, or hallucinogenic experiences; and extended improvisational passages in live performance, accompanied by liquid light shows and elaborate poster art.

History

The genre emerged around 1965–66. In the United States, the 13th Floor Elevators — who coined the term "psychedelic rock" in 1966 — the Grateful Dead, Jefferson Airplane, and the Byrds (with "Eight Miles High") were pioneers; in Britain, Pink Floyd, the Beatles (whose albums Revolver and Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band are landmarks), Cream, and Traffic led the movement. The 1967 "Summer of Love" and festivals such as Monterey Pop brought the style to a mass audience. By the end of the decade the genre had fragmented into progressive rock, hard rock, and other styles, though its influence persisted.

Legacy and revivals

Psychedelic rock influenced progressive rock, krautrock, heavy metal, and later neo-psychedelia acts such as the Flaming Lips and Tame Impala, as well as the Australian psychedelic scene of the 2010s. Its visual language — swirling colours, Art Nouveau-inspired lettering, and poster art by artists such as Wes Wilson and Victor Moscoso — remains iconic of the 1960s counterculture.
