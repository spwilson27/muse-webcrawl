The Orientalizing period (also spelled Orientalising) is a phase of ancient Greek and Mediterranean art, roughly 750–580 BC, in which Greek culture absorbed a wide range of artistic motifs, techniques and goods from the peoples of the Near East and Egypt — "the Orient" — through trade, mercenaries and colonies. It follows the austere Geometric period and precedes the Archaic period.

Characteristics

Pottery of the period, notably Proto-Corinthian and Proto-Attic wares, displays flowing curvilinear designs and rows of animals — lions, sphinxes, griffins and lotus-palmette chains — borrowed from Phoenician, Syrian and Assyrian art. The black-figure technique was developed in Corinth early in the period. Motifs arrived on imported ivories, metal bowls and textiles, and itinerant craftsmen may have worked in Greek cities.

Broader impact

The same centuries saw the Greeks adopt and adapt the Phoenician alphabet, new religious imagery, and luxury crafts such as repoussé metalwork. Orientalizing influence was equally strong in Etruria, where rich princely tombs contained Near Eastern-style goldwork and ivory.
