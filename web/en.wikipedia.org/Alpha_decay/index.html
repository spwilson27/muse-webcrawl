Alpha decay

In alpha decay a nucleus emits a helium-4 nucleus.

Alpha decay or α-decay is a type of radioactive decay in which an atomic nucleus emits an alpha particle (a helium-4 nucleus, consisting of two protons and two neutrons) and thereby transforms into a nucleus with atomic number reduced by two and mass number reduced by four.

AZX → A−4Z−2Y + 42He

Mechanism

Alpha decay occurs most commonly in heavy nuclei, where the electrostatic repulsion between protons makes it energetically favourable to expel a tightly bound helium nucleus. In quantum mechanics the process is explained by tunnelling: the alpha particle escapes through the Coulomb barrier even though it lacks the energy to surmount it classically, an explanation first given by George Gamow in 1928. This tunnelling accounts for the enormous range of alpha-decay half-lives, from fractions of a second to billions of years, described by the Geiger–Nuttall law.

Examples and occurrence

Well-known alpha emitters include uranium-238, radium-226, polonium-210 and americium-241, the last used in household smoke detectors. Alpha decay is a major source of the Earth's internal heat through the decay chains of uranium and thorium.

Safety

Alpha particles are heavy and strongly ionising but have very short range: they are stopped by a sheet of paper or the outer layer of skin. They are hazardous mainly when alpha-emitting material is inhaled or ingested, as with radon gas accumulating in buildings.
