Proton decay

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In particle physics, proton decay is a hypothetical form of particle decay in which the proton decays into lighter subatomic particles, such as a neutral pion and a positron. The proton decay hypothesis was first formulated by Andrei Sakharov in 1967. Despite significant experimental effort, proton decay has never been observed. If it exists, the life span of the proton must be greater than 1.67×1034 years (for decay into a positron and a neutral pion), according to limits from the Super-Kamiokande detector in Japan.

Theory

In the Standard Model, protons are theoretically stable because baryon number is approximately conserved and the proton is the lightest baryon, so its decay would require baryon-number violation. However, grand unified theories (GUTs), which unify the strong, weak, and electromagnetic interactions at very high energies, generically predict that baryon number is not exactly conserved and that protons decay through the exchange of extremely massive X and Y bosons. Typical lifetimes predicted by GUTs range from 1031 to 1036 years, depending on the model.

Proton decay would be a landmark discovery: it would demonstrate baryon number violation — one of the Sakharov conditions required to explain the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe — and provide strong evidence for grand unification. Because a decaying proton converts mass into lighter particles, proton decay would also imply the eventual decay of all ordinary matter over extremely long timescales.

Experiments

Searches for proton decay began in the early 1980s with large underground water Cherenkov and tracking detectors such as IMB in the United States and Kamiokande in Japan. These experiments found no decays, pushing the lifetime limit beyond the predictions of the simplest GUTs (such as minimal SU(5)), which were thereby ruled out. The current leading experiment, Super-Kamiokande, watches 50,000 tonnes of ultra-pure water for the characteristic light signatures of decay products such as e+ + π0. No events have been observed, setting a partial lifetime limit exceeding 1034 years.

Future detectors, including Hyper-Kamiokande in Japan and the DUNE liquid-argon detector in the United States, will probe lifetimes up to about 1035 years and additional decay channels, such as antineutrino plus kaon modes favored by supersymmetric models.
