Coffey still

The Coffey still is a type of continuous column still patented in 1830 by Aeneas Coffey, an Irish excise officer and inventor. Also known as a patent still, it allows the continuous distillation of spirits such as grain whisky and gin, in contrast to the batch process of the traditional pot still.

TypeDistillation apparatus
InventorAeneas Coffey
Patented1830
Used forWhisky, gin, vodka

Design

The Coffey still consists of two tall columns, an analyser (rectifier column) and a rectifier. Fermented wash is pumped continuously through the system while steam rises through perforated plates. As wash descends and vapour ascends, alcohol is stripped and progressively concentrated, emerging at strengths of up to about 94–96% alcohol by volume.

Impact

Because it operates continuously and cheaply, the Coffey still transformed the spirits industry, making possible large-scale production of grain whisky for blending. Its adoption in Scotland in the mid-19th century fuelled the rise of blended Scotch whisky, though purists argued the lighter spirit lacked the character of pot-still malt whisky.

Legacy

Coffey stills, and modern column stills derived from the design, remain standard equipment in distilleries worldwide. A surviving early example is preserved at the Old Midleton Distillery in Ireland.
