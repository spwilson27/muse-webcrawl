Calvinism

Calvinism, also called Reformed theology or the Reformed tradition, is a major branch of Protestantism that follows the theological traditions set down by John Calvin and other Reformation-era theologians. It emphasises the sovereignty of God, the authority of the Bible, and salvation by grace through faith.

History

Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536, expanded to 1559) and his Geneva ministry systematised Reformed teaching, which spread to France (Huguenots), the Netherlands, Scotland under John Knox, and parts of Germany and Hungary, and was carried to North America by Puritans and Presbyterians.

Doctrine

The 'Five Points' summarised by the acronym TULIP from the Canons of Dort (1619) — Total depravity, Unconditional election, Limited atonement, Irresistible grace, Perseverance of the saints — articulate its doctrine of predestination, though Reformed theology is broader: covenant theology, a spiritual presence in the Lord's Supper, and the regulative principle of worship. Confessions include the Westminster Confession, the Belgic Confession, and the Heidelberg Catechism.

Church government

Reformed churches are typically presbyterian or congregational, governed by elders rather than bishops.

Influence

Max Weber famously linked a Calvinist work ethic to the rise of capitalism; Reformed churches today number tens of millions of members worldwide, united loosely in the World Communion of Reformed Churches.
