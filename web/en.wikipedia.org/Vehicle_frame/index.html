Vehicle frame

A vehicle frame, also historically known as its chassis, is the main supporting structure of a motor vehicle to which all other components are attached, comparable to the skeleton of an organism. Until the 1930s, virtually every car had a structural frame separate from its body and interior. This construction method is known as body-on-frame. By the 1960s, unibody construction — in which the body and frame are integrated — had become common for passenger cars, while trucks and many SUVs retain separate frames.

Functions

The frame supports the engine, drivetrain, suspension, and body; carries static and dynamic loads, including the weight of passengers and cargo and torsional forces from uneven terrain; and provides a rigid mounting structure in collisions. A strong frame resists bending (beam) and twisting (torsion) loads.

Types

The most common design is the ladder frame: two longitudinal rails joined by cross members, resembling a ladder. Variants include perimeter frames, X-frames (cruciform), and backbone frames with a central spine. Modern passenger cars mostly use unibody (monocoque) construction, which is lighter and improves crash performance and handling through greater torsional stiffness per unit mass, though it is harder to repair and modify.

Materials

Frames were traditionally steel; high-strength steels and aluminium alloys are now common to reduce weight. Some high-performance cars use carbon-fibre monocoques. Frame rails are often formed as C-channels on trucks for strength and ease of mounting attachments.
