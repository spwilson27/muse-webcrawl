Platt Rogers Spencer (1800–1864)

BornNovember 7, 1800, East Fishkill, New York
DiedMay 16, 1864, Geneva, Ohio
Known forSpencerian script

Platt Rogers Spencer (November 7, 1800 – May 16, 1864) was an American penman and educator, the originator of Spencerian script, a form of cursive handwriting that became the standard for business and personal correspondence in the United States during the mid-to-late nineteenth century.

Life and work

Born in East Fishkill, New York, Spencer developed an early passion for penmanship, practising letters with whatever materials were at hand. After working as a clerk and teacher, he settled in Ohio, where from the 1840s he travelled teaching his system of writing and trained scores of teachers who spread the method through commercial colleges across the country. In 1848 he published the first of his copybooks, Spencer and Rice's System of Business and Ladies' Penmanship, and his sons and associates published the influential Spencerian Key to Practical Penmanship (1866) shortly after his death.

Spencerian script

Spencerian script drew its inspiration from forms in nature — rolling waves, pebbles and clouds — and is characterised by smooth oval-based letterforms, graceful loops and dramatic shading on downstrokes made with flexible pointed pens. It dominated American handwriting instruction from about 1850 to 1925, was used in famous commercial signatures such as the original Coca-Cola logo (and the Ford logo), and was superseded in schools by the simpler Palmer Method. Spencer was also an ardent abolitionist and temperance advocate, and his calligraphy school in Geneva, Ohio was a stop on the Underground Railroad. Interest in his ornate hand has revived among modern calligraphers, whose fine writing tools are the descendants of the pointed pens Spencer used on paper akin to that handled by a playwright or a clerk of his era.
