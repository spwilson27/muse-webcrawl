Cave diving

Cave diving is underwater diving in water-filled caves. It is a specialized form of technical diving performed in an overhead environment where direct ascent to the surface is impossible. Cave diving is widely considered one of the most hazardous recreational diving activities, and it requires specialized training, equipment, and procedures beyond open-water scuba certification.

Procedures

The core safety practices of cave diving include: always following a continuous guideline to open water laid from a reel; carrying at least three independent lights; reserving at least two-thirds of the breathing gas supply for the exit (the "rule of thirds"); specialized training; and using the proper equipment configuration, typically redundant gas supplies such as twin cylinders or sidemount. Buoyancy and finning techniques are adapted to avoid disturbing silt, which can reduce visibility to zero.

History

Pioneers such as Graham Balcombe and Jack Sheppard in Britain and, later, Sheck Exley in Florida developed the sport's techniques and safety analyses. Exley's book Basic Cave Diving: A Blueprint for Survival codified the causes of cave diving accidents and the rules derived from them.

Notable sites

Famous cave diving regions include the cenotes of Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, the springs of north Florida, and the Lot region of France. The world's longest underwater cave systems, such as Sistema Ox Bel Ha in Mexico, extend for hundreds of kilometres.
