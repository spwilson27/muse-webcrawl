Henry George (September 2, 1839 – October 29, 1897) was an American political economist and journalist. His writing was immensely popular in 19th-century America and sparked several reform movements of the Progressive Era. He inspired the economic philosophy known as Georgism, based on the belief that people should own the value they produce, but that the economic value of land (including natural resources) should belong equally to all members of society.

Life

Born in Philadelphia to a lower-middle-class family, George went to sea as a teenager, worked as a printer, and settled in California, where he became a newspaper editor. Witnessing poverty amid rapid development in San Francisco — and a visit to New York in 1869, where he was struck by the contrast between wealth and destitution — he set out to explain why progress seemed to accompany worsening poverty.

Progress and Poverty

His magnum opus, Progress and Poverty (1879), sold millions of copies worldwide. George argued that the ownership of land and natural resources by private individuals, and the resulting unearned increase in land values (economic rent), was the primary cause of poverty amid progress. His remedy was a "single tax" on land values — capturing ground rent for public purposes while abolishing taxes on labour, trade, and improvements.

Political career

George moved to New York in 1880. In 1886 he ran for mayor of New York City on the United Labor Party ticket, finishing second ahead of Theodore Roosevelt. He ran again in 1897 on an independent ticket but died of a stroke four days before the election; his funeral drew one of the largest public outpourings in the city's history.

Legacy

George's ideas influenced figures as diverse as Leo Tolstoy, John Dewey, and Sun Yat-sen, and land value taxation has been adopted in varying forms in places such as Denmark, Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore, and parts of Australia and Pennsylvania. Economists continue to regard the land value tax as unusually efficient because land is in fixed supply.
