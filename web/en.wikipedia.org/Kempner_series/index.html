Kempner series

The Kempner series omits every term whose denominator contains the digit 9.

The Kempner series is a modification of the harmonic series, formed by taking the sum over all positive integers n whose decimal representation does not contain the digit 9:

1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ... + 1/8 + 1/10 + 1/11 + ... + 1/18 + 1/20 + ...

That is, terms such as 1/9, 1/19, 1/90 and 1/99 are all excluded. The series was first studied by A. J. Kempner in 1914. Kempner showed that, counterintuitively, this modified series converges, in striking contrast to the harmonic series, which diverges. The sum is less than 80; a sharper bound gives a value of roughly 22.920676619264150.

Convergence

Kempner's proof exploits the fact that among n-digit numbers, the proportion that avoid a given digit shrinks exponentially: of the 9·10n−1 numbers with exactly n digits, only 8·9n−1 contain no 9 at all. Grouping the surviving terms by the number of digits in the denominator, the contribution of the n-digit group is at most 8·(9/10)n−1, which is a convergent geometric bound.

Generalizations

The same argument shows that the subseries of the harmonic series obtained by deleting every integer containing any prescribed finite string of digits still converges. Robert Baillie computed precise values for such generalized Kempner sums in 1979, and later authors obtained rapidly convergent algorithms for the sums. The convergence is extremely slow: an enormous number of terms must be summed before the partial sums approach the limit to any reasonable accuracy.
