Frailty index

The frailty index is a measure of the state of health and vulnerability of older adults, developed within geriatric medicine by Kenneth Rockwood and Arnold Mitnitski. It quantifies frailty as the proportion of age-related health deficits a person has accumulated.

Calculation

The index is computed as the ratio of deficits present to the total number of deficits considered: FI = (number of deficits present) / (total deficits counted). Deficits may include symptoms, signs, diseases, disabilities, and abnormal laboratory values. A person with 10 of 40 deficits considered has a frailty index of 0.25. The index ranges from 0 to a theoretical 1, though in practice values rarely exceed about 0.7.

Properties and use

The frailty index increases on average by roughly 3% per year of age, is higher in women than in men at every age (despite women's longer life expectancy), and strongly predicts mortality, institutionalisation, and adverse outcomes after surgery or hospitalisation. It can be derived from comprehensive geriatric assessment data or, in electronic health records, from routinely coded diagnoses (the electronic Frailty Index, eFI).

Comparison with phenotype

The deficit-accumulation approach contrasts with the physical frailty phenotype of Linda Fried, which classifies a person as frail when three or more of five specific criteria (weakness, slowness, exhaustion, low activity, weight loss) are present. The two approaches overlap but capture different aspects of vulnerability.
