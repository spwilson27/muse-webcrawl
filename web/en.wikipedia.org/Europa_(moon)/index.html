Europa (moon)

Europa, or Jupiter II, is the smallest of the four Galilean moons orbiting Jupiter, and the sixth-closest to the planet of all the moons known. It is also the sixth-largest moon in the Solar System. Europa was discovered in 1610 by Galileo Galilei and was named after Europa, the Phoenician mother of King Minos of Crete in Greek mythology. Slightly smaller than Earth's Moon, Europa is made of silicate rock and has a water-ice crust and probably an iron–nickel core.

Europa's smooth ice crust is scored by dark red-brown cracks called lineae.

Subsurface ocean

Europa has the smoothest surface of any known solid object in the Solar System. The apparent youth and smoothness of the surface have led to the hypothesis that a water ocean exists beneath the icy crust, heated by tidal flexing, which could potentially harbour extraterrestrial life. Induced magnetic field measurements by the Galileo spacecraft strongly support a global salty ocean perhaps 100 km deep beneath an ice shell of uncertain thickness.

Exploration

Europa was imaged by Pioneer 10 and 11, Voyager 1 and 2, and studied extensively by the Galileo orbiter (1995–2003). Hubble observations have suggested plumes of water vapour erupting from the surface. NASA's Europa Clipper mission launched in October 2024 to make repeated close flybys and assess the moon's habitability, while ESA's JUICE mission, launched in 2023, will focus on Ganymede with some Europa observations.

Lineae and chaos terrain

The surface is marked by red-brown lineae — cracks in the ice — and by lenticulae and "chaos terrains" where the crust appears broken into rafted blocks, interpreted as signs of exchange between the surface and the ocean below.
