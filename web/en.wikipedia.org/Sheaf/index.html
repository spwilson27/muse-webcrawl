Sheaf

Wheat sheaves set up in stooks to dry

A sheaf (plural: sheaves) is a bundle of cereal plant stems bound together after reaping, traditionally by hand or with the help of a mechanical reaper-binder. Sheaves were the standard unit in which grain crops — wheat, barley, oats, rye, and rice — were gathered, dried in the field, transported, and stored before threshing separated the grain from the straw and chaff. A group of sheaves propped against one another to dry in the field is called a stook or shock.

Use in traditional harvest

After grain was cut with a sickle or scythe, workers (often women and children following the reapers) gathered armfuls of stalks and tied them near the ears with a band made of twisted straw. Sheaves were stood upright in stooks of typically six to twelve, arranged so that rain ran off the ears and air circulated to cure the grain. Once dry, the sheaves were carted to the farmyard and built into stacks or stored in barns until threshing — by flail through the winter, or later by threshing machine.

Mechanisation

The reaper-binder (or binder), developed in the later 19th century, mechanised the cutting and tying of sheaves, ejecting finished bundles which workers still had to stook by hand. The combine harvester, which cuts and threshes in a single pass, made sheaves obsolete in industrialised agriculture during the mid-20th century; straw is now usually left loose, chopped, or baled rather than bound into sheaves. Sheaves persist in small-scale and traditional farming, in sheaf-throwing competitions at Highland games (where a sheaf of straw is tossed over a bar with a pitchfork), and in ceremonial and decorative contexts.

In culture and heraldry

The sheaf — in heraldry called a garb — is an ancient emblem of harvest, fertility, and agricultural abundance, appearing in the arms of farming regions such as Cheshire and on the emblems of many agricultural institutions. Harvest festivals traditionally displayed a decorated final sheaf (the "corn dolly" or harvest queen). The sheaf also gives its name to mathematical "sheaves" by analogy: just as a sheaf binds individual stalks into one bundle, a sheaf in category theory binds local data into a coherent global structure.
