Droit moral

Droit moral is a French legal term meaning moral rights: the rights of creators of copyrighted works that protect their personal and reputational connection with the work, as distinct from the economic rights of exploitation. Originating in French law and in the civil-law tradition more broadly, moral rights are generally considered perpetual, inalienable and imprescriptible in France.

Content of the rights

Moral rights typically include:

  • The right of attribution (paternity) — the right to be named as the author, or to remain anonymous or pseudonymous.
  • The right of integrity — the right to object to distortion, mutilation or other modification of the work prejudicial to the author's honour or reputation.
  • The right of disclosure — the right to decide whether and when the work is made public.
  • The right of withdrawal — in some jurisdictions, the right to retract a work from circulation, usually subject to compensating the rights-holder.

International framework

Article 6bis of the Berne Convention requires member states to grant rights of attribution and integrity independently of the author's economic rights, and at least until those rights expire. The common-law world historically resisted broad moral rights: the United Kingdom introduced limited moral rights in the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, while the United States recognises them mainly for works of visual art under the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990.
