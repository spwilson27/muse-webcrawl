The Battle of Megiddo was fought in the 15th century BC, conventionally dated to 1457 BC, between the Egyptian forces of Pharaoh Thutmose III and a coalition of Canaanite vassal states led by the king of Kadesh, who had revolted against Egyptian domination. It is the first battle in history recorded in relatively reliable detail, from annals inscribed on the walls of the Temple of Karnak.

Background

On the death of Hatshepsut, subject rulers in Syria and Canaan, encouraged by the kingdom of Mitanni, rebelled against Egypt. Thutmose III marched north with an army of perhaps 10,000–20,000 men, including chariotry, to reassert control. The rebels concentrated at the fortress city of Megiddo, guarding the pass from the coastal plain into the Jezreel Valley.

The battle

Against his generals' advice, Thutmose chose the narrow, direct Aruna pass, emerging unexpectedly close to the enemy camp. The Egyptian army deployed in a concave formation and attacked at dawn; the Canaanite forces broke and fled into Megiddo, abandoning their camp — which the Egyptians stopped to plunder, allowing the garrison to close the gates.

Aftermath

Denied an immediate capture of the city, Thutmose besieged Megiddo for several months until it surrendered. The victory restored Egyptian supremacy in the Levant: the defeated princes submitted, sent tribute and hostages to Egypt, and Thutmose went on to campaign in Syria for nearly two decades.
