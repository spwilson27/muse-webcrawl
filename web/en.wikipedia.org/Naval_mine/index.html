Naval mine

A naval mine is a self-contained explosive device placed in water to damage or destroy surface ships or submarines. Unlike depth charges, mines are laid in advance and detonate when triggered by a vessel's passage, making them a cheap and effective weapon of sea denial.

Types

Moored contact mines float tethered to the seabed and explode on physical contact; bottom (ground) mines rest on the seabed and use influence fuzes sensitive to a ship's magnetic field, acoustic signature or water-pressure change. Limpet mines are attached directly to a hull by divers or special forces. Mines may be laid by specialised minelayers, submarines, aircraft or converted vessels.

History and law

Early mines ("torpedoes") were used in the American Civil War — Farragut's famous order at Mobile Bay referred to them. Mines were used on a vast scale in both world wars, and mine clearance (minesweeping and mine hunting) remains a major naval task. The 1907 Hague Convention VIII regulates the use of automatic submarine contact mines, requiring them to become harmless when they break loose.
