Cassowary

Cassowaries (genus Casuarius) are flightless ratite birds native to the tropical forests of New Guinea, the Aru Islands, and northeastern Australia. Three species survive: the southern (or double-wattled) cassowary, the northern (single-wattled) cassowary, and the dwarf cassowary. They are among the largest living birds, standing 1.5–1.8 m tall.

The southern cassowary, with its prominent keratin casque.

Description

Cassowaries have coarse, hair-like black plumage, a bare blue-and-red neck with hanging wattles, and a tall keratin casque atop the head, whose function may involve display, sound resonance, or pushing through vegetation. Their powerful legs bear three toes, the inner toe carrying a dagger-like claw up to 12 cm long. Females are larger and more brightly coloured than males.

Behaviour and ecology

Solitary and shy, cassowaries are frugivores: they swallow fallen fruit whole and disperse the seeds across the rainforest, making them a keystone species for many large-seeded trees. Unusually among birds, the male alone incubates the eggs — laid in a leaf-litter nest — and raises the striped chicks for up to 16 months. Cassowaries can run at 50 km/h and jump 2 m; when cornered they can be dangerous, and they are sometimes called the world's most dangerous bird.

Conservation

The southern cassowary is listed as Least Concern globally by the IUCN but is endangered in Australia, where habitat fragmentation and vehicle strikes are major threats; all three species face hunting pressure in New Guinea. Cassowaries appear prominently in the cultures and ceremonies of Indigenous peoples of both regions.
