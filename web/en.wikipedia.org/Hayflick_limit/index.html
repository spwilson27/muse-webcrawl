The Hayflick limit, or Hayflick phenomenon, is the number of times a normal somatic human cell population will divide before cell division stops and the cells enter senescence. The concept was advanced by Leonard Hayflick in 1961 at the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia, overturning the long-held belief—dating to Alexis Carrel—that normal cells are intrinsically immortal in culture.

Discovery

Working with Paul Moorhead, Hayflick showed that cultured normal human fibroblasts divide only a finite number of times—roughly 40 to 60 population doublings—before entering a degenerative phase (Phase III) in which division ceases but the cells remain alive and metabolically active for a time. In carefully controlled mixing experiments, old cells "remembered" their age even after their nuclei were surrounded by young cytoplasm, demonstrating that the counting mechanism resides in the nucleus. Hayflick also found that cells frozen after a given number of divisions resumed dividing after thawing until the same total was reached, suggesting a built-in replicometer.

Mechanism

The molecular basis of the limit is the progressive shortening of telomeres, the repetitive DNA sequences capping the ends of chromosomes. Because DNA polymerase cannot fully replicate the end of a linear chromosome (the end-replication problem), each division removes a small segment of telomeric DNA. When telomeres become critically short, the cell activates DNA-damage responses involving p53 and p16, and enters replicative senescence or apoptosis. The enzyme telomerase can extend telomeres, and is active in germ cells, stem cells, and most cancer cells—but largely absent from ordinary somatic cells.

Significance

The Hayflick limit connects cell biology to ageing and cancer. Senescent cells accumulate in tissues with age and secrete inflammatory factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype), contributing to age-related disease. Conversely, the limit functions as a tumour-suppression mechanism: malignant cells must bypass it, usually by reactivating telomerase, to achieve immortality. Species-level data broadly correlate fibroblast replicative capacity with lifespan, and patients with telomere-shortening disorders (such as dyskeratosis congenita) show premature ageing of proliferative tissues.
