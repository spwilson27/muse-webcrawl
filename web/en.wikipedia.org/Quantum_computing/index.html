Quantum computing is a type of computation whose operations can harness the phenomena of quantum mechanics, such as superposition, interference, and entanglement. A device that performs quantum computing is known as a quantum computer.

Whereas classical computers encode information in bits that are always in one of two definite states (0 or 1), quantum computers use qubits, which can be in a superposition of states. This allows some quantum algorithms to solve certain problems much faster than any known classical algorithm — most famously Shor's algorithm for factoring integers and Grover's algorithm for searching unsorted databases.

History

The field began in the early 1980s, when Paul Benioff proposed a quantum-mechanical model of the Turing machine, and Yuri Manin and Richard Feynman independently suggested that a quantum computer could simulate quantum systems efficiently. David Deutsch described a universal quantum computer in 1985. Breakthroughs in algorithms followed: Peter Shor published his factoring algorithm in 1994, threatening widely used public-key cryptography, and Lov Grover published his search algorithm in 1996.

Experimental progress proceeded through the 2000s and 2010s with nuclear magnetic resonance, ion traps, and superconducting circuits. In 2019, Google claimed to have achieved "quantum supremacy" with its 53-qubit Sycamore processor, performing a sampling task in 200 seconds that it estimated would take a state-of-the-art supercomputer 10,000 years; IBM disputed the estimate. Since then the field has focused on the "noisy intermediate-scale quantum" (NISQ) era and on quantum error correction.

Principles

A quantum computation begins by preparing qubits in an initial state, applies a sequence of quantum logic gates (unitary transformations), and ends with a measurement that yields classical output with probabilities determined by the qubits' amplitudes. Interference causes amplitudes of paths leading to wrong answers to cancel, while entanglement creates correlations between qubits that have no classical analogue.

Physical implementations

Leading hardware platforms include superconducting circuits (used by IBM and Google), trapped ions (used by IonQ and Quantinuum), photonic systems, neutral atoms, semiconductor quantum dots, and topological qubits. Each faces challenges of decoherence, gate fidelity, and scalability.

Applications and challenges

Anticipated applications include simulation of molecules and materials for drug discovery and chemistry, optimization, machine learning, and cryptanalysis. Because Shor's algorithm could break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, governments and industry are developing post-quantum cryptography. The principal obstacles to practical large-scale quantum computing are noise and the overhead of fault-tolerant error correction, which may require thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit.
