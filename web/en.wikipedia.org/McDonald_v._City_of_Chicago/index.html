McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 U.S. 742 (2010), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court holding that the right of an individual to "keep and bear arms", as protected by the Second Amendment, is incorporated by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and is thereby enforceable against the states.

Background

In District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) the Court had held that the Second Amendment protects an individual's right to possess a handgun in the home, but because the District of Columbia is a federal enclave the ruling did not apply to state or local laws. Otis McDonald, a Chicago resident who wanted a handgun for home defence, challenged Chicago's effective handgun ban, as did other plaintiffs in companion cases against Chicago and Oak Park, Illinois.

Decision

Writing for a five-justice majority, Justice Samuel Alito held that self-defence is a fundamental right deeply rooted in the nation's history and tradition, and that the Second Amendment is therefore fully applicable to the states. The Court reversed the Seventh Circuit and remanded. Justice Clarence Thomas concurred on the alternative ground of the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause.

Aftermath

Chicago replaced its ban with a licensing ordinance, and the decision opened the way to challenges against state and local firearms regulations nationwide, a line of litigation continued in later cases including New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen (2022).
