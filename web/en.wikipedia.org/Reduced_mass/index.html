Reduced mass

In physics, the reduced mass is the "effective" inertial mass appearing in the two-body problem of Newtonian mechanics. It is a quantity that allows the two-body problem to be solved as if it were a one-body problem. The reduced mass μ of two bodies with masses m1 and m2 is defined as:

μ = m1m2 / (m1 + m2),   i.e.   1/μ = 1/m1 + 1/m2

Two bodies orbiting their barycentre, equivalent to one body of reduced mass.

Derivation

For two bodies interacting through a force that depends only on their separation, Newton's equations can be rewritten in terms of the position of the centre of mass and the relative position vector. The centre of mass moves uniformly, while the relative motion obeys a single equation identical to that of a particle of mass μ moving in the same potential. The reduced mass is always smaller than either individual mass, and approaches the smaller mass when the two are very unequal (as for an electron orbiting a nucleus, where μ ≈ me).

Applications

  • Orbital mechanics: binary stars and planets are analysed using reduced mass and the relative orbit.
  • Atomic physics: the Rydberg constant for hydrogen must be corrected using the electron–proton reduced mass; the small difference between hydrogen and deuterium spectral lines follows from the same correction.
  • Vibrational spectroscopy: the frequency of a diatomic molecule's vibration depends on the reduced mass of its two atoms.
  • Collisions: in the centre-of-mass frame, kinetic energy is expressed compactly with the reduced mass.

Generalisation

The same formal combination of masses occurs for springs or capacitors in series, and for two-body problems in quantum mechanics, where the Schrödinger equation for two interacting particles separates into centre-of-mass and relative motion governed by μ.
