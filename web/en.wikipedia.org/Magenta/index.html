Magenta is a purplish-red color, an extra-spectral color that cannot be generated by a single wavelength of light, produced instead by the human visual system when red and blue light are received in roughly equal amounts with little green. In the RGB color model magenta is a secondary color, made by combining equal amounts of red and blue light; in the CMYK model used in printing it is one of the three primary colors, along with cyan and yellow.

History

The name comes from the aniline dye fuchsine, patented in 1859 by the French chemist François-Emmanuel Verguin and marketed by the Renard brothers in Lyon. The dye was renamed "magenta" after the Battle of Magenta, fought that same year between the Franco-Sardinian alliance and Austria near the Italian town of Magenta in Lombardy, in a burst of patriotic publicity—a naming shared with the color solferino, after another battle of the same campaign. Magenta dyes and pigments became a fashion sensation of the 1860s, and the color was fixed in printing practice when William Baxter's process and later the four-color system standardized cyan, magenta, and yellow inks.

On the color wheel

On the RGB color wheel magenta lies exactly between red and blue, at a hue of 300 degrees. Its complementary color is green. Because no single wavelength corresponds to it, magenta appears in the spectrum only as a mixture, a fact often used in explaining opponent-process color vision; the visual system constructs it to bridge the two ends of the visible spectrum.

Usage

Beyond printing, magenta is used in astronomy filter systems and false-color imaging (where it often marks ionized gases), in the "magenta" stage lighting beloved of theatre, and in corporate branding, most famously by Deutsche Telekom and its T-Mobile subsidiaries, which have litigated to protect their brand shade. A lighter, pinkish form predominates in fashion and design usage.
