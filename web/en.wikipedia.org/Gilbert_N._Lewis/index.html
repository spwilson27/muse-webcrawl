Gilbert Newton Lewis (October 23 or October 25, 1875 – March 23, 1946) was an American physical chemist and a dean of the College of Chemistry at the University of California, Berkeley. Lewis was best known for his discovery of the covalent bond and his concept of electron pairs; his Lewis dot structures and other contributions to valence bond theory have shaped modern theories of chemical bonding. Lewis successfully contributed to chemical thermodynamics, photochemistry, and isotope separation, and is also known for his concept of acids and bases. He also researched relativity and quantum physics, and in 1926 he coined the term "photon" for the smallest unit of radiant energy.

Biography

Lewis was born in Weymouth, Massachusetts, and educated at home until age 13. He studied at the University of Nebraska and then Harvard, earning his PhD in 1899 under T. W. Richards. After a period in Germany with Ostwald and Nernst and a post in the Philippines, he joined the faculty of MIT in 1905, then moved in 1912 to Berkeley, where as dean he built the College of Chemistry into one of the world's leading centres of chemical research. He died in his laboratory in 1946 while conducting an experiment on fluorescence.

Scientific work

Chemical bonding

In his landmark 1916 paper "The Atom and the Molecule", Lewis proposed that a chemical bond is a pair of electrons shared by two atoms (the cubical atom model and the origin of Lewis dot structures, the octet rule, and the portrayal of single, double, and triple bonds as one, two, or three shared pairs). His 1923 monograph Valence and the Structure of Atoms and Molecules codified the electronic theory of valence. In 1923 he also generalised acid–base theory: a Lewis acid is an electron-pair acceptor and a Lewis base an electron-pair donor, a definition that encompasses reactions without protons.

Thermodynamics

With Merle Randall, Lewis published Thermodynamics and the Free Energy of Chemical Substances (1923), which modernised chemical thermodynamics with the concepts of fugacity, activity, and standard states, and made entropy calculations practical through the third law. In 1916 he had formulated the idea later stated as the third law of thermodynamics for perfect crystals.

Isotopes and other work

In 1933 Lewis was the first to produce a pure sample of heavy water (deuterium oxide) and studied the effects of deuterium on living organisms. He coined the term "photon" in a 1926 letter to Nature (with a meaning different from Einstein's light quantum), worked on the colour of substances and phosphorescence, and late in life proposed that the triplet state explains organic photochemistry.

Honours and the Nobel question

Lewis was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 41 times but never received it — one of the most famous omissions in Nobel history, attributed in part to a bitter feud with Walther Nernst and to internal committee disputes. He received many other honours, including the Davy Medal and the Willard Gibbs Award, and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences. The lunar crater Lewis is named after him. His students included Nobel laureates William Giauque, Harold Urey (who discovered deuterium while working in related circles), and Glenn Seaborg.
