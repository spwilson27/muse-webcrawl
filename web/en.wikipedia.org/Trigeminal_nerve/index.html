Trigeminal nerve


The three divisions of the trigeminal nerve

The trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V) is the largest of the twelve cranial nerves. It is responsible for sensation in the face and certain motor functions such as biting and chewing. Its name derives from its three major branches: the ophthalmic nerve (V1), the maxillary nerve (V2), and the mandibular nerve (V3).

Structure

The nerve emerges from the pons of the brainstem and expands into the trigeminal (Gasserian) ganglion in Meckel's cave, from which the three divisions arise. The ophthalmic and maxillary divisions are purely sensory; the mandibular division carries both sensory fibers and motor fibers to the muscles of mastication.

Function

Sensory fibers convey touch, pain, temperature, and proprioception from the face, scalp, cornea, and much of the oral and nasal cavities. Motor fibers supply the masseter, temporalis, and pterygoid muscles, among others.

Clinical significance

Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic pain condition affecting the nerve, characterized by brief, intense episodes of facial pain. Compression of the nerve by a blood vessel is a common cause, and treatments range from medication such as carbamazepine to microvascular decompression surgery.
