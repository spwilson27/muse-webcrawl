Crime fiction

Crime fiction, detective story, murder mystery, mystery novel, and police novel are terms used to describe narratives that centre on criminal acts and especially on the investigation, either by an amateur or a professional detective, of a crime, often a murder. It is usually distinguished from mainstream fiction and other genres such as historical fiction or science fiction, but the boundaries are indistinct.

History

Crime and detection have ancient literary roots, from early Chinese gong'an fiction to the biblical story of Susanna and the Elders. Modern crime fiction is often traced to Edgar Allan Poe's “The Murders in the Rue Morgue” (1841), which introduced the detective C. Auguste Dupin. In the later 19th century, Wilkie Collins's The Moonstone (1868) and Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes stories (from 1887) established the genre's enduring popularity.

The Golden Age and hardboiled schools

The interwar “Golden Age” of detective fiction was dominated by British authors such as Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, G. K. Chesterton and Margery Allingham, typically writing puzzle-like “whodunits” set among the upper classes. In the United States, the hardboiled school of Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler offered a tougher, more realistic style centred on private investigators. Rules codified by Ronald Knox and S. S. Van Dine attempted to define fair play between author and reader.

Subgenres

Major subgenres include the police procedural, the noir novel, the legal thriller, the spy thriller, the cozy mystery and Nordic noir. Crime fiction is among the best-selling genres worldwide and has been extensively adapted for film, radio and television.
