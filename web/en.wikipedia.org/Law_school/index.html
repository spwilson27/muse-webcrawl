Law school

A law school is an institution specialising in legal education, preparing students for practice as lawyers and for careers in government, business and academia. Models differ worldwide: in the United States law is a postgraduate professional degree (the Juris Doctor, J.D.), typically three years after a bachelor's degree and admission via the LSAT; in the United Kingdom and Commonwealth, law is commonly a first degree (LL.B.) followed by vocational training.

Curriculum

First-year staples include contracts, torts, property, civil procedure, criminal law and constitutional law, taught in the US largely by the case method and Socratic questioning, pioneered at Harvard under Christopher Columbus Langdell. Upper years add electives, clinics, moot court and law review work.

Admission to practice

Graduates must usually pass a bar examination and character review. Prestigious schools — Harvard, Yale, Oxford, Cambridge among them — exert outsized influence on judiciaries and legal scholarship. See Law review.
