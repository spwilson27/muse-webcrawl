Klinefelter syndrome (KS), also known as 47,XXY, is a chromosomal condition in which a male is born with an extra X chromosome. The extra chromosome arises from random nondisjunction during meiosis in either parent. Most people with the condition have one extra X (47,XXY); rarer variants include 48,XXXY or mosaic forms such as 46,XY/47,XXY, which tend to differ in severity.

Signs and symptoms

Features vary widely, and many affected men are never diagnosed. Common findings include tall stature with long limbs, small and firm testes, reduced facial and body hair, decreased muscle mass, and gynaecomastia (breast tissue development). Testosterone production is typically reduced, and infertility is usual because of impaired sperm production (azoospermia), although sperm can sometimes be retrieved surgically for assisted reproduction. Children may show delayed speech and language development, reading and learning difficulties, and relative difficulties with executive function; intelligence is usually within the normal range, sometimes slightly reduced.

Long-term health risks include an increased incidence of osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome, autoimmune disorders, breast cancer (risk much higher than in other men, though still low in absolute terms), and certain extragonadal germ cell tumours.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by chromosome analysis (karyotyping) of blood cells, or increasingly by chromosomal microarray. The condition may be detected prenatally through amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling, or after non-invasive prenatal screening suggests a sex-chromosome abnormality. Because symptoms are often subtle, studies suggest that only a minority — perhaps a quarter to a third — of affected individuals are diagnosed during their lifetimes.

Treatment

There is no cure, but many features can be managed. Testosterone replacement therapy, usually begun around puberty, promotes virilisation, increases muscle mass and bone density, and may improve mood and energy. Speech therapy, occupational therapy and educational support help children with language and learning difficulties. Breast tissue may be removed surgically if desired. Testicular sperm extraction combined with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) has enabled some men with the condition to father biological children.

History

The syndrome is named after Harry Klinefelter, an American endocrinologist who, with Fuller Albright and Edward Reifenstein, described the clinical picture in 1942. The chromosomal basis — an extra X chromosome in males — was established in 1959 by Patricia Jacobs and John Strong.
