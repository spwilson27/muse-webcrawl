Triode

A triode is an electronic amplifying vacuum tube (or valve in British English) consisting of three electrodes inside an evacuated glass envelope: a heated filament or cathode, a grid, and a plate (anode). Developed from the earlier diode by the addition of the control grid, it was invented by the American engineer Lee de Forest in 1906 (his "Audion") and independently developed in other forms by others around the same period. The triode was the first device capable of electronic amplification and made practical long-distance telephony, radio broadcasting, and early electronic computers possible.

Operation

The cathode is heated so that it emits electrons by thermionic emission. The plate is held at a positive voltage and attracts the electrons, producing a current through the tube. The grid, a mesh or helix of wire placed between cathode and plate, is normally held slightly negative with respect to the cathode. Because the grid is much closer to the cathode than the plate is, a small change in grid voltage produces a large change in the plate current. This gives the triode its amplifying action: a weak signal applied to the grid controls a much stronger current in the plate circuit.

The amplification factor (μ) of a triode expresses the ratio of a change in plate voltage to the equivalent change in grid voltage. Typical small-signal triodes have μ values from about 10 to 100.

History and applications

De Forest's Audion of 1906 was initially insensitive and erratic, but by 1912 improved high-vacuum triodes became the key element in telephone repeaters, allowing transcontinental telephone service in the United States by 1915. Triodes served as amplifiers, detectors and oscillators in virtually all radio equipment through the first half of the twentieth century, and banks of them formed the logic elements of early computers such as ENIAC (which mostly used later multi-grid tubes). After the invention of the transistor in 1947, solid-state devices displaced tubes in most applications, but triodes survive in guitar amplifiers and high-end audio equipment, where their distortion characteristics are prized, and in some high-power radio transmitters.
