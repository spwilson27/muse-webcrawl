Ellen Churchill Semple, founder figure of human geography in the United States

Ellen Churchill Semple (8 January 1863 – 8 May 1932) was an American geographer and the first female president of the Association of American Geographers (1921). She was a leading exponent of anthropogeography and of the theory — now largely rejected in its strong form — of environmental determinism: the idea that the physical environment, especially climate and terrain, shapes human societies and their development.

Education and career

Semple graduated from Vassar College in 1882, at nineteen its youngest graduate, and took a master's degree there in 1891. Drawn to the ideas of the German geographer Friedrich Ratzel, she travelled to Leipzig to study with him, but as a woman was not permitted to matriculate and attended lectures informally. She taught geography at the University of Chicago (1906–) and at Clark University, where from 1923 she held a full professorship in her final years.

Works

Her first book, American History and Its Geographic Conditions (1903), interpreted United States history through its landforms, rivers and frontiers. Influences of Geographic Environment (1911), her best-known work, restated and extended Ratzel's Anthropogeographie for English readers, arguing that man is "a product of the earth's surface". Later fieldwork in the Mediterranean produced The Geography of the Mediterranean Region (1931).

Legacy

Semple helped establish geography as an academic discipline in the United States and opened doors for women scholars. Her deterministic framework was later criticised as oversimplified and supplanted by possibilism and other approaches, but her historical-geographical scholarship remains respected.
