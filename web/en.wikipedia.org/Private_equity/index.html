Private equity (PE) is an investment fund that pools capital from institutional and accredited investors to acquire ownership stakes in private companies, or to take public companies private. Private equity firms typically raise closed-end funds structured as limited partnerships: the firm acts as general partner, and investors (limited partners) commit capital that is drawn down over an investment period of roughly five years, with the fund liquidated after about ten. The industry's best-known strategy is the leveraged buyout (LBO), in which an acquisition is financed largely with debt secured on the target company's own assets and cash flows.

How it works

A private equity firm charges limited partners an annual management fee (conventionally around 2% of committed capital) and a share of profits called carried interest (conventionally 20% above a hurdle return). After acquiring a company, the firm seeks to increase its value over a holding period of roughly three to seven years — through operational improvements, cost cutting, acquisitions, financial engineering, or multiple expansion — and then exits via a sale to a strategic buyer, a sale to another fund, or an initial public offering. Returns are measured by internal rate of return (IRR) and multiple of invested capital (MOIC).

History

The modern industry traces to post-war venture firms such as American Research and Development (1946) and to pioneers like Kohlberg Kravis Roberts (founded 1976) and Blackstone (1985). The 1980s saw the first LBO boom, capped by the $25 billion buyout of RJR Nabisco in 1988 — chronicled in Barbarians at the Gate. A second boom in 2005–2007 produced mega-buyouts such as TXU Energy ($45 billion). After the 2008 financial crisis, cheap credit and investor demand for yield drove sustained growth; global private equity assets under management exceeded $4 trillion in the early 2020s, and firms expanded into private credit, infrastructure, and real estate.

Strategies

  • Leveraged buyouts — controlling stakes in mature companies, funded mostly with debt.
  • Growth capital — minority investments in expanding companies.
  • Venture capital — early-stage and start-up financing.
  • Distressed and special situations — buying debt or equity of troubled companies.
  • Mezzanine and private credit — lending, often subordinated, outside the banking system.

Criticism

Critics argue that private equity's debt loading can bankrupt portfolio companies, that cost cutting harms workers and — in sectors such as healthcare, nursing homes, and retail — consumers; that fee practices are opaque; and that favourable tax treatment of carried interest is unjustified. Defenders respond that PE improves productivity, rescues underperforming firms, and delivers pension funds strong long-term returns. Empirical studies find mixed effects: faster productivity growth at target firms, but also higher bankruptcy rates and, on average, modest employment losses in buyouts.
