Electric dipole moment

The electric dipole moment is a measure of the separation of positive and negative electrical charges within a system — a measure of the system's overall polarity. For a pair of opposite charges ±q separated by a displacement vector d, the dipole moment is p = qd, pointing from the negative to the positive charge. Its SI unit is the coulomb-metre (C·m); in atomic physics the Debye (D) is commonly used for molecular dipole moments.

Physical significance

A dipole in an external electric field E experiences a torque τ = p × E tending to align it with the field, and has potential energy U = −p·E. The electric field produced by a dipole falls off as 1/r³, faster than that of a single charge, and its pattern is the basis of the multipole expansion used throughout electrostatics.

Molecular dipoles

Molecules with asymmetric charge distributions, such as water (1.85 D) or hydrogen chloride, possess permanent dipole moments that determine properties like solubility, boiling point, and microwave absorption. Dipole moments are measured from dielectric properties and molecular spectra.

Particle electric dipole moments

A permanent electric dipole moment of a fundamental particle such as the electron or neutron would violate parity and time-reversal symmetry (hence CP symmetry). The Standard Model predicts values far below experimental reach, so a measurement above those predictions would signal new physics; precision experiments with electrons bound in molecules have set extraordinarily tight limits (below 10−29 e·cm).
