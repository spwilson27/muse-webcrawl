One-state solution

The one-state solution is a proposed approach to resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict through the creation of a single state encompassing present-day Israel, the West Bank and the Gaza Strip — the territory between the Jordan River and the Mediterranean Sea. Variants range from a unitary democratic state with equal citizenship for all inhabitants, to a binational federation or confederation preserving two national communities within one state.

History

Before 1948, some Zionist and Arab thinkers advocated various single-state arrangements in Mandatory Palestine: Brit Shalom and later Ihud, associated with figures such as Martin Buber and Judah Magnes, promoted a binational state. The Palestine Liberation Organization originally sought a single democratic secular state before accepting partition in principle in 1988. Interest in one-state proposals revived in the 1990s and 2000s as the Oslo process faltered and Israeli settlement expansion made a contiguous Palestinian state appear less feasible to critics.

Arguments

Supporters, including writers such as Edward Said and Tony Judt, argue that a single state with equal rights is the only arrangement consistent with liberal democracy and the facts on the ground, and some describe the existing situation as an already-existing "one-state reality." Opponents argue that a single state would end Israel's character as a Jewish state, that Palestinian majorities would soon outnumber Jews, and that two communities emerging from violent conflict could not safely share one government; most Israeli and many Palestinian political movements formally endorse or have endorsed the two-state solution instead.

Public opinion

Polls over the years have generally shown one-state proposals supported by minorities on both sides, though support fluctuates with the state of negotiations and violence. Some advocates propose a confederal "two states, one homeland" model as a middle path.
