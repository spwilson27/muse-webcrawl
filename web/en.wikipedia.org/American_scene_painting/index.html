American scene painting

American scene painting is the name later given to the Regionalist movement in American art during the 1920s and 1930s. The movement encompassed rural American Realism and Social Realism, and was driven by work showing scenes of daily life in small towns and farming communities, distinctly American landscapes, and the hardships of the Great Depression.

History

The movement arose in reaction to European modernism and abstraction. Its leading painters — Grant Wood, Thomas Hart Benton, and John Steuart Curry — depicted the Midwest and rural America with affection and nostalgia, though critics debated whether the work was celebratory or quietly critical of provincial life.

Characteristics

American scene painting favored representational imagery, smooth rolling landscapes, elongated figures, and narratives drawn from American folklore, agriculture, and small-town life. In the cities, Social Realists such as Reginald Marsh and Isabel Bishop turned the same attention to urban crowds, subways, and burlesque halls.

Legacy

The movement dominated American art patronage and public mural programs during the New Deal era, before giving way after World War II to Abstract Expressionism.
