Internal audit

Definition (IIA)An independent, objective assurance and consulting activity designed to add value and improve an organisation's operations
Professional bodyInstitute of Internal Auditors (founded 1941)
StandardsGlobal Internal Audit Standards
CertificationCertified Internal Auditor (CIA)
Reports toChief audit executive → audit committee of the board

Internal auditing is an independent, objective assurance and consulting activity conducted within an organisation. Unlike external audit, which focuses on the fairness of financial statements for outside users, internal audit evaluates and improves the effectiveness of risk management, control, and governance processes across the whole organisation.

Internal auditors provide the board with independent assurance over risk and control.

Role and scope

Internal audit functions typically follow a risk-based plan approved by the audit committee. Engagements may cover financial controls, operational efficiency, regulatory compliance, information-technology controls and cybersecurity, fraud investigation, and major projects. The function is deliberately independent of the management activities it reviews: the chief audit executive ordinarily reports functionally to the audit committee and administratively to senior management.

Standards

The Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA), founded in 1941 and headquartered in Florida, issues the Global Internal Audit Standards, which became effective in January 2025, succeeding the International Professional Practices Framework. Core principles require internal auditors to demonstrate integrity, objectivity, competency, due professional care, and confidentiality. The IIA also administers the Certified Internal Auditor designation.

Relationship to other assurance

Internal audit is sometimes described using the "three lines" model: management owns risk (first line), risk and compliance functions oversee it (second line), and internal audit provides independent assurance (third line). Legislation such as the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 increased demand for internal audit work over internal control over financial reporting, although the external auditor retains responsibility for the opinion on those controls.

Methods

  • Risk assessment and annual audit planning
  • Walkthroughs and testing of internal controls
  • Data analytics and continuous auditing of transactions
  • Interviews, observation, and substantive sampling
  • Recommendations and follow-up on agreed management actions
