Angkor Thom (Khmer for "Great City") was the last and most enduring capital city of the Khmer Empire. It was established in the late twelfth century by King Jayavarman VII and covers an area of 9 km2, within which are located several monuments from earlier eras as well as those established by Jayavarman and his successors.

History

After the sack of the earlier capital Yasodharapura by Cham forces in 1177, Jayavarman VII rebuilt a fortified city on the site. At the centre of Angkor Thom stands his state temple, the Bayon, famous for its massive stone faces of the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara. The city remained the capital until it was largely abandoned in the 16th–17th centuries.

Sites

The walled city is entered through five gates, each fronted by a causeway lined with statues of gods and demons holding a naga serpent, evoking the Hindu myth of the Churning of the Ocean of Milk. Major monuments within the walls include the Bayon, Baphuon, Phimeanakas, the Terrace of the Elephants, the Terrace of the Leper King, and Prasat Suor Prat. The Victory Gate leads east to the earlier temple of Ta Keo and beyond to Angkor Wat, which lies about 1.5 km to the south.

Layout

The city is enclosed by a square laterite wall 8 metres high and 3 km on each side, surrounded by a wide moat. Canals and the nearby Siem Reap River integrated the city into the hydraulic network of the Angkor plain.
