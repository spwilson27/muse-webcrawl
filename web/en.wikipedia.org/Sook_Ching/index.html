Sook Ching


Memorial at the site of the former War Memorial in Singapore

Sook Ching (simplified Chinese: 肃清; traditional Chinese: 肅清; pinyin: Sùqīng, meaning "purge through cleansing") was a mass killing and screening operation carried out by the Imperial Japanese Army against the Chinese population of Singapore shortly after the British colony fell in February 1942, during the Second World War. Between 18 February and 4 March 1942, Chinese men aged 18 to 50 were rounded up at screening centres; those identified — often arbitrarily or by hooded informants — as anti-Japanese were transported to remote beaches and executed.

Background

Japan invaded Malaya in December 1941, and Singapore surrendered on 15 February 1942. The Japanese military, particularly the Kempeitai military police, sought to eliminate Chinese elements regarded as hostile: supporters of China in the Second Sino-Japanese War, communists, criminals, and others. The operation was ordered under the authority of General Tomoyuki Yamashita and planned by staff officers including Masanobu Tsuji.

The massacre

Victims were taken to execution sites such as Changi Beach, Punggol Point, and Tanah Merah, where they were machine-gunned and their bodies disposed of in the sea. Estimates of the death toll vary widely: Japanese sources suggested about 5,000–6,000, the Singapore Chinese community estimated 40,000–50,000, and historians commonly cite figures between 25,000 and 50,000.

Aftermath

After the war, several Japanese officers were tried for war crimes; the screening officer corps largely escaped prosecution, and Tsuji was never tried. In 1963 mass graves were discovered, and the remains of victims were reinterred at the Civilian War Memorial in Singapore, unveiled in 1967. Singapore's government received a settlement from Japan in 1966 including compensation and a formal apology.
