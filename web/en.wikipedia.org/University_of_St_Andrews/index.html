The University of St Andrews is a public university in St Andrews, Scotland. It is the oldest of the four ancient universities of Scotland and, following the universities of Oxford and Cambridge, the third-oldest university in the English-speaking world. St Andrews was founded in 1413 when the Avignon Antipope Benedict XIII issued a papal bull to a small founding group of Augustinian clergy.

History

Teaching began in 1410 under Henry Wardlaw, Bishop of St Andrews, and the university received its charter by papal bull in 1413. Early colleges included St John's (1415), St Salvator's (1450) and St Leonard's (1512). During the Scottish Reformation the university lost its Catholic character, and in the 18th century it went through a period of decline before reviving in the Victorian era, when it incorporated University College Dundee in 1897.

Organisation

Teaching is organised into four faculties: Arts, Divinity, Medicine and Science. The university is a collegiate institution made up of three colleges — St Salvator's, St Leonard's and the United College — though these are largely ceremonial. Students are members of one of the colleges and live in halls of residence and university-managed housing across the town.

Traditions

St Andrews is known for its distinctive traditions, including the wearing of red academic gowns, the pier walk after Sunday chapel, the May Dip at dawn on 1 May, and Raisin Weekend, in which new students ("bejants" and "bejantines") are mentored by academic families. Students who step on the PH initials set in the cobbles outside St Salvator's are said to be cursed to fail their degree unless they take part in the May Dip.

Reputation

The university consistently ranks among the top universities in the United Kingdom and has frequently placed first in domestic league tables. Alumni include William, Prince of Wales, and Catherine, Princess of Wales, who met while studying at the university.
