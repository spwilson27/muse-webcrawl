Rotational grazing

Rotational grazing is a livestock management practice in which pastures (paddocks) are grazed in sequence and then rested, so forage plants recover between grazings. It contrasts with continuous grazing, where animals range the same land all season.

Practice

A pasture is divided by permanent or temporary electric fencing into paddocks; herds are moved every few days or, in management-intensive and "mob" grazing, several times a day at high stock density. Rest periods — typically 30 to 90 days depending on growth — let grasses rebuild roots and leaf area, distribute manure evenly, reduce selective overgrazing of favoured species and break parasite cycles.

Claims and evidence

Advocates such as practitioners of holistic management claim improved soil cover, water infiltration and carbon sequestration; controlled studies show benefits depend heavily on climate, stocking rate and management, and outcomes versus well-managed continuous grazing are mixed. Rotational systems underpin much dairy grazing in New Zealand. See also pasture and regenerative agriculture.
