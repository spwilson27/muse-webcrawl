The second voyage of HMS Beagle, from 27 December 1831 to 2 October 1836, was the second survey expedition of HMS Beagle, under Captain Robert FitzRoy, who had taken over command of the ship on its first voyage following the suicide of its previous captain. FitzRoy, thinking of the scientific advantages, brought a young naturalist, Charles Darwin, who transformed the expedition into one of the most famous voyages in the history of science.

Objectives

The Admiralty charged the expedition with completing the survey of Patagonia and Tierra del Fuego begun on the first voyage, charting the coasts of Chile, Peru and Pacific islands, and carrying a chain of chronometric measurements around the world to establish accurate longitudes.

The voyage

Beagle sailed from Plymouth after delays, reached South America in February 1832, and spent three and a half years surveying the continent's coasts. Darwin landed for long excursions inland — across the pampas, into the Andes, and through Chile — collecting fossils of extinct giant mammals and observing the effects of the 1835 Concepción earthquake and the uplift of the coast. The ship also returned three Fuegian captives taken to England on the first voyage, including Jemmy Button, to a mission settlement in Tierra del Fuego, an experiment that quickly failed.

The Galápagos and homeward leg

In September–October 1835 Beagle visited the Galápagos Islands, where Darwin collected the finches and mockingbirds whose island-to-island variation later fed into his theory of evolution. The voyage continued to Tahiti, New Zealand, Australia, the Cocos (Keeling) Islands — where Darwin tested his theory of coral atoll formation — Mauritius, Cape Town and St Helena, before recrossing the Atlantic and reaching Falmouth on 2 October 1836, after four years and nine months.

Legacy

Darwin's Journal of Researches (1839), published as part of FitzRoy's narrative and better known as The Voyage of the Beagle, made his reputation as a writer and geologist. The specimens and observations of the voyage underpinned his later development of evolution by natural selection, announced in 1858 and published in On the Origin of Species in 1859.
