Crown of Thorns

According to the New Testament, a woven crown of thorns (Greek: στέφανος ἐξ ἀκανθῶν, stephanos ex akanthōn) was placed on the head of Jesus during the events leading up to his crucifixion. It was one of the instruments of the Passion, employed by Jesus' captors both to cause him pain and to mock his claim of authority. It is mentioned in the gospels of Matthew (27:29), Mark (15:17) and John (19:2, 5), and is often alluded to by the early Church Fathers.

The relic

A relic traditionally identified as the Crown of Thorns was kept in Jerusalem and later in Constantinople. In 1238 the Latin Emperor Baldwin II offered it to King Louis IX of France, who brought it to Paris in 1239 and built the Sainte-Chapelle to house it, along with other Passion relics. The crown, a circlet of rushes (Juncus balticus) from which individual thorns were distributed to churches across Europe, was kept at Notre-Dame de Paris from 1806.

Notre-Dame fire, 2019

During the fire of 15 April 2019, the Crown of Thorns was rescued from the burning cathedral by firefighters and the chaplain Jean-Marc Fournier, and transferred for safekeeping, first to the Paris City Hall and then to the Louvre. It was returned to display at Notre-Dame following the cathedral's reopening in December 2024.

Veneration and symbolism

The crown is among the most venerated relics of Christendom and a potent symbol in Christian art, frequently depicted in representations of the Passion, the Man of Sorrows and the Ecce Homo. Plants popularly associated with it include the Christ thorn (Euphorbia milii) and the Jerusalem thorn.
