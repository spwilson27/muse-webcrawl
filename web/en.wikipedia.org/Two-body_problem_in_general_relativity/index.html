Two-body problem in general relativity

The two-body problem in general relativity (or relativistic two-body problem) is the determination of the motion and gravitational field of two bodies interacting gravitationally, as described by Einstein's theory of general relativity. Unlike the Newtonian two-body problem, it has no exact closed-form solution for two comparable masses. Solving it is essential for modelling binary pulsars and the merging black holes and neutron stars observed through gravitational waves.

Approaches

For a much lighter body orbiting a massive one, the problem reduces to geodesic motion in a fixed background such as the Schwarzschild or Kerr metric. For general systems, physicists use the post-Newtonian expansion, which treats relativistic corrections as a series in small velocities and weak fields; numerical relativity, which solves Einstein's equations on supercomputers and achieved the first stable binary black hole simulations in 2005; and the effective one-body formalism, which maps the two-body dynamics onto the motion of a single body in a deformed black-hole geometry.

Physical effects

Relativistic two-body dynamics predicts effects absent from Newtonian gravity: the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury, the inspiral of binary systems through the emission of gravitational radiation (confirmed by the orbital decay of the Hulse–Taylor pulsar), and the eventual merger and ringdown of compact binaries, whose waveforms are matched against detector data by LIGO and Virgo.
