The Khedivate of Egypt (Arabic: الخديوية المصرية) was an autonomous tributary state of the Ottoman Empire, established in 1867 and lasting until 1914. Its rulers, descendants of Muhammad Ali Pasha, bore the title of Khedive ("viceroy"), a rank formally granted to Isma'il Pasha by the Ottoman Sultan in recognition of Egypt's de facto independence and in return for increased tribute.

History

Under Isma'il Pasha, Egypt underwent rapid modernisation: the Suez Canal was opened in 1869, railways, ports and cotton production expanded, and Egypt extended its rule deep into Sudan. However, extravagant borrowing drove the state into bankruptcy in 1876, bringing Anglo-French financial control; Isma'il was deposed in 1879. The nationalist ʻUrabi revolt of 1882 prompted British military intervention, and Britain occupied Egypt, ruling it through a consul-general (notably Lord Cromer) while it formally remained Ottoman and Khedival — the "veiled protectorate".

End

On the outbreak of the First World War, after the Ottoman Empire joined the Central Powers, Britain deposed Khedive Abbas II in December 1914, declared Egypt a British protectorate as the Sultanate of Egypt under Hussein Kamel, and ended the Khedivate.
