Closed-end fund

A closed-end fund (CEF) is a type of investment fund that raises a fixed amount of capital through an initial public offering and then trades on a stock exchange like an ordinary share. Unlike an open-end mutual fund, a closed-end fund does not continuously issue or redeem shares at net asset value; instead, investors buy and sell existing shares among themselves at prices set by the market.

Premium and discount

Because the market price of a closed-end fund's shares is determined by supply and demand, it frequently differs from the per-share value of the fund's underlying holdings, its net asset value (NAV). When shares trade above NAV the fund is said to trade at a premium; when below, at a discount. Persistent discounts are a long-studied puzzle in finance, and activist investors sometimes campaign for measures such as share buybacks, tender offers, or conversion to an open-end structure to narrow them.

Structure and leverage

Closed-end funds are professionally managed and invest in a portfolio of securities according to a stated strategy, which may cover equities, bonds, municipal securities, real estate, or foreign markets. Many funds employ leverage—through borrowing or preferred shares—to amplify returns, a practice more feasible than in open-end funds because the fund does not face daily redemption pressure.

History

Closed-end investment companies are among the oldest pooled investment vehicles, predating mutual funds; early examples appeared in nineteenth-century Britain and the Netherlands. In the United States they are regulated under the Investment Company Act of 1940.
