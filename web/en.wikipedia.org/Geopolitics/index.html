Geopolitics

Geopolitics is the study of the effects of Earth's geography — human and physical — on politics and international relations. It analyses how spatial factors such as location, terrain, climate, resources, and access to the sea shape the power and behaviour of states and other political actors.

Geopolitics studies how geography shapes the relations between states.

History

The term was coined by the Swedish political scientist Rudolf Kjellén around the turn of the twentieth century, building on the work of Friedrich Ratzel. Early geopolitical thought included Halford Mackinder's Heartland theory (1904), which held that control of the Eurasian interior conferred world dominance, and Alfred Thayer Mahan's emphasis on sea power. In Germany, Karl Haushofer's school of Geopolitik became associated with Nazi expansionism, which discredited the term after the Second World War.

Cold War and after

During the Cold War, geopolitical reasoning underlay the Western strategy of containment and theories such as Nicholas Spykman's Rimland thesis. After 1991, scholars debated whether geography had lost its force in a globalised world, but conflicts over territory, sea lanes, energy resources, and chokepoints such as the Strait of Malacca and the South China Sea have kept geopolitical analysis central to foreign policy.

Critical geopolitics

Since the 1980s, critical geopolitics has examined how political leaders and media represent the world — through categories such as "the West", "rogue states", or "the Global South" — arguing that geopolitical knowledge is itself a form of power rather than a neutral description of geography.
