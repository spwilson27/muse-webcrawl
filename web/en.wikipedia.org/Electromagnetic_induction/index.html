Electromagnetic induction is the production of an electromotive force (voltage) across an electrical conductor in a changing magnetic field. Michael Faraday is generally credited with the discovery of induction in 1831, while Joseph Henry independently made the same discovery in 1832.

Faraday's law

Faraday's law of induction states that the induced electromotive force ℰ in a closed circuit is equal to the negative rate of change of the magnetic flux ΦB through the circuit:

ℰ = − dΦB / dt

The negative sign, expressing that the induced current opposes the change that produces it, is Lenz's law. Induction underlies the operation of electric generators, transformers and induction motors, and hence most of the world's electricity generation and distribution.

History

Faraday's breakthrough experiments of 1831 used an iron ring wound with two coils: a current in one coil induced a momentary current in the other. He soon demonstrated induction with a magnet thrust into a coil, and built the first electromagnetic generator, the Faraday disc. Maxwell later incorporated induction into his equations of electromagnetism as one of Maxwell's equations.

Applications

Beyond generation and transformation of electric power, induction is used in induction cooking, metal detectors, wireless (inductive) charging, guitar pickups and non-destructive testing.
