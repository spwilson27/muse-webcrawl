Euler angles

Euler angles
ParametersThree angles (e.g., φ, θ, ψ)
Named forLeonhard Euler
UsesRigid body orientation, aerospace, robotics, crystallography

The Euler angles are three angles introduced by Leonhard Euler to describe the orientation of a rigid body with respect to a fixed coordinate system. Any orientation can be reached by composing three elementary rotations about coordinate axes.

Conventions

There are twelve possible sequences of axes. "Proper" Euler angles repeat an axis (e.g., z–x–z), while Tait–Bryan angles use three distinct axes (e.g., z–y–x, commonly called yaw, pitch, and roll in aviation). Rotations may be intrinsic (about the moving axes) or extrinsic (about fixed axes), which reverses the composition order.

Applications

Euler angles parameterise spacecraft attitude, aircraft orientation, robot joint configurations, camera orientation in computer graphics, and crystal orientations in texture analysis (Bunge angles). They connect to rotation matrices and to Euler's rotation theorem, which states that any rotation has a single fixed axis.

Gimbal lock

When the middle rotation aligns the first and third axes, one degree of freedom is lost — the singularity called gimbal lock. Because of it, and because interpolating Euler angles is awkward, quaternions are often preferred in graphics and aerospace simulation.
