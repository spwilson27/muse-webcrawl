Field: Geometric group theory, computability
Proposed by: Max Dehn (1911)
Status: Undecidable in general

Word problem for groups

In mathematics, the word problem for groups is the algorithmic problem of deciding whether two words in the generators of a finitely presented group represent the same element — equivalently, whether a given word represents the identity element. It is one of the three fundamental decision problems proposed by Max Dehn in 1911, alongside the conjugacy problem and the isomorphism problem.

Undecidability

In the 1950s, Pyotr Novikov and, independently, William Boone proved that there exist finite group presentations for which the word problem is undecidable: no algorithm can solve it in all cases. The proofs connect group theory to Turing machines and the halting problem, making the word problem a landmark application of computability theory to algebra.

Solvable cases

Many important classes of groups have solvable word problems. Dehn himself gave an algorithm for hyperbolic groups (in modern terms, groups admitting a Dehn presentation), and word problems are solvable for free groups, abelian groups, braid groups, automatic groups and one-relator groups (via Magnus's method). The word problem for a group is solvable exactly when the group can be embedded in a simple group with a finite presentation, among other characterisations. The difficulty of the word problem for specific groups is also of interest in cryptography.
