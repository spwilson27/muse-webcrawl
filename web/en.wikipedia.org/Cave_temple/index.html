Cave temple

A cave temple is a temple or shrine constructed within a natural cave or excavated into a rock face. Rock-cut cave temples are found across Asia and elsewhere, serving Buddhist, Hindu, Jain, and other religious traditions, and often combine carved architecture, sculpture, and painting.

India

India has the richest tradition of rock-cut architecture. The Buddhist caves at Ajanta and Ellora in Maharashtra are renowned for their paintings and sculpture; Ellora also contains Hindu and Jain caves, including the monumental Kailasa temple carved from a single rock. Other major complexes include the Elephanta Caves near Mumbai, the Badami cave temples in Karnataka, and the Udayagiri and Khandagiri caves in Odisha.

China and Central Asia

Along the Silk Road, Buddhist grottoes were carved at Mogao (Dunhuang), Yungang, and Longmen, filled with statues and murals. The Bamiyan caves in Afghanistan once housed colossal Buddha statues.

Elsewhere

Cave shrines occur in Sri Lanka (such as the Dambulla cave temple), Myanmar, Thailand, Ethiopia (rock-hewn churches of Lalibela, though these are monolithic churches rather than caves), and Cappadocia in Turkey, where early Christians carved churches into soft volcanic rock.
