Folio

EtymologyLatin folium, "leaf"
Related termsQuarto, octavo, duodecimo
UsesBook sizes, paper sizes, pagination

A folio is a method of referring to the size of a book, a single leaf of paper, or a page number in a manuscript or early printed book. The word derives from the Latin folium, meaning "leaf". In bibliography, a folio (abbreviated fo or 2°) is a book or pamphlet made up of full sheets of paper, each folded once to produce two leaves, or four pages. The most famous example is the First Folio of Shakespeare's plays, published in 1623.

Book format

In the hand-press period, books were commonly described by the number of times a full sheet of paper was folded to form the leaves of the book. A folio sheet was folded once, producing the largest standard format; a quarto was folded twice (four leaves), an octavo three times (eight leaves), and so on down to duodecimo and smaller formats. Because paper sizes varied, "folio" describes the folding rather than an exact dimension, though folio volumes are generally the largest in a library's holdings, often around 38 cm (15 in) tall or more.

Manuscripts and pagination

In manuscripts and archival documents, leaves rather than pages are numbered: each leaf has a recto (front) and verso (back), cited as, for example, "f. 12r" and "f. 12v". This system, called foliation, contrasts with the pagination of printed books. Archivists and palaeographers rely on foliation when citing medieval codices, legal records, and account books.

Paper sizes

"Folio" also survives as a paper size name. In the United States, "legal" paper (8.5 × 14 in) is sometimes called folio, and in some countries foolscap folio (8.5 × 13.5 in) remains a standard office paper size. The term is also used for a sheet of manuscript or typescript, and in finance for a page of a ledger.
