Engine knocking (also called detonation, pinging or pinking) is a phenomenon in spark-ignition internal combustion engines in which combustion of part of the air–fuel mixture does not result from the propagation of the flame front ignited by the spark plug, but instead pockets of mixture explode spontaneously ("detonate") outside the envelope of the normal combustion front. It produces a characteristic metallic "pinging" sound and sharp pressure spikes that can damage pistons, rings and cylinder heads.

Mechanism

As the flame front advances after ignition, the unburned "end gas" ahead of it is compressed and heated. If its temperature exceeds the auto-ignition threshold before the flame arrives, the end gas burns almost instantaneously, generating collisions between pressure waves that are heard as knock. Knocking is distinct from pre-ignition, in which a hot spot ignites the mixture before the spark.

Causes and control

Knock is promoted by low-octane fuel, high compression ratio, excessive spark advance, high intake temperatures, heavy load at low speed, and carbon deposits. Fuels are rated for knock resistance by their octane rating; historically tetraethyl lead was added to petrol as an anti-knock agent before being phased out for health and environmental reasons. Modern engines use knock sensors — piezoelectric microphones on the block — allowing the engine control unit to retard ignition timing or adjust boost pressure in real time, so engines can run close to the knock limit for efficiency.
