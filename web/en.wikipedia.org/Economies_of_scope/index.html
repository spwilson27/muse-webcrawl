Economies of scope

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Economies of scope are "efficiencies wrought by variety, not volume" (whereas economies of scale arise from efficiencies of volume). Economies of scope occur when it is cheaper to produce a wider variety of products together in a single firm rather than producing each product separately in specialised firms. The term and concept were developed by economists John C. Panzar and Robert D. Willig (1977, 1981) and by David Teece (1980, 1982).

Economies of scope: joint production is cheaper than separate production

Formally, economies of scope exist when the cost of producing two (or more) products jointly is less than the sum of the costs of producing them separately:

C(q1, q2) < C(q1, 0) + C(0, q2)

Sources

Economies of scope can arise from shared inputs and capabilities, for example:

  • Inputs, such as distribution networks, brand names, or research facilities, that can serve several products without being used up (quasi-public inputs).
  • Complementary resources and skills, such as marketing expertise applied across a product range.
  • By-products: a process yielding one product can yield another valuable product at low extra cost (e.g., meat and hides from cattle).
  • Shared fixed costs spread across more product lines.

Examples

A classic example is a firm that produces both gasoline and diesel from crude oil: the joint refining process is cheaper than running separate specialised plants. In banking, a single branch network can offer deposits, loans, and insurance products. Procter & Gamble applies shared marketing and distribution capabilities across many household brands. Flexible manufacturing systems, which allow a plant to switch between product variants at low cost, increase economies of scope in modern industry.

Relation to diversification

Economies of scope provide the principal efficiency rationale for corporate diversification and conglomerates: merging firms can profit if their combined operations exploit shared resources. However, diversification can also generate diseconomies (coordination costs, management overload), and empirical studies find that unrelated diversification often trades at a discount in capital markets.
