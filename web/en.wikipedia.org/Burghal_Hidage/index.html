Burghal Hidage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Burghal Hidage is an Anglo-Saxon document that provides a list of over thirty fortified places (burhs) in Wessex and English Mercia, together with the number of hides assigned to each for its maintenance and defence. It is the key surviving evidence for the system of fortifications built against Viking attack in the late ninth and early tenth centuries.

Contents and purpose

The document gives, for each burh, an assessment in hides — the standard unit of land measurement used for taxation. A formula in the text explains the relationship between hides and wall length: an acre's breadth of wall was to be maintained by sixteen hides, with one man supplied from each hide, so that every pole (5½ yards) of wall was manned by four men. From the hidage figures historians can therefore calculate the length of each burh's defences, and these calculations match the surviving ramparts at places such as Winchester, Wallingford and Wareham remarkably well.

Dating and context

The Burghal Hidage is generally dated to the reign of Edward the Elder (899–924), though the system it describes was begun under his father, Alfred the Great, whose network of burhs helped Wessex withstand the Viking invasions. The document survives in several later medieval recensions, two main versions of which differ slightly in the places listed.

Significance

The text is central to understanding how Alfredian and Edwardian Wessex organised military defence on a kingdom-wide, assessed basis — an early example of state-planned fortification in early medieval Europe.
