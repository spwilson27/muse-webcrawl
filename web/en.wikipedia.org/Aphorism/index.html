Aphorism

An aphorism is a concise statement of a truth or principle, expressed in a memorable and often witty form. The term derives from the Greek aphorismos, meaning a definition or distinction. Aphorisms differ from proverbs in usually having a known author and a literary, sometimes paradoxical, character.

History

The earliest famous collection is the Aphorisms of Hippocrates, brief medical maxims that begin "Life is short, and art long." The genre flourished in early modern Europe with writers such as Erasmus, Francis Bacon, and the French moralists La Rochefoucauld and Chamfort. Later masters include Oscar Wilde, Friedrich Nietzsche, Mark Twain, and the Polish writer Stanisław Jerzy Lec.

Characteristics

An aphorism compresses an insight into a few words, often using antithesis, paradox or wordplay. Because of their brevity they are easily quoted and remembered, and they are used in philosophy, literature, law, medicine and everyday rhetoric.
