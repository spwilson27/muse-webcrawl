Negotiation

TypesDistributive, integrative, principled
Key conceptsBATNA, ZOPA, anchoring, interests vs. positions
FieldsBusiness, diplomacy, law, labour relations

Negotiation is a dialogue between two or more parties designed to reach a beneficial outcome on one or more issues in which a conflict exists. The outcome may be beneficial for all parties involved, or for only some of them. Negotiations occur in business, non-profits, government branches, legal proceedings, among nations, and in personal situations.

Approaches

In distributive negotiation (win–lose), parties divide a fixed quantity of value. In integrative negotiation (win–win), parties seek to expand the value available by trading on differing preferences. Principled negotiation, developed by the Harvard Negotiation Project and set out in the bestseller Getting to Yes (1981) by Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton, advises negotiators to focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, insist on objective criteria, and understand their BATNA — the best alternative to a negotiated agreement.

Stages and tactics

Negotiations typically proceed through preparation, exchange of information, bargaining, and closure. Common tactics include making the first offer to exploit the anchoring effect, asking open questions to discover interests, making concessions gradually, and using deadlines. Skilled negotiators distinguish the people from the problem and manage emotion and communication.

Applications

Formal study of negotiation draws on game theory, behavioural economics, and psychology, and is taught in business and law schools. Related practices include mediation and arbitration, in which a third party helps resolve disputes — see alternative dispute resolution.
