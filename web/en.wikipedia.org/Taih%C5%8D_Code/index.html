Taihō Code

The Taihō Code (大宝律令, Taihō-ritsuryō) was an administrative reorganisation enacted in 701 in Japan under Emperor Monmu, during the Taihō era. Modelled on the legal and bureaucratic codes of Tang-dynasty China, it consolidated the ritsuryō system — penal laws (ritsu) and administrative laws (ryō) — that structured the imperial Japanese state for centuries.

Contents

The code organised central government around the Council of State (Daijō-kan) and the Department of Worship (Jingikan), divided the country into provinces, districts, and villages, and prescribed court ranks, official posts, taxation, and a periodic census and land-allotment system. It also formalised the structure of ministries, the imperial guard, and state-supported Buddhism and court ritual.

Revision and survival

The Taihō Code was revised and superseded in 718 by the Yōrō Code, which was not fully implemented until 757 but became the standard reference. The original Taihō text is lost and is known mainly through the later Yōrō code and its official commentaries, the Ryō no Gige (833) and Ryō no Shūge (c. 880). Together the codes mark the high point of Japan's adoption of Chinese-style bureaucratic government in the Asuka and Nara periods.
