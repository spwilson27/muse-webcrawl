Obsequiousness

Obsequiousness is the quality of being excessively eager to please or obey, showing a servile deference toward someone regarded as important. An obsequious person — sometimes called a sycophant, toady, or brown-noser in informal usage — offers exaggerated praise, agreement, and attentiveness, usually in hope of personal advantage.

Etymology

The word comes from the Latin obsequiosus, "compliant, obedient", from obsequium, "compliance, obedience", from obsequi, "to follow after, to comply". In the 15th century the word could still carry the neutral sense of being dutiful and compliant, but by the 16th century it had acquired its modern pejorative connotation of servility.

Related concepts

Obsequiousness overlaps with flattery, ingratiation, sycophancy, and fawning, but implies in particular a posture of obedience and submission. In literature, stock obsequious characters include Shakespeare's courtiers who echo whatever a ruler says, and Charles Dickens's Uriah Heep, who professes to be "ever so 'umble".
