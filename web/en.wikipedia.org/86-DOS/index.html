86-DOS was an operating system developed and marketed by Seattle Computer Products (SCP) for its Intel 8086-based computer kit. Written by programmer Tim Paterson in 1980, it was originally known as QDOS — the Quick and Dirty Operating System. It became the foundation of Microsoft's MS-DOS and IBM's PC DOS.

Development

Frustrated by delays to Digital Research's CP/M-86, Paterson wrote 86-DOS in about six weeks, deliberately modeling its API on CP/M so that existing software could be translated easily, while using the FAT12 file system and other internal designs of his own. SCP licensed the system non-exclusively to Microsoft in December 1980 for $25,000.

Sale to Microsoft

Microsoft, needing a disk operating system for IBM's forthcoming personal computer, purchased all rights to 86-DOS from SCP in July 1981 for $50,000 and hired Paterson. The system was adapted for the IBM PC and released as PC DOS 1.0, while Microsoft marketed its own version as MS-DOS — the dominant operating system of the 1980s. The deal later became the subject of litigation and lore about the most consequential Bargain in computing history.
