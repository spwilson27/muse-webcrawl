An oncogene is a gene that has the potential to cause cancer. In tumor cells, these genes are often mutated or expressed at high levels. Most normal cells undergo a programmed form of rapid cell death (apoptosis) when critical functions are altered; activated oncogenes can cause those cells designated for apoptosis to survive and proliferate instead.

An oncogene is a sequence of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) that has been altered or mutated from its original form, the proto-oncogene, which normally regulates cell growth and differentiation. Oncogenes may cause the growth of cancer cells.

History

The first oncogene was discovered in 1970 and termed src (pronounced "sarc" as in sarcoma). Src was in fact first discovered as an oncogene in a chicken retrovirus, the Rous sarcoma virus, studied by Peyton Rous in 1911. In the 1970s and 1980s, J. Michael Bishop and Harold E. Varmus demonstrated that oncogenes in viruses were derived from normal cellular genes (proto-oncogenes), work for which they received the 1989 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Proto-oncogenes and activation

Proto-oncogenes encode proteins involved in signal transduction and execution of mitogenic signals, such as growth factors, growth factor receptors, and protein kinases. They can be converted into oncogenes by point mutation, gene amplification, chromosomal translocation, or viral insertion. Because proto-oncogenes are often essential to normal cellular function, their activation is typically only one of several genetic changes needed for a full cancer phenotype; loss of tumor suppressor genes is also usually required.

Examples

  • RAS family (KRAS, HRAS, NRAS) — among the most frequently mutated oncogenes in human cancer.
  • MYC — a transcription factor deregulated in many tumours, including Burkitt lymphoma.
  • HER2/neu (ERBB2) — amplified in a subset of breast cancers; target of trastuzumab.
  • BCR-ABL — fusion protein of the Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukaemia; target of imatinib.
