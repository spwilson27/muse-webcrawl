Bee learning and communication

Bee learning and communication encompasses the ways in which bees, especially honey bees, acquire information and convey it to nestmates. Bees are capable of associative learning, navigation using landmarks and celestial cues, and social learning. Their best-known communication system is the waggle dance, by which a successful forager advertises the direction and distance of food or a new nest site to other bees.

The waggle dance

Decoded by Karl von Frisch in the 20th century, the waggle dance is performed on the vertical comb. The dancer traces a figure-eight pattern; the angle of the straight waggle run relative to gravity encodes the direction of the resource relative to the Sun, while the duration of the waggle phase correlates with distance. Dancers also share odour and taste samples of the food. Round dances are used for nearby resources. Von Frisch's work earned a share of the 1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Other communication

Bees communicate extensively by pheromones: the queen's mandibular pheromone regulates colony cohesion and worker behaviour, alarm pheromones recruit defenders, and the Nasonov pheromone helps orient returning foragers. Vibrational signals, such as the stop signal and tremble dance, modulate recruitment and foraging activity.

Learning and cognition

Despite small brains, bees learn colours, patterns, odours and routes, can be trained in laboratory tasks involving counting-like discrimination and same/different concepts, and remember profitable flowers. Bumblebees have even been trained to pull strings and roll balls for rewards, with skills spreading by social learning. This research has made bees a model system in the study of insect cognition.
