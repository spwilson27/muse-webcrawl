TypeAssistive technology software
PurposeConverts on-screen text to speech or braille

A screen reader is a form of assistive technology that renders text and image content as speech or braille output. Screen readers are essential to people who are blind, and are also useful to people who are visually impaired, illiterate, or have a learning disability. They are usually combined with other assistive technology, such as screen magnifiers.

A screen reader works by communicating with the operating system or application through accessibility APIs, which expose the structure of the user interface — text, headings, links, form controls, and their roles and states — as a tree the software can navigate and announce.

How screen readers work

Modern screen readers build a virtual representation of the document or application window. On the web, this is derived from the DOM and the browser's accessibility tree, which is populated by semantic HTML elements and ARIA attributes. Users navigate by headings, landmarks, links, and form fields, jump through content with quick-navigation keys, and interact with controls using a virtual cursor or browse mode. Spoken output is produced by a text-to-speech synthesizer, while a refreshable braille display can present the same content tactually.

History

Early talking computers appeared in the 1970s and 1980s, when speech synthesizers were first connected to personal computers. The transition from command-line systems to graphical user interfaces in the late 1980s posed a major challenge, prompting the development of "out-of-spoken" and off-screen model techniques. JAWS (Job Access With Speech), released in 1995, became a dominant screen reader for Windows. The free and open-source NVDA (NonVisual Desktop Access), released in 2006, greatly expanded access worldwide. Apple built VoiceOver into macOS in 2005 and into the iPhone in 2009, a milestone that made mainstream touchscreen devices accessible to blind users.

Popular screen readers

Common screen readers include JAWS and NVDA on Windows, Microsoft Narrator (built into Windows), VoiceOver on macOS and iOS, TalkBack on Android, and Orca on Linux. Annual surveys of screen reader users, such as those by WebAIM, track usage patterns and inform web accessibility practice.

Web accessibility

For a website to work well with a screen reader, authors should use semantic markup, provide text alternatives for images, label form controls, maintain a logical heading structure, and ensure keyboard operability. Standards such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) codify these requirements. See also accessibility and assistive technology.
