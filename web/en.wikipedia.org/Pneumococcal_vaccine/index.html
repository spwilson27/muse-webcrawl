TypePolysaccharide / conjugate
TargetStreptococcus pneumoniae
RoutesIntramuscular

Pneumococcal vaccines are vaccines against the bacterium Streptococcus pneumoniae. Their use can prevent some cases of pneumonia, meningitis, and sepsis. In the United States and many other countries they are routinely recommended for infants, older adults, and people with certain medical conditions.

There are two main types: pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV), in which capsular polysaccharides are linked to a carrier protein to elicit a stronger immune response, including in infants; and the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23), which contains purified polysaccharides from 23 serotypes but is not effective in children under two years of age.

Types

  • PCV13 (Prevnar 13) — 13-valent conjugate vaccine formerly standard in childhood schedules.
  • PCV15 (Vaxneuvance) — 15-valent conjugate vaccine.
  • PCV20 (Prevnar 20) — 20-valent conjugate vaccine approved for adults and later for children.
  • PCV21 (Capvaxive) — 21-valent conjugate vaccine approved in 2024 for adults.
  • PPSV23 (Pneumovax 23) — 23-valent polysaccharide vaccine.

Medical use

The World Health Organization recommends inclusion of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines in national childhood immunisation programmes worldwide. Introduction of conjugate vaccines produced large declines in invasive pneumococcal disease among children and, through herd immunity, among unvaccinated adults. Because more than 100 pneumococcal serotypes exist, vaccines protect against only the serotypes they contain, and serotype replacement has been observed after vaccine introduction.

Adverse effects

Most adverse effects are mild and include pain, redness, or swelling at the injection site, mild fever, irritability, and decreased appetite in children. Serious allergic reactions are rare.

History

Early whole-cell pneumococcal vaccines were tested in the early 1900s. A 14-valent polysaccharide vaccine was licensed in 1977 and replaced by the 23-valent version in 1983. The first conjugate vaccine, PCV7, was licensed in 2000 and dramatically reduced invasive pneumococcal disease in young children.
