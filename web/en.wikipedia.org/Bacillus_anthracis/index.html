Bacillus anthracis

Bacillus anthracis is a Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacterium that causes anthrax, a serious disease of livestock and occasionally of humans. It forms highly resistant endospores that can survive in soil for decades.

Biology

The bacterium grows in long chains of large (1–8 μm) rods and, when exposed to air, produces central spores. Its virulence depends on two plasmids: pXO1, which encodes the three-part anthrax toxin (protective antigen, lethal factor and oedema factor), and pXO2, which encodes its anti-phagocytic poly-glutamic acid capsule — unusual among bacteria for being made of protein rather than polysaccharide.

History

B. anthracis was the first bacterium conclusively shown to cause a disease: Robert Koch demonstrated its life cycle and its role in anthrax in 1876, work that helped found medical microbiology. Louis Pasteur's public demonstration of a live anthrax vaccine at Pouilly-le-Fort followed in 1881. Because of its durability and lethality, the spores have been weaponised by state programmes and were used in the 2001 anthrax letter attacks in the United States.
