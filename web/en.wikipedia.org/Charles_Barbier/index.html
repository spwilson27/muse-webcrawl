Charles Barbier

Charles Barbier de la Serre (18 May 1767 – 22 April 1841) was a French artillery officer and inventor known for creating "night writing" (écriture nocturne), a system of raised dots and dashes intended to let soldiers communicate silently in darkness. His system became the direct inspiration for braille.

Night writing

Barbier devised his code after hearing of the difficulties of battlefield communication at night. His system used a 6×6 grid: each letter was represented by two columns of raised dots, the first giving the row and the second the column. The dots were embossed into paper with a special slate and stylus. Although the military showed little interest, Barbier promoted the system for blind readers, demonstrating it at the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris.

Influence on Braille

At the institute, the young Louis Braille learned Barbier's system and recognised both its promise and its flaws: the cells were too large for a fingertip to read at once, and there was no provision for punctuation or numbers. Braille reduced the cell to six dots in two columns of three, creating the system published in 1829 that is still used worldwide. Barbier, who corresponded with Braille, at first reacted coolly but later acknowledged the young man's achievement.
