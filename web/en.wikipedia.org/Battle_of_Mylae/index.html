Battle of Mylae

The Battle of Mylae took place in 260 BC during the First Punic War and was the first real naval battle between the fleets of Carthage and the Roman Republic. Fought off Mylae (modern Milazzo) on the north coast of Sicily, it ended in a major Roman victory and marked Rome's emergence as a naval power.

Background

At the outbreak of the First Punic War, Rome had no war fleet worthy of the name, while Carthage dominated the western Mediterranean. Using a wrecked Carthaginian quinquereme as a model, the Romans built a fleet of about 120 ships and equipped them with the corvus, a spiked boarding bridge that could be dropped onto an enemy deck, turning a sea fight into an infantry fight at which Roman legionaries excelled.

The battle

The Carthaginian fleet under Hannibal Gisco, confident in its superiority, approached the Roman fleet under Gaius Duilius in loose order. The Roman ships grappled the leading Carthaginian vessels with the corvus and boarded them; the Carthaginians lost around 30–50 ships captured or sunk, including Hannibal's flagship, and fled. It was Rome's first naval victory.

Aftermath

Duilius celebrated a naval triumph in Rome, and the Columna Rostrata, decorated with the beaks of captured ships, was erected in the Forum in his honour. The victory encouraged Rome to carry the naval war to Sardinia, Corsica and eventually Africa.
