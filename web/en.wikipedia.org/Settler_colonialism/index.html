Settler colonialism

TypeForm of colonialism
Defining featurePermanent settlement and replacement of Indigenous populations
Key scholarsPatrick Wolfe, Lorenzo Veracini
Examples studiedUnited States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Israel/Palestine, French Algeria, Liberia

Settler colonialism is a form of colonialism in which foreign settlers arrive in a territory with the intention of staying permanently and establishing a new society, typically displacing, removing or assimilating the Indigenous population. Scholars distinguish it from extractive or "franchise" colonialism, where the primary aim is to exploit labour and resources while the colonising population remains largely transient.

Theory

The field of settler colonial studies was shaped by anthropologist Patrick Wolfe, who argued that settler colonialism is governed by a "logic of elimination" and famously described invasion as "a structure, not an event" — an ongoing system rather than a completed historical moment. Lorenzo Veracini theorised settler colonialism as a distinct formation in which settlers demand sovereignty and seek to supersede the Indigenous societies they encounter. The journal Settler Colonial Studies was founded in 2011.

Historical cases

Classic cases include the British settlement of North America, Australia and New Zealand; the displacement of Native Americans through removal policies, reservations and allotment; the dispossession of Aboriginal Australians under the doctrine of terra nullius (overturned in the Mabo judgment of 1992); and the treatment of Māori land in New Zealand despite the Treaty of Waitangi (1840). Other frequently studied cases include French Algeria, South Africa (the 1913 Natives Land Act), Liberia, and Israeli settlements. Settler colonialism is also analysed in internal colonisation by states such as Japan in Hokkaido and Ainu lands.

Legacies

Contemporary debates concern land rights, treaty obligations, reparations, official apologies and truth commissions (for example in Canada), and how settler-colonial structures persist in law and institutions. The concept is influential but also contested: critics argue it can flatten important differences between cases and sideline class, race and Indigenous agency.
