Assyria


Stone relief from an Assyrian palace, showing the king and attendants.

Assyria was a major ancient Mesopotamian civilisation centred on the city of Assur on the upper Tigris River in what is now northern Iraq. Over a history spanning from the Early Bronze Age to the late Iron Age — roughly from the 21st century BC to the 7th century BC — Assyria developed from a city-state into a territorial kingdom and ultimately into the Neo-Assyrian Empire, the largest empire the world had yet seen.

History

Historians divide Assyrian history into the Early Assyrian, Old Assyrian, Middle Assyrian and Neo-Assyrian periods. In the Old Assyrian period, merchants from Assur ran a celebrated trading network reaching into Anatolia. The Middle Assyrian kingdom expanded under kings such as Tiglath-Pileser I. After a period of retreat, the Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–609 BC) conquered Babylonia, the Levant, Egypt and parts of Anatolia and Iran under rulers including Ashurnasirpal II, Shalmaneser III, Tiglath-Pileser III, Sargon II, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon and Ashurbanipal.

The empire collapsed rapidly at the end of the seventh century BC under pressure from a Babylonian and Median coalition; Nineveh fell in 612 BC and the last Assyrian resistance ended by 609 BC.

Culture

The Assyrians spoke a dialect of the Akkadian language written in cuneiform, later increasingly supplemented by Aramaic. Assyrian palaces were decorated with monumental stone reliefs narrating royal campaigns and hunts. Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh preserved thousands of clay tablets and is a principal source for Mesopotamian literature. Assyria was famous — and feared — for its professional army, siege engineering and policy of mass deportation of conquered peoples.
