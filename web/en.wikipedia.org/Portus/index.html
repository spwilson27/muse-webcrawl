Portus

Portus was a large artificial harbour of ancient Rome, situated on the Tyrrhenian coast northwest of Ostia, at the mouth of the Tiber. Built to remedy the silting and exposure of the older river port at Ostia, it became the principal maritime gateway supplying the city of Rome with grain and other goods from across the Mediterranean.

History

The emperor Claudius began construction of the first harbour in AD 42, creating a large basin protected by two curving moles, with a lighthouse modelled on the Pharos of Alexandria built upon a sunken, concrete-filled ship. Although Tacitus records that a storm destroyed some 200 ships within the harbour in AD 62, the port greatly improved the security of Rome's grain supply.

Around AD 112 the emperor Trajan expanded Portus with a second, inner harbour: a hexagonal basin roughly 358 metres across on each side, excavated in the land behind Claudius's basin and connected by canals to the Tiber. The hexagon's sides were lined with warehouses (horrea), and its geometric form reduced silting and provided quays on every side. The complex was joined to the river port by the Fossa Traiana (now the Fiumicino canal).

Later history and archaeology

Portus flourished through the imperial period; Constantine raised it to city status as Civitas Flavia Constantiniana Portuensis. It declined with the fall of the Western Empire and the silting of the coast, which today leaves the site several kilometres inland, near modern Fiumicino and Rome's Leonardo da Vinci airport. The Trajanic hexagon survives as a lake-like basin and is the centrepiece of an archaeological park; aerial photography and geophysical survey have revealed the extensive warehouses, canals, and necropolises of the port town.
