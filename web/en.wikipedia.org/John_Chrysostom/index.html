John Chrysostom

Born: c. 349, Antioch, Syria
Died: 14 September 407, Comana, Pontus
Venerated in: Eastern Orthodox, Eastern Catholic, Roman Catholic, Anglican Communion
Feast: 13 November (Eastern); 13 September (Western)
Occupation: Archbishop of Constantinople; theologian

John Chrysostom (Greek: Ἰωάννης ὁ Χρυσόστομος; c. 349 – 407) was an important Early Church Father who served as archbishop of Constantinople. He is known for his preaching and public speaking, his denunciation of abuse of authority by both ecclesiastical and political leaders, the Divine Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom, and his ascetic sensibilities. The epithet Chrysostom, meaning "golden-mouthed" in Greek, refers to his celebrated eloquence.

Early life

John was born in Antioch (in modern day Turkey) in around 349. Different scholars describe his mother as either a Christian or a pagan; his father Secundus was a high-ranking military officer who died soon after his son's birth, and John was raised by his mother Anthusa. He was baptised in 368 or 373 and spent several years as a hermit in the mountains near Antioch, damaging his health through severe asceticism.

Preaching in Antioch

Ordained a priest in 386, John became famous for his sermons in Antioch's Golden Church, including the series of homilies known as On the Statues, preached during the crisis of 387 when the city rioted against imperial taxation. His homilies against Jews and Judaizing Christians remain controversial but reflect the fierce rhetoric of the period.

Archbishop and exile

In 398 John was secretly arrested and brought to Constantinople, where he was made archbishop. His reforms of the clergy and attacks on corruption made him enemies at court, particularly the empress Aelia Eudoxia. At the Synod of the Oak in 403 he was deposed and exiled; recalled briefly, he was exiled again in 404 to Cucusus in Armenia and finally toward Pitiunt, dying at Comana in Pontus on 14 September 407.

Legacy

John is honoured as a saint in the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Catholic, Anglican and Lutheran churches. The Divine Liturgy named after him is celebrated on most Sundays in the Byzantine Rite. His extensive writings — homilies, treatises and letters — are among the largest surviving corpora of any early Christian author.
