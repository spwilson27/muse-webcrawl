Workhouse

A workhouse was an institution in the British Isles where those unable to support themselves were offered accommodation and employment in return for labour. Workhouses formed the backbone of poor relief under the old Poor Law and, more systematically, under the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 in England and Wales, with similar systems in Ireland and Scotland.

The 1834 system

The New Poor Law grouped parishes into unions, each building a union workhouse administered by a board of guardians. Conditions inside were deliberately harsh — governed by the principle of "less eligibility", under which the pauper's situation was to be worse than that of the lowest-paid independent labourer — so as to deter all but the genuinely destitute. Inmates were classified and segregated by sex and age, families were split up, uniforms were worn, and labour included stone-breaking, oakum-picking and bone-crushing. The Andover workhouse scandal of 1845, in which starving inmates were found eating the marrow from bones they were meant to crush, led to tighter central control.

Decline

From the late 19th century, workhouse infirmaries became an important source of medical care for the poor, and other functions were gradually transferred to specialised institutions. The workhouse system was formally abolished in 1930 in England and Wales, when its buildings passed to local authorities as hospitals and public assistance institutions; many later became National Health Service hospitals. The workhouse remains a powerful symbol of Victorian attitudes to poverty, familiar from Charles Dickens's Oliver Twist.
