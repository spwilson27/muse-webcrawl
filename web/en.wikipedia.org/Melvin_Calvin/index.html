Melvin Ellis Calvin (April 8, 1911 – January 8, 1997) was an American biochemist known for discovering the Calvin cycle (also called the Calvin–Benson–Bassham cycle), along with Andrew Benson and James Bassham, for which he was awarded the 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Working at the University of California, Berkeley, Calvin and his team used the carbon-14 isotope as a tracer, along with paper chromatography, to reveal the complete route that carbon travels through a plant during photosynthesis, starting from its absorption as atmospheric carbon dioxide to its conversion into carbohydrates and other organic compounds.

Career

Calvin studied at the Michigan College of Mining and Technology and earned his PhD at the University of Minnesota in 1935. After postdoctoral work with Michael Polanyi in Manchester, he joined Berkeley in 1937 at the invitation of G. N. Lewis and spent his entire career there, founding and directing the Laboratory of Chemical Biodynamics. Besides photosynthesis, he worked on organic geochemistry, chemical evolution and the origins of life, radiation chemistry, and solar-energy conversion, including "artificial photosynthesis". He served on the science advisory committee to President John F. Kennedy and received the U.S. National Medal of Science in 1989.
