Reverse engineering

Reverse engineering (also called back engineering) is the process of analysing a product, system or piece of software in order to understand how it works, usually with the aim of duplicating, repairing, improving or interoperating with it. The process examines the finished artefact rather than its original design documentation.

Applications

  • Software: analysing compiled programs with disassemblers and decompilers to understand algorithms, file formats and protocols, or to find security vulnerabilities; also used by malware analysts and, conversely, by malware authors.
  • Hardware and machinery: measuring and digitising a physical part (e.g. with 3D scanning) to reproduce it when drawings are unavailable, common in maintaining legacy aircraft and industrial equipment.
  • Electronics: delayering integrated circuits and analysing circuit boards to understand their design.
  • Interoperability: "clean room" reverse engineering, in which one team documents a system and another reimplements it, used historically to produce compatible PC BIOSes and file-format converters.

Legal issues

The legality of reverse engineering varies by jurisdiction and purpose. It may be restricted by patent, copyright, anti-circumvention laws (such as the US DMCA), licence agreements, and trade-secret law, while many jurisdictions allow it for interoperability, security research, or repair.
