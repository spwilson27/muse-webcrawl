Euhemerism

Euhemerism is an approach to the interpretation of mythology in which mythological narratives are presumed to originate from real historical events or personages. It takes its name from the Greek mythographer Euhemerus (late 4th century BC), whose fictional travelogue Sacred History claimed the Olympian gods had been great kings and benefactors later deified by grateful subjects.

Ancient and medieval use

Euhemeristic readings were taken up by Hellenistic historians and by early Christian apologists such as Lactantius and Eusebius, who used them to demote pagan gods to mortal status. Medieval chroniclers euhemerised Norse and classical gods alike; Snorri Sturluson's Prose Edda prologue presents the Æsir as migrating human kings from Asia.

Modern approaches

Enlightenment and 19th-century scholars applied similar reasoning to heroes and folk tales. Modern mythography is more cautious: while some myths may preserve historical kernels — cities, wars or rulers — wholesale euhemerism is criticised for ignoring ritual, symbolic and structural functions of myth. The term also describes rationalising readings of scripture and legend in general.
