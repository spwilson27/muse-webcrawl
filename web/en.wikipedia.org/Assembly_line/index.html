Assembly line

An assembly line is a manufacturing process in which parts are added as a semi-finished product moves from workstation to workstation, where parts are added in sequence until the final assembly is produced. By mechanically moving the parts to the assembly work and moving the semi-finished product between stations, a finished product can be assembled faster and with less labour than by having workers carry parts to a stationary piece for assembly.

History

The principles of interchangeable parts and division of labour predate the modern line: Venetian shipyards, the Portsmouth Block Mills, and the armories of the early United States all used sequential production. Ransom Olds used a stationary assembly line in automobile production from 1901. Henry Ford's engineers introduced the moving assembly line at the Highland Park plant in 1913, dramatically cutting the time to build a Model T chassis from over 12 hours to under 2 hours, enabling drastic price reductions.

Impact

Assembly lines became the backbone of mass production and "Fordism", transforming industry and society: they enabled cheap consumer goods but were also criticised for repetitive, deskilled work — satirised in Chaplin's Modern Times. Modern lines increasingly use robots and flexible manufacturing systems, and concepts such as Toyota's just-in-time production rethought the balance between flow and inventory.
