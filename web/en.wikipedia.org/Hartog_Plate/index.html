Hartog Plate

The Hartog Plate is a pewter plate left at Cape Inscription on Dirk Hartog Island in Shark Bay, Western Australia, by Dutch explorer Dirk Hartog and his crew on 25 October 1616. It records the first known landing by Europeans on the western coast of Australia and is the oldest surviving European artefact associated with Australia.

History

Hartog, captain of the Dutch East India Company ship Eendracht, arrived at the island after being blown off course while sailing from the Netherlands to Batavia using the Brouwer Route. Before departing, his crew nailed a flattened pewter dish, inscribed with a record of their visit, to a post at what is now called Cape Inscription.

In 1697 the Dutch captain Willem de Vlamingh landed at the same spot, found the plate weathered at the foot of the post, and replaced it with a new plate of his own that copied Hartog's inscription and added a record of his own voyage; he took Hartog's original plate back to Amsterdam, where it is now held by the Rijksmuseum. In 1801 the French explorer Louis de Freycinet, serving under Nicolas Baudin, found Vlamingh's plate and took it to France; it was returned to Australia in 1947 and is now displayed at the Western Australian Museum's Shipwrecks Gallery in Fremantle.

Inscription

The plate's Dutch inscription records the arrival of the Eendracht of Amsterdam on 25 October 1616 under commander Dirk Hartog of Amsterdam, naming senior merchant Gilles Michels of Liège and, under the skipper, the under-steersman Jan Steyn of Amsterdam, and noting the ship's departure for Bantam on 27 October. The plate's survival makes Hartog's landfall the earliest documented European contact with Western Australia, predating James Cook's east-coast voyage by more than 150 years. In 2016 Australia commemorated the 400th anniversary of the landing with exhibitions and the renaming ceremonies on Dirk Hartog Island.
