Living Constitution

The Living Constitution, or judicial pragmatism, is the claim that the United States Constitution — and to some extent other constitutions — holds a dynamic meaning that evolves and adapts to new circumstances even without formal amendment. The competing view, originalism, holds that the Constitution has a fixed meaning as understood at the time of its enactment.

Origins

The idea grew in the early 20th century. Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. wrote in Missouri v. Holland (1920) that the Constitution's words called into life "a being the development of which could not have been foreseen completely by the most gifted of its begetters". Howard Lee McBain's 1927 book popularised the phrase "living constitution".

Arguments

Supporters argue that constitutional language is often deliberately broad, that precedent allows doctrine to develop, and that a constitution intended to endure must be interpreted in light of contemporary society. Critics respond that living constitutionalism licenses judges to impose their own policy preferences and circumvents the amendment process, and that change should come through Article V amendments or democratic politics.

Use in jurisprudence

Living-constitution reasoning has been associated with the expansion of federal power under the Commerce Clause, substantive due process, and the incorporation of the Bill of Rights against the states, while originalist critiques gained prominence from the 1980s onward.
