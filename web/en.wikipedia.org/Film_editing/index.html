Film editing

Film editing is both a creative and a technical part of the post-production process of filmmaking. The term is derived from the traditional process of working with film which increasingly involves the use of digital technology. When it is being used in contemporary times, film editing may also be referred to as the process of selecting and combining shots into sequences to create a finished motion picture.

The film editor works with the raw footage, selecting shots and combining them into sequences which create a finished motion picture. Film editing is described as an art or skill, the only art that is unique to cinema, separating filmmaking from other art forms that preceded it, although there are close parallels to the editing process in other art forms such as poetry and novel writing. Film editing is an extremely important part of the aesthetic and storytelling language of film, and it is often referred to as the "invisible art" because when it is well-practised, the viewer can become so engaged that they are unaware of the editor's work.

History

Early films were short films that were one long, static shot. Motion was all that was required to amuse an audience, so the first films simply showed activity such as traffic moving along a city street. There was no story and no editing. Each film ran as long as there was film in the camera.

The use of film editing to create continuity, involving action moving from one sequence into another, is generally attributed to British film pioneer Robert W. Paul. George Albert Smith was another British pioneer to have the first films featuring sequences edited together. The Americans Porter and Griffith further developed continuity editing in the first decades of the twentieth century.

Until the advent of digital non-linear editing systems in the early 1990s, film editing was done by physically cutting and taping together pieces of film. The editor would work with a flatbed editing table such as a Steenbeck or Moviola, using a synchronizer to keep picture and sound in alignment.

Techniques

Continuity editing

Continuity editing is the predominant style of film editing and video editing in the post-production process of filmmaking of narrative television and film. The purpose of continuity editing is to smooth over the inherent discontinuity of the editing process and to establish a logical coherence between shots, so that the story proceeds smoothly and the viewer is not distracted by edits.

Montage

Montage is a technique in film editing in which a series of short shots are edited into a sequence to condense space, time, and information. The term has been used in various contexts. Soviet filmmakers such as Sergei Eisenstein developed montage theory, in which the juxtaposition of shots creates meaning not present in any single shot alone. The Kuleshov effect demonstrated that viewers derive more meaning from the interaction of two sequential shots than from a single shot in isolation.

Transitions

Common transitions include the cut, the dissolve, the fade in and fade out, and the wipe. The cut, an instantaneous change from one shot to another, is by far the most common. Dissolves suggest a passage of time or a change of location, while fades are often used at the beginning and end of sequences.

The editor's role

The editor typically works closely with the director, who often has final say over the cut. During production the editor may assemble a rough cut from dailies; after principal photography wraps, the editor produces a more refined editor's cut, followed by the director's cut and, eventually, the final cut approved for release. Notable editors such as Thelma Schoonmaker, Walter Murch, and Dede Allen have been credited with shaping the style of the films they cut.

Technology

Modern editing is performed on non-linear editing systems (NLEs) such as Avid Media Composer, Adobe Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve, and Final Cut Pro. These systems allow random access to any frame, instant rearrangement of shots, and integration of visual effects, colour grading, and sound design within a digital workflow.
