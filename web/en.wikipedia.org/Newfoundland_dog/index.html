The Newfoundland is a large breed of working dog originating from the island of Newfoundland in Canada. Renowned for its strength, swimming ability, and gentle temperament, it was traditionally used by fishermen to haul nets, carry lines between ships, and rescue people from the water. Newfoundlands have a thick, water-resistant double coat, webbed feet, and a rudder-like tail that suit them to work in cold seas.

History

The breed developed on Newfoundland from dogs kept by Indigenous peoples and European settlers and fishermen. By the 19th century, Newfoundlands were famous worldwide for dramatic water rescues; one is said to have saved survivors of a shipwreck by carrying a line ashore, and a Newfoundland accompanied the Lewis and Clark Expedition. Export restrictions and wartime declines in Britain twice nearly extinguished the breed, and it was rebuilt from North American stock.

Characteristics

Newfoundlands are among the largest dog breeds, with males commonly weighing 60–70 kilograms. Despite their size, they are noted for a sweet, patient disposition, and are often recommended as family dogs and companions for children — the fictional dog Nana in Peter Pan was a Newfoundland. They are still used in water-rescue work and training in several countries and are strong, willing draft animals. Their size and thick coat make them prone to overheating in warm climates, and like other giant breeds they have a relatively short lifespan, typically around 9–10 years, with orthopedic and cardiac conditions being breed concerns.
