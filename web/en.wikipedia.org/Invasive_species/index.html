An invasive species is an introduced species that harms its new environment. Although often used loosely, the term generally denotes a species that is not native to a specific location, has a tendency to spread to a degree believed to cause damage to the environment, human economy or human health. The study of invasive species, known as invasion biology, expanded rapidly after Charles Elton's 1958 book The Ecology of Invasions by Animals and Plants.

Pathways

Species spread beyond their native ranges mainly through human activity: shipping (ballast water, hull fouling), the pet and horticultural trades, aquaculture and agriculture, and transport infrastructure such as canals. Some introductions were deliberate — the cane toad in Australia for pest control, or kudzu in the United States for erosion control — and later became notorious invaders.

Impacts

Invasive species can outcompete, prey upon or hybridise with native species, alter habitats and nutrient cycles, and spread disease. They are implicated in a large share of recorded species extinctions, especially on islands, where the brown tree snake devastated Guam's birds and introduced rats and cats have destroyed seabird colonies worldwide. Economic costs arise in agriculture, forestry, fisheries, water infrastructure (for example zebra mussels clogging intakes) and public health; a 2021 estimate put the global cost at a minimum of US$1.3 trillion over 1970–2017.

Management

Strategies follow a hierarchy: prevention through biosecurity and border inspection; early detection and rapid response; eradication (feasible mainly on islands); biological control using natural enemies; and long-term containment or control. Legislation such as the US Lacey Act and the EU's Invasive Alien Species Regulation lists species whose import and trade are restricted.
