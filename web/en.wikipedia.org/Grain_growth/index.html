Grain growth

Coarsening of crystallites in a polycrystalline material

In materials science, grain growth is the increase in size of grains (crystallites) in a material at high temperature. It occurs when recovery and recrystallisation are complete and further reduction in the internal energy can only be achieved by reducing the total area of grain boundary. Grain growth follows the same principles of interface curvature-driven migration seen in other coarsening phenomena.

Mechanism

Grain boundaries migrate toward their centre of curvature, so small, highly curved grains shrink and disappear while larger grains grow. The driving force is the reduction of total grain boundary energy. The average grain diameter typically follows a parabolic law, D² − D₀² = kt, where the rate constant k increases strongly with temperature; exponents deviating from 2 are common when boundaries are pinned.

Normal and abnormal growth

In normal grain growth, the grain-size distribution remains self-similar and roughly log-normal. In abnormal grain growth (secondary recrystallisation), a few grains grow very large at the expense of a fine-grained matrix, often because particles or texture inhibit ordinary boundary motion. Abnormal growth is exploited in producing single-crystal-like turbine blade materials and grain-oriented electrical steel.

Practical importance

Because fine grains strengthen metals (the Hall–Petch relationship) and improve toughness, uncontrolled grain growth during annealing, welding, or high-temperature service usually degrades properties. Metallurgists control it through alloying additions and second-phase particles that exert a Zener pinning pressure on boundaries.
