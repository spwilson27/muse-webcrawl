Phrenology

Phrenology is a pseudoscience that involves the measurement of bumps on the skull to predict mental traits. Developed by the German physician Franz Joseph Gall in 1796, it was based on the concept that the brain is the organ of the mind, that it consists of localized faculties, and that the shape of the skull reflects their relative development. It was very popular in the 19th century, particularly from about 1810 to 1840, but is now discredited.

History

Gall proposed that character traits corresponded to distinct "organs" of the cerebral cortex, and that their size could be read from cranial contours. His collaborator Johann Spurzheim popularized the system in Britain and America, and popularizers such as the Fowler brothers turned phrenology into a mass commercial enterprise offering character readings, vocational advice, and self-improvement guidance.

Scientific assessment

Phrenology's specific claims — that skull shape mirrors brain faculties and that its catalogue of dozens of organs was correct — failed empirical testing and are rejected by neuroscience. Nonetheless, historians note that Gall's core idea of cerebral localization, the notion that different brain regions serve different functions, influenced later neurology, even though phrenology's method of reading the skull was invalid. Phrenology was also entangled with scientific racism, being used to rank human groups.
