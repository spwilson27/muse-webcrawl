The Sierra Gorda is an ecological region centred on the northern third of the Mexican state of Querétaro, extending into neighbouring Guanajuato, Hidalgo, and San Luis Potosí. It is part of the Sierra Madre Oriental and is characterised by rugged karst mountains, deep canyons, and an exceptional variety of ecosystems packed into a relatively small area, from desert scrub to cloud forest. In 1997 the Mexican government created the Sierra Gorda Biosphere Reserve, one of the most biodiverse protected areas in the country.

The region is also culturally significant for its five Franciscan missions, built in the 1750s–1760s under the direction of Junípero Serra among the Pame people; together they were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2003.

Geography and ecology

The Sierra Gorda's steep terrain, formed of uplifted and folded limestone, rises from about 300 metres in the river valleys to over 3,100 metres at Cerro el Zamorano. Rivers such as the Santa María, Extóraz, and Moctezuma have carved deep canyons, and the karst landscape is dotted with sinkholes and caves. Because moisture-laden winds from the Gulf of Mexico strike the mountains, the eastern slopes receive heavy rain while sheltered valleys are semi-arid. This compression of climates produces an unusual mosaic: tropical deciduous forest, cloud forest, pine–oak forest, and xeric scrub, harbouring jaguars, pumas, military macaws, and hundreds of bird and butterfly species.

History

Pre-Hispanic peoples including the Pame and Jonaz inhabited the region, and mining towns such as those of the Tecozautla and Escanela districts supplied silver and other minerals in the colonial era. Franciscan evangelisation in the 18th century culminated in the five mission churches — Santiago de Jalpan, San Miguel Concá, San Francisco del Valle de Tilaco, Santa María del Agua de Landa, and Nuestra Señora de la Luz de Tancoyol — famous for their exuberant mestizo Baroque facades carved with Indigenous motifs. The region remained remote and poor through much of the 20th century, with high rates of out-migration.

Conservation

The biosphere reserve is administered with strong community participation, notably through the Grupo Ecológico Sierra Gorda, founded by local conservationists including Martha Isabel "Pati" Ruiz Corzo. Its model of paying local landowners for ecosystem services — carbon sequestration, watershed protection, and biodiversity — has won international recognition. Threats include illegal logging, poaching, and agricultural clearing, but the reserve is considered one of Mexico's most successful conservation projects. Ecotourism, waterfalls such as Chuveje and El Salto, and the mission route draw growing numbers of visitors.
