Red Cross (emblem)

The emblems of the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement are protective symbols prescribed by the Geneva Conventions and their Additional Protocols. The red cross on a white background — a reversal of the Swiss flag, adopted in 1864 in honour of Henry Dunant's homeland — signifies neutral protection for military medical services, humanitarian workers, and medical facilities and transports in armed conflict. Attacking persons or objects displaying the emblem is a war crime.

The emblems

Three emblems are recognised under the Geneva Conventions: the Red Cross, the Red Crescent (adopted by the Ottoman Empire during the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 and formally recognised in 1929, now used by most Muslim-majority national societies), and the Red Crystal (adopted by the Third Additional Protocol in 2005), a neutral frame within which national societies such as Israel's Magen David Adom — whose red Star of David is not itself a recognised protective emblem — may incorporate their own symbol for use abroad. Persia additionally used a Red Lion and Sun, recognised in 1929 but fallen out of use since 1980.

Protective and indicative use

The emblem has two uses: a protective use in armed conflict, where the large emblem marks persons and objects entitled to immunity from attack, and an indicative use in peacetime, in smaller form, identifying national Red Cross and Red Crescent societies. Misuse — including perfidious use to shield military operations — is prohibited by international humanitarian law, and unauthorised commercial use is restricted in most countries, which has famously affected video games depicting the red cross on health items.
