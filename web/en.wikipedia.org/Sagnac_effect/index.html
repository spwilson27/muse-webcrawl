The Sagnac effect, also called Sagnac interference, is a phenomenon encountered in interferometry: when two beams of light travel in opposite directions around a closed rotating loop, the beam travelling in the direction of rotation takes longer to complete the circuit than the counter-rotating beam. The resulting phase or time difference is proportional to the area enclosed by the loop and to the rotation rate, and it underlies practical gyroscopes.

History

French physicist Georges Sagnac demonstrated the effect in 1913 with a rotating ring interferometer, interpreting it as evidence for the luminiferous aether. Although an aether is not required — the effect follows from special relativity applied to a rotating (non-inertial) frame, and indeed the timing difference can be computed purely from the geometry of the rotating platform — the experiment has repeatedly featured in debates about relativity. Michelson, Gale and Pearson's large 1925 interferometer used the effect to measure Earth's rotation itself.

Explanation

In the laboratory (inertial) frame, both beams travel at speed c, but the detector moves while the light is in flight: the co-rotating beam must chase a receding detector and the counter-rotating beam meets an approaching one, giving a time difference Δt ≈ 4Aω/c² for loop area A and angular velocity ω. The effect is thus independent of the refractive index of the medium — distinguishing it from Fresnel drag — and is used to sense absolute rotation.

Applications

Ring laser gyroscopes and fibre-optic gyroscopes are standard in inertial navigation systems for aircraft, ships and spacecraft, and a Sagnac correction for Earth's rotation must be applied in GPS timing and in very-long-baseline interferometry.
