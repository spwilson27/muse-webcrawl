Other nameLunar cataclysm

The Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB), or lunar cataclysm, is a hypothesised event thought to have occurred approximately 4.1 to 3.8 billion years ago, during the Neoarchean era of Earth, in which a disproportionately large number of asteroids and comets collided with the early terrestrial planets of the Solar System — Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars and especially the Moon.

Evidence

The hypothesis rests on radiometric dating of impact melt rocks collected by the Apollo missions. The ages of melt samples from several large lunar basins — including Imbrium and Serenitatis — cluster around 3.9 billion years, younger than the Moon itself (about 4.5 billion years old), suggesting a spike in the impact rate rather than a smooth decline from accretion. Under the hypothesis, the great multi-ringed basins of the Moon were excavated in a relatively short interval.

Proposed mechanism: the Nice model

The leading explanation is the Nice model, in which the giant planets, originally in a compact configuration, migrated outward. When Jupiter and Saturn crossed a 2:1 orbital resonance, the resulting gravitational instability scattered the remaining planetesimal disk: huge numbers of asteroids and Kuiper-belt objects were thrown into the inner Solar System, producing the bombardment and also driving the capture of irregular moons and the reshaping of the Kuiper Belt. Alternative proposals invoke the destruction of a fifth terrestrial planet or the breakup of a large asteroid.

Implications for Earth and life

Because Earth has a larger gravitational cross-section, it would have received many times the Moon's bombardment; impacts of the period are inferred to have repeatedly vaporised oceans and resurfaced the planet, postponing the emergence of stable conditions for life. The event may also have delivered water and organic compounds, although much of Earth's water probably arrived earlier. The oldest evidence of life on Earth dates to roughly the end of the bombardment interval, a coincidence often noted in discussions of abiogenesis.

Scientific debate

The existence of a sharp "cataclysm" remains contested. Critics argue that the Apollo sampling sites were dominated by ejecta from the single Imbrium impact, biasing the age distribution, and that the data fit a monotonically declining bombardment with a modest uptick ("sawtooth" profile). Studies of lunar meteorites and of shocked zircons from the Earth's Jack Hills have been used to argue both for and against a spike. More recent work on giant-planet migration has suggested the instability may have occurred much earlier, within the first 100 million years of Solar System history, which would shift heavy bombardment into an era before the Apollo-recorded basins formed.
