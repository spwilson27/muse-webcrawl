Company town

A company town is a place or community in which much or all real estate, buildings, and services are owned by a single company that is also the main employer. Company towns are often planned communities built to house workers near a factory, mine, mill, or other industrial site, and the employer may operate the shops, schools, churches, and utilities as well.

History

Company towns proliferated during the Industrial Revolution in Britain, continental Europe, and the United States. Model industrial villages such as Saltaire and Bournville in England were built by paternalistic employers who provided housing and amenities. In the United States, coal towns in Appalachia, textile mill towns in New England and the South, and George Pullman's eponymous town of Pullman, Illinois (founded 1880), became famous — Pullman notoriously for the bitter strike of 1894 that followed wage cuts without corresponding rent reductions.

Characteristics and criticism

Employers in isolated company towns sometimes paid workers in scrip redeemable only at the company store, a practice that could trap workers in debt. Because the company controlled housing, dissent or union organizing could be punished by eviction. Critics saw company towns as instruments of social control; defenders pointed to housing quality and services often superior to those in surrounding areas.

Modern examples

Resource extraction in remote areas continues to produce company towns, including mining towns in Australia and Chile, oil camps, and Arctic settlements. Most older company towns have since been sold off to residents or municipalities, though their distinctive planned layouts and uniform housing remain visible.
