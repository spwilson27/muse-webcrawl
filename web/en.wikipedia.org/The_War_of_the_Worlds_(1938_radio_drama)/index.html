"The War of the Worlds" is an episode of the American radio drama anthology series The Mercury Theatre on the Air directed and narrated by Orson Welles. An adaptation of H. G. Wells's novel The War of the Worlds (1898), it aired as a Halloween special on October 30, 1938, over the CBS radio network. Written by Howard Koch, the script relocated the Martian invasion to contemporary New Jersey and presented much of the first two-thirds as a series of simulated news bulletins interrupting a program of dance music.

The broadcast

The episode begins with a standard introduction, after which a weather report gives way to music by "Ramón Raquello and his orchestra" from the Meridian Room of the Hotel Park Plaza. The music is repeatedly interrupted by news flashes: explosions on Mars, a meteorite — actually a cylindrical spacecraft — landing near Grover's Mill, New Jersey, and a correspondent's live report as a tentacled Martian emerges and incinerates the onlookers with a heat ray. The "news" escalates: Martian tripods devastate the countryside, poison black smoke spreads, communications fail, and the Secretary of the Interior addresses the nation. The final act shifts into a conventional dramatic format, following a survivor (played by Welles) in a ruined New York before the Martians succumb to terrestrial bacteria, as in Wells's novel.

Public reaction and the "panic"

Newspaper coverage the following day reported that some listeners believed the broadcast was a real news report of an invasion, citing jammed police switchboards, frightened crowds, and people fleeing their homes. The scale of the panic remains debated: later scholarship has argued the newspapers — then competing with radio for advertising revenue — exaggerated both the size of the audience (ratings suggested only about 2 percent of radio listeners tuned in) and the extent of hysteria. Nonetheless, the episode made Welles famous and became a landmark case study in mass media effects, notably in Hadley Cantril's 1940 study The Invasion from Mars.

Aftermath and legacy

Welles and CBS apologized; CBS promised the FCC it would not use simulated newscasts again. The broadcast's notoriety boosted Welles's career and helped secure his contract with RKO Pictures, leading to Citizen Kane (1941). The recording is preserved in the National Recording Registry, and the Grover's Mill site in West Windsor Township, New Jersey, is marked with a monument. The broadcast has been restaged and invoked in popular culture ever since, from the television film The Night That Panicked America (1975) to repeated Halloween re-enactments.
