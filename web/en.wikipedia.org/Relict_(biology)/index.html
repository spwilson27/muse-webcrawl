Relict (biology)

Relict
FieldBiogeography, ecology, evolutionary biology
DefinitionA population or taxon surviving in a restricted area after a formerly wider distribution
Related termLiving fossil
ExampleGinkgo biloba; coelacanths; Wollemi pine

In biogeography and ecology, a relict is a population or taxon that was once much more widespread but now survives only in a restricted area, often in a habitat that preserves conditions of an earlier era. Relict populations are remnants left behind as environmental change — such as the retreat of glaciers, the drying of a climate, or competition from newer species — shrinks the range of a once-successful group.

Types of relicts

Taxonomic relicts are the sole survivors of a formerly diverse evolutionary lineage — for example the ginkgo, the only living species of the order Ginkgoales, or the coelacanths, lobe-finned fishes once thought extinct. Biogeographic (relictual) populations are populations of a species isolated in a small part of its former range, such as alpine plants left on mountain summits as ice-age glaciers retreated. Relict habitats, such as raised bogs or ancient woodland fragments, preserve communities assembled under past climates.

Causes

Relicts commonly form through climate change. During the last glacial period, cold-adapted species ranged widely across lowlands; as the climate warmed, they persisted only in cool refuges (refugia) such as high mountains or deep lakes. Human activity can also create relicts by fragmenting habitats, and can threaten relict populations, whose small size and narrow environmental tolerance make them especially vulnerable to further change.

Significance

Relict species are of great scientific interest because they preserve ancient lineages and genetic diversity found nowhere else, offering insight into past ecosystems and evolution. They are often priorities for conservation, since their entire global population may occupy a single valley, cave or lake. The concept overlaps with, but is distinct from, the living fossil — a species resembling fossil relatives — and the Lazarus taxon, a group that reappears in the fossil record after an apparent absence.

Examples

  • Ginkgo biloba — sole survivor of a once global group of trees
  • Coelacanths (Latimeria) — rediscovered off South Africa in 1938
  • Wollemi pine (Wollemia nobilis) — known from fossils before living trees were found in Australia in 1994
  • Tuatara — sole survivor of the order Rhynchocephalia, in New Zealand
