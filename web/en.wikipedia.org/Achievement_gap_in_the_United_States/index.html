Achievement gap in the United States

The achievement gap in the United States refers to the observed, persistent disparity in measures of educational performance among subgroups of U.S. students, especially groups defined by socioeconomic status, race or ethnicity, and gender. The achievement gap can be observed through a variety of measures, including standardized test scores, grade point average, dropout rates, and college enrollment and completion rates.

Causes

Researchers have proposed many contributing factors, including differences in school funding, teacher quality, class size, early childhood education access, family income, parental involvement, health and nutrition, and summer learning loss. Students from lower-income families tend to enter school with smaller vocabularies and less exposure to books than their more affluent peers, a difference sometimes called the "word gap."

Trends

Long-term trend data from the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), often called the Nation's Report Card, showed that gaps between white and Black students narrowed substantially during the 1970s and 1980s before stagnating. Gaps in mathematics and reading scores between high- and low-income students have been described in some studies as widening over recent decades.

Efforts to close the gap

Policies aimed at reducing the achievement gap have included the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965, the No Child Left Behind Act of 2001, and the Every Student Succeeds Act of 2015, which required states to report achievement data disaggregated by student subgroup. Other interventions include expanded pre-kindergarten programs, tutoring, and extended learning time.
