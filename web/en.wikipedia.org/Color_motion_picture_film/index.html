Color motion picture film

Color motion picture film refers to the various technologies and film stocks developed to record and reproduce motion pictures in colour. From the earliest days of cinema, filmmakers sought ways to add colour to black-and-white images, first by hand-colouring and stencil methods, later by photographic processes that captured colour information directly.

Early processes

The British Kinemacolor system (1908) was the first commercially successful colour process, using alternating red and green filters. Technicolor, developed from 1916 onwards, evolved from a two-colour to a three-strip process in 1932, recording red, green and blue components on separate strips of film and recombining them by dye transfer. Technicolor was used for landmark films such as The Wizard of Oz and Gone with the Wind (both 1939).

Monopack stocks

Kodachrome (1935) and later Eastmancolor (introduced around 1950) placed the colour layers in a single strip of film, greatly simplifying cameras and processing. Eastmancolor and similar stocks made colour filmmaking cheap and routine by the 1960s, although the dyes in early prints proved prone to fading. Modern colour negative stocks remain in use alongside digital cinematography.
