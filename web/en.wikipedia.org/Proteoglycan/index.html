Proteoglycan

Proteoglycans are proteins that are heavily glycosylated. The basic proteoglycan unit consists of a "core protein" with one or more covalently attached glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains. The chains are long, linear carbohydrate polymers that are negatively charged under physiological conditions due to the occurrence of sulfate and uronic acid groups.

Types

Proteoglycans are categorized by their size and the nature of their GAG chains. Major classes include the small leucine-rich proteoglycans such as decorin, biglycan, fibromodulin and lumican; the large aggregating proteoglycan aggrecan, a major component of cartilage; heparan sulfate proteoglycans such as perlecan in basement membranes and syndecans and glypicans on cell surfaces; and chondroitin sulfate proteoglycans such as versican and neurocan.

Function

Proteoglycans occur in the connective tissue extracellular matrix, where their highly charged GAG chains attract water and cations, giving tissues such as cartilage their resistance to compression. They also bind and sequester growth factors and cytokines, modulate cell adhesion and migration, and act as co-receptors on cell surfaces. In cartilage, aggrecan forms enormous aggregates with hyaluronic acid, stabilized by link protein.

Synthesis and disorders

The core protein is synthesized in the rough endoplasmic reticulum, and GAG chains are added and modified in the Golgi apparatus. Genetic defects in proteoglycan synthesis or sulfation cause a range of skeletal dysplasias and connective tissue disorders; failure to degrade GAG chains underlies the mucopolysaccharidoses.
