In Greek mythology, Pasiphaë (Greek: Πασιφάη, "wide-shining") was a queen of Crete. The daughter of the sun god Helios and the Oceanid Perse, she was the sister of Circe and Aeëtes and the wife of King Minos. She is best known as the mother of the Minotaur.

Myth

Minos had prayed to Poseidon for a sign of his right to the Cretan throne, and the god sent a magnificent white bull from the sea — the Cretan Bull — which Minos was to sacrifice but instead kept. To punish him (in some versions because Minos, or Pasiphaë herself, had offended Aphrodite), Poseidon caused Pasiphaë to fall in love with the bull. The court craftsman Daedalus built her a hollow wooden cow covered in hide, in which she concealed herself, and from the union she conceived the Minotaur (Asterius), a creature with the body of a man and the head of a bull. Minos ordered Daedalus to build the Labyrinth to contain the monster.

Pasiphaë also appears in stories of her jealousy of Minos's lovers: she was said to have cursed him so that his affairs were fatal to the women involved, until Procris cured him. In Euripides' fragmentary Cretans, Pasiphaë defends herself in a famous speech, blaming the gods and Minos's own broken vow for her madness.

Worship and interpretation

Pasiphaë was worshipped as an oracular goddess at Thalamae in Laconia, where her sanctuary gave dream-oracles — according to Cicero and Plutarch, the Spartan ephors slept there to receive prophetic dreams — and she was sometimes identified with Ino or with a lunar deity, fitting her descent from Helios and her name, "she who shines for all". Scholars of Minoan religion have linked her myth, and the bull imagery of Knossos, to the central place of bulls in Bronze Age Cretan cult.
