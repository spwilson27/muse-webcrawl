The Scottish fiddle tradition is one of the most distinctive regional fiddle styles of the British Isles, characterised by repertoire including strathspeys, reels, jigs, marches and slow airs, and by characteristic bowing techniques such as the vigorous up-driven bow in strathspeys and the "Scotch snap" — a short accented note followed by a longer one. While physically identical to the classical violin, the Scottish fiddle is distinguished by its repertoire, ornamentation and rhythmic drive, which is directed primarily toward dancing.

Scottish fiddling has distinct regional styles. The Northeast (Aberdeenshire) tradition, often called the "Doric" style, is known for its elegant, technically demanding strathspeys, epitomised by J. Scott Skinner (1843–1927), the self-styled "Strathspey King". The Highland style is closely linked to the rhythms of the bagpipe repertoire, with pipe marches and strathspeys adapted to the fiddle and the use of drones and double-stopping to imitate pipe sonority. The West Coast and Hebridean tradition shares repertoire and style with Gaelic song. The Borders and the Southwest have their own tunes and a style influenced by both Scottish and Northumbrian playing. Shetland fiddling, influenced by Norwegian music and the Hardanger fiddle, features ringing strings and driving reel rhythms; its best-known modern exponent was Aly Bain.

History

The violin arrived in Scotland in the 17th century and by the early 18th century was being played in all levels of society. Niel Gow (1727–1807) of Dunkeld, fiddler to the Duke of Atholl, is the most celebrated figure of the tradition's first golden age; his compositions and the tune collections published by the Gow family helped codify the strathspey-reel repertoire. Robert Burns was himself an amateur fiddler, and many of his songs are set to fiddle tunes. In the 19th century the tradition spread across the Scottish diaspora to Canada — where Cape Breton fiddling preserves 18th-century Scottish style — the United States, Australia and New Zealand.

Technique and repertoire

Characteristic techniques include the Scotch snap, grace notes and "birls" borrowed from piping, double stops and drones on open strings, and, in the Northeast style, an intricate "staccato" bowing for strathspeys. Standard repertoire includes thousands of tunes preserved in printed collections from the 18th century onwards, including the collections of the Gows, Skinner's The Scottish Violinist (1900) and The Harp and Claymore, and the five volumes of The Scots Fiddle by J. Murdoch Henderson. Competitive fiddling, ceilidh bands and sessions sustain the tradition today.
