A galaxy is a system of stars, stellar remnants, interstellar gas, dust, and dark matter bound together by gravity. The word is derived from the Greek galaxias (γαλαξίας), literally 'milky', a reference to the Milky Way galaxy that contains the Solar System. Galaxies, averaging an estimated 100 million stars, range in size from dwarfs with less than a thousand stars to the largest galaxies known — supergiants with one hundred trillion stars, each orbiting its galaxy's centre of mass.

Most of the mass in a typical galaxy is in the form of dark matter, with only a few percent of that mass visible in the form of stars and nebulae. Supermassive black holes are a common feature at the centres of galaxies.

Classification

Galaxies are categorised according to their visual morphology, following the Hubble sequence devised by Edwin Hubble in 1926: elliptical galaxies (E0–E7) of smooth, featureless appearance; spiral galaxies (S) with arms wound around a central bulge, including barred spirals (SB) such as the Milky Way; lenticular galaxies (S0) intermediate between the two; and irregular galaxies, often distorted by gravitational interaction. Active galaxies, such as quasars and Seyfert galaxies, radiate enormous energy from accretion onto their central black holes.

Formation and evolution

Galaxies formed from density fluctuations in the early universe after the Big Bang, growing by gas accretion and mergers within dark matter halos. The first galaxies are now observed by the James Webb Space Telescope at redshifts beyond 13, within a few hundred million years of the Big Bang. Interactions and mergers — the Milky Way and Andromeda are expected to merge in about 4–5 billion years — reshape galaxies, trigger starbursts and feed central black holes.

Distribution

Galaxies are arranged in groups (such as the Local Group), clusters of hundreds or thousands, and superclusters threaded along cosmic filaments around vast voids. Deep-field surveys suggest the observable universe contains on the order of hundreds of billions to two trillion galaxies.

History of understanding

That the Milky Way is a band of stars was recognised by Democritus and Aristotle; Galileo resolved it into stars in 1610. Immanuel Kant and Thomas Wright speculated that "spiral nebulae" were distant island universes, a question settled in the 1920s by the Shapley–Curtis "Great Debate" and Edwin Hubble's Cepheid measurements of the Andromeda Galaxy, which proved it lies far outside the Milky Way.
