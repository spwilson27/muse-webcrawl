Heraclius

Reign: 5 October 610 – 11 February 641
Predecessor: Phocas
Successor: Constantine III and Heraclonas
Born: c. 575, Cappadocia
Died: 11 February 641, Constantinople

Heraclius (Greek: Ἡράκλειος, Herakleios; c. 575 – 11 February 641) was Byzantine emperor from 610 to 641. He rose to power by overthrowing Phocas, and his reign was dominated by the last great war with Sasanian Persia and, at its end, the first Muslim conquests. He is credited with transforming the empire's administration, making Greek its official language, and adopting the title basileus.

Rise to power

Heraclius was the son of Heraclius the Elder, exarch of Africa. In 608 the younger Heraclius sailed from Carthage to Constantinople, deposed the unpopular Phocas in 610, and had him executed. The empire he inherited was in chaos: the Persians under Khosrow II were overrunning the eastern provinces, and the Avars and Slavs pressed the Balkans.

War with Persia

By 619 Persia had conquered Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, carrying off the True Cross from Jerusalem, and Persian forces stood at Chalcedon, across from the capital. From 622 Heraclius personally led a series of brilliant counter-campaigns deep into Persian territory — among the first Byzantine emperors to command in the field since Theodosius I. The decisive victory came at the Battle of Nineveh in 627. Khosrow was overthrown and killed, and the ensuing peace restored the pre-war frontiers and the True Cross, which Heraclius solemnly returned to Jerusalem in 630.

Muslim conquests

The exhausted empire could not withstand the Arab Muslim armies that erupted from Arabia in the 630s. After the Byzantine defeat at the Battle of Yarmouk (636), Syria was lost, and by Heraclius's death Egypt was falling as well. These provinces were never recovered.

Legacy

Heraclius reformed the currency, reorganised the military (later tradition associated him with the theme system), and in 629 began styling himself basileus ("king/emperor") instead of augustus. His religious policy — promoting monothelitism to reconcile Chalcedonians and Monophysites — was later condemned. Historians regard his reign as a turning point between the late Roman and the medieval Byzantine world.
