Fibrinolysis

Plasmin breaks down the fibrin mesh of a clot

Fibrinolysis is a process that prevents blood clots from growing and becoming problematic, and that dissolves them once a vessel has healed. It is the body's natural mechanism for removing fibrin, the protein mesh of a clot, and involves the enzymatic breakdown of fibrin by plasmin.

Mechanism

Plasmin is generated from the inactive precursor plasminogen by activators including tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), released from endothelial cells, and urokinase. Plasmin cleaves fibrin into soluble fibrin degradation products, of which the D-dimer is measured clinically as a marker of clot breakdown. The system is tightly regulated by inhibitors such as α2-antiplasmin and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1).

Clinical relevance

Defective fibrinolysis contributes to thrombosis, while excessive fibrinolysis causes bleeding. Recombinant tPA (alteplase) is used as a thrombolytic drug to dissolve clots in heart attack and ischaemic stroke, and antifibrinolytic drugs such as tranexamic acid are used to reduce bleeding in surgery and trauma.
