Tagar culture

Tagar culture
Region: Minusinsk Basin, upper Yenisei, South Siberia
Period: c. 800–300 BC (Early Iron Age)
Preceded by: Karasuk culture
Followed by: Tashtyk culture

The Tagar culture was an archaeological culture of the Early Iron Age flourishing from about the 8th to the 3rd century BC in the Minusinsk Basin on the upper Yenisei River in southern Siberia, in the modern Russian republic of Khakassia and Krasnoyarsk Krai. It takes its name from Tagar Island in the Yenisei near Minusinsk.

The Tagar people were settled farmers and herders, unusual among the largely nomadic peoples of the Eurasian steppe at this period. They grew millet and wheat with bronze and later iron tools, raised cattle and sheep, and mined and smelted copper and bronze on a considerable scale.

Burial mounds

The culture is famous for its large kurgan burial mounds, enclosed by fences of tall standing stones with still taller corner slabs. The Great Salbyk kurgan, among the largest in Siberia, contained the remains of an elite ruler. Tagar bronzes — daggers, knives, mirrors and animal-style ornaments — show clear affinities with the Scythian art of the western steppe, while genetic studies indicate the Tagar population was largely of West Eurasian origin.

Decline

From about the 3rd century BC the Tagar culture gave way to the Tashtyk culture, amid the upheavals associated with the expansion of the Xiongnu.
