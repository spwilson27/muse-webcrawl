Ecovillage

An ecovillage is a traditional or intentional community that aims to become more socially, culturally, economically and ecologically sustainable. Ecovillages are built according to principles of permaculture and green building, and their residents typically strive for a low-impact, self-sufficient lifestyle that regenerates rather than depletes the surrounding environment.

The term gained wider currency in the 1990s, when organisations such as the Global Ecovillage Network (founded in 1995) began linking hundreds of such communities worldwide. Although they vary widely in size, culture and spiritual outlook, ecovillages share commitments to local food production, renewable energy, consensus-based or participatory governance, and reduced consumption.

History

The modern ecovillage movement has roots in the intentional communities of the 1960s and 1970s, in earlier experiments such as the kibbutz and ashram movements, and in the appropriate-technology and bioregional movements. Robert Gilman influentially defined an ecovillage in 1991 as a "human-scale, full-featured settlement in which human activities are harmlessly integrated into the natural world in a way that is supportive of healthy human development and can be successfully continued into the indefinite future."

Notable long-established examples include Findhorn in Scotland, Auroville in India, Crystal Waters in Australia, and Dancing Rabbit Ecovillage and Earthaven in the United States.

Principles

Ecovillages generally organise their design around ecological, social, cultural and economic dimensions of sustainability. Common practices include organic agriculture and permaculture gardens, natural building with materials such as cob, straw bale and rammed earth, composting toilets and constructed wetlands, solar and wind power, car-sharing, local currencies, and regular community meetings using consensus or sociocratic decision-making.

Criticism

Critics note that some ecovillages remain dependent on income from outside the community, that their per-capita footprint may not always be lower than that of dense urban living, and that small scale limits their direct global impact. Supporters respond that ecovillages function as living laboratories whose techniques diffuse into mainstream housing and planning.
