Color vision

Color vision is the ability of an organism to distinguish objects by the wavelengths (or frequencies) of the light they reflect, emit or transmit. It arises from the differential sensitivity of photoreceptor cells in the eye to different parts of the spectrum, with the nervous system comparing the signals to derive hue.

Mechanism

In humans and other primates, color vision is normally trichromatic: the retina contains three types of cone cells with photopigments (opsins) most sensitive to short (blue, ~420 nm), medium (green, ~530 nm) and long (red, ~560 nm) wavelengths. The brain compares the relative excitation of the cones — organised in opponent channels (red–green, blue–yellow, light–dark) as proposed by Ewald Hering — to construct color perception. Rods, which serve night vision, contribute little to color.

Variation across species

Many mammals are dichromats with only two cone types. Most birds, reptiles, fish and many insects are tetrachromats, and some butterflies and mantis shrimps have far more receptor types, though not necessarily finer color discrimination. Old World primates evolved trichromacy, probably for detecting ripe fruit and young leaves; New World monkeys show polymorphic color vision in which some females are trichromats and males dichromats.

Deficiency

Color vision deficiency, commonly called color blindness, affects about 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry. The most common forms are red–green deficiencies (protan and deutan types) caused by altered or missing cone opsin genes on the X chromosome; blue–yellow (tritan) defects and total color blindness (achromatopsia) are much rarer. The Ishihara test is widely used for screening.
