Emperor Taizong of Tang (28 January 598 – 10 July 649), personal name Li Shimin, was the second emperor of China's Tang dynasty, reigning from 626 to 649. He is traditionally regarded as one of the greatest emperors in Chinese history: under his Zhenguan era the dynasty was consolidated, its administration reformed, and its territory expanded, laying the foundation for more than a century of Tang prosperity and cultural achievement.

Rise to power

Li Shimin was the second son of Li Yuan, who founded the Tang dynasty in 618 as Emperor Gaozu. A brilliant cavalry commander in his teens, Shimin played the decisive role in the campaigns that unified China after the fall of Sui, defeating rival warlords at Hulao (621) and elsewhere. Rivalry with his elder brother, the crown prince Li Jiancheng, culminated in the Xuanwu Gate Incident of 2 July 626, in which Shimin ambushed and killed Jiancheng and another brother, Li Yuanji, at the palace gate in Chang'an. Two months later Gaozu abdicated in his favour.

Reign

Taizong surrounded himself with talented and outspoken officials — most famously the remonstrating minister Wei Zheng — and made a point of accepting criticism, a virtue celebrated by later Confucian historians. His administration codified law (the Tang Code tradition), rationalized the bureaucracy and examination system, equalized land distribution, and kept taxation light. In foreign affairs he destroyed the Eastern Turkic Khaganate in 630, taking the title of Heavenly Khagan, extended Tang influence into the Tarim Basin, and received embassies from across Asia; the pilgrim Xuanzang's journey to India also took place in this era. His campaigns against Goguryeo in 644–645, however, failed to conquer the Korean kingdom and strained imperial resources.

Taizong died in 649, possibly hastened by pills prescribed by an Indian alchemist, and was buried at Zhaoling, guarded by the celebrated stone reliefs of his six favourite chargers. His reign became the model of enlightened rule for subsequent Chinese historiography, and the Zhenguan Zhengyao ("Essentials of Government of the Zhenguan Era") compiled his dialogues with ministers as a handbook for emperors.
