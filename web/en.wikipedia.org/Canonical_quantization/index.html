Canonical quantization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, canonical quantization is a procedure for quantizing a classical theory, while attempting to preserve the formal structure, such as symmetries, of the classical theory to the greatest extent possible. Historically, this was not quite Werner Heisenberg's route to obtaining quantum mechanics, but Paul Dirac introduced it in his 1926 doctoral thesis as a method of "quantizing" classical systems.

Method

The procedure promotes classical dynamical variables, such as position and momentum, to operators acting on a Hilbert space, and replaces Poisson brackets with commutators: for canonical coordinates the relation {q, p} = 1 becomes [q̂, p̂] = iħ. The classical Hamiltonian then generates time evolution through the Schrödinger equation.

Field theory

Applied to fields, canonical quantization treats field operators as satisfying equal-time commutation (or anticommutation) relations, giving rise to quantum field theory. The alternative approach is the path integral formulation developed by Richard Feynman.
