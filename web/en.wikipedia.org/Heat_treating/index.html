Heat treating

Heat treating (or heat treatment) is a group of industrial, thermal and metalworking processes used to alter the physical, and sometimes chemical, properties of a material. The most common application is metallurgical: metals and alloys are heated and cooled under controlled conditions to obtain desired characteristics such as hardness, toughness, ductility or strength. Heat treatment techniques include annealing, quenching, tempering, case hardening, normalising and precipitation strengthening.

Principles

The effects arise from changes in the material's microstructure — the arrangement of its crystal phases and grains. In steels, heating above a critical temperature transforms the structure to austenite; the cooling rate then determines whether the result is soft pearlite (slow cooling), hard but brittle martensite (rapid quenching in water, oil or air), or intermediate structures. Diffusion of atoms such as carbon is temperature- and time-dependent, so the outcome depends on the whole thermal cycle.

Common processes

  • Annealing — heating and slow cooling to soften the metal, relieve internal stresses and improve machinability.
  • Quenching — rapid cooling to lock in a hard phase such as martensite in steel.
  • Tempering — reheating quenched steel to a moderate temperature to reduce brittleness while retaining much of its hardness.
  • Normalising — heating followed by air cooling to refine grain structure and homogenise properties.
  • Case hardening (e.g. carburising, nitriding) — diffusing carbon or nitrogen into the surface so the exterior is hard and wear-resistant while the core stays tough.
  • Precipitation hardening — used for aluminium, nickel and some stainless alloys, in which fine particles form during ageing to obstruct dislocation motion.

Applications

Heat treatment is essential in manufacturing tools, gears, springs, engine components, rails, structural steels and countless other products. Glass is also heat treated — tempered (toughened) glass is produced by controlled heating and rapid surface cooling. Furnaces used range from small batch ovens to continuous production lines, often with controlled atmospheres (vacuum, inert gas or salt baths) to prevent oxidation and decarburisation.
