The Satanic Verses

AuthorSalman Rushdie
Published26 September 1988
PublisherViking Press
GenreMagic realism

The Satanic Verses is the fourth novel by the Indian-born British-American writer Salman Rushdie, published in September 1988. Inspired in part by the life of the Islamic prophet Muhammad, the magic-realist novel follows two Indian actors, Gibreel Farishta and Saladin Chamcha, who miraculously survive the bombing of their airliner over the English Channel and are transformed — one seemingly angelic, the other devilish — as the narrative interweaves their London lives with dream-sequences retelling episodes from early Islamic history and other stories of faith and migration.

The title

The title refers to the "satanic verses," a small group of verses said by some early sources to have been briefly included in the Qur'an, praising three Meccan goddesses, before being abrogated as a deception of Satan. The novel's dream passages involving the prophet figure "Mahound" and the scribe Salman provoked the deepest offence among Muslim readers.

Controversy and fatwa

The novel was banned in India within weeks of publication and protests spread internationally. On 14 February 1989 Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, Supreme Leader of Iran, issued a fatwa calling for Rushdie's death and that of the book's publishers. Rushdie was forced into hiding under British police protection for nearly a decade. Translators and publishers were attacked: the Japanese translator Hitoshi Igarashi was murdered in 1991, the Italian translator Ettore Capriolo was stabbed and survived, and the Norwegian publisher William Nygaard was shot in 1993 and survived. Bookshops were firebombed, and riots in Pakistan and elsewhere killed protesters.

Iran's government distanced itself from the fatwa in 1998, though it was never formally revoked by Iran's supreme religious authority. On 12 August 2022, Rushdie was stabbed repeatedly on stage in Chautauqua, New York, losing an eye and the use of one hand; he recounted the attack in his memoir Knife (2024).

Literary reception

The novel won the 1988 Whitbread Award for Novel of the Year and was shortlisted for the Booker Prize. Critics have treated it as a major work about migration, identity and religious doubt, and the affair around it remains a touchstone in debates over blasphemy, free expression and artistic freedom.
