Faraday's law of induction

Discovered byMichael Faraday (1831)
Also formulated byJoseph Henry; Franz Ernst Neumann
FieldElectromagnetism

Faraday's law of induction (or simply Faraday's law) is a law of electromagnetism predicting how a magnetic field will interact with an electric circuit to produce an electromotive force (emf). This phenomenon, known as electromagnetic induction, is the fundamental operating principle of transformers, inductors, and many types of electric motors, generators and solenoids.

Statement

The law states that the induced emf in a closed circuit is equal to the negative of the rate of change of magnetic flux through the circuit: emf = −dΦ/dt. The negative sign, explained by Lenz's law, indicates that the induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it. For a coil of N turns, the total emf is N times the flux change through one turn.

History

Electromagnetic induction was discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831, and independently by Joseph Henry in 1832. Faraday's experiments showed that moving a magnet through a coil of wire induced a current. The law was later incorporated into the Maxwell–Faraday equation, one of Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism.

Applications

Faraday's law underlies electric generators, in which mechanical rotation changes the magnetic flux through coils to produce alternating current, as well as transformers, induction motors, and wireless charging.
