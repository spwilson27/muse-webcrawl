Impostor syndrome

People with impostor feelings doubt their competence despite clear evidence of achievement.
TypePsychological phenomenon
First described1978, by Pauline R. Clance and Suzanne A. Imes
Original term"Impostor phenomenon"
Key featuresSelf-doubt, fear of exposure as a fraud, discounting success
StatusNot a recognised mental disorder (absent from DSM-5)

Impostor syndrome, also known as the impostor phenomenon or impostorism, is a psychological experience in which a person doubts their skills, talents or accomplishments and harbours a persistent, internalised fear of being exposed as a fraud. Despite external evidence of their competence, those experiencing it attribute success to luck, timing or other people's overestimation of them, and fear that sooner or later they will be found out.

Origins of the concept

The psychologists Pauline Rose Clance and Suzanne Imes introduced the term in a 1978 paper, "The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women", based on clinical work with successful women who believed themselves unworthy of their positions. Clance later developed the Clance Impostor Phenomenon Scale to measure its intensity. Subsequent research found the feelings across genders and in many professions — students, academics, physicians, artists and executives among them.

Patterns of thinking

Clance described a characteristic cycle: faced with an achievement-related task, the person feels anxiety and either over-prepares or procrastinates and then scrambles; success brings brief relief but no lasting confidence, since it is credited to effort, luck or charm rather than ability. Common features include perfectionism, "superperson" striving, fear of failure and of success, and denial of one's own competence. Studies link impostor feelings with anxiety, depression and low self-esteem, though impostor syndrome is not itself a formal diagnosis.

Prevalence and context

Review studies suggest impostor feelings are common — many surveys report that a majority of people have experienced them at some point, with higher reported rates in some studies among women and members of underrepresented groups, though findings vary and some researchers caution that workplace environments, rather than individual pathology, may generate the feelings. The concept has become a staple of popular psychology and career advice, and the term "syndrome" is sometimes criticised for implying an illness where "phenomenon" — Clance and Imes' original wording — is more accurate.
