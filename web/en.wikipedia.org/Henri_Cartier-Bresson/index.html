Henri Cartier-Bresson

Henri Cartier-Bresson

DiedAugust 3, 2004, Montjustin, France

Known forStreet photography; "the decisive moment"; co-founding Magnum Photos

Henri Cartier-Bresson (1908–2004) was a French artist and humanist photographer considered a master of candid photography and an early user of 35 mm film. He pioneered the genre of street photography and viewed photography as capturing a "decisive moment" — the instant when form and meaning align. In 1947 he co-founded Magnum Photos with Robert Capa, David "Chim" Seymour, George Rodger and William Vandivert.

Early life and formation

Cartier-Bresson studied painting, including in the studio of Cubist André Lhote, and was drawn to Surrealism before taking up photography around 1930. Traveling in Africa and Mexico, he developed his unobtrusive style with a Leica rangefinder camera and a 50 mm lens. His first exhibitions in the early 1930s established his reputation. He also worked in film, assisting Jean Renoir.

War and photojournalism

Captured by German forces in 1940 while serving in the French Army's film unit, he escaped on his third attempt in 1943 and documented the liberation of Paris. After the war, Magnum's assignments took him around the world: he photographed Gandhi shortly before the assassination, the last stages of the Chinese Civil War, and life across the Soviet Union, India and Europe, producing defining photo essays for magazines.

The Decisive Moment

His landmark book Images à la sauvette (1952), published in English as The Decisive Moment with a cover by Matisse, codified his philosophy: compositions found spontaneously in the street, shot without cropping, staging or manipulation. His image Behind the Gare Saint-Lazare (1932), of a man leaping over a puddle, is among the most famous photographs of the twentieth century.

Later life

From the late 1960s Cartier-Bresson withdrew from photojournalism to return to drawing and painting, though his photographic legacy grew; the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson opened in Paris in 2003. He died in 2004, aged 95.
