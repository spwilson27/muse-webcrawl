Surplus value

Surplus value (German: Mehrwert) is a concept in Karl Marx's critique of political economy: the value produced by a worker in excess of the value of their own labour power, which the capitalist who employs them appropriates as profit. For Marx, surplus value is the source of profit, interest and rent in capitalist production, and its extraction constitutes the exploitation of labour.

Theory

According to Marx's labour theory of value, the value of a commodity is determined by the socially necessary labour time embodied in it. The worker sells labour power for a wage equal to the value of the goods needed to reproduce that labour power (subsistence and training). If that necessary labour time is four hours but the working day is eight hours, the remaining four hours are surplus labour, whose product is surplus value. The ratio of surplus labour to necessary labour is the rate of surplus value, or rate of exploitation.

Forms of increasing surplus value

Marx distinguished absolute surplus value, obtained by lengthening the working day, from relative surplus value, obtained by raising productivity so that necessary labour time falls while the working day stays the same. Competition drives capitalists to introduce machinery and reorganise production (the real subsumption of labour) to capture relative surplus value.

Criticism and legacy

The concept is central to Das Kapital (1867) and to Marxian economics, but has been criticised by mainstream economists, who generally reject the labour theory of value and explain profit through marginal productivity, time preference, risk and entrepreneurship. Critics also raise the transformation problem — the difficulty of deriving prices of production from values. Defenders argue the concept retains analytical value for understanding profit, class and accumulation.
