The Union of Lublin (Polish: Unia lubelska; Lithuanian: Liublino unija) was an act signed on 1 July 1569 in Lublin, Poland, that replaced the personal union between the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania with a real union — a single, jointly ruled state, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, which endured until 1795.

Background

Poland and Lithuania had been linked since the Union of Krewo (1385) and the marriage of Jogaila to Jadwiga, but the union was dynastic: the two states kept separate institutions, and Lithuanian magnates repeatedly asserted independence, most notably under Vytautas. By the 1560s pressures for a closer union mounted: the Livonian War (from 1558) exposed Lithuania's inability to resist Muscovy alone; the Lithuanian lesser nobility envied the political rights of their Polish counterparts; and King Sigismund II Augustus, the last Jagiellon, faced the extinction of his dynasty with no heir.

Negotiations and terms

The joint Sejm at Lublin opened in January 1569 amid deadlock: the Lithuanian delegation, led by Mikołaj "the Red" Radziwiłł, walked out in March to resist Polish terms. Sigismund responded by unilaterally incorporating the Lithuanian palatinates of Podlasie, Volhynia, Bracław, and Kyiv into the Polish Crown — a fait accompli that forced the Lithuanians back to negotiate on weakened footing. The act was sworn on 1 July 1569.

The Commonwealth created by the Union had a single, jointly elected monarch (who held the titles King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania), a common Sejm, and a common foreign policy and currency; Lithuania retained its own army, treasury, offices, and legal code (the Lithuanian Statute). The transfer of the Ukrainian lands to the Crown proved fateful, exposing Cossack and Ruthenian grievances that erupted in the Khmelnytsky Uprising of 1648.

Legacy

The Union created one of Europe's largest and most populous states, notable for its elective monarchy, powerful parliament of nobles, and relative religious tolerance under the Warsaw Confederation (1573). The original act of the Union is inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register, and Jan Matejko's painting of the signing is among Poland's most famous historical canvases.
