The Victoria and Albert Museum (often abbreviated as the V&A) in London is the world's largest museum of applied arts, decorative arts and design, with a permanent collection of over 2.27 million objects. Founded in 1852 and named after Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, it is a non-departmental public body sponsored by the Department for Culture, Media and Sport, and admission to its permanent galleries is free.

History

The museum grew out of the Great Exhibition of 1851, whose profits purchased land in South Kensington. Its first director, Henry Cole, opened the Museum of Manufactures at Marlborough House in 1852; the collection moved to South Kensington in 1857, where it became the South Kensington Museum. Queen Victoria laid the foundation stone of the present Cromwell Road building in 1899, when the institution was renamed the Victoria and Albert Museum. The grand facade and entrance by Aston Webb were completed in 1909.

Collections

The V&A covers 5,000 years of art from most of the world's cultures: ceramics, glass, textiles, costume, jewellery, furniture, metalwork, sculpture, prints, drawings and photographs. Highlights include the Raphael Cartoons (on loan from the Royal Collection), the world's oldest dated carpet, the Ardabil Carpet, the Great Bed of Ware, Tipu's Tiger, and the cast courts, whose Victorian plaster casts include a full-scale copy of Trajan's Column. The museum also houses the National Art Library and the Theatre and Performance galleries.

Branch institutions include the V&A Museum of Childhood (now Young V&A) in Bethnal Green, V&A Dundee in Scotland, and V&A East in Stratford, London.

Galleries and architecture

The museum occupies some 12.5 acres with 145 galleries. Its buildings evolved over more than a century, from the Victorian red-brick ranges and the ceramic-clad Henry Cole Wing to the 2017 Exhibition Road Quarter, with its porcelain-tiled courtyard designed by Amanda Levete Architects.
