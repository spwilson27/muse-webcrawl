Passive management

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Index funds have grown to hold a large share of equity fund assets

Passive management, also called passive investing, is an investment strategy that aims to replicate the performance of a market index or other benchmark rather than to beat it through security selection or market timing. It is the opposite of active management. The most common vehicles are index funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) that hold all, or a representative sample, of the securities in a target index in the same proportions.

Rationale

The intellectual basis of passive investing is the efficient-market hypothesis and the arithmetic of costs: because the average actively managed dollar must, before costs, earn the market return, and because active funds charge higher fees and incur more trading costs, the average active fund tends to underperform a comparable index after costs. Studies of fund performance, such as the SPIVA scorecards, regularly find that a majority of active funds underperform their benchmarks over long horizons, particularly in large-capitalisation equities.

History

The first index fund for individual investors was launched by John Bogle's Vanguard Group in 1976, tracking the S&P 500; initially derided as "Bogle's folly", it became the foundation of the largest fund complex in the world. The arrival of ETFs in the 1990s broadened passive investing to bonds, commodities and international markets. By the end of the 2010s, passively managed US equity funds held more assets than actively managed ones, and the largest asset managers — BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street — became the biggest shareholders of many public companies, raising debates about corporate governance and market concentration.

Criticism

Critics argue that passive flows distort prices because index funds buy regardless of valuation, that common ownership may soften competition, and that a market dominated by indexers would lack the price discovery on which indexing itself depends. Defenders respond that active traders still dominate trading volume, so prices remain informative, and that low-cost diversification is an unambiguous benefit to ordinary savers.
