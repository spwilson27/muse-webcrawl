Introgression

Introgression, also known as introgressive hybridization, in genetics is the transfer of genetic material from one species into the gene pool of another by the repeated backcrossing of an interspecific hybrid with one of its parent species. Introgression is a long-term process, requiring many hybrid generations before the genome of the recipient species is noticeably altered.

The process differs from simple hybridization, which produces an intermediate mixture in a single generation. Through backcrossing, particular genes or traits from the donor species can become permanently established in the recipient — a process called adaptive introgression when the transferred traits confer a fitness advantage.

Examples

Introgression is widespread in plants, where hybrid swarms and introgressed lineages are common, and increasingly documented in animals. Modern humans carry Neanderthal ancestry from interbreeding tens of thousands of years ago, and the high-altitude adaptation gene EPAS1 in Tibetans was inherited from Denisovans — a classic case of adaptive introgression. In agriculture, breeders use introgression to transfer disease resistance from wild relatives into crops such as wheat and tomato.

Introgression can also threaten rare species when their gene pools are swamped by hybridization with abundant relatives, a conservation concern known as genetic pollution.

Detection

Genomic methods such as the ABBA–BABA (D-statistic) test detect introgression by finding excess shared derived alleles between populations. Introgressed tracts can be mapped along chromosomes, revealing where foreign ancestry has persisted or been purged by selection, particularly in regions such as the human MHC.
