Mirror neuron

A mirror neuron is a neuron that fires both when an animal acts and when the animal observes the same action performed by another. Thus, the neuron "mirrors" the behaviour of the other, as though the observer were itself acting. Mirror neurons were first described in the early 1990s by a team of Italian researchers at the University of Parma, led by Giacomo Rizzolatti, who found neurons in the ventral premotor cortex (area F5) and inferior parietal lobule of macaque monkeys that responded both to the execution and the observation of grasping movements.

Discovery

The discovery was serendipitous: electrodes implanted to study motor control in monkeys recorded cells that also fired when the monkey watched an experimenter grasp an object. Subsequent single-cell recordings confirmed two broad classes — strictly congruent neurons, responding to the same action executed and observed, and broadly congruent neurons, responding to similar actions.

Proposed functions

Mirror neurons have been proposed to underlie action understanding, imitation, empathy, and aspects of language evolution (because the human homologue region includes Broca's area). The "broken mirror" hypothesis suggested that dysfunction of this system contributes to autism spectrum conditions; this hypothesis has been influential but remains contested, as later studies found largely normal mirror responses in many autistic people. Neuroscientist V. S. Ramachandran predicted mirror neurons would prove as significant for psychology as DNA was for biology, while critics such as Gregory Hickok argue that the evidence does not support claims that these neurons are the basis of action understanding.

Evidence in humans

Direct single-neuron evidence in humans is rare (recordings in epilepsy patients have identified mirror-like cells in medial frontal and temporal areas). Most human evidence is indirect, from fMRI, EEG mu-rhythm suppression, and TMS studies, leading some to speak of a "mirror neuron system" rather than mirror neurons proper.
