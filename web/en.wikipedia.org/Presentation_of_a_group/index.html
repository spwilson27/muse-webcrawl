In mathematics, a presentation is one method of specifying a group. A presentation of a group G comprises two sets: a set S of generators, so that every element of the group can be written as a product of powers of some of these generators, and a set R of relations among those generators. In formal terms, G ≃ ⟨S | R⟩ indicates that G is isomorphic to the quotient of the free group on S by the smallest normal subgroup generated by the elements of R.

Definition

Let S be a set and let FS be the free group on S. Let R be a set of words in the generators, so R is a subset of FS. The group presented by ⟨S | R⟩ is the quotient group FS / N, where N is the normal closure of R in FS. Intuitively, the presented group is the "freest" group in which the generators satisfy exactly the given relations and the group axioms.

Examples

Cyclic groups

The cyclic group of order n has presentation ⟨ a | an ⟩: a single generator whose n-th power is the identity. The infinite cyclic group is ⟨ a | ⟩, with no relations.

Dihedral groups

The dihedral group of order 2n (symmetries of a regular n-gon) has presentation ⟨ r, s | rn = s2 = 1, srs = r−1 ⟩, where r is a rotation and s a reflection.

Other examples

  • The symmetric group Sn: generators are the adjacent transpositions, with braid and commutation relations.
  • The braid group Bn: ⟨ σ1, …, σn−1 | σiσi+1σi = σi+1σiσi+1, plus far-commutation relations ⟩.
  • The quaternion group Q8 has presentation ⟨ x, y | x4 = 1, x2 = y2, y−1xy = x−1 ⟩.

Finitely presented groups

A group is finitely presented if it has a presentation with both finitely many generators and finitely many relations. Every finite group is finitely presented. The study of finite presentations is central to combinatorial and geometric group theory. Determining whether a given word in the generators equals the identity — the word problem — is undecidable in general, as shown by Pyotr Novikov and William Boone in the 1950s.

Tietze transformations

Any two presentations of the same group can be converted into one another by a sequence of elementary moves known as Tietze transformations: adding or removing a relation that follows from the others, or adding/removing a generator together with a relation defining it in terms of the existing generators. These moves allow one to simplify presentations, though finding a minimal one can be computationally infeasible.
