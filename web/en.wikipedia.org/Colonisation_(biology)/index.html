Colonisation (biology)

Colonisation (or colonization) in biology is the process by which a species spreads into and establishes itself in a new habitat or geographical area where it was previously absent. It is a central concept in ecology, biogeography, and invasion biology.

Mechanisms

Colonisation requires dispersal — by wind, water, animal vectors, or human transport — followed by successful establishment, which depends on the colonists surviving, reproducing, and overcoming barriers such as competition, predation, and unsuitable conditions. Long-distance dispersal events, though rare, explain the presence of species on remote islands, a phenomenon Charles Darwin studied in the Galápagos.

Ecological succession

After disturbances such as volcanic eruptions or glacier retreat, pioneer species colonise bare ground and modify it, enabling later species to follow in ecological succession. The eruption island of Surtsey and the recolonisation of Krakatoa are classic natural laboratories for studying the process.

Human-mediated colonisation

People have deliberately and accidentally moved thousands of species worldwide. Some introduced species become invasive, outcompeting natives and altering ecosystems, which makes colonisation dynamics important for conservation and biosecurity.
