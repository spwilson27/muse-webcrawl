The Tetragrammaton (from Greek Τετραγράμματον, "four letters") is the four-letter Hebrew theonym יהוה, transliterated YHWH, the personal name of God in the Hebrew Bible. It occurs nearly 6,800 times in the Masoretic Text, more often than any other divine name.

Pronunciation and avoidance

Because Hebrew script originally wrote consonants only, the original pronunciation is not preserved with certainty; most scholars reconstruct it as "Yahweh". Out of reverence, Jews ceased pronouncing the name aloud by the Second Temple period, substituting Adonai ("my Lord") in reading; when the Masoretes added vowel points, they wrote the vowels of Adonai with the consonants YHWH. Christian readers who combined the consonants with those vowels produced the hybrid form "Jehovah", attested in Latin works from the 13th century. In most English Bible translations the name is rendered "the LORD" in small capitals.

History and interpretation

The name is often connected with the verb "to be" and with God's self-designation in Exodus 3:14, "I am that I am" (Ehyeh asher ehyeh). In the Torah the Tetragrammaton is revealed to Moses and used throughout, though narratives also use Elohim. Rabbinic tradition forbids pronouncing the name except, historically, by the High Priest in the Temple on Yom Kippur; Samaritans preserve a different traditional pronunciation ("Yahwe" or "Yahaba"). Jehovah's Witnesses regard "Jehovah" as the proper name of God and use it prominently.
