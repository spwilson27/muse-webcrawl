Social influence

Social influence comprises the ways in which individuals adjust their behavior to meet the demands of a social environment. It takes many forms, including conformity, socialization, peer pressure, obedience, leadership, persuasion, and social facilitation. A person affected by social influence may come to agree or disagree with others or behave differently than they would on their own.

Types

Herbert Kelman identified three broad varieties: compliance (agreeing publicly while privately disagreeing, to gain reward or avoid punishment), identification (adopting behavior from someone admired), and internalization (accepting influence because its content is intrinsically rewarding or congruent with one's values). Morton Deutsch and Harold Gerard distinguished normative influence (to fit in) from informational influence (to be right).

Classic studies

Muzafer Sherif's autokinetic experiments, Solomon Asch's conformity experiments of the 1950s, and Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments of the 1960s demonstrated how powerfully group pressure and authority can shape judgment and behavior. Serge Moscovici later showed that consistent minorities can also influence majorities.

Factors

The size of the group, unanimity, cohesion, status, and prior commitment all moderate influence. Influence operates through reference groups, social norms, and, increasingly, online networks, where metrics of popularity can amplify conformity and polarization.
