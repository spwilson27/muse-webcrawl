Horsecar

A horsecar in service in the late nineteenth century

A horsecar, also called a horse-drawn tram, is an animal-powered streetcar: a passenger vehicle running on rails in city streets and drawn by one or more horses (occasionally mules). Horsecars were the first form of mass public transport in many cities and dominated urban transit from the 1830s until they were displaced by cable cars and electric trams in the 1890s and early 1900s.

History

The first street railway in the world, the Swansea and Mumbles Railway in Wales, began horse-drawn passenger service in 1807. In the United States the New York and Harlem Railroad introduced horse-drawn streetcars on rails in 1832, demonstrating that steel rails reduced friction enough for a single horse to pull a car carrying dozens of passengers. Systems spread rapidly through the cities of Europe and the Americas.

Operation and decline

Operating horsecars required enormous numbers of animals — a single car's horses might work only a few hours a day, so companies kept many teams per car, together with stables, fodder and veterinary care. Manure and carcasses posed persistent public-health problems. Electrification, demonstrated successfully in Richmond, Virginia, in 1888, cut costs dramatically; most systems converted by about 1910, though a few lines — such as the horse tram on the Isle of Man — survived much longer.
