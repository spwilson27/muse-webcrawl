The Sprawl trilogy defined the visual and thematic language of cyberpunk.

The Sprawl trilogy (also known as the Neuromancer, Cyberspace, or Matrix trilogy) is William Gibson's first trilogy of novels, composed of Neuromancer (1984), Count Zero (1986), and Mona Lisa Overdrive (1988). The novels are set in the same fictional future, a dystopian near-future world dominated by multinational corporations and a globally networked virtual reality dataspace known as cyberspace.

The trilogy takes its name from "the Sprawl", the Boston–Atlanta Metropolitan Axis (BAMA), a vast urban sprawl stretching along the East Coast of the United States. The novels are foundational works of the cyberpunk subgenre of science fiction, and Neuromancer became the first novel to win the Nebula Award, the Philip K. Dick Award, and the Hugo Award.

Setting

The Sprawl trilogy is set in a near future in which multinational corporations hold more power than nation-states, the environment has been badly degraded, and a global computer network — the matrix — links nearly all data systems. Characters include console cowboys who jack their nervous systems directly into cyberspace, razorgirls augmented with implanted weapons, and powerful artificial intelligences maneuvering toward autonomy.

Novels

Neuromancer (1984)

Neuromancer follows Henry Dorsett Case, a washed-up computer hacker hired by a mysterious employer for an impossible run against a powerful artificial intelligence. It introduced terms such as "cyberspace" and popularized the concept of a global virtual reality network years before the World Wide Web.

Count Zero (1986)

Count Zero weaves together three storylines: a young hobbyist hacker who encounters entities resembling voodoo loa in the matrix, a mercenary hired to extract a defecting scientist, and an art dealer searching for the maker of mysterious Cornell-like boxes.

Mona Lisa Overdrive (1988)

Mona Lisa Overdrive concludes the trilogy, drawing together threads from the earlier books, including the fate of the AIs liberated at the end of Neuromancer and the character Angie Mitchell, who can access cyberspace without hardware.

Influence

The trilogy shaped subsequent science fiction and real-world technology culture, influencing works such as The Matrix film series and contributing vocabulary — "cyberspace", "ICE", "microsofts" — to computing culture. Gibson followed it with the Bridge trilogy, set in a later period of the same broad imagined future.
