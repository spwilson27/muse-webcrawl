Protest art is creative work produced by activists and social movements as a means of communication, persuasion, and resistance. It includes posters, banners, murals, prints, songs, performance, and digital media intended to challenge authority, publicise grievances, and mobilise support. Because it often must be produced quickly and cheaply, protest art frequently relies on bold graphics, stencils, screen printing, and photocopying.

History

Dissident imagery has a long history, from Reformation-era woodcuts to the engravings of William Hogarth and the revolutionary prints of the nineteenth century. In the twentieth century, movements such as Dada reacted against the First World War, and Pablo Picasso's Guernica (1937), painted in response to the bombing of the Basque town of Guernica, became an enduring anti-war symbol. The Mexican muralists and the graphics of the Mexican Taller de Gráfica Popular influenced poster workshops worldwide.

During the 1960s and 1970s, poster collectives supported the civil rights movement, opposition to the Vietnam War, and the May 1968 uprising in France, where the Atelier Populaire produced anonymous silkscreen posters. Feminist, anti-apartheid, and AIDS-activist art followed, including the Silence = Death poster (1987) and the work of the Guerrilla Girls, founded in 1985 to expose sexism in the art world.

Contemporary practice

Street art and stencil graffiti, associated with artists such as Banksy, extended protest imagery into public space, while social media made protest graphics rapidly reproducible and remixable, as with Shepard Fairey's "Hope" poster of Barack Obama (2008). Protest art remains contested: it is variously celebrated as democratic expression, criticised as propaganda, and policed as vandalism or subversion.
