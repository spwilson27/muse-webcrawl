Oil well
PurposeExtraction of petroleum and natural gas from underground reservoirs
First modern wellDrake Well, Titusville, Pennsylvania, 1859
Key componentsCasing, production tubing, wellhead ("Christmas tree"), pump
TypesVertical, directional, horizontal; onshore and offshore

An oil well is a borehole drilled into the Earth to bring petroleum and natural gas from subsurface rock formations to the surface. Wells are created by drilling with a rotary rig, lining the hole with steel casing, and completing it so hydrocarbons flow in a controlled manner to a wellhead assembly of valves known as a Christmas tree.

History

Salt drillers in China bored brine wells that occasionally struck oil and gas as early as the fourth century AD, and oil was hand-dug in Persia and at Baku for centuries. The modern industry began with Edwin Drake's well at Titusville, Pennsylvania, in 1859, drilled to 21 metres and producing about 25 barrels a day. Rotary drilling, developed around 1900, enabled far deeper wells and the Spindletop gusher of 1901.

Drilling and completion

A drilling rig rotates a drill string with a cutting bit while circulating drilling mud that cools the bit, carries cuttings up the annulus and controls formation pressure. Casing strings are cemented in place for safety and isolation. In the completion stage the casing opposite the reservoir is perforated, and increasingly the well is hydraulically fractured or drilled horizontally to maximise contact with the pay zone. When reservoir pressure is insufficient, artificial lift — pumpjacks, gas lift or electric submersible pumps — brings oil to the surface.

Life cycle and abandonment

Wells pass through exploration, appraisal, development, production decline and finally plugging and abandonment, in which cement plugs seal the borehole. Poorly sealed "orphan wells" can leak methane and brine and are a growing environmental concern.
