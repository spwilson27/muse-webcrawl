Pulley

A pulley is a wheel on an axle or shaft that supports a flexible cable, rope, chain, or belt along its circumference, used to change the direction of a force, transmit rotation, or gain mechanical advantage in lifting loads. Pulleys are one of the classical simple machines described in antiquity.

Types

A fixed pulley has its axle attached to a support; it changes only the direction of the force, not its magnitude. A movable pulley has the load attached to its axle, so the effort is shared between the supporting segments of rope, giving a mechanical advantage of two. Combinations of fixed and movable pulleys mounted in housings called blocks form a block and tackle, whose mechanical advantage equals the number of rope segments supporting the moving block.

History

Pulleys were used in ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt for drawing water and raising building stones. The compound pulley system is traditionally attributed to Archimedes, who was said to have demonstrated its power by single-handedly moving a laden ship. Pulleys became essential in ships' rigging, cranes, mines, elevators, and industrial machinery.

Applications

Modern pulleys serve in belt drives for machinery, cranes and hoists, cable cars, gym equipment, sailboat rigging, and rescue and climbing systems. Toothed or V-groove sheaves engage belts precisely, while flat pulleys guide belts in line shafts.

Physics

In an ideal frictionless system, the tension is uniform throughout the rope, and the mechanical advantage is the ratio of supporting rope segments to the effort. In practice, bearing friction and rope stiffness reduce efficiency, though large-diameter sheaves minimize the losses.
