Pontic steppe

The Pontic steppe, also called the Pontic–Caspian steppe, is the western portion of the vast Eurasian Steppe, extending from the northern shores of the Black Sea (the ancient Pontus) eastward to the Caspian Sea. It covers parts of modern-day Romania, Moldova, Ukraine, and southern Russia as far as the Ural River, and forms part of the Palearctic temperate grasslands ecoregion.

Geography and ecology

The steppe is a broad plain of fertile chernozem ("black earth") soils covered with grasses, interrupted by river valleys of the Danube, Dniester, Dnieper, Don, and Volga. It receives moderate rainfall, decreasing to the east, and historically supported immense herds of wild horses, saiga antelope, and other grazing animals. Much of the steppe has been converted to agriculture, and Ukraine and southern Russia remain major wheat-producing regions.

History

The Pontic steppe is central to the Kurgan hypothesis, which identifies it as the homeland (Urheimat) of the Proto-Indo-Europeans, whose Yamnaya culture (c. 3300–2600 BC) is associated with the domestication of the horse, wheeled vehicles, and kurgan burial mounds. Thereafter the steppe was the highway of successive nomadic peoples: Cimmerians, Scythians, Sarmatians, Goths, Huns, Avars, Bulgars, Khazars, Pechenegs, Cumans, and the Mongols of the Golden Horde, followed by Nogai Tatars and Cossacks. Control of the steppe was contested for centuries between settled states — Kievan Rus', Poland–Lithuania, the Crimean Khanate, the Ottoman Empire, and Muscovy — until the Russian Empire annexed the region in the 18th century.
