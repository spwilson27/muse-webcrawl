The labor history of the United States describes the development of organized labor, wage workers and their movements from the colonial era to the present. For most of American history, unions struggled for legal recognition against hostile courts and employers; they achieved their greatest strength in the mid-20th century before a long decline in membership density.

Early organizing

Craft unions of journeymen appeared in the 1790s. The first national federation, the National Labor Union (1866), was followed by the Knights of Labor, which grew to some 700,000 members in the 1880s, and the American Federation of Labor (AFL, 1886) under Samuel Gompers, which focused on skilled crafts, wages and hours.

Conflict and repression

The era saw violent confrontations: the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, the Haymarket affair (1886), the Homestead strike (1892) and the Pullman strike (1894). Radical alternatives such as the Industrial Workers of the World (1905) sought to organize all workers, including the unskilled, immigrants and Black workers excluded by many craft unions.

The New Deal and the CIO

The National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act of 1935 guaranteed private-sector workers the right to organize and bargain collectively. The Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) unionized mass-production industries using tactics such as the Flint sit-down strike (1936–37). Union membership peaked at about a third of the non-agricultural workforce in the 1950s after the AFL and CIO merged in 1955.

Post-war limits and decline

The Taft–Hartley Act of 1947 restricted union tactics and allowed state "right-to-work" laws. From the 1970s, deindustrialization, globalization, employer opposition and legal changes drove private-sector union density down to roughly 6 percent by the 2020s, while public-sector unionism remained comparatively strong.
