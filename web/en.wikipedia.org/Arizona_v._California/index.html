Arizona v. California

Arizona v. California is a series of decisions of the Supreme Court of the United States, beginning with 373 U.S. 546 (1963), that apportioned the waters of the Colorado River among the states of the Lower Colorado River Basin — principally Arizona, California and Nevada — and five Native American tribes. It is one of the most consequential water-law cases in American history and was litigated, in various phases, for decades.

Background

The Colorado River Compact of 1922 divided the river's flow between the Upper and Lower Basins but did not apportion water among the Lower Basin states. California's rapid growth, the construction of Hoover Dam and the All-American Canal, and Arizona's desire to build the Central Arizona Project produced a prolonged conflict. Arizona refused to ratify the Compact until 1944 and filed suit in the Supreme Court under its original jurisdiction; the case was heard by a special master, Simon Rifkind, whose report formed the basis of the Court's decision.

Decision

Justice Hugo Black's majority opinion held that Congress, in the Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928, had itself apportioned the Lower Basin's mainstream water: 4.4 million acre-feet per year to California, 2.8 million to Arizona, and 300,000 to Nevada. The Court also recognized federal reserved water rights for five tribes along the river — the Chemehuevi, Cocopah, Colorado River Indian Tribes, Fort Mojave, and Quechan — quantified principally by practicably irrigable acreage, and charged those rights against the apportionment of the state in which each reservation lay. The United States was held to have reserved water for other federal lands as well.

Later proceedings

A decree followed in 1964 (376 U.S. 340), and the Court retained jurisdiction, issuing supplemental decrees in 1979, 1984 and 2000 addressing, among other things, tribal boundaries and the quantification of additional reserved rights. The decision cleared the legal way for the Central Arizona Project, authorized by Congress in 1968.
