Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive lung disease characterised by the formation of scar tissue (fibrosis) within the lungs for no known reason ("idiopathic"). The scarring thickens the walls of the alveoli, making gas exchange progressively harder, and the lungs become stiff and shrunken over time.

Signs and symptoms

The typical patient is between 50 and 70 years old and presents with gradually worsening exertional breathlessness and a persistent dry cough. Examination often reveals fine inspiratory "Velcro" crackles at the lung bases and, in many patients, clubbing of the fingers. The disease usually progresses over years; median survival after diagnosis has historically been about three to five years, though the course varies and some patients suffer sudden acute exacerbations.

Causes and diagnosis

By definition the cause is unknown, but risk factors include cigarette smoking, certain environmental dusts, and genetic factors — a variant in the promoter of the MUC5B gene is strongly associated with the disease, and rare familial forms involve telomerase genes. Diagnosis rests on the characteristic pattern of "usual interstitial pneumonia" (UIP) on high-resolution CT — subpleural, basal honeycombing with reticulation — after exclusion of other causes of interstitial lung disease, sometimes confirmed by lung biopsy and multidisciplinary discussion.

Treatment

Two antifibrotic drugs, pirfenidone and nintedanib, slow the decline in lung function but do not reverse established fibrosis. Management also includes oxygen therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, and treatment of comorbidities. Lung transplantation is the only intervention that improves survival substantially and is considered in suitable patients. Earlier treatments based on immunosuppression were abandoned after a major trial showed harm from the combination of prednisone, azathioprine, and N-acetylcysteine.
