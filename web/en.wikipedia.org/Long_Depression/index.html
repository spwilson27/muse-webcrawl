Long Depression

The Long Depression was a worldwide price and economic recession, beginning in 1873 and running either through March 1879 or 1896, depending on the metrics used. It was the most severe depression in Europe and the United States since the adoption of the gold standard by many countries. It began with the Panic of 1873, the collapse of the Vienna Stock Exchange and a wave of bank failures, and was known at the time as the "Great Depression" until the 1930s downturn assumed that name.

Course and character

The period was marked above all by persistent deflation: prices fell steadily for more than two decades in the industrialising countries, squeezing profits and raising the real burden of debts. Britain's mid-Victorian boom gave way to slower growth and intensified foreign competition from Germany and the United States, while in the United States the depression saw railroad overbuilding, bank failures, labour unrest including the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, and agrarian protest movements that demanded inflationary measures such as free silver.

Economic historians debate how "depressed" the era truly was: output continued to grow in many countries, and the hardship was concentrated in prices, profits and particular sectors rather than overall production. The deflation is commonly attributed to the spread of the international gold standard, which constrained money supplies, alongside rapid productivity growth in transport and manufacturing.

Legacy

The Long Depression reshaped politics, encouraging protectionism in much of Europe, colonial expansion, and populist and labour movements, and it entrenched the gold standard monetary order that lasted until 1914.
