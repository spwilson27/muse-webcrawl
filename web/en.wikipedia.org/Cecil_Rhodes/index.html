Cecil John Rhodes (1853–1902) was an English mining magnate and politician in southern Africa who served as Prime Minister of the Cape Colony from 1890 to 1896. An ardent imperialist, he founded the De Beers diamond company, which until recently controlled much of the world's diamond trade, and the British South Africa Company, whose conquests created the territories named after him — Rhodesia (now Zimbabwe and Zambia). He also established the Rhodes Scholarship at Oxford, and his will funded what became the Rhodes Trust.

Early life and diamonds

Rhodes, a vicar's son from Hertfordshire, was sent to Natal for his health in 1870 and soon joined the diamond rush at Kimberley. With his partner Charles Rudd, and financed by the Rothschilds, he bought up claims, amalgamating them into De Beers Consolidated Mines (1888), achieving a near-monopoly of world diamond production. Simultaneously he built a fortune in Transvaal gold through Gold Fields of South Africa. He studied intermittently at Oxford, graduating in 1881.

Politics and empire

Entering the Cape Parliament in 1881, Rhodes pursued his vision of British expansion "from the Cape to Cairo". In 1889 his British South Africa Company received a royal charter; its pioneer columns occupied Mashonaland and Matabeleland (after war with Lobengula's Ndebele kingdom in 1893), founding Salisbury (now Harare). As Cape premier he enacted measures that entrenched racial discrimination, including the Glen Grey Act (1894), a foundation of later segregation. His career collapsed after the disastrous Jameson Raid (1895–96), an unauthorised attempt to overthrow the Boer government of Paul Kruger, which forced his resignation and foreshadowed the Second Boer War.

Death and legacy

Rhodes died of heart failure in 1902, aged 48, and was buried at World's View in the Matobo Hills. His legacy is fiercely contested: the Rhodes Scholarship has educated generations of leaders, but Rhodes's role in colonisation and dispossession has made his statues targets of protest — most prominently the 2015 "Rhodes Must Fall" movement at the University of Cape Town, which removed his statue, and continuing debates at Oriel College, Oxford.
