Dilated cardiomyopathy

Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a condition in which the heart becomes enlarged and cannot pump blood effectively. The left ventricle (and often the right) stretches and thins, its chamber dilates, and systolic function declines, commonly leading to heart failure. Symptoms include breathlessness, fatigue, leg swelling and palpitations; the condition can also cause arrhythmias, blood clots and sudden cardiac death.

Causes

Many cases are genetic: variants in genes encoding sarcomeric and cytoskeletal proteins, most notably truncating variants in titin (TTN), account for a substantial share. Other causes include myocarditis (often viral), chronic alcohol misuse, certain chemotherapy agents such as doxorubicin, tachycardia-induced injury, peripartum cardiomyopathy, haemochromatosis and endocrine disorders. A significant proportion remain idiopathic after investigation.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on imaging, principally echocardiography showing ventricular dilatation with reduced ejection fraction; cardiac MRI refines tissue characterisation and scar detection. ECG, blood tests (including natriuretic peptides), coronary assessment to exclude ischaemic disease, and increasingly genetic testing with family screening are part of the work-up.

Treatment

Treatment follows heart-failure guidelines: ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and SGLT2 inhibitors, with diuretics for congestion. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators and cardiac resynchronisation therapy are used in selected patients to prevent sudden death and improve coordination. Advanced disease may require ventricular assist devices or heart transplantation. Alcohol abstinence and treating underlying causes can partially reverse dysfunction in some patients.
