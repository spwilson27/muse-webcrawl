Orthodoxy

Orthodoxy (from Greek: ὀρθοδοξία, orthodoxia, "correct opinion") is adherence to correct or accepted creeds, especially in religion. In the Christian context, the term refers to the condition of those doctrines affirmed by the early church and formulated in the ecumenical councils and creeds, in contrast to heterodoxy and heresy.

The word is also used as part of the proper names of several Christian traditions — most prominently the Eastern Orthodox Church and Oriental Orthodoxy — and in other religions, such as Orthodox Judaism, which denotes strict adherence to traditional Jewish law and practice.

Christianity

From the early centuries of Christianity, "orthodoxy" was used for belief conforming to the rule of faith as defined by the councils — beginning with the First Council of Nicaea (325) and its creed. The East–West Schism of 1054 separated the churches of the Greek East and Latin West; the eastern churches thereafter became known collectively as the Eastern Orthodox Church, which today comprises a communion of autocephalous churches including the Ecumenical Patriarchate of Constantinople and the Russian Orthodox Church, with some 220 million adherents worldwide. Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Armenian, Syriac, Ethiopian, Eritrean, and Malankara) separated after the Council of Chalcedon in 451 over Christological doctrine.

Judaism

Orthodox Judaism views the Torah, both written and oral, as divinely given and binding, and is characterized by strict observance of Jewish law (halakha). It encompasses a spectrum from Modern Orthodox to Haredi ("ultra-Orthodox") communities.

General usage

Outside religion, "orthodoxy" denotes generally accepted theory or practice in any field — for example, "economic orthodoxy" — with its opposite often termed "heterodoxy."
