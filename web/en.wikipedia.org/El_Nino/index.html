El Nino

"El Nino" (without the tilde) redirects here in this recreation. For the coupled ocean–atmosphere phenomenon, see also El Niño–Southern Oscillation.

El Niño (Spanish for "the Boy", a reference to the Christ child, because the warming often peaks near Christmas) is the warm phase of the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), a recurring climate pattern in the tropical Pacific Ocean. During an El Niño event, sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific become unusually warm, the trade winds weaken, and the normal pattern of rainfall and atmospheric circulation is disrupted worldwide.

Effects

El Niño events typically occur every two to seven years and last nine to twelve months. They bring drought to Indonesia, Australia and parts of South Asia, floods to the normally arid coasts of Peru and Ecuador, wetter winters to the southern United States, and drought in southern Africa and Amazonia. The collapse of the Peruvian anchoveta fishery during the 1972–73 event is a classic example of its economic impact. The opposite, cool phase is called La Niña.

Notable events

Particularly strong El Niño events occurred in 1982–83, 1997–98 and 2015–16; the 1997–98 event caused tens of thousands of deaths and tens of billions of dollars in weather-related damage worldwide. A further strong event developed in 2023–24. The name was originally used by fishermen off the coast of Peru for the warm current that appeared around Christmas; its connection to pressure oscillations across the Pacific — the Southern Oscillation, described by Gilbert Walker in the 1920s — was established by Jacob Bjerknes in the 1960s.
