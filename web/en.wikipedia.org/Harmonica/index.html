Harmonica

The harmonica, also known as a French harp or mouth organ, is a free reed wind instrument used worldwide in many musical genres, notably in blues, American folk music, classical music, jazz, country, and rock. It is played by using the lips and tongue to direct air into or out of holes along a mouthpiece, with reeds inside vibrating to produce sound.

Harmonica
Classification: Wind, free reed
Invented: early 19th century
Related instruments: Melodica, accordion, sheng

History

The harmonica was developed in Europe in the early nineteenth century, drawing on older free-reed instruments such as the Chinese sheng. Christian Friedrich Ludwig Buschmann is often credited with an early version around 1821. Mass production began with Matthias Hohner's company in Trossingen, Germany, from 1857, and the instrument spread rapidly to the United States.

Types

The most common type is the ten-hole diatonic harmonica, tuned to a single key and central to blues playing, where bending notes is a key technique. Chromatic harmonicas have a button-activated slide giving access to all semitones. Other types include tremolo, octave, and bass harmonicas, as well as chord harmonicas used in ensembles.

Technique

Players produce single notes and chords by positioning the mouth over the holes and can alter pitch by bending — changing the oral cavity shape — and by overblowing or overdrawing to reach notes outside the natural layout.
