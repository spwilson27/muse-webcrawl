Béchamel sauce

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

Béchamel sauce (French: sauce béchamel) is a white sauce made from a roux of butter and flour cooked in milk. It is one of the five "mother sauces" of classical French cuisine codified by Auguste Escoffier. Seasoned typically with salt, white pepper, and nutmeg, béchamel forms the base for many other sauces and dishes, including Mornay sauce (with cheese), lasagne, moussaka, and croque-monsieur.

History

The sauce is named after Louis de Béchameil, Marquis de Nointel (1630–1703), a financier and steward in the household of Louis XIV, though similar milk-and-roux sauces existed earlier in Italy (where it is called besciamella) and the recipe was likely refined by the king's cooks. Marie-Antoine Carême included it among his foundational sauces, and Escoffier's Le Guide Culinaire (1903) gave the classic proportions.

Preparation

Butter is melted and an equal weight of flour whisked in and cooked briefly to form a white roux, without browning. Hot or cold milk is added gradually with constant stirring to avoid lumps, and the sauce is simmered until it thickens and loses any raw flour taste. Traditional recipes infuse the milk with an onion studded with cloves and a bay leaf. The finished sauce should coat the back of a spoon (nappe consistency).

Derivatives and uses

Adding Gruyère or Parmesan yields Mornay sauce; cream gives sauce crème; mustard, soubise (onion), and cheddar variants are used in French, Italian, Greek, and British cooking. In Greek cuisine a thickened béchamel enriched with egg yolk crowns moussaka and pastitsio.
