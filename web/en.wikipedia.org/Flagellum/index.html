Flagellum

A flagellum (plural: flagella) is a hairlike appendage that protrudes from the cell body of certain cells and is used for locomotion. Flagella occur in bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes, but the three types differ fundamentally in structure, composition and mechanism — a classic case of convergent evolution. The word derives from the Latin flagellum, "whip".

Bacterial flagella

The bacterial flagellum is a helical filament of the protein flagellin, turned by a rotary motor embedded in the cell envelope — one of very few true biological rotary engines. The motor is driven by a proton (or in some species sodium) gradient and can switch between counterclockwise rotation, which bundles the flagella and produces a smooth "run", and clockwise rotation, which disperses the bundle and causes a "tumble". This run-and-tumble behaviour underlies bacterial chemotaxis.

Eukaryotic flagella

Eukaryotic flagella are structurally identical to cilia: a membrane-bound axoneme of nine doublet microtubules surrounding a central pair (the "9+2" arrangement), bent by the motor protein dynein. They beat in waves rather than rotating. Examples include the tails of sperm cells and the flagella of protists such as Chlamydomonas. Because only length and movement pattern conventionally distinguish them, many biologists refer to both as cilia.

Archaeal flagella (archaella)

Archaea swim using the archaellum, which superficially resembles a bacterial flagellum but is assembled like a type IV pilus and powered by ATP hydrolysis. Its proteins are unrelated to bacterial flagellin, reinforcing the conclusion that nature evolved swimming organelles independently at least three times.
