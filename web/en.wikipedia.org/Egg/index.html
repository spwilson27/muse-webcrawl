Egg

An egg is an organic vessel grown by an animal to carry a possibly fertilized egg cell (a zygote) and to incubate it until the contained embryo has developed sufficiently for animal survival.

An egg is an organic vessel grown by an animal to carry a possibly fertilized egg cell (a zygote) and to incubate it until the contained embryo has developed sufficiently for animal survival, at which point the animal hatches. Most arthropods, vertebrates (excluding live-bearing mammals), and mollusks lay eggs, although some, such as scorpions, do not.

Structure

Reptile eggs, bird eggs, and monotreme eggs are laid out of water and are surrounded by a protective shell, either flexible or inflexible. Inside, the embryo is nourished by the yolk, suspended in the albumen (egg white), which also cushions it. Membranes such as the amnion and chorion manage gas exchange and waste. Eggs laid on land or in nests are usually kept within a warm range of temperature while the embryo grows.

Classification

Scientists classify eggs by the amount and distribution of yolk. Isolecithal eggs have scant, evenly distributed yolk, as in most mammals; telolecithal eggs, including those of birds and reptiles, concentrate the yolk at one pole. Fish and amphibian eggs are typically mesolecithal.

Evolution

The amniotic egg, which appeared in the Carboniferous period, freed vertebrate reproduction from dependence on open water and was key to the success of reptiles, birds and mammals on land. Egg-laying animals are described as oviparous, in contrast to viviparous animals that give birth to live young.

Human use

Eggs have been eaten by humans for millennia; the chicken egg is the most common worldwide. Eggs are also used in vaccine production, scientific research and numerous cultural and religious symbols of fertility and rebirth.
