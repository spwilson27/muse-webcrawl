CRISPR gene editing

CRISPR gene editing is a genetic engineering technique in molecular biology by which the genomes of living organisms may be modified. It is based on a simplified version of the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 antiviral defense system. By delivering the Cas9 nuclease complexed with a synthetic guide RNA (gRNA) into a cell, the cell's genome can be cut at a desired location, allowing existing genes to be removed or new ones added in vivo.

The CRISPR-Cas9 system works naturally by exploiting the way bacteria use short DNA sequences captured from viruses to recognize and destroy viral DNA. Researchers adapted this system into a general-purpose tool: a guide RNA directs the Cas9 enzyme to a matching DNA sequence adjacent to a short motif known as a PAM, where Cas9 creates a double-stranded break. The cell then repairs the break, either by error-prone non-homologous end joining, which often knocks a gene out, or by homology-directed repair using a supplied template, which can write in new sequences.

History

Key demonstrations that the system could be repurposed for genome editing appeared in 2012, when Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier and colleagues showed that Cas9 could be programmed in vitro, and in early 2013, when Feng Zhang, George Church and others showed editing in human and mouse cells. Doudna and Charpentier received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020. A patent dispute between the University of California and the Broad Institute followed.

Applications

CRISPR editing is used in basic research, agriculture, and medicine. In 2023 the first CRISPR therapy, Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel), was approved in the United Kingdom and the United States for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia. Research continues into treatments for genetic blindness, muscular dystrophy and HIV, as well as gene drives, engineered crops and xenotransplantation.

Concerns

Off-target edits, mosaicism, delivery challenges and ethical questions surround the technology. In 2018 Chinese researcher He Jiankui announced the birth of genome-edited babies, an act widely condemned and followed by his imprisonment.
