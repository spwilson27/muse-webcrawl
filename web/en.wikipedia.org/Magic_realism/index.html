Magic realism

Magic realism, also spelled magical realism, is a style of literary fiction and art in which realistic narrative is combined with surreal elements of dream or fantasy. In a work of magic realism, the world is recognisably real, yet fantastical events occur and are accepted matter-of-factly by the characters.

Origins of the term

The term was first applied to painting by the German art historian Franz Roh in 1925, describing post-expressionist art that returned to depicting ordinary objects with strange clarity. It was later taken up in Latin American criticism, notably by Alejo Carpentier, who described lo real maravilloso — "the marvelous real" — as an inherent quality of Latin American reality and history.

Literature

Magic realism became closely associated with the Latin American Boom of the 1960s and 1970s. Gabriel García Márquez's One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), set in the fictional town of Macondo, is its most famous example; other major practitioners include Jorge Luis Borges, Isabel Allende, Laura Esquivel, and Miguel Ángel Asturias. Writers elsewhere — Salman Rushdie, Toni Morrison, Haruki Murakami, and Günter Grass among them — have used related techniques.

Characteristics

Typical features include an "irreducible element" of magic that cannot be explained by natural laws, a matter-of-fact narrative tone toward the extraordinary, plentiful realistic detail, hybridity of the real and the fantastic, and distorted or cyclical time. Critics debate its boundaries with fantasy and surrealism, and some object to the label when applied to all Latin American fiction.
