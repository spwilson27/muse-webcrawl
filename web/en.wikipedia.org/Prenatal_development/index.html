Prenatal development
StagesGerminal, embryonic, fetal
Duration (human)c. 38 weeks from fertilisation

Prenatal development is the process by which an embryo or fetus (or, in humans, the developing child) develops during pregnancy, from fertilisation to birth. In human development it is conventionally divided into the germinal stage (weeks 1–2), the embryonic stage (weeks 3–8) and the fetal stage (week 9 to birth).

Stages

The germinal stage begins at fertilisation, when the sperm and egg fuse to form a zygote. The zygote undergoes rapid mitotic cleavage as it travels down the fallopian tube, becoming a morula and then a blastocyst, which implants in the uterine wall around day 6–7. During the embryonic stage, the three germ layers — ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm — form by gastrulation, and the foundations of all major organs are laid down (organogenesis): the neural tube closes, the heart begins to beat, and limb buds appear. This is the period of greatest sensitivity to teratogens such as alcohol, certain drugs and infections.

In the fetal stage the organs mature and the body grows rapidly: recognisable human features, reflexes, and movements appear, the skeleton ossifies, and the brain undergoes extensive development through the third trimester. Viability outside the womb becomes possible from around 22–24 weeks with modern intensive care. Full-term birth normally occurs at about 40 weeks of gestational age.

Influences

Prenatal development is influenced by genetics, maternal nutrition (notably folate, which prevents neural tube defects), infections, chronic disease, environmental exposures and placental function. Monitoring by ultrasound and biochemical screening is a standard part of antenatal care in most countries.
