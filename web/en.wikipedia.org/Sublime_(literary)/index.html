Sublime (literary)

In aesthetics, the sublime (from the Latin sublīmis) is the quality of greatness, whether physical, moral, intellectual, metaphysical, or artistic. The term especially refers to a greatness beyond all possibility of calculation, measurement, or imitation. In literature and rhetoric, the sublime denotes language or subject matter that lifts the audience into a state of ecstasy and awe.

Longinus

The earliest surviving treatment is On the Sublime (Peri Hypsous), an ancient Greek treatise traditionally attributed to Longinus (1st or 3rd century AD). Longinus identified sources of sublimity in great thoughts, strong emotion, figures of speech, noble diction, and elevated composition. The work was recovered to wide influence through Nicolas Boileau's French translation of 1674.

Burke and Kant

Edmund Burke's A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757) contrasted the sublime — founded on terror, vastness, obscurity, and power — with the beautiful, founded on pleasure and smallness. Immanuel Kant, in the Critique of Judgment (1790), distinguished the mathematical sublime (of overwhelming magnitude) from the dynamical sublime (of overwhelming might), locating sublimity not in nature itself but in the mind's awareness of its own rational capacities.

Romanticism and after

The sublime became a central category of Romantic art and literature: the poetry of Wordsworth, the paintings of Caspar David Friedrich and J. M. W. Turner, and Gothic fiction all exploited overwhelming landscapes and forces. Later thinkers — including Schiller, Schopenhauer, and in the 20th century Lyotard — continued to develop the concept, Lyotard linking it to postmodern art's presentation of the unpresentable.
