Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress

The Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress is an American four-engined heavy bomber aircraft developed in the 1930s for the United States Army Air Corps (USAAC). A fast and high-flying bomber, the B-17 was used primarily in the European Theater of Operations during World War II, where it dropped more bombs than any other aircraft during the war.

Development

The prototype Model 299 first flew in July 1935. A Seattle news reporter, struck by its defensive machine guns, dubbed it the "Flying Fortress", a name Boeing quickly trademarked. Although the prototype crashed, the Air Corps ordered the type into production, and successive variants added armor, self-sealing fuel tanks, and greatly increased defensive armament.

Operational history

The definitive B-17G carried thirteen .50-caliber machine guns and was flown by the U.S. Eighth Air Force from England in daylight precision bombing raids over Nazi Germany, alongside the B-24 Liberator. Formations flew in "combat box" arrangements for mutual defense, but unescorted deep-penetration missions in 1943, such as the Schweinfurt–Regensburg raids, suffered heavy losses until long-range fighters like the P-51 Mustang could escort them all the way to their targets. The B-17 was renowned for toughness, frequently returning home with severe battle damage.

Production and legacy

A total of 12,731 B-17s were built by Boeing, Douglas, and Vega (Lockheed). After the war the type served in air-sea rescue, weather reconnaissance, transport, and civilian roles; a small number remain airworthy today as warbirds, including famous examples such as the Memphis Belle.
