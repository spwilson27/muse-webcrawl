Satan

Satan as depicted in a medieval manuscript illumination.

Satan, also known as the Devil, is an entity in the Abrahamic religions that seduces humans into sin or falsehood. In Judaism, Satan is seen as an agent subservient to God, typically regarded as the heavenly prosecutor or accuser. In Christianity and Islam, he is usually seen as a fallen angel or jinn who has rebelled against God, who nevertheless allows him temporary power over the fallen world and a host of demons.

Etymology and origins

The Hebrew word śāṭān (שָּׂטָן) means "accuser" or "adversary" and appears in the Hebrew Bible both for human adversaries and for a heavenly figure, notably in the Book of Job, where "the Satan" acts as a prosecuting attorney within God's court. During the Second Temple period, influenced in part by Zoroastrian dualism, Satan developed into a more independent embodiment of evil, a development reflected in the New Testament.

In Christianity

In the New Testament, Satan is the tempter of Jesus in the desert, the ruler of demons, and the adversary of God and humanity, identified with the serpent of Eden and the dragon of the Book of Revelation. Christian tradition holds that he was an angel cast out of heaven for pride and rebellion, and that he will be finally defeated at the Last Judgment. Satan figures prominently in medieval and early modern literature, including Dante's Inferno and Milton's Paradise Lost.

In Islam

In Islam, Satan is known as Iblis (or Shaytan). According to the Quran, Iblis was a jinn who refused God's command to bow before Adam and was expelled, vowing to lead humanity astray until Judgment Day. He whispers temptation but has no power over the righteous.

In modern culture and religion

Satan remains a major figure in literature, art, film, and music, from Goethe's Faust to modern horror. Some modern religious movements, such as LaVeyan Satanism and The Satanic Temple, invoke Satan symbolically as an emblem of individualism and rebellion rather than as a literal being.
