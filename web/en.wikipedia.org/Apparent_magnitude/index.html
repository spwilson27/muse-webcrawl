Apparent magnitude

Apparent magnitude (m) is a measure of the brightness of a star or other astronomical object as seen from Earth. An object's apparent magnitude depends on its intrinsic luminosity, its distance from Earth, and any extinction of its light by interstellar material. The scale is reverse logarithmic: the brighter an object appears, the lower its magnitude value.

The scale

The system originates with Hipparchus, who classed the brightest stars as first magnitude and the faintest visible as sixth. In 1856 Norman Pogson formalised the scale so that a difference of five magnitudes corresponds exactly to a brightness ratio of 100; each magnitude step is a factor of about 2.512. The Sun's apparent magnitude is about −26.7, the full Moon about −12.7, Venus reaches about −4.6, and Sirius, the brightest star, is −1.46. The faintest stars visible to the unaided eye are around magnitude +6.

Absolute magnitude

To compare objects' true luminosities, astronomers use absolute magnitude: the apparent magnitude an object would have at a standard distance of 10 parsecs (about 32.6 light-years). The relation between apparent magnitude, absolute magnitude, and distance is given by the distance modulus, m − M = 5 log10(d/10 pc).
