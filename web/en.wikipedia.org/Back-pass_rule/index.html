Back-pass rule

The back-pass rule is a law in association football, introduced in 1992, that prohibits the goalkeeper from handling the ball when it has been deliberately kicked to them by a teammate.

Background

Before the rule, defenders could waste time by passing the ball back for the goalkeeper to pick up and hold. Time-wasting of this kind marred the 1990 FIFA World Cup, prompting the International Football Association Board to act.

The law

Under Law 12, an indirect free kick is awarded if a goalkeeper, inside their own penalty area, touches the ball with the hand or arm after it has been deliberately kicked by a teammate, or thrown to them directly from a teammate's throw-in. Headers, chest passes and other non-kicked returns may still be handled, and a ball that a teammate plays with a "trick" to circumvent the law is punished as unsporting behaviour.

Impact

The rule is widely credited with speeding up the game, increasing effective playing time and encouraging goalkeepers to develop skill with their feet, a trend that reshaped the position in modern football. See also Goalkeeper and Laws of the Game.
