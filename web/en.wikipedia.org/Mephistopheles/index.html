Mephistopheles

Mephistopheles (also spelled Mephistophilus, Mephostopheles and other variants) is a demon featured in German folklore and literature, originally appearing as the devil with whom the scholar Faust makes his fatal pact. He became one of the most famous figures of Western literature through Christopher Marlowe's Doctor Faustus and, above all, Goethe's Faust.

First appearanceFaustbuch (1587)
RoleDemon; agent of Lucifer
Associated figureJohann Georg Faust

Origins

The name first appears in the anonymous Historia von D. Johann Fausten (1587), the earliest Faust chapbook, where Mephostophiles is the demon summoned by Faust and bound to serve him for 24 years in exchange for his soul. The name's etymology is uncertain; it is often formed from Greek elements meaning "not loving light", though a Hebrew origin has also been proposed.

In Goethe's Faust

In Goethe's two-part drama (1808, 1832), Mephistopheles wagers with God in the Prologue in Heaven that he can corrupt Faust. He defines himself as "part of that power which eternally wills evil and eternally works good" and as "the spirit that negates". Witty, ironic and urbane, he is less a traditional devil than a personification of cynical negation; in the end Faust's soul is saved despite the pact, and Mephistopheles is cheated of his prize.

Other depictions

The character appears in operas by Gounod (Faust), Boito (Mefistofele) and Berlioz (La damnation de Faust), in Liszt's Mephisto Waltzes, and in countless later works. In popular culture "Mephistophelean" describes a sinister, smoothly evil character, and the name has been given to figures from Marvel Comics to chess-playing automata — the 18th-century chess "Turk" was succeeded by a machine named Mephisto.
