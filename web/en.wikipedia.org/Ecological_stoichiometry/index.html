Ecological stoichiometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ecological stoichiometry (more broadly referred to as biological stoichiometry) considers how the balance of energy and elements influences living systems. Similar to chemical stoichiometry, ecological stoichiometry is founded on constraints of mass balance as they apply to organisms and their interactions in ecosystems. Specifically, how does the balance of energy and elements affect and how is it affected by organisms and their interactions in ecosystems? Concepts of ecological stoichiometry have a long history in ecology with early references to the constraints of mass balance made by Liebig, Lotha, and Redfield. These earlier concepts have been extended to explicitly include the elemental physiology of organisms and their interactive roles in food webs and ecosystem processes.

C:N:P ratios across organisms

Most work in ecological stoichiometry focuses on the interface between an organism and its resources. This interface, whether it is between plants and their nutrient resources or large herbivores and grasses, is often characterized by dramatic differences in the elemental composition of each participant. Consider termites, which have a carbon:nitrogen ratio (C:N) of about 5, but consume wood with a C:N ratio of about 500 — the termite therefore must solve the problem of obtaining enough nitrogen while disposing of excess carbon, a problem it solves in part through nitrogen-fixing symbionts in its gut.

Redfield ratio

A foundational observation of the field is the Redfield ratio: marine phytoplankton and the deep ocean waters they inhabit both tend toward an elemental composition of roughly 106 carbon : 16 nitrogen : 1 phosphorus atoms. Alfred Redfield's discovery that organismal elemental composition reflects the chemistry of the environment became a central reference point for stoichiometric studies.

Growth rate hypothesis

The growth rate hypothesis links fast growth to low C:P and N:P ratios in organisms, because rapid growth requires large allocations to phosphorus-rich ribosomal RNA. This hypothesis connects elemental composition at the cellular level to organismal traits such as body size, development time, and maximal population growth rate, and onward to community and ecosystem processes such as nutrient recycling.

Applications

Ecological stoichiometry has been applied to topics including nutrient limitation, consumer–resource dynamics, food web structure, population dynamics, and biogeochemical cycling. It is used to predict how ecosystems respond to changes in nutrient supply, such as eutrophication, and to global change drivers including elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide.
