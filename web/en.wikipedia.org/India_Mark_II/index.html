India Mark II

The India Mark II is a human-powered hand pump designed to lift water from boreholes at depths of roughly 12 to 45 metres (40–150 ft). It is one of the most widely used hand pumps in the world, forming the backbone of rural drinking-water supply in India and installed in large numbers across Africa and Asia. The pump is considered a public-domain design: its specifications are standardized and it may be manufactured by any suitable producer.

History

The Mark II was developed in India during the 1970s through collaboration between the Government of India, UNICEF, and Indian manufacturers, improving on the earlier India Mark I and on designs such as the Sholapur pump. It was engineered for "village-level operation and maintenance" (VLOM): robust enough for heavy communal use, simple enough to be repaired by a trained local caretaker with basic tools, and built from standardized, interchangeable parts.

Design

The pump uses a lever handle acting through a chain-and-quadrant mechanism to drive a reciprocating rod connected to a piston in a cylinder at the bottom of the borehole. Its cast-iron head, galvanized steel rising main, and brass or stainless cylinder components are intended for durability and corrosion resistance. The pump can typically deliver roughly 15–20 litres per minute with moderate effort.

Successors and criticism

The deeper-lift India Mark III and extra-deep-well variants followed. Field studies have found that downtime due to maintenance failures remains a significant problem for rural water supply, prompting newer designs (such as the India Mark II's rivals, the Afridev pump) and improved maintenance systems, though the Mark II remains ubiquitous.
