The International Style is a major architectural style that developed in Europe and the United States in the 1920s and 1930s and dominated modern architecture through the mid-twentieth century. The term was popularized by Henry-Russell Hitchcock and Philip Johnson's 1932 exhibition "Modern Architecture: International Exhibition" at the Museum of Modern Art in New York and their accompanying book The International Style: Architecture Since 1922.

Characteristics

Hitchcock and Johnson identified three principles: architecture as volume rather than mass; emphasis on balance and regularity rather than symmetry; and the rejection of applied ornament. Buildings in the style typically feature steel or reinforced-concrete frames, glass curtain walls, flat roofs, smooth white or neutral surfaces, strip windows, and open, flexible floor plans made possible by freeing façades and interiors from load-bearing walls. Exemplars include the Bauhaus building in Dessau (Gropius, 1926), the Villa Savoye (Le Corbusier, 1931), the Barcelona Pavilion (Mies van der Rohe, 1929) and, later, New York's Lever House and Seagram Building.

Spread and decline

Emigration of European architects fleeing Nazism carried the style to the United States, where it became the default idiom for corporate headquarters and civic buildings after the Second World War. Its ubiquity drew criticism for monotony and disregard of local climate and culture; by the 1970s postmodern architects such as Robert Venturi were explicitly reacting against it, though its glass-tower descendants still dominate skylines worldwide.
