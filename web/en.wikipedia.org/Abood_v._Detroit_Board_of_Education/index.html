Abood v. Detroit Board of Education

DecidedMay 23, 1977
Citation431 U.S. 209
CourtSupreme Court of the United States
Overruled byJanus v. AFSCME (2018)

Abood v. Detroit Board of Education, 431 U.S. 209 (1977), was a decision of the United States Supreme Court concerning labor unions and the First Amendment. The Court held that public-sector employees who decline to join a union may nevertheless be required to pay "agency fees" (fair share fees) covering the union's costs of collective bargaining, contract administration, and grievance adjustment, but may not be compelled to fund the union's political and ideological activities.

Background

Michigan law permitted agency shop agreements for public school teachers. A group of Detroit teachers objected to the Detroit Federation of Teachers' use of fees for political purposes and challenged the arrangement as compelled association and speech.

Holding

Writing for the Court, Justice Potter Stewart distinguished between fees spent on bargaining-related activities, which the state could require to prevent "free riders", and expenditures on political candidates and causes, which nonmembers could not be forced to support. The decision drew on earlier cases involving railway unions, such as Railway Employees' Dept. v. Hanson and Machinists v. Street.

Overruling

The Abood compromise stood for four decades, though it was progressively questioned by the Court. In Janus v. AFSCME (2018), a 5–4 majority overruled Abood, holding that agency fees for public employees violate the First Amendment.
