Greenhouse

A greenhouse is a structure with walls and roof made chiefly of a transparent material, such as glass, in which plants requiring regulated climatic conditions are grown. These structures range in size from small sheds to industrial-sized buildings and are used to extend growing seasons, protect plants from cold and pests, and cultivate crops out of season or outside their native climates. A miniature greenhouse is known as a cold frame.

How it works

Solar radiation passes through the transparent glazing and heats the interior surfaces; the warmed air, unable to escape by convection, raises the internal temperature. (Although the phenomenon lends its name to the atmospheric greenhouse effect, the atmospheric effect works mainly by radiative absorption rather than blocked convection.) Ventilation, shading, heating and cooling systems regulate temperature and humidity; modern commercial houses automate these controls and may add supplemental lighting and carbon-dioxide enrichment to accelerate growth.

History

The Roman emperor Tiberius was said to eat a cucumber-like vegetable daily, grown in wheeled beds covered with oiled cloth or mica — an early forcing technique. In the 17th–19th centuries, orangeries and botanical glasshouses spread through Europe; the Victorian era produced vast structures such as the Palm House at Kew Gardens (1840s) and Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace (1851). In the 20th century plastic films and polycarbonate replaced or supplemented glass, enabling the huge plastic-covered growing region of Almería in Spain, visible from space.

Uses

Greenhouses are used commercially for vegetables (tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers), flowers and bedding plants, fruit, nursery stock and research. The Netherlands is a leading producer and exporter of greenhouse technology and produce. Specialised forms include botanical-garden conservatories, hydroponic and vertical-farm houses, and high tunnels (polytunnels) used for season extension.
