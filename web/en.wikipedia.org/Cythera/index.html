Cythera (Greek: Κύθηρα, Kythira) is an island in Greece lying opposite the south-eastern tip of the Peloponnese peninsula, at the meeting point of the Ionian and Aegean seas. It is traditionally considered one of the Ionian Islands, although it lies far from the rest of the group, and administratively it belongs to the regional unit of Islands in the Attica region. The island is administratively united with the smaller island of Antikythera, famous for the ancient Antikythera mechanism recovered from a nearby shipwreck.

Mythology

In Greek mythology Cythera was closely associated with Aphrodite, the goddess of love. According to one tradition, Aphrodite rose from the sea foam near Cythera before travelling on to Cyprus, and the goddess was worshipped under the epithet Cytherea. The island's sanctuary of Aphrodite Urania was among the most ancient in Greece.

History

The island was inhabited from the Neolithic period and was an important station in Minoan and Phoenician trade across the Mediterranean; the Phoenicians may have introduced the production of purple dye from murex shells, from which the island's ancient name Porphyrousa derives. In classical times Cythera was contested between Sparta and Athens because of its strategic position off the Laconian coast. It later passed through Roman, Byzantine, Venetian, Ottoman, French, and British hands before being ceded to the modern Greek state in 1864 along with the other Ionian Islands.

Geography

Cythera has an area of about 300 square kilometres and a rugged, rocky terrain cut by valleys and gorges. Its main town is the hilltop capital, Kythira or Chora, in the south of the island; the principal port is Diakofti. The island has a population of a few thousand, much reduced from its early twentieth-century peak by emigration, particularly to Australia, where a large Kytherian diaspora community exists.
