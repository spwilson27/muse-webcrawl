Remembrance poppy

The remembrance poppy is an artificial flower worn in many Commonwealth countries to commemorate military personnel who have died in war. It was adopted after the First World War, inspired by John McCrae's 1915 poem "In Flanders Fields", which describes red poppies growing among soldiers' graves in Flanders.

History

The American humanitarian Moina Michael promoted the poppy as a symbol of remembrance in 1918, and the French campaigner Anna Guérin spread the idea internationally. In 1921 the (Royal) British Legion held its first Poppy Appeal in Britain, and veterans' organisations in Canada, Australia, New Zealand and other Commonwealth nations adopted the custom as well.

Use

Poppies are distributed by charities such as the Royal British Legion in return for donations supporting veterans and their families. They are worn in the weeks leading up to Remembrance Sunday and Remembrance Day (11 November), and laid on war memorials and at services of remembrance. In Scotland, the poppy distributed by Poppyscotland traditionally has no leaf, unlike the English version.

Variations and debate

Alternative poppies exist: the white poppy, promoted by the Peace Pledge Union since 1933, symbolises remembrance for all victims of war and a commitment to peace; other colours commemorate particular groups, such as animals or civilian victims. The poppy periodically prompts public debate about whether wearing it should be a matter of personal choice.
