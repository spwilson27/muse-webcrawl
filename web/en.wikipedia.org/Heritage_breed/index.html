Heritage breed

A heritage breed is a traditional livestock breed that was raised by farmers in the past before the mid-twentieth-century rise of industrial agriculture made a small number of specialised, high-output breeds dominant. Heritage breeds are valued for genetic diversity, hardiness, adaptation to local conditions and outdoor life, good mothering ability, longevity and — often — distinctive flavours in meat, milk and eggs that differ from commercial products.

Characteristics

Conservation organisations such as The Livestock Conservancy in the United States and the Rare Breeds Survival Trust in the United Kingdom define heritage breeds by criteria including a documented history, a breed standard, and reproduction through true breeding over generations. Examples include Bourbon Red and Narragansett turkeys, Tamworth and Gloucestershire Old Spots pigs, Devon and Highland cattle, Jacob and Cotswold sheep, and Dominique and Buckeye chickens. Unlike modern commercial hybrids, heritage animals generally grow more slowly, reproduce naturally, and thrive on pasture rather than requiring intensive feeding regimes.

Conservation

Many heritage breeds became rare or endangered as agriculture consolidated around a few breeds optimised for confinement production — the broad-breasted white turkey, for instance, cannot reproduce naturally, while a handful of pig and chicken lines supply most commercial output. Conservationists argue that preserving heritage breeds safeguards genetic resources that may be needed for future disease resistance, climate adaptation and changing consumer demands. Strategies include maintaining breeding populations, cryopreservation of genetic material, and creating markets for heritage-breed products, on the principle of "conservation through use": breeds survive when farmers can make a living raising them.
