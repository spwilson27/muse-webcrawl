Brain in a vat

The brain-in-a-vat scenario

The brain in a vat is a thought experiment in philosophy, used most prominently by Hilary Putnam in his book Reason, Truth and History (1981). It imagines a brain removed from the body, suspended in a vat of life-sustaining liquid, and connected by electrodes to a supercomputer that feeds it electrical impulses identical to those a brain normally receives — so that the brain has experiences indistinguishable from ordinary life, although nothing it "perceives" is real.

Purpose

The scenario is a modern version of Descartes's evil demon and is used to explore scepticism about the external world: if you cannot tell whether you are a brain in a vat, how can you know anything about reality? Putnam famously argued, on semantic externalist grounds, that the hypothesis is self-refuting: a brain that had always been in a vat could not meaningfully think the thought "I am a brain in a vat," because its words would not refer to real brains or vats.

Influence

The idea is closely related to the simulation hypothesis and inspired depictions in popular culture, most famously The Matrix.
