Computer science

Computer science is the study of computation, information, and automation. It spans theoretical disciplines — such as algorithms, theory of computation, and information theory — and applied disciplines including the design and implementation of hardware and software. Algorithms and data structures are central to the field. The theory of computation concerns abstract models of computation, such as the Turing machine, and the classification of computational problems by the resources they require.

History

The foundations predate the digital computer: algorithms were studied by al-Khwarizmi (from whose name the word derives), and Ada Lovelace wrote what is regarded as the first algorithm intended for a machine, Babbage's Analytical Engine. In the 1930s, Turing and Church formalised computability; Shannon founded information theory in 1948. The first computer science departments appeared in the 1960s — Purdue's, founded 1962, is often cited as the first in the United States — and the ACM's Turing Award became the field's highest honour.

Major areas

Theoretical computer science covers computability, computational complexity (including the P versus NP problem), formal languages, and automata. Systems areas include computer architecture, operating systems, networks, databases, and security. Software engineering studies the disciplined development of large programs; programming language research designs the notations of computation. Artificial intelligence and machine learning, human–computer interaction, computer graphics, and robotics apply computational methods to perception, reasoning, and action.

Relation to other fields

Computer science overlaps mathematics, electrical engineering, linguistics, and cognitive science, and supplies tools to nearly every discipline, from computational biology to digital humanities. As Edsger Dijkstra observed, it is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes.
