The Battle of Lesnaya was fought on 9 October 1708 (28 September Old Style) near the village of Lesnaya, south-east of Mogilev in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (modern Belarus), during the Great Northern War. A Russian flying corps personally commanded by Tsar Peter I intercepted the Swedish corps of General Adam Ludwig Lewenhaupt, which was marching from Livonia to join Charles XII's main army in Ukraine with a huge train of supplies, artillery and reinforcements. The Russians mauled Lewenhaupt's corps and forced him to abandon his guns and wagon train; his surviving troops reached Charles's army empty-handed. Peter called Lesnaya "the mother of the Battle of Poltava", fought nine months later.

Background

In the summer of 1708 Charles XII invaded Russia. Running short of supplies, he ordered Lewenhaupt, commanding in Livonia, to bring south some 12,950 men, 16 guns and a convoy of several thousand wagons loaded with food and ammunition sufficient for the main army. The convoy's slow march through forests and marshes gave Peter time to gather a corps of roughly comparable size and strike before the junction could occur.

The battle

The Russians attacked the Swedish corps as it crossed the Lesnyanka stream near the village. Fighting raged all day in dense woodland, with the Swedes defending the wagon laager and the bridge over the stream. Repeated Russian assaults gradually wore down the defenders; Swedish counter-attacks by crack regiments such as the Hälsinge and Björneborg regiments kept the position intact until nightfall. Under cover of darkness and a snowstorm, Lewenhaupt slipped away, burning the remaining wagons, spiking or abandoning all his artillery, and leaving his wounded behind. About 6,000–7,000 Swedes — roughly half the corps — reached Charles XII's army weeks later, bringing no supplies.

Consequences

The loss of the convoy was strategically disastrous for Charles XII: deprived of food, powder and reinforcements, his army entered the harsh winter of 1708–09 in Ukraine dependent on the doubtful support of Hetman Ivan Mazepa's Cossacks. The Russian army, by contrast, gained confidence from having beaten a Swedish corps in open battle for the first time. The two armies met again at Poltava on 8 July 1709, where the Swedish army was destroyed.
