Buddhist meditation

Buddhist meditation is the practice of meditation in Buddhism. The closest words for meditation in the classical languages of Buddhism are bhāvanā ("mental development") and jhāna/dhyāna (mental training resulting in a calm and luminous mind). Buddhist meditation techniques have the aim of developing mindfulness, concentration, tranquility, and insight, ultimately leading to awakening and liberation from suffering.

Early Buddhist meditation

Early Buddhist texts describe meditation as part of the Noble Eightfold Path, particularly right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. Two broad qualities are cultivated: samatha (calm-abiding), which steadies the mind through the successive absorptions called jhānas, and vipassanā (insight), which investigates the three marks of existence — impermanence, suffering, and non-self. The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta sets out the four foundations of mindfulness: body, feelings, mind, and mental phenomena. Other classical practices include mindfulness of breathing (ānāpānasati), loving-kindness meditation (mettā), and contemplations of impermanence and death.

Later developments

In the Theravāda tradition, the Visuddhimagga of Buddhaghosa systematized meditation subjects for samatha and the stages of insight; the modern "vipassanā movement" of teachers such as Mahasi Sayadaw and S. N. Goenka spread lay meditation practice worldwide. Mahāyāna traditions added practices such as the recitation of a Buddha's name in Pure Land Buddhism, koan introspection and shikantaza in Zen (which developed from Chinese Chan), visualization and deity yoga in Tibetan Buddhism, and mantra recitation.

In the modern world

Since the late twentieth century, Buddhist-derived mindfulness practices have been adapted into secular clinical programs such as mindfulness-based stress reduction, and Buddhist meditation has become the subject of extensive scientific research on attention, emotion, and brain function.
