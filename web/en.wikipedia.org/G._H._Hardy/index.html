Godfrey Harold Hardy (7 February 1877 – 1 December 1947) was an English mathematician, known for his achievements in number theory and mathematical analysis. In biology, he is known for the Hardy–Weinberg principle, a basic principle of population genetics. In addition to his research, Hardy is remembered for his 1940 essay on the aesthetics of mathematics, A Mathematician's Apology, and for his mentorship of the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan.

Life and career

Hardy was educated at Winchester College and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he became a fellow in 1900. Unhappy with Cambridge's examination-driven culture, he helped reform the Mathematical Tripos. He held the Sadleirian Chair at Cambridge and, from 1919 to 1931, the Savilian Chair of Geometry at Oxford, before returning to Cambridge. A committed atheist and pacifist, he opposed both world wars and delighted in cricket, which he treated with mathematical seriousness.

Work

Hardy's long collaboration with J. E. Littlewood — conducted largely by correspondence under a famous set of informal "axioms" — produced over a hundred papers, including the Hardy–Littlewood circle method for additive number theory and the first and second Hardy–Littlewood conjectures. With Ramanujan, whom he brought to Cambridge in 1914 after recognising the genius in an unsolicited letter, he proved the Hardy–Ramanujan theorem on the number of prime factors of integers and derived the celebrated asymptotic formula for the partition function. With Wilhelm Weinberg (independently), he stated the equilibrium law of population genetics in 1908.

A Mathematician's Apology

Written late in life as his powers declined, the Apology (1940) defends pure mathematics as a creative art, valuable independently of any application — Hardy boasted that nothing he did would ever make "the least difference to the amenity of the world". C. P. Snow's foreword made the book a classic account of a mathematician's inner life.
