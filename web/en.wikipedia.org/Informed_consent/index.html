Informed consent

Informed consent is the process by which a person voluntarily agrees to a proposed course of action — most commonly medical treatment or participation in research — after being given adequate information about its nature, benefits, risks and alternatives, and comprehending that information. It is a foundational principle of medical ethics and health law, grounded in respect for personal autonomy: a competent person has the right to decide what happens to their own body. Treatment given without consent may constitute battery or negligence in law, and research conducted without it violates international ethical codes.

Elements

Valid informed consent generally requires:

  • Disclosure — the practitioner explains the diagnosis, the proposed intervention, its material risks and expected benefits, reasonable alternatives (including no treatment), and the consequences of each.
  • Comprehension — the person understands the information, conveyed in accessible language, ideally confirmed by questions or "teach-back".
  • Competence (capacity) — the person is legally and cognitively able to make the decision; where capacity is lacking, a surrogate or advance directive may be used.
  • Voluntariness — agreement is given freely, without coercion or manipulation, and may be withdrawn at any time.

Consent may be express (written or spoken) or, for routine low-risk care, implied by the patient's actions. Exceptions exist for emergencies, where treatment may proceed under an implied-consent doctrine to prevent death or serious harm, and narrow "therapeutic privilege" is recognised in some jurisdictions.

History

The doctrine developed from early consent cases such as Schloendorff v. Society of New York Hospital (1914), in which Justice Cardozo wrote that "every human being of adult years and sound mind has a right to determine what shall be done with his own body". The atrocities of Nazi medical experiments led to the Nuremberg Code (1947), whose first principle makes voluntary consent "absolutely essential" in research; the World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964) extended and refined these standards. Abuses such as the Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972), in which treatment was withheld from African American men without their knowledge, drove reforms including institutional review boards (ethics committees) and, in the United States, the Belmont Report (1979), which articulated respect for persons, beneficence and justice as the framework for research ethics. Landmark cases such as Canterbury v. Spence (1972) shifted the disclosure standard from professional custom to what a reasonable patient would want to know.

In research and beyond

In clinical research, consent is documented through detailed consent forms approved by an ethics committee, and special protections apply to children, prisoners, pregnant women and others considered vulnerable. Beyond medicine, the logic of informed consent informs data-protection law (consent to processing of personal data), organ donation, biobanking and, increasingly, debates about consent in digital services, where critics argue that click-through agreements rarely satisfy genuine comprehension or voluntariness.
