Sandpaper

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sandpaper, also known as glasspaper or as coated abrasive, is a type of material that consists of sheets of paper or cloth with an abrasive substance glued to one face. In the modern manufacture of these products, sand and glass have been replaced by other abrasives such as aluminium oxide or silicon carbide. Sandpaper is produced in a range of grit sizes and is used to remove material from surfaces, whether to make them smoother (for example, in painting and wood finishing), to remove a layer of material (such as old paint), or sometimes to roughen the surface.

History

The earliest recorded example of sandpaper-like material is in 13th-century China, when crushed shells, seeds, and sand were bonded to parchment using natural gum. Shark skin was used as an abrasive in Japan. Glass paper was manufactured in London from 1833 by John Oakey, and in the United States Isaac Fisher Jr. of Springfield, Vermont, received patents for sandpaper manufacture in 1834. Mass production made sandpaper a standard workshop material in the 20th century.

Grit sizes

Grit size refers to the size of the abrasive particles. Common standards include the European FEPA "P" scale and the American CAMI scale; higher numbers indicate finer abrasives. Coarse grades (40–60 grit) remove material quickly, medium grades (80–120) prepare surfaces, and fine and superfine grades (220 and above) are used for final smoothing between coats of finish. Wet-and-dry paper, usually silicon carbide with a waterproof backing, can be used with water or oil as a lubricant.

Types and uses

Sandpaper is sold as sheets, rolls, discs, and belts, and is used by hand, with a sanding block, or on power sanders. Backings range from paper to cloth and fibre, chosen according to flexibility and durability requirements in woodworking, metalworking, automotive refinishing, and construction.
