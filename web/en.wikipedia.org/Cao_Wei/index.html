Cao Wei (Chinese: 曹魏; pinyin: Cáo Wèi), commonly known as Wei, was one of the three major states that competed for supremacy during the Three Kingdoms period (220–280) of Chinese history. With its capital at Luoyang, it controlled northern China and was the largest and most populous of the three kingdoms, which also included Shu Han in the southwest and Eastern Wu in the southeast.

History

The foundations of Wei were laid by the warlord Cao Cao, who in the final years of the Eastern Han dynasty became chancellor and King of Wei, effectively ruling northern China in the name of Emperor Xian. In 220, Cao Cao's son Cao Pi forced Emperor Xian to abdicate and proclaimed himself emperor, formally ending the Han dynasty.

Wei and Shu Han fought repeatedly over the Hanzhong and Guanzhong regions during Zhuge Liang's northern expeditions (228–234). After Cao Pi's death, power increasingly shifted to the Sima clan: Sima Yi's victory in the Incident at Gaoping Tombs (249) made the Sima family the de facto rulers of Wei. Wei's greatest military triumph came in 263, when its armies under Zhong Hui and Deng Ai conquered Shu Han. In 266, Sima Yi's grandson Sima Yan forced the last Wei emperor, Cao Huan, to abdicate and founded the Jin dynasty, ending Wei.

Government and society

Wei employed the nine-rank system for selecting officials, which favoured aristocratic families, and promoted military-agricultural colonies (tuntian) to restore production in war-devastated regions. The period saw significant cultural achievements, including the poetry of the Jian'an era and the beginnings of Chinese calligraphy as a fine art under Zhong Yao.
