Cellular network

A cellular network or mobile network is a telecommunications network where the link to and from end nodes is wireless and the network is distributed over land areas called cells, each served by at least one fixed-location transceiver known as a base station. These base stations provide the cell with network coverage which can be used for transmission of voice, data and other types of content.

A cell typically uses a different set of frequencies from neighboring cells to avoid interference and provide guaranteed bandwidth within each cell. When joined, these cells provide radio coverage over a wide geographic area. This enables numerous portable devices, such as mobile phones and laptops equipped with mobile broadband modems, to communicate with each other and with fixed transceivers and telephones anywhere in the network via base stations, even if some of the equipment is moving through more than one cell during transmission.

History

The concept of a cellular network was developed by researchers at Bell Labs in the 1970s, notably Douglas H. Ring and W. Rae Young, who described hexagonal cells and frequency reuse. The first commercial cellular network, the 1G generation, was launched by Nippon Telegraph and Telephone (NTT) in Tokyo, Japan, in 1979. Subsequent generations — 2G (GSM, digital voice), 3G (mobile data), 4G LTE, and 5G — progressively increased data rates and capacity.

Cell types

Cells vary in size depending on terrain, capacity needs and radio propagation. Macrocells cover several kilometres and are usually mounted on tall masts; microcells cover a few hundred metres in dense urban areas; picocells serve buildings and venues; and femtocells connect over residential broadband to serve individual homes. The type of a cell is determined by its transmission power, antenna height and interference potential.

Frequency reuse

The key enabling technology of cellular networks is frequency reuse: because mobile transmitters have limited range, the same set of radio channels can be reused in cells far enough apart that interference stays acceptable. The reuse pattern determines total network capacity. Modern systems supplement reuse with sectorization, code division (CDMA), and orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDMA) to increase spectral efficiency.

Handover

As a mobile device moves from one cell to another, the network performs a handover (or handoff), transferring the call or data session to the new base station without dropping it. Handover decisions are based on signal strength measurements reported by the handset and processed by the network.
