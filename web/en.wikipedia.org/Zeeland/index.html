Zeeland (Dutch: [ˈzeːlɑnt], "sea-land") is the westernmost and least populous province of the Netherlands, in the country's southwest. It consists largely of islands and peninsulas in the delta of the Rhine, Meuse and Scheldt rivers, together with a strip of mainland bordering Belgium (Zeelandic Flanders). The capital is Middelburg.

Geography

Much of Zeeland lies at or below sea level, reclaimed from the sea by dikes and polders. The province was devastated by the North Sea flood of 1953, which killed more than 1,800 people in the Netherlands, most of them in Zeeland. In response the Netherlands built the Delta Works, a vast system of dams and storm-surge barriers — including the Oosterscheldekering — that shortened the coastline while leaving the Eastern Scheldt estuary partly open for its ecology and oyster fisheries.

History

Zeeland was contested for centuries between the counts of Holland and Flanders before passing to the Burgundian and Habsburg Netherlands. Middelburg became a major trading city of the Dutch East and West India Companies in the 17th century, and also a significant port in the Dutch slave trade. The province's name travelled far: Abel Tasman sighted the islands he named after Zeeland in 1642 — New Zealand.

Today

Ferries and fixed links — notably the Westerschelde Tunnel (2003) — have ended the islands' isolation. Tourism (especially German visitors drawn to its beaches), agriculture, the port of Vlissingen and chemical industry around Terneuzen, and mussel and oyster cultivation around Yerseke anchor the economy. Zeeuws, a group of dialects closely related to Dutch, is still spoken locally.
