In chemistry, an electrophile is a chemical species that forms a bond to its reaction partner (a nucleophile) by accepting both bonding electrons from that partner. Because electrophiles accept electrons, they are Lewis acids. Most electrophiles are positively charged, have an atom bearing a partial positive charge, or have an atom that does not have a complete octet of electrons.

Electrophilic reactions

The primary reactions of electrophiles are electrophilic addition and electrophilic substitution. In electrophilic aromatic substitution, typical of aromatic compounds, an electrophile such as the nitronium ion (NO2+) attacks the π system of the ring, and a proton is subsequently lost to restore aromaticity. Nitration, halogenation, sulfonation and Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation all proceed by this mechanism. In electrophilic addition to alkenes, the π bond donates electrons to an electrophile (for example Br+ equivalent in bromination), forming a carbocation or bridged intermediate that is then captured by a nucleophile.

Electrophilicity scale

Attempts have been made to rank electrophiles quantitatively. Robert Parr's electrophilicity index, derived from conceptual density functional theory, defines electrophilicity in terms of chemical potential and hardness. Earlier work by Herbert Mayr produced empirical scales relating rate constants of electrophile–nucleophile combinations, widely used to predict reactivity in synthesis.

Superelectrophiles

George Olah proposed the concept of superelectrophiles: cationic electrophiles whose reactivity is greatly enhanced by additional protonation or coordination in superacid media, enabling reactions such as the activation of alkanes.
