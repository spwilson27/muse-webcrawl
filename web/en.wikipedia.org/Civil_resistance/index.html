Civil resistance

Civil resistance is political action by ordinary civilians using nonviolent methods—such as protests, strikes, boycotts, and civil disobedience—to challenge a particular power, policy, or regime. It is closely related to nonviolent resistance and to Gene Sharp's concept of "political jiu-jitsu".

Methods and theory

Theorists including Sharp catalogued hundreds of methods of nonviolent action, grouped into protest and persuasion, noncooperation, and nonviolent intervention. The theory holds that political power depends on the consent and obedience of subjects and institutions; withdrawing that cooperation undermines a ruler's pillars of support. Sharp described how repression against disciplined nonviolent resisters can backfire, shifting loyalties toward the movement.

Historical examples

Prominent cases include Gandhi's campaigns in South Africa and India, notably the Salt March of 1930; the American civil rights movement; the Solidarity movement in Poland; the People Power revolution in the Philippines (1986); the largely peaceful revolutions of 1989 in Eastern Europe; and Serbia's Otpor movement in 2000.

Effectiveness research

Political scientists Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan, studying campaigns from 1900 to 2006, found that nonviolent campaigns seeking regime change succeeded roughly twice as often as violent ones, and that no campaign failed once about 3.5% of the population participated actively at its peak—a finding popularized as the "3.5% rule". Later research has debated the rule's limits and noted declining success rates in the 2010s.
