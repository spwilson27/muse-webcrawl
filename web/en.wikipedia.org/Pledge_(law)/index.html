Pledge (law)

A pledge traditionally involves delivering possession of goods to the creditor.

A pledge is a form of security interest in which a debtor (the pledgor) delivers possession of movable property to a creditor (the pledgee) as security for a debt or other obligation, on the understanding that the property will be returned when the obligation is discharged. The pledge is one of the oldest devices in secured lending: it corresponds to the Roman pignus and underlies the business of pawnbroking, in which small loans are made against pledged personal goods.

Features

Delivery of possession — actual or constructive — is the defining feature of the pledge at common law, distinguishing it from a mortgage or a lien. Constructive delivery may suffice where the goods are bulky, for instance by handing over the keys to a warehouse or documents of title such as bills of lading. The pledgee has a "special property" in the goods: they must take reasonable care of them, may not use them unless agreed, and on default may sell them after giving reasonable notice, accounting to the pledgor for any surplus. The pledgor retains the general property and an equity of redemption.

In practice

Beyond pawnbroking, pledges are used in banking and commerce: shares and securities may be pledged for loans, warehouse receipts pledged for crop finance, and goods in transit pledged against letters of credit. In modern secured-transactions law — such as Article 9 of the US Uniform Commercial Code — the pledge has largely been absorbed into a unitary concept of the security interest, under which "perfection by possession" remains one way to make an interest effective against third parties. In civil-law systems the pledge (gage in French law, Pfandrecht in German law) likewise historically required dispossession, though many jurisdictions now allow registered non-possessory pledges over business assets.

Related concepts

The pledge differs from the hypothecation, in which the debtor retains possession; from the chattel mortgage, which transfers legal title; and from the lien, which is generally a mere right to retain goods already lawfully in the creditor's possession. In finance, securities "pledged" as collateral in repurchase agreements and margin lending follow similar principles.
