Thermoregulation is the ability of an organism to keep its body temperature within certain boundaries, even when the surrounding temperature is very different. A thermoconforming organism, by contrast, simply adopts the temperature of its environment. Maintenance of a stable internal temperature is a key aspect of homeostasis.

Endotherms and ectotherms

Endotherms — chiefly mammals and birds — generate most of their body heat metabolically and actively defend a set-point temperature. Ectotherms — most other animals, including reptiles, amphibians, and invertebrates — rely mainly on behavioral means such as basking and seeking shade, and on environmental heat sources. In humans, the preoptic area of the hypothalamus acts as a thermostat, integrating signals from temperature receptors in the skin and core.

Mechanisms

Responses to cold include vasoconstriction, piloerection, shivering thermogenesis, and heat production in brown adipose tissue; responses to heat include vasodilation and sweating, whose evaporation cools the body. Failure of thermoregulation produces hypothermia or hyperthermia (heat stroke), both potentially fatal. Fever is a regulated rise in the set point, usually in response to infection, rather than a failure of control.
