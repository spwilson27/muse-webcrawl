German reunification (German: Deutsche Wiedervereinigung) was the process in 1990 in which the German Democratic Republic (GDR, East Germany) became part of the Federal Republic of Germany (FRG, West Germany) to form the reunified nation of Germany. The end of the unification process is officially referred to as German unity (Deutsche Einheit), celebrated each year on 3 October as German Unity Day (Tag der Deutschen Einheit).

The East German government started to falter in May 1989, when the removal of Hungary's border fence with Austria opened a hole in the Iron Curtain. The exodus of thousands of East Germans fleeing to West Germany via Hungary intensified the effects of the Monday demonstrations in Leipzig and elsewhere, which grew from people demanding their rights to people demanding unification. The GDR leadership resigned in October 1989, and on 9 November 1989 the Berlin Wall fell, ending nearly three decades of division of the city.

Background

After the end of World War II in Europe, Germany was divided by the Potsdam Agreement among the four Allied powers: the Soviet Union, the United States, the United Kingdom, and France. The western zones merged to form the Federal Republic of Germany on 23 May 1949, while the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic on 7 October 1949. Berlin, though deep within the Soviet zone, was itself divided into four sectors; the construction of the Berlin Wall in August 1961 sealed the last gap in the border between East and West.

For most of the Cold War, reunification was considered unlikely. West German Chancellor Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik in the early 1970s normalised relations between the two states, but the division hardened into a seemingly permanent feature of European politics.

The Peaceful Revolution

In 1989, reform movements swept Eastern Europe. Poland held semi-free elections in June; Hungary began dismantling its border fortifications in May and in September allowed more than 13,000 East German tourists to cross into Austria. In Leipzig, prayers for peace at the St. Nicholas Church grew into the Monday demonstrations, which by 23 October drew some 300,000 people. Protesters chanted Wir sind das Volk ("We are the people").

On 18 October, longtime GDR leader Erich Honecker resigned. On the evening of 9 November 1989, Politburo member Günter Schabowski, in a press conference, mistakenly announced that new travel regulations applied "immediately, without delay." Thousands of East Berliners massed at border crossings, and overwhelmed guards at Bornholmer Straße opened the gates. The Berlin Wall had fallen.

Road to unification

West German Chancellor Helmut Kohl moved quickly. On 28 November 1989 he presented a ten-point programme for gradual unification. In February 1990 he secured Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev's agreement in principle that a united Germany could choose its own alliances, a question settled in July 1990 when Gorbachev accepted German NATO membership in exchange for German financial assistance and limits on the size of the German armed forces.

East Germany's first free elections, on 18 March 1990, were won by the Alliance for Germany, which favoured rapid unification. A Monetary, Economic and Social Union between the two states took effect on 1 July 1990, extending the Deutsche Mark eastward. The Unification Treaty (Einigungsvertrag) was signed on 31 August 1990.

Two Plus Four Treaty

The Treaty on the Final Settlement with Respect to Germany, signed in Moscow on 12 September 1990 by the two German states and the four wartime Allies, terminated the Allies' remaining rights and restored full German sovereignty. The unified Germany confirmed the Oder–Neisse line as its eastern border with Poland.

Unification Day

At midnight on 3 October 1990, the German Democratic Republic acceded to the Federal Republic under Article 23 of the Basic Law and ceased to exist. Its five re-established states — Brandenburg, Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia — became states of the Federal Republic, and reunified Berlin became the capital. Huge celebrations took place at the Reichstag building in Berlin, where the flag of unity, now displayed there permanently, was raised.

Aftermath

Reunification brought enormous economic challenges. The Treuhandanstalt privatised or closed thousands of East German enterprises, and eastern unemployment soared even as infrastructure was rebuilt with transfers financed partly by a "solidarity surcharge" on income tax. Political and social integration — sometimes described as overcoming "the wall in the head" (Mauer im Kopf) — remained an ongoing process decades later. Germany's population rose to about 80 million, making it the most populous country in the European Community, and its reunification is widely regarded as one of the defining events marking the end of the Cold War.
