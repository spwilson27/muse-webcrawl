Académie française

The Académie française is France's principal authority on the French language. Founded in 1635 by Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister to King Louis XIII, it is the oldest of the five academies of the Institut de France. Its mission is to standardize and perfect the French language, and its most visible product is the Dictionnaire de l'Académie française, the official dictionary of French.

The immortals

The Academy consists of forty members, known as les immortels ("the immortals"), who hold their seats for life. New members are elected by the existing body when a seat falls vacant; past members include Corneille, Racine, Voltaire, Victor Hugo and Alexandre Dumas. Members wear the distinctive green embroidered uniform, the habit vert, with a ceremonial sword, at formal sessions under the dome of the Institut de France.

Work

The Academy publishes its dictionary in successive editions — the first appeared in 1694 and the ninth, begun in the late 20th century, was completed in the 2020s. It also issues rulings on grammar, spelling and neologisms, recommending French terms in place of borrowings (for example courriel for "e-mail"). Its recommendations carry moral rather than legal authority, though French law, such as the 1994 Toubon Law, sometimes adopts them for official usage.

History

Suppressed during the French Revolution in 1793, the Academy was restored within the Institut de France in 1803. It also awards literary prizes, most notably the Grand Prix du roman, and administers numerous foundations and prizes for literature and scholarship.
