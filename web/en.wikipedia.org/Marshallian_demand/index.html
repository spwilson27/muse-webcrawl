Marshallian demand function

In microeconomics, a consumer's Marshallian demand function (named after Alfred Marshall) specifies how much of each good the consumer purchases as a function of prices and the consumer's income (or wealth), holding utility maximisation as the underlying problem.

Definition

The Marshallian demand for good i, written xi(p, w), solves the utility maximisation problem: maximise utility u(x) subject to the budget constraint p·x ≤ w. It is "ordinary" or "uncompensated" demand, in contrast to Hicksian (compensated) demand, which minimises expenditure subject to a target utility level.

Properties

Marshallian demands are homogeneous of degree zero in prices and income, satisfy Walras's law (the budget binds), and connect to the indirect utility function via Roy's identity. The total price effect on Marshallian demand decomposes, via the Slutsky equation, into a substitution effect (always negative for a price rise of the good itself) and an income effect.

Applications

Because Marshallian demand depends on observable prices and income, it is the demand concept estimated in empirical work and used to compute consumer surplus, the area under the Marshallian demand curve — a practice originating with Marshall himself.
