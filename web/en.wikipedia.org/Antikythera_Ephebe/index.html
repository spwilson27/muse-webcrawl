Antikythera Ephebe

The Antikythera Ephebe, National Archaeological Museum of Athens

The Antikythera Ephebe, also known as the Antikythera Youth, is a well-preserved ancient Greek bronze statue of a young man recovered in 1900 from the Antikythera wreck off the island of Antikythera in Greece. Standing about 1.94 metres (6 feet 4 inches) tall, the figure is dated to around 340–330 BC and is now housed in the National Archaeological Museum of Athens.

Discovery

Sponge divers working off Antikythera in 1900 located a Roman-era shipwreck dating to the first century BC. Among the cargo of luxury goods being carried to Rome were numerous bronze and marble statues, including the Ephebe, which was recovered in pieces. The same wreck famously yielded the Antikythera mechanism, the earliest known analogue computer.

Description

The statue depicts a nude youth in a dynamic pose, his weight shifted and right arm raised, as though holding an object that has since been lost—variously identified as a sword, an apple, or the Perseus attributes of the hero. His left arm hangs down and the body follows the relaxed contrapposto typical of the school of Lysippos, whose influence is evident in the proportions and the three-dimensional movement of the figure.

Attribution

Scholars have debated the statue's authorship; it is generally attributed to the circle of Lysippos or to Euphranor. The identity of the youth remains uncertain: candidates include the hero Perseus, Paris of Troy, and a generic athlete or ephebe (a young man in military training).
