Peripheral nervous system

The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is one of two components that make up the nervous system of bilateral animals, the other part being the central nervous system (CNS). The PNS consists of nerves and ganglia outside the brain and spinal cord. Its main function is to connect the CNS to the limbs and organs, essentially serving as a relay of signals between the brain and spinal cord and the rest of the body.

Structure

The PNS is divided into the somatic nervous system and the autonomic nervous system. The somatic system controls voluntary movements and transmits sensory information from the skin, muscles and joints. The autonomic system regulates involuntary functions such as heart rate, digestion and respiration, and is subdivided into sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric divisions.

The nerves of the PNS include 12 pairs of cranial nerves arising from the brain and 31 pairs of spinal nerves arising from the spinal cord. Bundles of axons within these nerves carry motor commands outward (efferent fibres) and sensory information inward (afferent fibres). In the PNS, axons are insulated by Schwann cells, which also allow damaged peripheral nerves a limited capacity for regeneration — unlike those in the CNS.

Disease

Diseases of the peripheral nervous system include peripheral neuropathies, in which nerves are damaged by diabetes, toxins, infections or autoimmune attack, producing numbness, tingling, pain and weakness. Guillain–Barré syndrome is an acute autoimmune polyneuropathy; carpal tunnel syndrome results from compression of a single nerve, the median nerve, at the wrist.
