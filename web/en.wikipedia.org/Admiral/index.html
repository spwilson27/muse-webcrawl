Admiral

Admiral is one of the highest naval ranks in some navies. In the Commonwealth nations and the United States, a full admiral is equivalent to a full general in the army or air force. The rank sits below admiral of the fleet (or fleet admiral) and above vice admiral in most systems.

Etymology

The word entered English via Old French from medieval Latin admiralis, derived from the Arabic amīr al-baḥr, "commander of the sea" — a title used in Muslim Sicily and the Crusader-era Mediterranean. The "d" in the English spelling arose through Latin influence. Variants of the title spread through the navies of Europe in the late Middle Ages.

Grades

Modern flag ranks typically include rear admiral, vice admiral, admiral, and (in some navies and often honorary or wartime only) admiral of the fleet. In NATO rank coding, a full admiral is OF-9. In the United States, admiral is a four-star rank, held by the Chief of Naval Operations, combatant commanders, and other senior officers, with fleet admiral (five stars) reserved for wartime and last awarded in 1944–45.

History

England formalised admiral ranks in the 16th century, dividing the fleet into squadrons designated by coloured flags — red, white, and blue — a system that gave rise to the graded titles of admiral, vice admiral, and rear admiral of each colour before being simplified in the 19th century.
