Gravity anomaly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A gravity anomaly is the difference between the observed value of gravity at a point on Earth's surface and the value predicted by a theoretical model of the planet's gravity field. Anomalies reveal variations in the density of subsurface rocks and are a fundamental tool of geophysics and gravimetry, used in mineral and oil exploration, geodesy, and the study of Earth's interior.

Types

The simplest measure, the free-air anomaly, corrects the observed gravity only for latitude and for the elevation of the station above the reference ellipsoid, treating the intervening space as empty ("free air"). The Bouguer anomaly additionally subtracts the gravitational attraction of the rock slab between the station and sea level, and usually a terrain correction for nearby hills and valleys. Isostatic anomalies go further, removing the effect of crustal roots that support mountain ranges under the principle of isostasy.

Causes and interpretation

Positive anomalies indicate excess mass — dense bodies such as ore deposits, mafic intrusions, or uplifted mantle rock — while negative anomalies indicate mass deficits, such as sedimentary basins, salt domes, or caverns. Units are typically milligals (mGal); anomalies of interest range from fractions of a milligal to a few hundred. Satellite missions such as GRACE and GOCE mapped the global gravity field and its time variations, revealing groundwater depletion, ice-sheet mass loss, and post-glacial rebound.

Applications

Gravity surveys are cheap and non-invasive, making them a first step in exploration geophysics. Combined with seismic and magnetic data, anomaly maps constrain models of crustal structure, plate boundaries, and impact craters buried beneath sediments.
