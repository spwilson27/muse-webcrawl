Lost film

A lost film is a film for which no copy is known to exist in any archive or private collection. The great majority of silent-era motion pictures are lost: a 2013 study prepared for the Library of Congress estimated that around 70 percent of American silent feature films are completely lost, and many early sound films have also vanished.

Causes

Most silent films were printed on cellulose nitrate stock, which is highly flammable and chemically unstable, decomposing or combusting in storage vaults; major fires at studio vaults (such as the 1937 Fox vault fire) destroyed entire catalogues. Studios also treated old films as worthless once their commercial run ended, junking prints to reclaim silver from the emulsion. Later losses arose from neglect of television prints and obscure regional productions.

Rediscoveries

Lost films occasionally resurface in foreign archives, private collections, or mislabeled cans — for example, prints found in Dawson City, Canada, in 1978, and the near-complete version of Metropolis discovered in Buenos Aires in 2008. Some films survive only in fragments or in alternate versions, and film historians reconstruct others from scripts, stills, and censorship records. Well-known lost works include London After Midnight (1927) and the Theda Bara version of Cleopatra (1917).
