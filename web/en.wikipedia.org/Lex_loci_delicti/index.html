Lex loci delicti

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The lex loci delicti is the law of the place where a tort was committed.

In conflict of laws (private international law), the lex loci delicti commissi, or simply lex loci delicti (Latin for "the law of the place where the delict [wrong] was committed"), is the substantive law of the jurisdiction in which a tort or delict occurred that is applied to resolve the dispute. It is the traditional choice-of-law rule for tort cases: a court asked to hear a claim arising from conduct in another jurisdiction applies that jurisdiction's tort law.

Application

Determining the place of the wrong is not always straightforward. A negligent act may occur in one state while the resulting injury manifests in another; courts have variously located the tort where the conduct took place, where the injury was sustained, or where the last event necessary to make the actor liable occurred. In the United States, the Restatement (First) of Conflict of Laws (1934), championed by Joseph Beale, adopted the place-of-injury rule, under which the law of the state where the injury occurred governs.

Criticism and reform

The rigid rule was criticized for producing arbitrary results in cases where the place of injury was fortuitous—for example, a car accident just across a state border between residents of another state. The influential New York case Babcock v. Jackson (1963) rejected the traditional rule in favour of an interest-analysis approach, asking which jurisdiction had the greatest concern with the specific issue. Many U.S. states now use more flexible approaches, embodied in the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws' "most significant relationship" test. In the European Union, the Rome II Regulation generally applies the law of the country where the damage occurs (lex loci damni), subject to exceptions.

Related concepts

Analogous Latin terms include lex loci contractus (law of the place of the contract), lex loci celebrationis (law of the place where a marriage was celebrated), lex fori (law of the forum), and lex situs (law of the place where property is situated).
