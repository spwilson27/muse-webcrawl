Matte painting

Matte painting
Type: Visual effects technique
Purpose: Create environments too expensive or impossible to build/film
Modern form: Digital matte painting (2.5D, 3D projection)

A matte painting is a painted representation of a landscape, set or distant location that allows filmmakers to create the illusion of an environment that is not present at the filming location. Historically, matte painters used oil or acrylic paints on large sheets of glass, which were positioned between the camera and the partial set so that the painted and live-action elements blended seamlessly in a single exposure. The term derives from the matte — a mask used to block out parts of the frame during compositing.

History

The technique dates to the earliest years of cinema: Norman Dawn pioneered the glass shot in California around 1907, using painted glass to add roofs, castles and landscapes to real footage. Matte painting became a staple of the studio era, creating the Emerald City in The Wizard of Oz, Xanadu in Citizen Kane, and the vistas of The Birds and Mary Poppins. Artists such as Peter Ellenshaw at Disney and Albert Whitlock at Universal were masters of the craft. The original Star Wars trilogy and Raiders of the Lost Ark used hundreds of matte paintings by artists including Michael Pangrazio and Ralph McQuarrie.

Digital era

From the 1990s, traditional glass paintings were largely replaced by digital matte painting: artists paint in software and project images onto simple 3D geometry ("2.5D"), letting virtual cameras move through painted environments. Films such as Titanic and The Lord of the Rings trilogy made extensive use of digital matte environments, and the discipline remains central to modern visual effects pipelines.
