Emulsion

Emulsion
TypeMixture of two immiscible liquids (colloid)
FormsOil-in-water (O/W); water-in-oil (W/O)
ExamplesMilk, mayonnaise, vinaigrette, photographic film coating

An emulsion is a mixture of two or more liquids that are normally immiscible (unmixable or unblendable). Emulsions are part of a more general class of two-phase systems of matter called colloids. Although the terms colloid and emulsion are sometimes used interchangeably, emulsion should be used when both phases, dispersed and continuous, are liquids.

Types and stability

In an oil-in-water emulsion, oil droplets are dispersed in a continuous water phase (as in milk and mayonnaise); in a water-in-oil emulsion the reverse applies (as in butter). Emulsions are usually unstable, tending to separate over time through creaming, flocculation, coalescence and Ostwald ripening, but they can be stabilised by emulsifying agents (emulsifiers) such as lecithin, egg yolk, mustard, soaps and certain proteins, which reduce interfacial tension and coat droplets.

Appearance

Because droplets scatter light, emulsions usually appear opaque or milky white; dilute emulsions may show a blue tint from the Tyndall effect. Droplet size determines stability and appearance: macroemulsions have droplets of roughly 0.1–100 μm, while microemulsions form spontaneously and are transparent.

Applications

Emulsions are central to foods (dressings, sauces, ice cream), cosmetics and lotions, pharmaceuticals, paints, and bitumen for road surfaces. In photography, the light-sensitive coating of silver halide crystals in gelatin is traditionally called a photographic emulsion, although it is technically a gel suspension rather than a liquid emulsion.
