Underwater glider
Type: Autonomous underwater vehicle
Propulsion: Buoyancy engine
Typical endurance: Months
Speed: ~0.25 m/s

An underwater glider is a type of autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) that employs small changes in its buoyancy together with fixed wings to convert vertical motion into forward motion, allowing it to travel long distances using very little energy. Gliders move in a sawtooth pattern through the water column, profiling the ocean as they go.

Operation

A buoyancy engine — typically a piston or bladder that expels or admits oil — makes the vehicle alternately negatively and positively buoyant. Wings generate lift as the glider sinks or rises, producing horizontal thrust. Steering is achieved by shifting internal mass, such as battery packs, to change the centre of gravity. At the surface the glider obtains a GPS fix and exchanges data by satellite.

Applications

Gliders such as the Slocum, Spray, and Seaglider are widely used in oceanography to measure temperature, salinity, currents, dissolved oxygen, and chlorophyll over missions lasting weeks to months. In 2009 a Slocum glider named Scarlet Knight completed the first transatlantic crossing by an unmanned underwater vehicle. Military and commercial variants are used for surveillance, mine countermeasures research, and environmental monitoring.
