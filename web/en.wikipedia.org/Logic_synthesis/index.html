Logic synthesis

Logic synthesis is a process in electronic design automation (EDA) by which an abstract specification of desired circuit behaviour, typically at the register-transfer level (RTL), is turned into a design implementation in terms of logic gates. It is a central step in the design of integrated circuits and FPGAs, bridging hardware description languages such as Verilog and VHDL and the physical layout of a chip.

Process

The synthesis flow begins with analysis and elaboration of the RTL description, followed by translation into a generic gate-level representation. Combinational logic is then optimised — using Boolean algebra techniques such as two-level minimisation and multi-level factorisation — and technology mapping selects gates from a specific cell library (for an ASIC) or look-up tables (for an FPGA) to meet constraints on timing, area and power. Sequential optimisation, including retiming, can move registers through combinational logic to improve clock speed.

Designers specify constraints, most importantly the target clock period, and the synthesis tool attempts to produce a netlist meeting them. The result feeds into physical design (placement and routing), with formal equivalence checking used to verify that the synthesised netlist implements the same function as the original RTL.

History

Commercial logic synthesis emerged in the 1980s, notably with Synopsys's Design Compiler, transforming digital design from manual gate-level work to the RTL methodology that dominates today. Higher-level synthesis, which generates RTL from algorithmic descriptions in languages such as C, builds on the same foundation.
