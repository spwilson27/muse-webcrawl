Viral vector vaccine

A viral vector vaccine is a vaccine that uses a viral vector to deliver genetic material (DNA or RNA) encoding an antigen from a target pathogen into the recipient's cells. The vector is typically a harmless or attenuated virus — most commonly an adenovirus, but also vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), vaccinia and others — engineered so that it cannot replicate (or replicates only in a limited way) in the human body.

How they work

After injection, the vector enters cells and the inserted gene is expressed, producing the antigen (for example, the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2). The immune system then mounts both antibody and T-cell responses against the antigen, providing protection without exposure to the disease-causing pathogen itself.

Examples

Viral vector vaccines came to prominence with the rVSV-ZEBOV Ebola vaccine (Ervebo, approved 2019) and during the COVID-19 pandemic, when the Oxford–AstraZeneca vaccine (using a chimpanzee adenovirus, ChAdOx1), the Janssen/J&J vaccine (Ad26), Sputnik V (Ad26/Ad5) and Convidecia were deployed at scale. A rare but serious adverse event, thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome, was associated with the adenoviral COVID-19 vaccines. A key limitation is anti-vector immunity, which can blunt booster doses using the same vector.
