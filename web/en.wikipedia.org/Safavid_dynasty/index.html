The Safavid dynasty (Persian: خاندان صفوی) was one of the most significant ruling dynasties of Iran. The Safavids ruled from 1501 to 1736, establishing Twelver Shi'ism as the official religion of the empire and marking the beginning of modern Iranian history. At its height the Safavid Empire stretched from the Caucasus and Mesopotamia to the borders of India, and Safavid Iran was a major centre of trade, art, and architecture.

Origins

The dynasty originated with the Safaviyya, a Sufi order founded by Sheikh Safi al-Din of Ardabil (1252–1334). Over generations the order became increasingly militant, and its Turkmen followers, the Qizilbash ("red heads", after their crimson headgear), provided the military force with which the young Ismail I conquered Tabriz in 1501 and proclaimed himself shah, declaring Twelver Shi'ism the state religion.

Height of the empire

After early conflicts with the Uzbeks and the Ottoman Empire — including the major Ottoman victory at Chaldiran in 1514 — the empire was consolidated under Tahmasp I and reached its zenith under Abbas I (r. 1588–1629), "Abbas the Great". Abbas moved the capital to Isfahan, built its celebrated mosques and palaces around Naqsh-e Jahan Square, reformed the army with the help of the Sherley brothers, expanded trade with European companies, and patronised Persian art, carpets, and miniature painting.

Decline and fall

The 17th and early 18th centuries saw gradually weakening shahs and growing clerical influence. In 1722 Afghan rebels under Mir Mahmud Hotak besieged Isfahan and deposed Sultan Husayn, ending effective Safavid rule. A Safavid claimant line persisted as a puppet of Nader Shah until 1736, when Nader took the throne himself, founding the Afsharid dynasty.

Legacy

The Safavids permanently shaped Iran's identity: the conversion to Shi'ism distinguishes Iran religiously from its Sunni neighbours to this day, and the unification of the Iranian plateau under a single state is often seen as a foundation of the modern nation.
