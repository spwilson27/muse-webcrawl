Claudius (Tiberius Claudius Caesar Augustus Germanicus; 1 August 10 BC – 13 October AD 54) was a Roman emperor, ruling from AD 41 to 54. Born to Drusus and Antonia Minor at Lugdunum in Roman Gaul, he was the first Roman emperor born outside Italy.

Claudius suffered from a limp and slight deafness, and was consequently excluded from public office by his family until his consulship under his nephew Caligula. After Caligula's assassination in AD 41, the Praetorian Guard found Claudius hiding behind a curtain and proclaimed him emperor — the only Roman emperor proclaimed in this manner. Despite his lack of experience, Claudius proved an able and efficient administrator.

Reign

Claudius expanded the empire, most notably with the Roman conquest of Britain in AD 43, which he celebrated with a triumph and the title Britannicus. He built roads, aqueducts (the Aqua Claudia and Anio Novus), and the harbour at Ostia, reformed the legal system, and extended Roman citizenship. His reliance on freedmen secretaries such as Narcissus and Pallas drew resentment from the Senate.

Personal life and death

He was married four times; his third wife, Valeria Messalina, was executed for treason and conspiracy. His fourth wife, his niece Agrippina the Younger, persuaded him to adopt her son Nero and displace his own son Britannicus. Claudius died in AD 54, traditionally said to have been poisoned with mushrooms by Agrippina, and was succeeded by Nero. He was deified shortly after his death.

Legacy

A scholar as well as an emperor, Claudius wrote histories of the Etruscans and Carthage and proposed letters to reform the Latin alphabet. Robert Graves's novels I, Claudius (1934) and Claudius the God (1935), and their BBC television adaptation (1976), shaped his modern reputation as an unlikely but capable ruler.
