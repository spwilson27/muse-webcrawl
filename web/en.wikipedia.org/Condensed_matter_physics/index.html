Condensed matter physics

Condensed matter physics is the field of physics that deals with the macroscopic and microscopic physical properties of matter, especially the solid and liquid phases, which arise from electromagnetic forces between atoms and electrons. More generally, the subject deals with condensed phases of matter: systems of many constituents with strong interactions among them. Examples include solids and liquids, which arise from the electromagnetic forces between atoms and electrons.

Condensed matter physicists seek to understand the behavior of these phases by experiments to measure various material properties, and by applying the physical laws of quantum mechanics, electromagnetism, statistical mechanics, and other theories to develop mathematical models.

History

The diversity of systems and phenomena available for study makes condensed matter physics the largest field of contemporary physics by percentage of physicists in the field. Historically, condensed matter physics grew out of solid-state physics, now considered one of its main subfields. The term condensed matter physics was arguably coined by Philip Anderson when he renamed his research group at the Cavendish Laboratory in 1967.

One of the reasons for renaming the field "condensed matter" is that many of the concepts and techniques developed for studying solids also apply to fluid systems. For example, the conduction electrons in an electrical conductor form a quantum fluid known as a Fermi liquid, which has many properties similar to classical liquids.

Topics

Major topics in condensed matter physics include superconductivity, magnetism, phase transitions, electronic band structure, topological phases of matter, and the study of soft materials such as polymers and glasses. The field overlaps with chemistry, materials science, and nanotechnology, and has close ties to atomic physics and biophysics.

Applications

Research in condensed matter physics has given rise to several important technological applications, such as the development of the semiconductor transistor, laser, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and the study of superconductors for lossless power transmission. The field is also central to the development of quantum computing.
