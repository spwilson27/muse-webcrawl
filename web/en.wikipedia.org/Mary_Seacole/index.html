Mary Seacole

Mary Jane Seacole (23 November 1805 – 14 May 1881) was a British-Jamaican businesswoman and nurse who set up the "British Hotel" behind the lines during the Crimean War, providing sustenance and care for wounded and convalescent British officers and soldiers. Coming from a tradition of Jamaican "doctresses" who used herbal remedies, she was posthumously awarded the Jamaican Order of Merit in 1991 and in 2004 was voted the greatest Black Briton.

Mary Seacole, Jamaican nurse of the Crimean War.

Early life

Mary Grant was born in Kingston, Jamaica, to a Scottish soldier father and a free Black Jamaican mother who ran a boarding house and practised traditional medicine. Mary learned nursing skills and herbal remedies from her mother and travelled widely in the Caribbean and Central America, nursing during cholera and yellow fever epidemics in Jamaica and Panama. In 1836 she married Edwin Horatio Hamilton Seacole, who died in 1844.

Crimean War

When the Crimean War began in 1853, Seacole travelled to London and applied to join the official nursing contingent but was refused — whether because of her race, age, or lack of formal credentials remains debated. Undeterred, she travelled to Crimea at her own expense with a business partner and established the "British Hotel" near Balaclava: a store, canteen, and informal hospital. She visited the battlefield, sometimes under fire, dispensing food, drink, and care, and was affectionately known to the soldiers as "Mother Seacole".

Later life and memoir

The war's end left Seacole bankrupt, but a benefit campaign supported by senior officers raised funds for her. In 1857 she published her autobiography, Wonderful Adventures of Mrs. Seacole in Many Lands, one of the earliest autobiographies by a mixed-race woman in Britain. She died in London in 1881, largely forgotten, before a revival of interest from the 1980s onward. A statue of Seacole was unveiled at St Thomas' Hospital, London, in 2016 — the first statue in the UK honouring a named Black woman.
