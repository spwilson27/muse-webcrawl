War on drugs

The war on drugs is a global campaign, led by the U.S. federal government, of drug prohibition, military aid, and military intervention, with the aim of reducing the illegal drug trade in the United States. The initiative includes a set of drug policies that are intended to discourage the production, distribution, and consumption of psychoactive drugs that participating governments and the UN have deemed illegal.

History

The term was popularized by the media after a press conference given on June 18, 1971, by President Richard Nixon, who declared drug abuse "public enemy number one." The campaign built on earlier prohibition regimes, including the Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 and the Controlled Substances Act of 1970. President Ronald Reagan greatly expanded the policy in the 1980s, with mandatory minimum sentences and the "Just Say No" campaign, amid the crack cocaine epidemic. In 1988 Congress created the Office of National Drug Control Policy, headed by a "drug czar."

Criticism

Critics argue the war on drugs has produced mass incarceration, racially disparate enforcement, and violence in producer and transit countries — notably in Mexico and Colombia — while failing to reduce drug supply or use. The U.S. has spent an estimated trillion dollars on the effort since 1971. In the 21st century, many jurisdictions have moved toward decriminalization and legalization of cannabis, and toward treating addiction as a public-health issue.
