Trace amine

Phenethylamine and tyramine, two classic trace amines

Trace amines are an endogenous group of compounds structurally related to the classical monoamine neurotransmitters (dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin), but present in the mammalian nervous system at trace concentrations, generally below 100 ng per gram of tissue. They are formed by decarboxylation of amino acids and include phenethylamine, p-tyramine, tryptamine, octopamine, synephrine, and N,N-dimethyltryptamine (DMT) among others.

History

Trace amines were long studied as "false neurotransmitters" and for their sympathomimetic effects — tyramine in aged cheese, for instance, can provoke hypertensive crises in patients taking monoamine oxidase inhibitors (the "cheese effect"). Interest was transformed in 2001 by the discovery of a dedicated family of receptors, the trace amine-associated receptors (TAARs), in particular TAAR1, which is expressed in monoamine neurons and modulates their firing.

Function

Through TAAR1 and transporter interactions, trace amines act as neuromodulators that fine-tune dopaminergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic signalling. Dysregulation has been implicated in schizophrenia, depression, ADHD, migraine, and disorders of appetite. Amphetamine-class drugs are themselves potent TAAR1 agonists in addition to their transporter actions, making the receptor a target of drug development, including antipsychotic candidates.

In invertebrates

In insects and other invertebrates, tyramine and octopamine are far more abundant and serve as major neurotransmitters and neurohormones — functional counterparts of vertebrate adrenaline — regulating flight, aggression, and reward.
