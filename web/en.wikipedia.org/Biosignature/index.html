Biosignature

A biosignature (sometimes called a chemical fossil or molecular fossil) is any substance — such as an element, isotope, or molecule — or phenomenon that provides scientific evidence of past or present life. Measurable attributes of life include its complex physical or chemical structures, its use of free energy, and the production of biomass and wastes.

Types

Proposed biosignatures include atmospheric gases in chemical disequilibrium, such as oxygen together with methane on an exoplanet; isotopic fractionation patterns characteristic of biological processing; specific organic molecules such as lipids preserved in rocks; and physical structures such as stromatolites. The Viking lander experiments on Mars and claims about the Martian meteorite ALH84001 illustrate the difficulty of ruling out abiotic explanations.

Search

Biosignatures are sought in the exploration of Mars and icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus, and in the spectroscopic study of exoplanet atmospheres by telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope. Because individual signals can usually be produced without life, scientists look for multiple, mutually reinforcing lines of evidence in geological and planetary context.
