Repressed memory

The concept originates in Freud's theory of repression.

A repressed memory is a memory of a traumatic event that is alleged to have been unconsciously blocked from conscious recall by the psychological defence mechanism of repression, sometimes for years or decades, and later recovered, often during psychotherapy. The concept derives from the early work of Sigmund Freud and became the centre of one of modern psychology's most heated controversies.

The memory wars

During the 1980s and early 1990s, a wave of "recovered memory therapy" produced numerous claims in which patients reported recovering memories of childhood abuse, sometimes in the context of the Satanic panic. Critics, including memory researcher Elizabeth Loftus, argued that the therapeutic techniques used — hypnosis, guided imagery, and suggestion — could create vivid false memories, and a number of accusations were retracted or overturned. Professional bodies concluded that while traumatic experiences can be forgotten and later recalled, there is little reliable evidence for repression as Freud described it, and that delayed recall cannot be assumed either true or false without corroboration.

Scientific status

Research on trauma generally finds that traumatic events are remembered all too well rather than banished from awareness, though ordinary forgetting and avoidance of reminders are also documented. The concept of repressed memory remains influential in popular culture and some clinical practice, but it is regarded with scepticism by most memory scientists.
