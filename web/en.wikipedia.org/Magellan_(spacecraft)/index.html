Magellan (spacecraft)

The Magellan spacecraft was a NASA robotic probe launched in 1989 to map the surface of Venus using synthetic-aperture radar, which could penetrate the planet's thick cloud cover. Named after the Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan, it produced the first high-resolution global maps of Venus and revealed a young, volcanically resurfaced planet. Magellan was the first interplanetary mission launched aboard a Space Shuttle.

Magellan used a large dish antenna both for radar mapping and Earth communications.

Mission

Magellan was released from the payload bay of Space Shuttle Atlantis (STS-30) and boosted toward Venus by an Inertial Upper Stage, arriving on 10 August 1990. Much of the spacecraft was built from spare parts from other missions, including the Voyager bus and the Galileo high-gain antenna, which served as its radar dish. In a near-polar elliptical orbit of about three hours, Magellan mapped a swath of the surface on each pass; over its first 243-day cycle it imaged about 84% of the planet, and subsequent cycles raised coverage to about 98% with resolution around 100 metres. Later cycles measured gravity by tracking the spacecraft's radio signal and gathered stereo and radiometry data.

Discoveries

The maps showed a surface dominated by volcanic plains, thousands of shield volcanoes, lava channels thousands of kilometres long, and pancake-shaped volcanic domes, yet with remarkably few impact craters — evidence that the surface is geologically young (roughly 300–600 million years old) and was extensively resurfaced, possibly in a catastrophic episode. Tesserae — highly deformed highland terrain — and arachnoids and coronae suggested a complex tectonic history without Earth-style plate tectonics.

End of mission

In 1993 Magellan pioneered aerobraking, dipping into Venus's upper atmosphere to circularise its orbit — a technique later used at Mars. In October 1994, during the "windmill" experiment measuring atmospheric drag, controllers deliberately lowered the orbit; contact was lost on 13 October 1994 and the spacecraft burned up in the Venusian atmosphere shortly afterwards.
