Militia Acts of 1792

The Militia Acts of 1792 were two statutes enacted by the Second United States Congress that organised the state militias and granted the President authority to call them into federal service. They implemented the militia clauses of the U.S. Constitution and defined the structure of the American militia for over a century.

Provisions

The first act (2 May 1792) authorised the President to call forth the militia to repel invasion, suppress insurrection, or execute federal law — famously first used against the Whiskey Rebellion in 1794 — initially requiring certification by a federal judge, a requirement removed in 1795. The second act, the Uniform Militia Act (8 May 1792), required every free able-bodied white male citizen aged 18 to 45 to enrol in his state militia and to arm himself at his own expense with a musket or firelock, bayonet, cartridge box and ammunition, organising the militia into divisions, brigades, regiments, battalions and companies with state-appointed officers.

History and legacy

Enforcement of the self-arming and muster requirements quickly decayed, and the enrolled "universal" militia became largely nominal, while volunteer uniformed companies did most actual service in the War of 1812 and the Civil War. The system was finally replaced by the Militia Act of 1903 (the Dick Act), which divided the militia into the organised militia — the National Guard — and the reserve (unorganised) militia. The 1792 acts' enrolment language survives today in modified form in 10 U.S.C. § 246, and the acts are frequently cited in debates over the Second Amendment and the historical meaning of the militia.
