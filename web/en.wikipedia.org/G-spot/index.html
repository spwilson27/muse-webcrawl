G-spot

The G-spot, also called the Gräfenberg spot (for German gynecologist Ernst Gräfenberg), is characterized as an erogenous area of the vagina that, when stimulated, may lead to strong sexual arousal, powerful orgasms and potential female ejaculation. It is typically reported to be located 5–8 cm (2–3 in) up the front (anterior) vaginal wall between the vaginal opening and the urethra and is a sensitive area that may be part of the female prostate.

History

The concept was popularized by Alice Kahn Ladas, Beverly Whipple, and John D. Perry in their 1982 book The G Spot and Other Recent Discoveries About Human Sexuality, building on Gräfenberg's earlier work in the 1950s and on Regnier de Graaf's 17th-century descriptions of female anatomy. The term entered popular culture rapidly, though scientific debate about its anatomical reality followed just as quickly.

Scientific debate

Whether the G-spot exists as a discrete anatomical structure is a topic of considerable debate among researchers. Reviews have noted that typical definitions are based on subjective reports, and anatomical and imaging studies have produced conflicting results; some researchers argue stimulation in the area acts through the internal structures of the clitoris and the urethral sponge (the clitourethrovaginal complex) rather than a distinct organ. A 2017 review concluded the evidence for a discrete G-spot remained unconvincing, while others maintain the area is functionally significant.
