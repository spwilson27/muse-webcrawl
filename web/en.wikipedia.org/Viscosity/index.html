Viscosity

Liquids with low (top) and high (bottom) viscosity

Common symbols: η, μ

SI unit: Pascal-second (Pa·s)

Derivations: Shear stress / shear rate

Viscosity is a measure of a fluid's resistance to deformation at a given rate — informally, its "thickness". Honey has a much higher viscosity than water. More precisely, viscosity describes the internal friction of a moving fluid: a fluid with large viscosity resists motion because its molecular makeup gives it internal friction, while a fluid with low viscosity flows easily.

Definition

For a Newtonian fluid, dynamic viscosity μ relates shear stress τ to the velocity gradient (shear rate) by τ = μ(du/dy). The SI unit is the pascal-second. Kinematic viscosity ν is the ratio of dynamic viscosity to density, ν = μ/ρ, measured in square metres per second. Viscosity is measured with viscometers and rheometers.

Temperature dependence

The viscosity of liquids generally decreases with increasing temperature, whereas the viscosity of gases increases with temperature. Engine oils are graded by viscosity, and multi-grade oils use additives to reduce this temperature sensitivity, quantified by the viscosity index.

Examples

At 20 °C, water has a viscosity of about 1.0 mPa·s, olive oil about 56 mPa·s, and honey roughly 2,000–10,000 mPa·s. Pitch is so viscous that it appears solid yet flows over years, as shown in the pitch drop experiment begun in 1927.
