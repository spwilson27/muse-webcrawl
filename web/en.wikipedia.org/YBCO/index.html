Yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO) is a family of crystalline chemical compounds famous for displaying high-temperature superconductivity. The best-known member, YBa2Cu3O7−x, was reported in 1987 by Maw-Kuen Wu, Paul Chu and colleagues, and was the first material discovered to become superconducting above 77 K, the boiling point of liquid nitrogen — a landmark that made cooling practical and cheap.

Structure and properties

YBCO is a layered cuprate with an oxygen-deficient perovskite structure. Copper-oxide (CuO2) planes carry the supercurrent, while chains of CuO along one axis make the material orthorhombic and anisotropic. Superconductivity is extremely sensitive to oxygen content: the fully oxygenated compound (x ≈ 0) has a critical temperature near 92 K, while the oxygen-poor tetragonal phase is not superconducting at all.

Applications

Because it is a brittle ceramic, YBCO is used mainly as a thin film deposited on flexible metal tapes — "coated conductors" — employed in high-field magnets, fault-current limiters, power cables, magnetic levitation demonstrations and prototype fusion devices. Bulk melt-textured YBCO can trap magnetic fields stronger than any permanent magnet and is studied for flywheels and motors.

History

The discovery, following Bednorz and Müller's 1986 finding of superconductivity in lanthanum-based cuprates, triggered intense worldwide research ("the Woodstock of physics", March 1987) and launched the era of cuprate high-temperature superconductors.
