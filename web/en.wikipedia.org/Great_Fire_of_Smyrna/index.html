Great Fire of Smyrna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Fire of Smyrna (also called the Burning of Smyrna) was a fire that destroyed much of the port city of Smyrna (modern İzmir, Turkey) in September 1922, in the closing phase of the Greco-Turkish War. Beginning on 13 September 1922, four days after Turkish forces retook the city from Greek occupation, the blaze consumed the Armenian and Greek quarters and the European commercial district, while the Turkish and Jewish quarters were largely spared.

Course of the fire

Hundreds of thousands of Greek and Armenian refugees had crowded into the city fleeing the Turkish advance. When the fire spread, driven by wind, the population was trapped between the flames and the sea; crowds gathered on the quay as Allied warships in the harbour initially declined to take refugees aboard. Over the following days, an evacuation organised largely by American relief workers, including Asa Jennings, ferried survivors to Greek islands. Estimates of the dead range from about 10,000 to 100,000; some 30,000 Greek and Armenian men were deported to labour battalions in the interior, few of whom survived.

Responsibility and aftermath

Responsibility for the fire remains disputed. Greek and Armenian sources and many historians attribute it to Turkish soldiers and irregulars as part of the destruction of the Christian population; Turkish official historiography has blamed Armenian or Greek arsonists. Eyewitness accounts by foreign consuls, journalists, and relief workers describe Turkish troops setting fires and preventing firefighting in the Christian quarters. The catastrophe effectively ended the millennia-old Greek presence in Anatolia; the subsequent Treaty of Lausanne (1923) formalised a compulsory population exchange between Greece and Turkey. Smyrna, once a cosmopolitan Levantine port, was rebuilt as Turkish İzmir.
