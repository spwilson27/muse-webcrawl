Commune (model of government)

For the intentional community, see Commune.

A commune, as a model of government, is a self-governing local community or municipality, typically the smallest administrative division in a state, governed directly by its citizens or through elected councils. The concept has deep roots in medieval European town government and was famously embodied by the Paris Commune of 1871.

Barricade during the Paris Commune, 1871

Medieval origins

In medieval Europe, communes were associations of townspeople who swore mutual oaths to govern themselves independently of feudal lords and bishops. They emerged most strongly in northern Italy and France during the 11th and 12th centuries, establishing elected consuls, assemblies and charters of liberties. Many modern municipal institutions descend from these medieval communes.

The Paris Commune

The Paris Commune of 1871 was a revolutionary government that ruled Paris from March to May 1871. Elected by universal male suffrage, its council enacted measures including the separation of church and state, workers' self-management of abandoned factories, and the abolition of night work for bakers. It was suppressed during the "Bloody Week" of May 1871. Karl Marx praised it in The Civil War in France as the first example of the dictatorship of the proletariat.

Communes in modern states

In France, the commune remains the smallest administrative unit, with over 34,000 communes each governed by a mayor and municipal council. Similar units exist in Italy (comune), Spain, Switzerland, China, and many other countries. In the People's Republic of China, "people's communes" were large collectivised units established during the Great Leap Forward of 1958.

Political theory

Political theorists from Marx and Bakunin to later libertarian municipalists have treated the commune as the fundamental cell of a free federation, emphasising direct democracy, recallable delegates, and the fusion of legislative and executive functions.
