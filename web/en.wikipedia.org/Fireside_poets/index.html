Fireside poets

The Fireside poets were America's first widely popular literary group.

The Fireside poets (also called the household poets) were a group of nineteenth-century American poets based in New England. The group is generally taken to include Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, William Cullen Bryant, John Greenleaf Whittier, James Russell Lowell, and Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. They were the first American poets whose popularity rivalled that of British poets, both at home and abroad.

Name and style

The name "Fireside poets" derives from their popularity as entertainment for families reading aloud by the fireside. Their poetry was often written for the general reader: it employed conventional forms and meters, standard diction, and themes of domestic life, nature, morality, patriotism, and legend, making it suitable for memorisation and recitation in schools and homes. Longfellow's narrative poems such as The Song of Hiawatha (1855) and Paul Revere's Ride (1860) became staples of American culture, as did Whittier's Snow-Bound and Holmes's "Old Ironsides".

Influence and reputation

Most of the group were also scholars and public figures associated with Harvard and with magazines such as The Atlantic Monthly, which Lowell and Holmes helped to found and name. The Fireside poets dominated American literary taste through much of the nineteenth century; in the twentieth century their work fell out of critical fashion as modernism prized experimentation, though their most famous poems remain widely anthologised and memorised.
