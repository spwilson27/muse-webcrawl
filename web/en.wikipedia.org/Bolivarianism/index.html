Bolivarianism

Bolivarianism is a set of doctrines and political ideas associated with Simón Bolívar (1783–1830), the Venezuelan military and political leader who led several South American countries to independence from Spanish rule. In its modern form, the term is most closely associated with the political movement of Hugo Chávez in Venezuela and his successors, which combined invocations of Bolívar with socialism, nationalism, and Latin American integration.

Bolívar's own ideas

Bolívar's writings, such as the Jamaica Letter (1815) and the Angostura Address (1819), envisioned independent Spanish American republics with strong central authority, lifetime senates tempered by republican institutions, and ultimately a league or confederation of American states. He was skeptical that the region was ready for full democracy and feared anarchy as much as tyranny.

Chavismo

Hugo Chávez, elected president of Venezuela in 1998, recast Bolivarianism as a "Bolivarian Revolution": a program of participatory democracy, social missions funded by oil revenue, land and industry nationalizations, opposition to United States influence, and regional alliances such as ALBA. In 1999 Venezuela was renamed the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela. Chávez described the project as "socialism of the 21st century".

Criticism and legacy

Critics argue that modern Bolivarianism departs sharply from Bolívar's thought, concentrating power, undermining institutions, and producing economic collapse under Chávez's successor Nicolás Maduro; supporters credit it with poverty reduction and asserting national sovereignty. The cult of Bolívar in Venezuela long predates Chávez, having been cultivated by 19th-century governments as a unifying national symbol.
