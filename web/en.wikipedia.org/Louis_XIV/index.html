Louis XIV (1638–1715), known as Louis the Great or the Sun King, was King of France from 1643 until his death in 1715. His reign of 72 years and 110 days is the longest of any monarch of a sovereign state in European history. He is the archetype of the absolute monarch, famously associated with the (likely apocryphal) phrase "L'État, c'est moi" ("I am the state").

Personal rule

Louis ascended the throne aged four; Cardinal Mazarin governed during his minority, which was troubled by the Fronde revolts. On Mazarin's death in 1661, Louis announced he would rule without a chief minister. With finance minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert, he reformed taxation and industry, curtailed the power of the nobility by drawing them to court, and professionalized the army.

Versailles and culture

In 1682 Louis moved the court to the Palace of Versailles, transformed from a hunting lodge into the most splendid court in Europe, whose Hall of Mirrors and gardens — designed by André Le Nôtre — became the model for absolutist display. He patronized Molière, Racine, Lully and the French Academy's cultural projects, making French the language of European diplomacy and fashion.

Wars and religion

Louis fought a series of major wars — the War of Devolution, the Franco-Dutch War, the Nine Years' War and the War of the Spanish Succession — expanding France's frontiers but straining its finances. In 1685 he revoked the Edict of Nantes, ending protections for Huguenots and driving hundreds of thousands of Protestants into exile. He died in 1715, succeeded by his five-year-old great-grandson Louis XV.
