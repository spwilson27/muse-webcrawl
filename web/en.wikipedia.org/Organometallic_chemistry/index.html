Organometallic chemistry

Organometallic chemistry is the study of organometallic compounds, chemical compounds containing at least one chemical bond between a carbon atom of an organic molecule and a metal, including alkaline, alkaline earth, and transition metals, and sometimes broadened to include metalloids like boron, silicon, or selenium. Aside from bonds to organyl fragments or molecules, bonds to inorganic carbon, such as carbon monoxide, cyanide, and carbides, are generally considered to characterize organometallic compounds as well.

The field combines aspects of traditional inorganic and organic chemistry. Organometallic compounds are widely used both stoichiometrically in research and industrially as homogeneous catalysts.

History

Early developments include Louis Claude Cadet's synthesis of methyl arsenic compounds around 1760, Edward Frankland's discovery of diethylzinc in 1849, and Ludwig Mond's discovery of nickel tetracarbonyl in 1890. The field transformed in 1951 with the accidental discovery of ferrocene, whose sandwich structure was deduced by Geoffrey Wilkinson and Ernst Otto Fischer, work that later earned them the 1973 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Karl Ziegler and Giulio Natta received the 1963 Nobel Prize for catalysts for polymerization.

Key concepts

The 18-electron rule, a counterpart of the octet rule, is useful for predicting the stability of metal carbonyls and related complexes. Organometallic reactivity is commonly classified by elementary steps: oxidative addition, reductive elimination, migratory insertion, and beta-hydride elimination. Important compound classes include metal carbonyls, metallocenes, alkyl and aryl complexes, carbene complexes, and Grignard reagents (organomagnesium compounds).

Applications

Organometallic catalysts underpin many industrial processes: hydroformylation, the Monsanto and Cativa acetic acid processes, Ziegler–Natta polymerization of olefins, and olefin metathesis. In synthesis, cross-coupling reactions such as the Suzuki, Heck, and Negishi reactions are indispensable for building carbon–carbon bonds, work recognized by the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
