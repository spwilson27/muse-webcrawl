Distortion (music)

Distortion

Type: Audio effect

Related effects: Overdrive, fuzz

Used in: Rock, blues, heavy metal, punk

Common devices: Amplifier overdrive, effects pedals

Distortion and overdrive are forms of audio signal processing used to alter the sound of amplified electric musical instruments, usually by increasing their gain, producing a "fuzzy", "growling", or "gritty" tone. Distortion is most commonly used with the electric guitar, but may also be used with other electric instruments such as electric bass, electric piano, synthesizer and Hammond organ.

How it works

Distortion effects occur when an audio signal is amplified beyond the point at which a device can reproduce it cleanly, causing the waveform to clip. Clipping adds harmonic and inharmonic overtones and compresses the signal, increasing sustain. Tube amplifiers produce a gradual, "soft" clipping when overdriven, which many guitarists consider warm and musical; solid-state circuits tend toward harsher "hard" clipping, emulated by devices using clipping diodes.

History

Guitarists in the 1940s and 1950s obtained distortion by turning small amplifiers up to full volume, sometimes with damaged speakers — as on the 1951 recording "Rocket 88". Deliberate distortion grew in the 1960s: The Kinks' "You Really Got Me" (1964) used a slashed speaker cone, while the Maestro FZ-1 Fuzz-Tone pedal, popularized by "(I Can't Get No) Satisfaction" (1965), made the effect reproducible on demand. By the late 1960s and 1970s, high-gain distortion became the foundation of hard rock and heavy metal.

Types

  • Overdrive — mild to moderate clipping, often simulating an overdriven tube amplifier.
  • Distortion — harder clipping with more sustain and harmonic content, typical of heavy rock and metal.
  • Fuzz — extreme clipping that produces an almost square-wave, "fuzzy" timbre, associated with 1960s psychedelic rock.

Use in genres

Distortion is a defining element of blues-rock, hard rock, punk rock, and virtually all forms of heavy metal, where layered high-gain rhythm guitar tones accompany techniques such as palm muting. It is also used in electronic music, industrial music, and hip hop production to toughen synthesizer and drum sounds.
