Georgian era

The Georgian era was a period in British history from 1714 to c. 1830–1837, named after the Hanoverian kings George I, George II, George III and George IV, who reigned in succession. The term is sometimes also applied to the short reign of William IV, ending with the accession of Queen Victoria in 1837. The last decade of the period, during George IV's regency and reign, is often treated separately as the Regency era.

Politics and society

The era began with the accession of George I after the death of Queen Anne, the last Stuart monarch, under the Act of Settlement 1701. It saw the rise of the office of prime minister — Robert Walpole is often called the first — and a political order dominated by Whig and Tory parties. Britain fought major wars, including the War of the Austrian Succession, the Seven Years' War, the American War of Independence, and the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars, emerging in 1815 as the world's leading naval and colonial power.

Economy and industry

The Georgian period encompassed the agricultural revolution and the beginnings of the Industrial Revolution, with the spread of canals, coal mining, textile mills and steam power. Enclosure transformed the rural landscape, while towns and ports such as London, Liverpool, Manchester and Bath grew rapidly. The era also saw Britain's deep involvement in the transatlantic slave trade and the campaign that led to the abolition of the trade in 1807 and of slavery itself in most of the empire in 1833.

Culture and architecture

Georgian architecture, characterised by symmetry, proportion and classical detail — terraces, crescents and squares of brick or stucco — still defines many British cityscapes. The period's literature includes the novels of Jane Austen, the poetry of William Blake and the Romantics, and the works of Samuel Johnson, whose dictionary appeared in 1755. In art, Thomas Gainsborough and Joshua Reynolds dominated portraiture, while the periodical essay, the coffeehouse and the circulating library shaped an expanding public sphere.

End of the era

George IV died in 1830 and was succeeded by his brother William IV. The era is generally taken to end with the start of the Victorian era in 1837, by which time the Reform Act 1832 had begun the transformation of British parliamentary politics.
