Kamikaze

Kamikaze (神風, "divine wind" or "spirit wind") were suicide attacks carried out by Japanese military aviators against Allied naval vessels in the closing stages of the Pacific theatre of World War II. Officially called tokubetsu kōgekitai ("special attack units"), the attacks began in October 1944 during the Battle of Leyte Gulf and continued until the end of the war in August 1945.

Origins of the name

The word kamikaze originally referred to the typhoons of 1274 and 1281 that destroyed the fleets of Kublai Khan attempting to invade Japan — storms celebrated as divine protection. The suicide units revived the name to evoke that deliverance.

The attacks

Vice Admiral Takijirō Ōnishi organised the first formal units in the Philippines in October 1944, as Japan's conventional air power collapsed. Pilots — many of them very young volunteers — flew explosive-laden aircraft, often Zero fighters, directly into enemy ships. Roughly 3,800 kamikaze pilots died. The attacks sank dozens of Allied ships (estimates range from about 34 to 47 sunk) and damaged hundreds more, with the heaviest toll during the Battle of Okinawa in spring 1945, where massed kikusui attacks were launched. Related special attacks included the kaiten human torpedoes and the rocket-powered Yokosuka MXY-7 Ohka.

Legacy

The kamikaze remain a powerful and controversial symbol in Japan, commemorated at museums such as Chiran, while for Allied veterans they epitomised the ferocity of the war's final phase. In modern English, "kamikaze" has broadened to describe any reckless, self-destructive act.
