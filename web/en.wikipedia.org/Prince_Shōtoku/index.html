Prince Shōtoku

Prince Shōtoku (聖徳太子, 574–622), also known as Prince Umayado, was a semi-legendary regent and statesman of the Asuka period in Japan. He served as regent under his aunt, Empress Suiko, from 593 until his death, and is credited with founding a strong, centralized government based on Chinese models, and with promoting Buddhism in Japan.

Reforms

Shōtoku is traditionally credited with the Twelve Level Cap and Rank System (603), which graded court officials by merit rather than heredity, and with the Seventeen-article Constitution (604), a moral and political code emphasising harmony, honesty in office, and obedience to imperial authority — more a set of Confucian and Buddhist-inspired injunctions than a constitution in the modern sense. He also resumed formal diplomatic missions to Sui China (the kentōshi), famously sending a letter addressing the Chinese emperor from "the Son of Heaven in the land where the sun rises" — an assertion of equality that reportedly displeased the Sui court.

Buddhism

A devout Buddhist, Shōtoku sponsored the construction of temples including Shitennō-ji in Osaka and Hōryū-ji near Nara, whose surviving buildings are among the oldest wooden structures in the world. He is traditionally regarded as an author of commentaries on Buddhist sutras and, in later centuries, was venerated almost as a saint and founder of Japanese Buddhism.

Historical debate

Modern scholars have questioned how much of the traditional account — drawn largely from the Nihon Shoki (720) — is factual, noting the absence of contemporary records and the possibility that some achievements attributed to Shōtoku belong to his contemporaries or were embellished to lend the imperial house a sage founder. His image appeared on Japanese banknotes for much of the twentieth century.
