Sympatric speciation

Sympatric speciation is the evolution of a new species from a surviving ancestral species while both continue to inhabit the same geographic region. Unlike allopatric speciation, it occurs without physical isolation, so reproductive isolation must evolve despite ongoing opportunities for interbreeding.

Mechanisms

Disruptive selection on an ecological trait can split a population into specialists, and if mating is linked to that trait (for example, host choice in insects), gene flow collapses. Sexual selection and chromosomal changes can also drive isolation. In plants, polyploidy — duplication of the chromosome set, often via hybridisation (allopolyploidy) — creates instant reproductive isolation and is the best-documented sympatric route; many crop species, including wheat, arose this way.

Examples

Famous proposed cases include the apple maggot fly Rhagoletis pomonella, which shifted from hawthorn to introduced apples in 19th-century North America; cichlid fish in small African crater lakes; and the palm species on Lord Howe Island that diverged by flowering time on different soils. Confirming sympatry requires showing the populations were panmictic at the outset, which is why cases remain debated.

History of the idea

Long regarded as implausible because recombination destroys incipient isolation, sympatric speciation gained theoretical support from models by John Maynard Smith and others, and is now accepted as real, though rarer than allopatric speciation.
