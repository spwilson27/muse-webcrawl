Discrete logarithm

Discrete logarithm
FieldNumber theory, group theory, cryptography
ProblemGiven g and h = gk, find the exponent k
StatusNo known efficient classical algorithm for well-chosen groups
Used inDiffie–Hellman key exchange, ElGamal, DSA, elliptic-curve cryptography

In mathematics, for given real numbers a and b, the logarithm logb a is a number x such that bx = a. Analogously, in any group G, powers bk can be defined for all integers k, and the discrete logarithm logb a is the integer k such that bk = a. In finite groups and modular arithmetic, the discrete logarithm is notoriously hard to compute, even though the inverse operation — exponentiation — is fast.

Problem statement

Let G be a finite cyclic group of order n with generator g. Every element h of G can be written as h = gk for a unique k mod n. Computing k from g and h is the discrete logarithm problem (DLP). In the multiplicative group of integers modulo a prime p, this means solving gk ≡ h (mod p).

Algorithms

Brute force is exponential in the size of the group. Generic algorithms, such as baby-step giant-step and Pollard's rho, run in time proportional to the square root of the group order. For finite fields, subexponential index-calculus methods (including the number field sieve) exist, which is why modular groups must be large — 2048-bit or more — for security. On elliptic curves, only generic algorithms are known, so far smaller keys suffice. Peter Shor's 1994 quantum algorithm solves the discrete logarithm in polynomial time, motivating post-quantum cryptography.

Applications in cryptography

The assumed hardness of the DLP underpins the Diffie–Hellman key exchange (1976), ElGamal encryption, the Digital Signature Algorithm, and elliptic-curve systems such as ECDSA and Curve25519 key exchange. Record computations have solved discrete logarithms in finite fields of several hundred digits and in special "trapdoor" groups deliberately weakened, underscoring the need for careful parameter choices.
