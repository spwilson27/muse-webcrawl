Alexandria

Alexandria (Arabic: الإسكندرية, al-Iskandariyya) is a major city in Egypt, located on the Mediterranean Sea at the western edge of the Nile Delta. Founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BC, it was one of the largest and most significant cities of the ancient world, famed for its Great Library and its lighthouse, the Pharos of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Today it is Egypt's second-largest city and its largest seaport, with a population of more than five million.

History

Alexander the Great founded the city in 331 BC after conquering Egypt from the Persians, choosing a site between Lake Mareotis and the sea, opposite the island of Pharos. The architect Dinocrates laid out a grid plan. After Alexander's death, his general Ptolemy took control of Egypt, brought Alexander's body to the city, and made Alexandria the capital of the Ptolemaic Kingdom. Under the Ptolemies the city grew into the intellectual and commercial centre of the Hellenistic world.

Alexandria came under Roman control in 30 BC after the defeat of Mark Antony and Cleopatra VII. It remained a great centre of learning and trade, and later became an important centre of early Christianity, seat of one of the five patriarchates. The city was conquered by Muslim forces in AD 641–642. Its importance declined as Cairo rose, but it revived in the 19th century under Muhammad Ali, becoming a cosmopolitan port city. The British bombardment of 1882 and the riots of 1952 mark turning points in its modern history.

Ancient Alexandria

The ancient city was famous for the Library of Alexandria, the largest library of the ancient world, and its associated research institution, the Mouseion. The Pharos lighthouse, built on the island of Pharos around 280 BC, stood over 100 metres tall and guided ships into the double harbour. Scholars who worked in Alexandria include Euclid, who systematised geometry there; the astronomer Ptolemy; the mathematician Hypatia; and Eratosthenes, who calculated the Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy. The Heptastadion causeway linked Pharos island to the mainland, dividing the harbour into eastern and western basins.

Geography

Alexandria stretches some 40 km along Egypt's Mediterranean coast, a narrow strip between the sea and Lake Mariout (ancient Mareotis). It has a hot desert climate moderated by the sea, with mild, wet winters and hot, humid summers; most of its roughly 200 mm of annual rainfall falls in winter. Strong northerly sea breezes make it noticeably cooler than Cairo in summer.

Landmarks

Notable landmarks include the Citadel of Qaitbay (1477), built on the site of the Pharos; the modern Bibliotheca Alexandrina (opened 2002), a vast library and cultural centre intended as a rebirth of the ancient library; the Roman amphitheatre at Kom El Deka; the catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa; Pompey's Pillar; and the Montaza and Antoniadis palaces and gardens. The Corniche promenade runs the length of the waterfront.

Economy

The Port of Alexandria handles the majority of Egypt's foreign trade. Industry includes petrochemicals, cement, textiles, food processing and shipbuilding. Tourism, both domestic beach tourism and cultural tourism, is significant, and the city is a summer retreat for Egyptians escaping the heat of Cairo.

Culture

Alexandria has a distinctive cosmopolitan heritage shaped by Greek, Roman, Arab, and European influences, reflected in its architecture, cuisine and dialect. It is the setting of Lawrence Durrell's Alexandria Quartet and much of the poetry of Constantine P. Cavafy, a native of the city. Alexandria University, founded in 1938, is one of Egypt's largest universities.
