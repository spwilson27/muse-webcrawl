Saladin (Ṣalāḥ ad-Dīn Yūsuf ibn Ayyūb; 1137/1138 – 4 March 1193) was a Kurdish commander who became the first sultan of Egypt and Syria and founded the Ayyubid dynasty. He led Muslim military efforts against the crusader states of the Levant, culminating in the recapture of Jerusalem in 1187 after his decisive victory at the Battle of Hattin, and he became the principal Muslim adversary of Richard I of England during the Third Crusade.

Rise to power

Born in Tikrit into a Kurdish military family, Saladin grew up in Baalbek and Damascus and entered the service of Nur al-Din, the Zengid ruler of Syria. He accompanied his uncle Shirkuh on expeditions to Fatimid Egypt, and on Shirkuh's death in 1169 became vizier of Egypt. There he abolished the Shi'a Fatimid Caliphate in 1171, restoring Sunni Islam and nominal Abbasid allegiance, and after Nur al-Din's death in 1174 he progressively united Egypt, Syria, the Hejaz, and Upper Mesopotamia under his own rule — building the Citadel of Cairo and refortifying Alexandria, whose walls and harbour defences he strengthened against crusader seaborne attack.

War with the crusaders

On 4 July 1187 Saladin annihilated the army of the Kingdom of Jerusalem at the Horns of Hattin, capturing King Guy of Lusignan and the relic of the True Cross. In the following months nearly every crusader city surrendered; Jerusalem itself capitulated on 2 October 1187 after a negotiated settlement — in marked contrast to the crusaders' massacre of 1099, Saladin allowed the inhabitants to ransom themselves and protected the holy places of all faiths. The fall of Jerusalem provoked the Third Crusade (1189–1192), in which Richard I retook Acre and defeated Saladin at Arsuf and Jaffa but could not recover Jerusalem; the war ended with the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), leaving the coast to the crusaders and Jerusalem — open to Christian pilgrims — in Muslim hands.

Reputation and legacy

Saladin died in Damascus in 1193, having given away much of his personal wealth to charity. His chivalry, generosity to defeated enemies, and forbearance at Jerusalem made him famous even in the Christian West, where medieval romances cast him as a paragon of knightly virtue and Dante placed him among the virtuous pagans in Limbo. The empire he founded passed to his sons and brother al-Adil, and his name remains a byword for Muslim unity and resistance, invoked from Cairo to his native Kurdistan.
