Émile Édouard Charles Antoine Zola (2 April 1840 – 29 September 1902) was a French novelist, journalist, and playwright, the best-known practitioner of the literary school of naturalism. He was a major figure in the political liberalisation of France and in the exoneration of the falsely accused army officer Alfred Dreyfus, an episode encapsulated in his renowned newspaper headline "J'Accuse…!".

Early life

Zola was born in Paris to an Italian-born engineer, François Zola, and a French mother. The family moved to Aix-en-Provence, where Zola befriended the painter Paul Cézanne. After his father's early death left the family in poverty, Zola returned to Paris in 1858 and worked as a clerk at the publisher Hachette, rising to head its publicity department while beginning to write fiction.

Les Rougon-Macquart

Zola's masterwork is the twenty-volume cycle Les Rougon-Macquart (1871–1893), subtitled "the natural and social history of a family under the Second Empire". Tracing the descendants of a single family across five generations, the novels examine how heredity and environment shape human behaviour. Individual volumes include L'Assommoir (1877), about alcoholism in working-class Paris; Nana (1880); Germinal (1885), his acclaimed novel of a coal miners' strike; and La Bête humaine (1890).

Naturalism

In essays such as The Experimental Novel (1880), Zola argued that the novelist should apply the methods of experimental science, observing characters as products of heredity and milieu. His naturalist novels are characterised by meticulous documentation, vivid descriptions of crowds and places, and frank treatments of poverty, sexuality, and violence that scandalised contemporary readers.

The Dreyfus affair

In January 1898 Zola published "J'Accuse…!", an open letter to President Félix Faure on the front page of the newspaper L'Aurore, accusing the French military and government of antisemitism and of wrongfully convicting Captain Alfred Dreyfus of treason. Zola was convicted of libel and fled to England for a year; the affair split French society, and Dreyfus was ultimately exonerated in 1906.

Death and legacy

Zola died in 1902 of carbon monoxide poisoning from a blocked chimney, a death some have suspected was arranged by his political enemies but which was officially judged accidental. In 1908 his remains were transferred to the Panthéon. He remains one of the most widely read and influential French novelists.
