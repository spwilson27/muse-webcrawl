Presupposition

In the branch of linguistics known as pragmatics, a presupposition (or presupposition trigger) is an assumption or belief that is taken for granted in an utterance and remains constant under negation — a property sometimes called constancy under negation. For example, the utterance "John said that he would stop smoking" presupposes that John used to smoke, and this presupposition survives in the negated form "John didn't say that he would stop smoking".

The concept originates with the philosophers Gottlob Frege and Peter Strawson, who used it in debates about whether sentences with non-referring subjects, such as "The present King of France is bald", are false or lack truth value altogether. Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions treated such sentences as false outright; Strawson argued instead that reference failure produces a presupposition failure.

Presupposition triggers

Many linguistic constructions trigger presuppositions, including:

  • Definite descriptions — "the X" presupposes that an X exists.
  • Factive verbs — know, regret, realise presuppose the truth of their complements.
  • Change-of-state verbs — stop, begin, continue presuppose a prior state.
  • Iteratives — again, another presuppose a previous occurrence.
  • Cleft sentences — "It was John who left" presupposes that someone left.
  • Questions — "When did John leave?" presupposes that John left.

Projection problem

A central puzzle is the projection problem: how the presuppositions of parts of a sentence combine, and sometimes fail to combine, into presuppositions of the whole. Responses include semantic accounts based on multi-valued or dynamic logics, and pragmatic accounts in which hearers accommodate missing presuppositions into the common ground of the conversation.
