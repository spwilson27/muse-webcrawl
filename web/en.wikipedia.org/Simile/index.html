Simile

Simile
TypeFigure of speech
DefinitionAn explicit comparison between two unlike things, typically using "like" or "as"
ContrastMetaphor (implicit comparison)
Famous subtypeHomeric (epic) simile

A simile is a figure of speech that directly compares two different things, typically using the words "like", "as", or "than" — for example, "as brave as a lion" or Robert Burns's "my love is like a red, red rose." Similes state the comparison explicitly, which distinguishes them from metaphors, in which one thing is spoken of as though it actually were the other.

Use in literature

Similes enliven description by linking the unfamiliar to the familiar, sharpening images and revealing unexpected connections. The Homeric or epic simile, a hallmark of the Iliad and Odyssey later imitated by Virgil, Dante, and Milton, extends the comparison over many lines, developing the vehicle of the comparison into a small scene of its own. Simile is also pervasive in religious texts: the Hebrew Bible, the New Testament parables, and Buddhist scriptures all teach through comparison.

Simile and metaphor

The boundary between simile and metaphor is sometimes debated: Aristotle treated the simile as a metaphor differing only in the manner of statement, and modern theorists often regard them as points on a continuum of comparison. A simile makes its two terms and the explicit comparative marker visible, which can make it feel more measured or tentative than metaphor's bolder identification. Over-familiar similes can harden into clichés — "as dead as a doornail," itself famously used by Shakespeare and Dickens. See also metaphor and figure of speech.
