The Grampian Mountains (Scottish Gaelic: Am Monadh) are one of the three major mountain ranges of Scotland, occupying a considerable portion of the Scottish Highlands in the north of the country. The range stretches from the Great Glen southwestward toward the Firth of Clyde and includes the highest ground in the British Isles, culminating in Ben Nevis (1,345 m / 4,413 ft), the highest mountain in the United Kingdom.

Geography

The Grampians form the southern part of the Highlands, separated from the Northwest Highlands by the Great Glen (Glen More), along which runs Loch Ness. The range includes the Cairngorms in the east — a high granite plateau with five of Scotland's six highest peaks — as well as the hills of Lochaber in the west, where Ben Nevis rises above Fort William. Rivers rising in the Grampians include the Tay, the Spey, the Dee, and the Don.

Geology

The mountains are the deeply eroded roots of the Caledonian mountain belt, formed roughly 400–490 million years ago as continents collided. Their rocks are mainly Dalradian schists and other metamorphics, intruded by granites such as those of the Cairngorms, and the landscape was further sculpted by glaciation during the Ice Ages, leaving corries, arêtes, and U-shaped glens.

Name and history

The name derives from Mons Graupius, site of the Roman victory over the Caledonians described by Tacitus (83/84 AD); a misreading of "Graupius" as "Grampius" in later editions gave the range its modern name. In 1791 the Battle of Mons Graupius's association helped popularize "the Grampians" for the whole range.
