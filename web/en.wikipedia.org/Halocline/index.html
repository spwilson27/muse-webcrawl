Halocline

FieldOceanography, limnology
Defined byRapid change in salinity with depth
RelatedThermocline, pycnocline, chemocline

In oceanography, a halocline is a vertical zone in a body of water across which salinity changes rapidly with depth. Because salinity and temperature together determine the density of seawater, a strong halocline contributes to stratification and often coincides with a pycnocline, the layer of rapid density change.

Formation

Haloclines form where waters of different salinity meet and mix slowly. They are common in estuaries, where fresh river water flows over denser seawater, and in flooded caves and cenotes, where divers can see a shimmering boundary between fresh and salt water layers. In polar oceans, surface freshening from melting ice and precipitation creates a pronounced halocline that insulates surface water from warmer deep water.

Effects

Strong haloclines inhibit vertical mixing, influencing nutrient supply, oxygen distribution, and the habitats of marine organisms. In the Arctic Ocean, the cold halocline layer plays an important role in the circulation and in protecting sea ice from the heat of Atlantic water below.
