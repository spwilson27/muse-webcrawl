Santa Monica (Spanish for "Saint Monica") is a city in western Los Angeles County, California, situated on Santa Monica Bay and completely surrounded by the city of Los Angeles except on its Pacific shoreline. Founded as a resort town in 1875 and incorporated in 1886, it is famed for its beaches, its pier, and its position as the western terminus of U.S. Route 66.

History

The Tongva village of Kuruvungna stood near the site. The city was laid out in 1875 by Senator John P. Jones and Robert Baker as a port and resort rival to Los Angeles. Douglas Aircraft, founded in 1921, made Santa Monica a centre of aviation; the Douglas plant at Clover Field built tens of thousands of aircraft, including the DC-3. After the Second World War the city diversified into beach tourism, health and fitness culture (the original Muscle Beach), and, from the 1980s, technology and media firms of the "Silicon Beach" corridor.

Geography and sights

The city occupies a coastal terrace sloping from the Palisades bluffs to a broad sandy beach. Landmarks include the Santa Monica Pier (1909) with its solar-powered Ferris wheel in Pacific Park, the Third Street Promenade shopping district, Palisades Park along Ocean Avenue, and the Annenberg Community Beach House. Santa Monica has a Mediterranean climate moderated by ocean breezes, with average highs around 20–24 °C.

Government and culture

Santa Monica is known for progressive municipal policies, including longstanding rent control, an early sustainability programme and a municipal minimum wage. It is home to Santa Monica College, the RAND Corporation headquarters, and studios and offices of major entertainment and gaming companies.
