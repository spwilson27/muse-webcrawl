Last universal common ancestor

The last universal common ancestor (LUCA) is the single organism from which all organisms currently living on Earth descend. LUCA is the most recent common ancestor of all current life, estimated to have lived some 3.5 to 4.3 billion years ago, early in Earth's history. LUCA was not the first life on Earth, but a survivor — possibly one of a community of organisms — whose descendants alone persisted.

Characteristics

By comparing the genomes of modern organisms, scientists infer that LUCA already possessed DNA, the genetic code, ribosomes and protein synthesis, a cell membrane, and metabolic pathways including the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway for carbon fixation. Comparative studies have identified several hundred genes likely present in LUCA. Some analyses suggest LUCA was a thermophile living near hydrothermal vents; others favour more temperate settings.

Evidence

The universality of the genetic code, of ATP as an energy currency, and of core biochemical machinery across bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes points to descent from a single ancestral population. Horizontal gene transfer complicates reconstructions, so LUCA is best understood as a population whose gene pool gave rise to all modern lineages.

Relation to origin of life

LUCA stands at the end of a long period of pre-cellular and early cellular evolution studied by abiogenesis research. All life before and beside LUCA that left no descendants is extinct.
