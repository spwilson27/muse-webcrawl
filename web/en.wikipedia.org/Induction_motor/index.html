Induction motor

An induction motor (or asynchronous motor) is an AC electric motor in which the electric current in the rotor needed to produce torque is obtained by electromagnetic induction from the magnetic field of the stator winding. Alternating current supplied to the stator creates a rotating magnetic field; this induces currents in the rotor conductors, which generate their own field and torque. The rotor always turns slightly slower than the field — the difference is called slip — making induction motors self-starting, rugged, and brushless.

A squirrel-cage rotor, the most common induction motor design.

Design and use

The most common type uses a squirrel-cage rotor: a cylindrical laminated core with bars of aluminium or copper shorted by end rings. Wound-rotor motors allow external resistance for starting control. Invented independently by Galileo Ferraris and Nikola Tesla in the 1880s, the three-phase induction motor became the workhorse of industry, driving pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors, and machine tools; with variable-frequency drives it also serves in traction, including electric vehicles and trains. Speed is set essentially by the supply frequency and the number of poles.

Characteristics

Induction motors are inexpensive, reliable, and require little maintenance because they have no brushes or commutator. Efficiency standards (such as IE3 and IE4 classes) govern modern designs. Single-phase versions power household appliances using auxiliary starting windings or capacitors.
