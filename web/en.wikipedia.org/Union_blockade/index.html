Union blockade

Union naval forces blockading the Confederate coastline.

The Union blockade in the American Civil War was a naval strategy by the United States to prevent the Confederacy from trading, above all exporting cotton and importing war materiel. Proclaimed by President Abraham Lincoln in April 1861, it required the monitoring of 3,500 miles (5,600 km) of Atlantic and Gulf coastline, including twelve major ports. It was a major component of General Winfield Scott's Anaconda Plan.

Operations

At the start of the war the U.S. Navy had few ships in commission, but it expanded enormously, purchasing and converting merchant vessels and building new warships. Blockading squadrons were stationed off the major Confederate ports, including Charleston, Wilmington, Mobile, and Galveston. Union amphibious operations seized bases such as Port Royal and Ship Island, and the capture of New Orleans (1862), Mobile Bay (1864), and Fort Fisher (1865) progressively closed the remaining ports.

Blockade runners

Fast, specially built steamers — blockade runners — attempted to evade the blockade carrying cotton out and arms and luxury goods in, usually via Bermuda, Nassau, or Havana. A majority of individual runs succeeded, especially early in the war, but the volume of trade was a small fraction of peacetime levels, and capture rates rose sharply by 1864.

Effects

The blockade strangled the Confederate economy: cotton exports fell by roughly 95 percent, depriving the South of foreign exchange, while shortages of imported goods fueled inflation. Historians debate the blockade's decisiveness, but most agree it was an important element in the Union victory.
