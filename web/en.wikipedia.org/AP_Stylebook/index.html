AP Stylebook

The Associated Press Stylebook, generally called the AP Stylebook, is a style and usage guide for American English grammar, spelling, punctuation, and principles of reporting, including rules and guidance on media law, ethics, and acceptable use of language. Published by the Associated Press, a not-for-profit cooperative of American newspapers and broadcasters, it is a standard reference in most U.S. newsrooms and is widely used by journalists, public-relations professionals, and writers.

History

The first AP style guide appeared in 1909 as a modest pamphlet, and a stylebook in the modern sense was published in 1953. In 1977 the AP and United Press International harmonized their stylebooks, producing the format that endures. New print editions are issued regularly — annually in recent years — and an online subscription version is continuously updated.

Contents and notable rules

The Stylebook is organized largely as an alphabetical reference of entries, plus chapters on business, sports, and media law, and a punctuation guide. Characteristic AP rules include spelling out numbers below 10 and using figures for 10 and above; omitting the serial (Oxford) comma in most simple series; using figures for ages, dates, and percentages, with the percent sign (adopted in 2019 in place of the word "percent"); state abbreviations that differ from postal codes (for example, "Ala." and "Ga."); lowercase "internet" (adopted 2016); and treating "more than" and "over" as interchangeable for quantities (a 2014 change that drew public comment). Updates frequently make news themselves, such as decisions on terms like "climate crisis", entries on race-related and gender-related terminology, and guidance on datelines from places in the news.

Influence and comparison

Along with The Chicago Manual of Style and the MLA and APA styles, the AP Stylebook is among the most influential English-language style guides. It differs from Chicago style in such matters as headline capitalization ("up style" in AP), comma usage, and number treatment, reflecting its origin in the space constraints and speed of wire-service journalism.
