Zutphen (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈzʏtfə(n)]) is a city and municipality located in the province of Gelderland, Netherlands. It lies a little to the south of Deventer on the eastern bank of the river IJssel, at the mouth of the Berkel. Zutphen is one of the oldest cities in the Netherlands: the place originated as a Frankish settlement on a river dune and received city rights around 1190 from Count Otto I of Guelders. It flourished as a member of the Hanseatic League, trading along the IJssel and with the Baltic, and preserves an unusually intact medieval centre.

The city's landmarks include the Walburgiskerk (St Walburga's Church), one of the oldest churches in the Netherlands, which houses the Librije, a 16th-century chained library; the Drogenapstoren gate tower; and rows of Hanseatic merchant houses. In 1586, during the Eighty Years' War, the Battle of Zutphen was fought nearby between Dutch-English forces and Spain; the English poet and soldier Sir Philip Sidney was mortally wounded there and died at Arnhem, an episode that made Zutphen famous in English literature.

Modern town

Zutphen suffered damage in the Second World War, including during fighting around Operation Market Garden's aftermath and at liberation in April 1945, but much of its medieval fabric survived. Today the municipality, which since 2005 has included Warnsveld, has around 48,000 inhabitants and is a regional centre known for its historic character, bookshops and anthroposophical institutions.
