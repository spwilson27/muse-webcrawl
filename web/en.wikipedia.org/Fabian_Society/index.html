Fabian Society

Formation: 4 January 1884
Headquarters: London, United Kingdom
Purpose: Advancement of democratic socialism

The Fabian Society is a British socialist organisation whose purpose is to advance the principles of social democracy and democratic socialism via gradualist and reformist effort in democracies, rather than by revolutionary overthrow. It takes its name from the Roman general Fabius Maximus, famed for his delaying tactics against Hannibal.

History

Founded in London in 1884, the society attracted prominent members including George Bernard Shaw, Sidney and Beatrice Webb, H. G. Wells, Annie Besant and Emmeline Pankhurst. Its pamphlets and research — notably the Fabian Essays in Socialism (1889) — were highly influential. The society was one of the founding organisations of the Labour Representation Committee in 1900, the forerunner of the Labour Party, to which it remains affiliated as a socialist society.

Influence

Fabians helped found the London School of Economics in 1895. After the Second World War, Fabian ideas contributed to the creation of the welfare state under Clement Attlee's Labour government. The society continues today as a think tank affiliated with the Labour Party, publishing pamphlets and research on public policy.
