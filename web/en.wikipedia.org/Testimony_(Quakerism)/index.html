Testimony (Quakerism)

Quaker testimonies
TraditionReligious Society of Friends (Quakers)
Common summarySPICES: Simplicity, Peace, Integrity, Community, Equality, Stewardship

In Quakerism, a testimony is an outward expression of a Friend's inward spiritual conviction — a way of living that witnesses to the truth experienced in worship. Testimonies are not a creed or a set of rules but shared patterns of conduct that have emerged over centuries of Quaker life, grounded in the belief that there is "that of God in everyone."

Principal testimonies

The best-known testimony is the peace testimony, declared to Charles II in 1660, by which Friends refuse military service and work to remove the causes of war. The testimony of integrity leads Quakers to refuse oaths, deal honestly in business — historically including fixed, fair pricing — and speak plainly. The testimony of equality historically led Friends to refuse hat honor and titles, use plain forms of address, and campaign early for the abolition of slavery and for women's ministry. The testimony of simplicity favors plain dress (in earlier centuries), plain speech, and restraint in consumption, while a more recent testimony of stewardship addresses care for the environment.

Modern summaries

Many Quaker organizations summarize the testimonies with the acronym SPICES: Simplicity, Peace, Integrity, Community, Equality, and Stewardship (sometimes Sustainability). Friends emphasize that the list is a teaching aid, not a definitive catalogue: testimonies continue to develop as communities discern new leadings, and individual Friends may understand them differently across the liberal, conservative, and evangelical branches of Quakerism.
