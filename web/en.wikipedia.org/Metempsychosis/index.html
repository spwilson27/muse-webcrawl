Metempsychosis

Metempsychosis (Greek: μετεμψύχωσις), in philosophy, is the transmigration of the soul: the doctrine that after death the soul passes into another body, human or animal. The term derives from the Greek meta (change) and empsychos (ensouled).

In Greek philosophy

The doctrine is associated with Pythagoras and his followers, who reportedly taught that the soul is immortal and is reborn in successive bodies, a belief linked to their vegetarianism. Empedocles described himself as a fallen spirit exiled through cycles of birth, and Plato incorporated metempsychosis into dialogues such as the Phaedo, Phaedrus and the Myth of Er in the Republic, tying rebirth to the moral quality of previous lives.

Comparison with reincarnation

Metempsychosis overlaps with the concepts of reincarnation and the Indian notion of saṃsāra, though scholars distinguish the Greek doctrine by its inclusion of transmigration into animal and even plant bodies and its connection with purification and release from the cycle of births.

Later use

The concept appears in Orphism, Neoplatonism, and later in the works of writers such as James Joyce, who used it as a motif in Ulysses.
