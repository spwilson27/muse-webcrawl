Random forest

A random forest is an ensemble learning method for classification, regression and other tasks that operates by constructing a multitude of decision trees during training. For classification tasks, the output of the random forest is the class selected by most trees. For regression tasks, the mean or average prediction of the individual trees is returned.

Algorithm

Random forests correct for decision trees' habit of overfitting to their training set by averaging many trees trained on different random samples of the data, a technique known as bootstrap aggregating or "bagging". In addition, at each split in a tree only a random subset of the features is considered, which decorrelates the trees and further reduces variance. The method was developed by Leo Breiman and Adele Cutler, building on earlier work by Tin Kam Ho and on Amit and Geman's random subspace ideas.

Properties

Random forests generally give good predictive accuracy with little tuning, handle large numbers of input variables, and provide measures of variable importance. Their out-of-bag error estimate allows performance to be assessed without a separate validation set. Drawbacks include reduced interpretability compared with a single tree, and slower prediction when the forest is very large.

Applications

Random forests are widely used in bioinformatics, ecology, remote sensing, finance and computer vision; a notable application was the body-part classification in Microsoft's Kinect depth-sensing system.
