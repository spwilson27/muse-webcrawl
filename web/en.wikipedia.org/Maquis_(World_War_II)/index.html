Maquis (World War II)

Members of the Maquis, the rural guerrilla bands of the French Resistance.

The Maquis (French pronunciation: [maˈki]) were rural guerrilla bands of French and Belgian Resistance fighters, called maquisards, during the Second World War. Maquis took refuge in the forests and mountains of occupied France, taking their name from the maquis, the high ground covered in dense scrubland of Corsica and southern France where it was said one could hide from police. Initially comprising men fleeing conscription into forced labour in Germany, the Maquis grew into an active armed resistance movement.

Origins

The Maquis expanded dramatically after February 1943, when the Vichy regime introduced the Service du travail obligatoire (STO), compelling young Frenchmen to work in Germany. Thousands of réfractaires fled to the countryside rather than report for labour, and resistance organisations supplied them with food, false papers and weapons. Camps were established in regions such as the Cévennes, the Vercors, the Massif Central, Brittany and the Pyrenees.

Operations

Maquis groups ambushed German and Milice patrols, sabotaged railways and power lines, sheltered Allied airmen and agents, and received arms drops from the British Special Operations Executive (SOE) and the American OSS. In 1944 the bands were formally incorporated into the French Forces of the Interior (FFI). During the Allied invasion of Normandy and Operation Dragoon in the south, the Maquis tied down German units and liberated many towns, but some uprisings ended in disaster — most famously at the Vercors plateau in July 1944, where a premature proclamation of a free republic was crushed, and at Mont Mouchet and the Glières plateau. German reprisals against civilians, including the massacres at Tulle and Oradour-sur-Glane, accompanied the anti-Maquis campaign.

Legacy

The estimated strength of the Maquis at their peak is debated, with figures ranging from tens of thousands to around 100,000 fighters. After the war the maquisard became a central figure in the French national memory of the Resistance, commemorated in memorials, the song Le Chant des partisans and ceremonies such as those at the Vercors. The term "maquis" was also adopted by Spanish anti-Franco guerrillas after the Spanish Civil War.
