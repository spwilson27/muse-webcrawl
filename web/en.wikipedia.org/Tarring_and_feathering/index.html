Tarring and feathering

Tarring and feathering was a form of public punishment and humiliation used in feudal Europe and, most famously, in colonial and early America. The victim was stripped, covered with hot or cold tar, and rolled in or showered with feathers, then typically paraded through town, sometimes "riding the rail". The punishment was meant to shame and expel rather than kill, though hot tar could cause severe burns.

Origins

An early record dates to 1189, when Richard I of England prescribed that a thief in his crusading fleet should be shaved, covered with hot pitch, and strewn with feathers. The practice appears sporadically in early modern Europe and was carried to British North America, where pine tar was plentiful in naval-stores regions.

American Revolutionary era

Tarring and feathering became an emblematic weapon of Patriot mobs against Loyalists, tax collectors, and customs officials in the run-up to the American Revolution. A famous case was that of Boston customs man John Malcolm, tarred and feathered in January 1774 and paraded to the Liberty Tree; a British engraving of the scene became iconic propaganda. The punishment declined after the Revolution but recurred in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries — against abolitionists, Mormons (Joseph Smith was tarred and feathered in 1832), striking workers, and during the First World War against alleged German sympathisers, such as John Meints in Minnesota in 1918.

Legacy

The phrase survives metaphorically for public shaming campaigns, and the practice stands in history as an archetype of mob justice operating outside formal law.
