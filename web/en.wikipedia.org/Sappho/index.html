Sappho

Sappho (c. 630 – c. 570 BC) was an Archaic Greek poet from Eresos or Mytilene on the island of Lesbos. She is known for her lyric poetry, written to be sung while accompanied by music. Most of Sappho's poetry is now lost, and what is extant has mostly survived in fragmentary form; only the "Ode to Aphrodite" is certainly complete.

Life

Sappho was from a wealthy family from Lesbos, though her parents' names are uncertain. Ancient sources say she had three brothers: Charaxos, Larichos, and Eurygios. She appears to have been exiled from Lesbos to Sicily around 600 BC, during a period of political upheaval on the island, and may have continued to work until around 570 BC. According to legend, she killed herself by leaping from the Leucadian rocks due to her love for the ferryman Phaon — a tale now generally regarded as unhistorical.

Poetry

The Alexandrian edition of Sappho's poetry comprised nine books of lyric meters. Her poetry was arranged by meter, and she is credited with the invention or popularization of the Sapphic stanza, a four-line stanza form used by later poets including Catullus and Horace. Her verse is written in the Aeolic dialect of Greek. Her poetry explores themes of love, desire, and devotion to Aphrodite, and much of it appears to have been composed for performance within a circle of female companions.

Legacy

Sappho was widely admired in antiquity: Plato reportedly called her the "tenth Muse." Her reputation survived the loss of most of her work, which likely declined in copying during late antiquity and the Middle Ages as her dialect fell out of fashion. Papyrus discoveries since the late 19th century, including the "Brothers Poem" published in 2014, have restored additional fragments. The words "sapphic" and "lesbian" derive from her name and birthplace.
