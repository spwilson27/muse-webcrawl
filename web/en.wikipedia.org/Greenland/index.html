Greenland is a North American island autonomous territory of the Kingdom of Denmark. It is the larger of two autonomous territories within the kingdom, the other being the Faroe Islands; the citizens of both territories are full citizens of Denmark. Greenland is the world's largest island (Australia and Antarctica are considered continents), lying between the Arctic and Atlantic oceans, east of the Canadian Arctic Archipelago. Though physiographically part of North America, it has been politically associated with Europe—specifically Norway and Denmark—for more than a millennium.

Geography

About 80% of Greenland is covered by the Greenland ice sheet, the second-largest body of ice in the world after the Antarctic ice sheet. The ice-free coastal fringe supports all of the island's towns; there are no roads between settlements, and travel is by air, sea, dog sled, or snowmobile. The population of roughly 56,000 is the lowest density of any comparable jurisdiction; the majority are Greenlandic Inuit (Kalaallit). The capital and largest city is Nuuk (population ~20,000), on the southwest coast.

History

Greenland was settled by Paleo-Eskimo cultures from about 2500 BCE, and later by the Thule people, ancestors of the modern Inuit, from around 1200 CE. Norse settlers led by Erik the Red colonised the southwest in the 980s and named the island in an early act of promotion; the Norse settlements died out by the 15th century. Denmark-Norway reasserted sovereignty in the 18th century. After the Second World War Greenland's colonial status ended (1953), home rule was granted in 1979, and self-government expanded in 2009, with Greenlandic made the official language and a recognised path toward possible independence. A 1985 referendum took Greenland out of the European Economic Community over fisheries policy.

Economy and environment

The economy rests on fishing (especially shrimp and Greenland halibut), public transfers from Denmark, tourism, and a small but politically charged mining sector. Melting of the ice sheet—driven by climate change—is a major contributor to global sea-level rise and a focus of Arctic geopolitics, including periodic foreign interest in the island's strategic position and mineral resources.
