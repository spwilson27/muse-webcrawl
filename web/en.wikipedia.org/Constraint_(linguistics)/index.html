Constraint (linguistics)

In linguistics, a constraint is a condition or restriction on the form that linguistic structures may take. Different theoretical frameworks use constraints in different ways: as inviolable filters on representations, as conditions on derivations, or — most famously in Optimality Theory — as ranked, violable requirements that grammars resolve by choosing the least-bad candidate.

Constraints in Optimality Theory

In Optimality Theory (OT), developed by Alan Prince and Paul Smolensky in the early 1990s, a grammar consists entirely of a set of universal constraints ranked in a language-specific hierarchy. Constraints fall into two families: markedness constraints, which require outputs to be structurally simple or unmarked (for example ONSET, requiring syllables to begin with a consonant), and faithfulness constraints, which require outputs to resemble their inputs (for example MAX, prohibiting deletion). Because constraints can conflict and be violated, the optimal output is the candidate incurring the least serious violations under the ranking.

Constraints in other frameworks

Earlier generative grammar used constraints as inviolable conditions: Ross's island constraints limited movement out of certain configurations, and Chomsky's Government and Binding theory proposed principles such as the binding conditions and the Empty Category Principle. Constraint-based formalisms such as Head-driven Phrase Structure Grammar and Lexical Functional Grammar encode grammatical knowledge directly as systems of constraints on feature structures rather than as rules.

Learnability and typology

Because OT holds the constraint set to be universal, cross-linguistic variation is predicted to arise only from different rankings — a hypothesis that makes strong, testable claims about possible and impossible languages (factorial typology).
