Ocean-effect snow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ocean-effect snow is a meteorological phenomenon analogous to lake-effect snow, in which cold air passing over relatively warmer ocean water picks up moisture and deposits it as intense, localised snow showers on downwind coasts. It occurs when a cold air mass — typically polar or Arctic air — moves across an unfrozen sea surface with a temperature difference large enough to destabilise the lower atmosphere.

Formation

As the cold, dry air traverses the water it gains heat and water vapour, forming convective clouds and narrow snow bands aligned with the wind. When these bands make landfall, friction and lifting over coastal hills can produce heavy snowfall over a small area while nearby locations stay dry. Convection over the sea can occasionally generate thundersnow and waterspout-like vortices.

Regions affected

Ocean-effect snow is well known around the Sea of Japan, where Siberian air crossing the water gives Japan's western mountains some of the heaviest snowfalls on Earth ("snow country"); in the Canadian Maritimes and New England from the Atlantic; in the Baltic and North Sea coasts of Europe; and around the Black Sea. The warm ocean current that keeps the water open in winter sustains the effect through the season.
