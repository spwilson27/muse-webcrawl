Serfdom in Russia

Serfdom in Russia was the system of unfree labour under which Russian peasants (serfs) were bound to the land and to their landlords from the 17th century until its abolition in 1861. At its height, serfs made up nearly half the population of the Russian Empire, and the institution shaped Russian society, the economy, and the countryside for two centuries.

Origins and development

Restrictions on peasant movement grew from the late 15th century onward: peasants' right to move was limited to a period around St. George's Day, then suspended through "forbidden years" under Boris Godunov, and finally the Law Code (Ulozhenie) of 1649 under Alexis I removed the time limit on recovering fugitive peasants, legally cementing hereditary serfdom. Landlords gained near-total authority over their serfs, who could be sold, gambled away, or conscripted. Under Peter the Great, serfs were also assigned to factories and subjected to a soul tax (poll tax) enumerated in periodic censuses called revisions.

Life of the serfs

Serfs owed their owners labour dues (barshchina, working the lord's land) or quitrent in cash or kind (obrok). Serf communities governed much of village life through the commune (mir). Serfdom generated repeated unrest, most famously the Pugachev Rebellion of 1773–1775.

Abolition

Tsar Alexander II's Emancipation Manifesto of 19 February (3 March) 1861 freed more than 23 million privately owned serfs — the largest emancipation of unfree labourers in history. Serfs received personal freedom and allotments of land, but had to pay redemption payments to the state over 49 years for land often smaller than what they had previously farmed, a settlement that embittered many peasants and left rural grievances that fed later revolutionary movements.
