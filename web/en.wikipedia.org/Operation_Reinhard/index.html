Operation Reinhard (German: Aktion Reinhard or Aktion Reinhardt) was the codename of the Nazi German plan to murder the Jews of the General Government in occupied Poland during the Holocaust. It was the deadliest phase of the Nazis' "Final Solution of the Jewish question", carried out at three purpose-built extermination camps — Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka — between March 1942 and November 1943.

Organisation

The operation is generally believed to have been named after Reinhard Heydrich, the Gestapo chief assassinated in 1942, though records spell it "Reinhardt", possibly after State Secretary Fritz Reinhardt. SS and Police Leader Odilo Globocnik directed the programme from Lublin, with Christian Wirth as inspector of the camps. The staff drew on personnel from the earlier Aktion T4 "euthanasia" programme, who brought experience of gassing. Victims were deported by train from ghettos across Poland and murdered on arrival in stationary gas chambers using engine exhaust; their property was sorted and shipped to the Reich as part of the operation's economic wing.

Toll and conclusion

By the operation's end, roughly 1.7 million Jews had been murdered at the three camps and in associated shootings, including the Aktion Erntefest massacres of 3–4 November 1943, in which about 42,000 Jews were shot. Prisoner revolts at Treblinka (August 1943) and Sobibor (October 1943) hastened the camps' closure; the sites were then dismantled and planted over to conceal the crimes. Very few victims survived; postwar trials prosecuted some of the perpetrators, though Globocnik died by suicide in 1945.
