Trijet

A trijet is a jet aircraft powered by three jet engines. In general, passenger airline trijets are considered to be second-generation jet airliners, due to their innovative engine configurations, in addition to the advancement of turbofan technology. Trijets are more efficient than quadjets, but not as efficient as twinjets, which replaced trijets as they grew more powerful and gained ETOPS certification for overwater routes.

Designs

Most trijets place the third engine in the tail: either at the base of the vertical stabilizer with an S-shaped duct feeding it (as on the Boeing 727 and Tupolev Tu-154), or mounted in a straight-through nacelle integral with the tail (as on the McDonnell Douglas DC-10 and Lockheed L-1011 TriStar). One unusual design, the Martin XB-51, placed two engines forward on the fuselage and one in the tail.

History

The first trijets flew in the early 1960s: the Hawker Siddeley Trident and the Boeing 727. Wide-body trijets — the DC-10 and L-1011 — entered service in the 1970s. Three engines were attractive because regulations then restricted twin-engine aircraft to routes within 60 minutes of a diversion airport, and a third engine was cheaper to operate than a fourth. As reliable high-thrust engines and extended-range twinjet approvals arrived, trijets disappeared from passenger service; production ended with the MD-11 in 2001. Business jets such as the Dassault Falcon 7X and 8X continue to use the configuration, which offers engine-out performance advantages from high or short runways.
