Knismesis and gargalesis

Knismesis and gargalesis are the two types of tickling distinguished by psychologists G. Stanley Hall and Arthur Allin in 1897. Knismesis is a light, feather-like tickling that produces an itching sensation; gargalesis is a harder, laughter-inducing tickling applied to sensitive areas of the body.

Knismesis

Knismesis refers to a light, moving touch that rarely causes laughter and is often accompanied by an itch sensation. It is thought to serve as a protective reflex against small crawling animals such as insects or parasites on the skin, and it can be induced by oneself.

Gargalesis

Gargalesis is heavy tickling that provokes laughter, squirming and pleasurable or aversive arousal. Unlike knismesis, it generally cannot be produced by self-stimulation, a fact attributed to the cerebellum's prediction of self-generated touch. It is associated with play behaviour and social bonding in humans and other primates.
