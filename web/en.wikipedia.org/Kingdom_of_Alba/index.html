Kingdom of Alba

The Kingdom of Alba (Latin: Scotia; Middle Gaelic: Alba) was the Gaelic kingdom of the Picts and Scots in northern Britain, the direct predecessor of the Kingdom of Scotland. It emerged in the 9th century from the union — traditionally dated to 843 under Kenneth MacAlpin — of the Pictish and Gaelic (Dál Riata) worlds, and lasted until the 13th century, when its kings took the title rex Scottorum, King of Scots, and "Scotland" became the kingdom's usual name.

Origins

The name Alba — cognate with Albion — originally denoted Britain as a whole, then the lands north of the Forth–Clyde line. After the Viking destruction of the Pictish over-kingship at Fortriu (839) and Dál Riata's collapse, the House of Alpin built a new kingship. Constantine II (900–943) is the first ruler Irish annals call "king of Alba" (rí Alban). The kingdom was Gaelic in culture and church (the Columban church centred first on Dunkeld, later St Andrews), though its population included Picts, Britons of Strathclyde and Angles of Lothian.

Development

Alba's kings — among them Malcolm II, who won Lothian at Carham (1018), and Macbeth (1040–1057) — extended their authority over Moray, Strathclyde and Lothian. Kingship alternated between branches of the royal kindred under tanistry, causing frequent violent successions. The reign of David I (1124–1153) transformed Alba through Norman-style feudal institutions, burghs, sheriffdoms and reformed monastic orders, while Gaelic mormaers became earls. Alexander III's death in 1286 ended the old line and opened the succession crisis leading to the Wars of Scottish Independence.
