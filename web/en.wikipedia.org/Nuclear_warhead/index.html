Nuclear warhead

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nuclear warhead is the explosive device — comprising fissile material, the physics package, and its fuzing and safety systems — that is delivered to a target by a missile, bomb, torpedo, or other delivery system in a nuclear weapon. In modern missile forces the warhead is carried inside a re-entry vehicle that protects it during descent through the atmosphere.

Design

Warheads derive their energy from nuclear fission, nuclear fusion, or both. First-generation weapons used pure fission of uranium-235 or plutonium-239. Thermonuclear (hydrogen) warheads use a fission primary to ignite a fusion secondary, multiplying yields enormously: yields range from less than one kiloton for small tactical weapons to several megatons for the largest designs. Modern warheads such as the US W76, W78, W87, and W88 are compact two-stage devices designed for delivery by submarine-launched and intercontinental ballistic missiles, often with multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs) carried on a single missile.

Because warheads must be safe in accidents, reliable after years of storage, and immune to unauthorised use, they incorporate features such as one-point safety, insensitive high explosives, permissive action links, and coded arming systems. Their stewardship without explosive testing is a central technical and political issue under the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty.

History

The first nuclear warheads were the gravity bombs used against Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945. During the Cold War the United States and the Soviet Union built stockpiles of tens of thousands of warheads of many types, deployed on bombers, land- and submarine-based missiles, artillery shells, torpedoes, and depth charges. Arms control agreements, especially START and New START, have limited deployed strategic warheads, but thousands remain in the inventories of the nine nuclear-armed states.
