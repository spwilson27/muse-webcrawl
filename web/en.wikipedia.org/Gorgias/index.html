Gorgias (Greek: Γοργίας; c. 483 – c. 375 BC) was an ancient Greek sophist, pre-Socratic philosopher, and rhetorician from Leontini in Sicily. He is considered one of the founders of sophism and of rhetoric as an art. Famous across Greece for dazzling improvisational oratory, he is best known today through Plato's dialogue named after him — in which Socrates attacks the teaching of rhetoric — and for his own surviving bravura pieces, the Encomium of Helen and the Defense of Palamedes.

Life

Gorgias, a pupil of Empedocles by tradition, came to Athens in 427 BC as an ambassador from Leontini seeking aid against Syracuse; his ornate speaking style caused a sensation. He spent the rest of his long career traveling and teaching rhetoric for high fees, amassing enough wealth to dedicate a golden statue of himself at Delphi. Ancient sources credit him with a lifespan of 105 to 109 years.

Philosophy: On Non-Existence

His treatise On Non-Existence (or On Nature), surviving only in paraphrase (in Sextus Empiricus and the pseudo-Aristotelian De Melisso Xenophane Gorgia), argued a famous three-part thesis: (1) nothing exists; (2) even if something existed, it could not be known; (3) even if it could be known, it could not be communicated. Whether this was a serious nihilism, a parody of Parmenidean logic designed to show that Eleatic reasoning could prove anything, or a demonstration of rhetoric's power remains debated.

Rhetoric

Gorgias pioneered a highly wrought prose style marked by balanced clauses, antithesis, rhyming endings and poetic diction — the "Gorgianic figures." In the Encomium of Helen he undertakes to acquit Helen of Troy of blame, arguing that speech (logos) is a powerful lord that can compel the soul; if Helen was persuaded, deceived, loved, or forced, she is blameless. The speech is both a display piece and a profound early theory of persuasion.

Legacy

Plato's Gorgias fixed his image as the amoral technician of persuasion, though it may caricature him. Aristotle credited him with spreading the use of rhetorical commonplaces. Modern scholars treat Gorgias as a serious thinker about language, being and knowledge — a forerunner of skepticism and of philosophical reflection on rhetoric itself.
