Rate of convergence

Comparison of different orders of convergence

In numerical analysis, the speed at which a convergent sequence approaches its limit is called the rate of convergence. Although strictly speaking a limit does not give information about any finite first part of the sequence, the concept of rate of convergence is of great practical importance when working with sequences of successive approximations for an iterative method, as, typically, fewer iterations are needed to yield a useful approximation if the rate of convergence is higher.

Definitions

Suppose a sequence (xk) converges to a value L. It is said to converge linearly if the ratio of successive errors approaches a constant μ with 0 < μ < 1; the number μ is called the rate of linear convergence. Convergence with order p > 1 means that the error at each step is asymptotically proportional to the previous error raised to the power p. Order 2 is called quadratic convergence, order 3 cubic convergence, and so on. A sequence that converges faster than linearly but with no fixed order greater than 1 is said to converge superlinearly.

Examples

The bisection method for root-finding converges linearly with rate 1/2. The secant method converges superlinearly with order equal to the golden ratio, approximately 1.618. Newton's method converges quadratically near a simple root, provided the initial guess is sufficiently close. Fixed-point iteration typically converges linearly when it converges at all.

Use in practice

Rates of convergence are used to compare iterative algorithms, to estimate the number of iterations needed to reach a prescribed tolerance, and in the analysis of discretisation methods, where the order of accuracy of a numerical scheme describes how its error decreases as the mesh is refined.
