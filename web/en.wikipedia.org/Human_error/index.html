Human error

Human error
Field: Human factors, safety science
Key figures: James Reason, Jens Rasmussen
Concepts: Slips, lapses, mistakes, violations; Swiss cheese model
Applications: Aviation, medicine, nuclear power

Human error is an action or decision by a person that was not intended, was outside the expected performance, or led to an undesired outcome. It is a central topic in human factors and safety science, where most accidents in complex systems — in aviation, medicine, railways and nuclear power — are found to involve human actions among their causes.

Classification

Psychologist James Reason distinguished slips and lapses (errors of execution, in which the plan was correct but the action went wrong, such as pressing the wrong button or forgetting a step) from mistakes (errors of intention, in which the action was performed as planned but the plan itself was wrong, through misapplied rules or faulty knowledge). Jens Rasmussen's skill-rule-knowledge framework locates these at different levels of cognitive control. Deliberate deviations from procedures — violations — are usually treated separately from error proper.

Causes and prevention

Modern safety research emphasises that errors are usually symptoms of system design rather than individual carelessness: Reason's "Swiss cheese model" pictures accidents as occurring when latent organisational weaknesses align with local triggers and active failures. Countermeasures include checklists, forcing functions and error-tolerant design, automation (with attention to its own failure modes), training, fatigue management and "just culture" reporting systems that encourage disclosure. Approaches that simply blame and retrain the operator ("the bad apple theory") are considered ineffective.
