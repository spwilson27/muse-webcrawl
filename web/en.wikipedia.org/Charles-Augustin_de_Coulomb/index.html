Charles-Augustin de Coulomb

Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (14 June 1736 – 23 August 1806) was a French physicist and military engineer best known for formulating Coulomb's law, which describes the electrostatic force between charged particles. The SI unit of electric charge, the coulomb, is named in his honour.

Life

Coulomb was born in Angoulême and educated in Paris. He entered the military engineering corps and spent nine years in Martinique supervising fortifications, work that damaged his health but gave him practical experience in mechanics. On returning to France he turned to experimental physics, winning prizes from the Academy of Sciences for work on compasses and on friction.

Scientific work

Using a sensitive torsion balance of his own design, Coulomb showed in the 1780s that the force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them — an inverse-square law analogous to gravitation. He also investigated the distribution of charge on conductors, magnetic forces, and the friction of machinery and fluids, and contributed to soil mechanics with his theory of earth pressure.

Later years

Coulomb lost his positions during the French Revolution but was later appointed an inspector of public instruction under Napoleon. He died in Paris in 1806. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
