The Soviet space dogs were dogs used in the Soviet space programme during the 1950s and 1960s in suborbital and orbital spaceflights, to determine whether living beings could survive launch and the conditions of space. Small stray dogs from the streets of Moscow were preferred: scientists believed strays had already learned to endure hunger and cold, and females were chosen for their temperament and the design of their suits.

Training

Dogs were trained to endure long confinement in small pressurised capsules, centrifuge acceleration, noise and vibration, and to eat a gelatinous food mixture. Favoured candidates weighed about 6–7 kilograms and were calm, photogenic and tolerant of handling.

Suborbital flights

Beginning with Dezik and Tsygan in July 1951 — the first dogs launched to the edge of space and recovered alive — pairs of dogs flew on R-1 and R-2 rockets to altitudes of about 100 km and above. Many flights succeeded, though some dogs died, including Lisa and Ryzhik's predecessors in early ejection tests and the parachute failures that killed several animals.

Orbital flights

The most famous space dog, Laika, flew aboard Sputnik 2 on 3 November 1957, becoming the first animal to orbit the Earth. Her craft had no recovery system, and she died within hours from overheating, a fact concealed for decades. On 19 August 1960, Belka and Strelka aboard Korabl-Sputnik 2 became the first living creatures to orbit and return safely, paving the way for Yuri Gagarin's flight the following April. Strelka later had puppies, one of which, Pushinka, was given to President John F. Kennedy's family. The last dog flight, Veterok and Ugolyok in 1966, set a still-standing canine record of 22 days in orbit aboard Kosmos 110.

Legacy

The dogs became icons of Soviet science and popular culture, appearing on stamps, postcards and monuments; a memorial to Laika stands in Moscow. Their flights are also a focus of debate about the ethics of animal testing in the space race.
