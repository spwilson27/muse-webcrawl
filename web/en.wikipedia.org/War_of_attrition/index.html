War of attrition

Attrition warfare is a form of warfare and a military strategy in which one side seeks to win a war of attrition by wearing down the enemy to the point of collapse through continuous losses in personnel, equipment, and supplies. The word attrition comes from the Latin atterere, "to rub against" or "to grind down."

Strategy

In a war of attrition, decisive maneuver is subordinated to the cumulative destruction of the enemy's forces and resources. The side with the greater reserves of manpower, materiel, and industrial capacity, and the political will to sustain losses, is favored. Attrition is often contrasted with maneuver warfare, which seeks rapid decision by dislocating the enemy, but most wars combine elements of both.

Historical examples

The trench warfare of the Western Front in the First World War — notably the battles of Verdun and the Somme in 1916 — is the classic example, with German chief of staff Erich von Falkenhayn intending at Verdun to "bleed France white." The American Civil War's final campaigns under Ulysses S. Grant, parts of the Eastern Front in the Second World War, and the Iran–Iraq War have also been characterized as wars of attrition. The named "War of Attrition" (1967–1970) between Egypt and Israel along the Suez Canal was an artillery and air war intended to wear down Israeli positions in the occupied Sinai.
