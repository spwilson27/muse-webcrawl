Cursive script (East Asia)

Cursive script (Chinese: 草書, cǎoshū; Japanese: 草書, sōsho) is a style of Chinese calligraphy characterised by flowing, abbreviated, and highly connected strokes. Developed for speed of writing, it simplifies character structures so extensively that texts in full cursive can be difficult for untrained readers to decipher.

Cursive script
Script family: Chinese characters
Developed: Han dynasty onward
Styles: Zhangcao, jincao (modern cursive)
Used in: China, Japan, Korea

Development

Cursive forms emerged during the Han dynasty as clerks simplified clerical script for rapid drafting (zhangcao, "chapter cursive"). In the Eastern Jin dynasty, masters such as Wang Xizhi refined jincao — a freer, more expressive cursive — into a major art form, influencing calligraphy across East Asia.

Characteristics

Cursive writing merges or omits strokes, links characters' internal elements in continuous movements, and substitutes conventional abbreviations for complex components. In Japan, cursive kanji forms fed into the development of hiragana, originally called onnade ("women's hand"). Semi-cursive script (xingshu) occupies a middle ground between cursive and standard script and remains common in everyday handwriting.
