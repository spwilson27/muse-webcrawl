Great Slave Lake is the second-largest lake in the Northwest Territories of Canada (after Great Bear Lake), the deepest lake in North America at 614 m (2,014 ft), and the tenth-largest lake in the world by area, at 27,200 km². It lies on the boundary between the Canadian Shield and the Interior Plains. Its name derives from the Slavey people, one of the Dene nations of the region — a rendering of an exonym given by their Cree rivals — and has no connection to the practice of slavery. Indigenous groups have proposed restoring Indigenous names for the lake, such as Tindeè, Tu Nedhé and Tucho.

Geography

The lake is roughly 469 km long and up to 203 km wide. The rugged eastern arm, studded with islands and narrow channels in the Precambrian rock of the Canadian Shield, contains the deepest water, including Christie Bay, and lies largely within and around Thaidene Nëné National Park Reserve. The shallow western arm, near the Mackenzie River outflow, has marshy shores and is a major staging area for migratory waterfowl. The main inflow is the Slave River, which carries sediment from the Peace–Athabasca Delta; the principal outflow is the Mackenzie River, Canada's longest river system. The lake is ice-covered for much of the year, typically from late autumn to early summer; an ice road links Yellowknife with the community of Dettah in winter.

History

Indigenous peoples have lived around the lake for thousands of years. The first European to see it was the fur trader Samuel Hearne in 1771, who crossed its frozen surface. Trading posts followed, including Old Fort Providence (1786) and Fort Resolution (1819), the oldest continuously occupied community in the Northwest Territories. In the 1930s gold was discovered on the north shore, leading to the founding of Yellowknife, now the territorial capital, on the lake's North Arm. Uranium and diamond mining later developed in the surrounding region.

Ecology and fisheries

The lake supports a commercial and subsistence fishery for lake whitefish, lake trout, northern pike, walleye and Arctic grayling. Its eastern arm's deep, cold, oligotrophic waters contrast with the more productive west. Caribou, moose, bison (in the Mackenzie Bison Sanctuary to the southwest) and large numbers of migratory birds inhabit its shores.

Communities

Communities on the lake include Yellowknife, Hay River, Fort Resolution, Behchokǫ̀, Łutselk'e, Dettah, Ndilǫ and Fort Providence (near the outflow). Together they house a large share of the territory's population.
