Wick effect

Wick effect
FieldForensic science, fire investigation
First described19th century; experimentally tested in the 20th–21st centuries
MechanismClothing acts as a candle wick; melted body fat as fuel
Associated withProposed explanation of some "spontaneous human combustion" reports

The wick effect is a forensic phenomenon in which a clothed human body, ignited by a small external flame source, burns slowly over many hours like an inside-out candle: charred clothing serves as the wick and rendered subcutaneous body fat as the fuel. It has nothing to do with the religion Wicca; the name refers to the wick of a candle.

Mechanism

After death (or incapacitation), a small ignition source — such as a cigarette or ember — smoulders through clothing and skin. Body fat liquefies, soaks into the charred fabric, and feeds a low, localised flame that can consume much of the torso while leaving extremities, nearby furniture and the rest of the room largely undamaged.

Evidence and cases

The effect has been demonstrated experimentally, most famously in a 1998 BBC demonstration using a pig carcass wrapped in cloth, which burned for hours with localised damage resembling the classic case pattern. Investigators have invoked the wick effect to explain cases once attributed to "spontaneous human combustion", such as the 1951 death of Mary Reeser in Florida. The scientific consensus is that such cases involve an external ignition source, often combined with alcohol or medication impairing the victim's response, rather than true spontaneous ignition.
