Córdoba is a city in Andalusia, southern Spain, and the capital of the province of Córdoba. Situated on the Guadalquivir River, it was an important Roman city and, under the Umayyad Caliphate of Córdoba in the 10th century, one of the largest and most advanced cities in the world — a center of learning, medicine, and philosophy. Its historic center, including the Great Mosque-Cathedral, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Founded as the Roman colony Corduba, the city became capital of Hispania Baetica and birthplace of Seneca the Younger and Lucan. After the Muslim conquest of 711 it rose as capital of the Emirate and then Caliphate of Córdoba; under Abd al-Rahman III and his successors its population may have reached several hundred thousand, with libraries, paved streets, and scholars such as Averroes and Maimonides associated with its intellectual life. Ferdinand III of Castile captured Córdoba in 1236 during the Reconquista.

Landmarks

The Great Mosque-Cathedral (Mezquita), begun in 785–786 and repeatedly enlarged, is famed for its forest of red-and-white double arches; a Renaissance cathedral was inserted into its center after the Christian conquest. Other sights include the Roman bridge and Calahorra Tower, the Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos, the synagogue in the Jewish quarter, the palatial ruins of Medina Azahara, and the flower-filled patios celebrated in the annual Festival of the Patios.
