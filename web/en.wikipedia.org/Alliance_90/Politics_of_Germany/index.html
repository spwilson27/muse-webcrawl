Politics of Germany

The politics of Germany are based on a system of federal parliamentary democracy. The Federal Republic of Germany is governed by the Basic Law (Grundgesetz), in force since 1949. The federal president is the head of state, while the federal chancellor is the head of government and exercises executive power, similar to the role of a prime minister in other parliamentary democracies.

Institutions

Federal legislative power is vested in the Bundestag, elected by universal suffrage under a mixed-member proportional system, and the Bundesrat, which represents the sixteen Länder (states) at the federal level. The Federal Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht), based in Karlsruhe, reviews the constitutionality of legislation and has the final word on constitutional disputes.

Political parties

Post-war German politics was dominated by the Christian Democratic Union (CDU/CSU) and the Social Democratic Party (SPD). The Free Democratic Party often served as a coalition partner, and from the 1980s Alliance 90/The Greens established itself as a further party of government, entering a federal coalition with the SPD in 1998. The Left and, later, the Alternative for Germany (AfD) also gained representation. Because proportional representation rarely gives a single party an outright majority, Germany is normally governed by coalition governments.

Federalism

The Länder retain substantial powers, especially over education, policing, and culture, and participate in federal legislation through the Bundesrat. This cooperative federalism, together with a strong constitutional court and an independent central bank tradition, is a defining feature of the post-war German political order.
