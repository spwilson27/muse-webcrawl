Monopolistic competition

Monopolistic competition is a market structure in which many firms sell products that are similar but not identical, giving each firm some degree of market power over its own differentiated offering while entry remains relatively free. The theory was developed independently in the 1930s by Edward Chamberlin and Joan Robinson.

Characteristics

Firms compete on price, quality, branding, and advertising as well as output. Each faces a downward-sloping demand curve for its own variety, but close substitutes limit its pricing power. Restaurants, clothing brands, and hairdressers are standard examples.

Short and long run

In the short run a firm may earn economic profits, but free entry attracts rivals and shifts its demand curve until, in the long run, price equals average cost and profits are normal — though price still exceeds marginal cost, and output is below the cost-minimising scale, the so-called "excess capacity" result.
