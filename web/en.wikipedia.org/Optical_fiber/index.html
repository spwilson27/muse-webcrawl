Optical fiber

An optical fiber (or optical fibre in Commonwealth English) is a flexible glass or plastic waveguide that transmits light along its axis by total internal reflection. It consists of a core surrounded by a cladding of lower refractive index and a protective coating. Optical fibers are the backbone of modern telecommunications, carrying telephone, internet and television signals over intercontinental distances, and are also used in medical endoscopy, sensors and lighting.

Principle

Light launched into the core at a sufficiently shallow angle is reflected repeatedly at the core–cladding boundary and propagates along the fiber. Single-mode fibers, with cores about 9 micrometres across, carry one transverse mode and suit long-distance, high-bandwidth links; multi-mode fibers have larger cores and are used over shorter distances. Attenuation in modern silica fiber can be as low as 0.2 dB/km at the 1550 nm wavelength, allowing signals to travel many tens of kilometres before amplification.

History

Guiding light by refraction was demonstrated in the nineteenth century, but practical fiber optics awaited low-loss glass. Charles Kao's 1966 work showed that attenuation was caused by impurities and could fall below 20 dB/km — research recognised by the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics. Corning produced the first such fibers in 1970, and by the 1980s optical systems, driven by laser diodes and erbium-doped fiber amplifiers, began replacing copper in trunk networks; wavelength-division multiplexing later multiplied capacity enormously.

Applications

Beyond communications, fibers serve in fiber-optic sensors for temperature, strain and rotation (gyroscopes), in endoscopes and imaging bundles, in high-power fiber lasers for cutting and welding, and in decorative and architectural lighting.
