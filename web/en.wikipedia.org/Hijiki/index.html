Hijiki

Hijiki (Sargassum fusiforme, syn. Hizikia fusiformis) is a brown seaweed that grows wild on rocky coastlines in Japan, Korea, and China. In Japanese cuisine it has been eaten for centuries, usually sold dried as short, black, twig-like strands that expand several times in size when soaked. Hijiki is rich in dietary fiber and minerals such as calcium, iron, and magnesium, but it also concentrates inorganic arsenic, which has led several food safety agencies to advise against eating it.

Culinary use

Dried hijiki is soaked in water for about half an hour, drained, and simmered — a classic preparation is hijiki no nimono, cooked in dashi, soy sauce, and mirin with julienned carrots, fried tofu (aburaage), lotus root, and soybeans. It is served as a small side dish in Japanese home cooking and school lunches, and is traditionally believed to promote healthy hair and skin.

Arsenic concerns

Testing by food safety authorities in the United Kingdom, Canada, Hong Kong, and elsewhere found that hijiki contains inorganic arsenic at levels far higher than other seaweeds such as nori, wakame, and kombu. Inorganic arsenic is a known carcinogen, and in the early 2000s several agencies issued advice recommending that consumers not eat hijiki. Japanese authorities responded that moderate consumption within a varied diet poses little risk and noted that washing and soaking reduce arsenic content; hijiki remains commonly eaten in Japan.

Biology

The plant grows in the intertidal zone, anchored to rocks by a holdfast, with cylindrical branching stems that can approach a metre in length. It is harvested in spring, then boiled and dried in the sun — a processing cycle repeated over several days that turns it from greenish-brown to nearly black.
