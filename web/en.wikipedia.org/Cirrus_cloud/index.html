Cirrus cloud

Cirrus (cloud classification symbol Ci) is a genus of high-altitude cloud made of ice crystals. Cirrus clouds typically form at altitudes above about 5,000 metres (16,500 ft) in temperate regions and are thin, wispy, and hair-like — the name comes from the Latin cirrus, meaning a lock of hair.

Description

Cirrus appears in delicate white filaments, patches, or narrow bands, often with a silky sheen. Species include cirrus fibratus, uncinus (the hooked "mare's tails"), spissatus, castellanus, and floccus, and cirrus is also a component of the related genera cirrostratus and cirrocumulus. Because the ice crystals are sparse, cirrus rarely blocks the sun completely, and it produces halo phenomena such as sundogs, the 22° halo, and circumzenithal arcs.

Weather significance

Isolated cirrus usually accompanies fair weather, but thickening and lowering cirrus spreading across the sky can be the first sign of an approaching warm front or tropical cyclone, sometimes giving 24–48 hours' warning of deteriorating weather. Contrails from aircraft are artificial cirrus that can spread into extensive sheets.

Climate effects

Cirrus clouds have a complex net effect on climate: they reflect some incoming sunlight (cooling) but also trap outgoing infrared radiation (warming), and thin cirrus generally exerts a net warming influence. Their treatment remains a significant uncertainty in climate models.
