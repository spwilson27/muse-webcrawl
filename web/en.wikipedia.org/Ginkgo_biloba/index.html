Ginkgo biloba, commonly known as ginkgo or the maidenhair tree, is a species of gymnosperm tree native to China and the only living species in the division Ginkgophyta, all others being extinct. It is a famous "living fossil": fossils recognizably related to modern ginkgo date back more than 200 million years to the Permian period, and the tree has changed little since the Jurassic. Ginkgo is widely planted as an ornamental and street tree around the world and is used in traditional medicine and as a dietary supplement.

Description

Ginkgo is a large, long-lived deciduous tree with an angular crown. Its distinctive fan-shaped leaves, often split into two lobes (hence biloba), have dichotomous venation unlike any other seed plant and turn a brilliant golden yellow in autumn before falling in a brief, near-simultaneous drop. The species is dioecious — male and female reproductive structures grow on separate trees. Female trees produce plum-like seeds whose fleshy outer layer (sarcotesta) smells strongly of rancid butter or vomit when ripe, owing to butyric acid; the inner seed is edible when cooked and is used in East Asian cuisine, though it contains toxins and must be eaten in moderation.

History and cultivation

Ginkgo survived in China, where monks cultivated it around temples — some temple trees are claimed to be over 1,500 years old. The German naturalist Engelbert Kaempfer described it from Japan in the early 1690s and brought seeds to Europe; a tree planted at Utrecht around 1730 is among Europe's oldest. Because male trees do not produce the malodorous fruit, male clones are preferred for street planting. Ginkgo is exceptionally tolerant of pollution, pests and urban stress; six ginkgo trees near the hypocentre of the Hiroshima atomic bombing survived and still live, making the species a symbol of endurance in Japan.

Uses and medical evidence

Standardized leaf extracts (such as EGb 761) are marketed for memory and circulation, and ginkgo is among the best-selling herbal supplements. However, large clinical trials — including the Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory study — found no evidence that it prevents dementia or cognitive decline, and it can interact with anticoagulant medications. Ginkgo seeds and leaves also contain ginkgotoxin, which can cause seizures in excess.
