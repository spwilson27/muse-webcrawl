Hooke's law

Hooke's law is a law of physics that states that the force (F) needed to extend or compress a spring by some distance (x) scales linearly with respect to that distance: F = kx, where k is the spring constant. It is named after the English physicist Robert Hooke, who published it in 1678 as ut tensio, sic vis — "as the extension, so the force".

Formal definition

For a spring displaced by x from equilibrium, the restoring force is F = −kx; the negative sign indicates the force acts opposite to the displacement. The law holds only within the elastic limit of the material; beyond the proportional limit, deformation becomes non-linear and eventually permanent.

Elastic materials

In continuum mechanics, Hooke's law generalises to a relationship between stress and strain: in one dimension, σ = Eε, where E is Young's modulus. In three dimensions it is expressed with tensors using the stiffness tensor, encompassing Young's modulus, shear modulus and Poisson's ratio for isotropic materials.

Applications

Hooke's law underpins spring scales, balances, seismometers, mechanical clocks and the theory of simple harmonic motion: a mass on a Hookean spring oscillates with period T = 2π√(m/k). It also provides the linear approximation for interatomic forces near equilibrium.

Limitations

Many materials deviate at large strains, under creep or plastic deformation, and rubber-like materials show strongly non-linear behaviour. Hooke's law is thus a first-order approximation, accurate for small deformations of most solids.
