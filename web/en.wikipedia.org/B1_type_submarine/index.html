The Type B1 submarine (Japanese: 巡潜乙型潜水艦), also called the I-15 class, was a class of twenty cruiser submarines of the Imperial Japanese Navy built before and during the Second World War. They were among the largest submarines of their day and were distinctive for carrying a single Yokosuka E14Y floatplane in a hangar forward of the conning tower, launched by catapult for reconnaissance.

Design

The B1s were designed for long-range scouting in support of the battle fleet. They displaced about 2,600 tons surfaced, had a range of some 14,000 nautical miles at 16 knots, and carried six bow torpedo tubes with 17 torpedoes and a 14 cm deck gun. The waterproof hangar and catapult allowed each boat to scout ahead of the fleet with its floatplane, an innovation shared with the related Type A and Type B2/B3/B4 designs.

Service history

B1 boats operated throughout the Pacific. Their floatplanes carried out the only aerial bombardment of the continental United States during the war, when in September 1942 the plane from I-25 dropped incendiary bombs near Brookings, Oregon, in the Lookout Air Raids. I-17 shelled the Ellwood oil field near Santa Barbara in February 1942. In the Indian Ocean, B1 submarines supported operations against Allied shipping and raided Madagascar. Among other notable actions, I-19 fired the famous torpedo salvo of 15 September 1942 that sank the carrier USS Wasp and damaged the battleship USS North Carolina and the destroyer USS O'Brien. Only one boat of the class, I-36, survived the war; she was scuttled by the US Navy off the Gotō Islands in 1946.
