Practical effect

Practical effects are a type of special effects produced physically, without computer-generated imagery or other post-production techniques. In some contexts, "special effect" is used as a synonym; however, "practical effect" usually refers to effects such as mechanical effects and pyrotechnics.

The term refers to the physical effects created on the set, in front of the camera, dating back to the early days of cinema. Common examples include animatronics, puppetry, prosthetic makeup, miniatures, squibs, rain and wind machines, and breakaway props.

History

Filmmakers have employed practical effects since the invention of motion pictures. Georges Méliès pioneered in-camera tricks in the 1890s, and throughout the 20th century effects artists such as Ray Harryhausen developed stop-motion animation. The rise of computer-generated imagery (CGI) in the 1990s, exemplified by films such as Jurassic Park (1993), displaced many traditional techniques, though CGI itself was combined with animatronics in that production.

Modern use

Many contemporary directors and cinematographers advocate a return to practical effects, valued for their physicality and for giving actors something tangible to react to. Films such as Mad Max: Fury Road (2015) drew praise for relying heavily on real vehicles and stunt work, supplemented rather than replaced by digital effects.
