Alfred Tennyson, 1st Baron Tennyson (6 August 1809 – 6 October 1892) was an English poet. He was the Poet Laureate of the United Kingdom during much of Queen Victoria's reign. In 1829, Tennyson was awarded the Chancellor's Gold Medal at Cambridge for one of his first pieces, "Timbuktu". He published his first solo collection of poems, Poems, Chiefly Lyrical, in 1830. "Claribel" and "Mariana", which remain some of Tennyson's most celebrated poems, were included in this volume.

Although described by some critics as overly sentimental for modern tastes, his verse soon proved popular and brought Tennyson to the attention of well-known writers of the day, including Samuel Taylor Coleridge. Tennyson's early poetry, with its medievalism and powerful visual imagery, was a major influence on the Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood. He was also a member of the Apostles society at Cambridge.

Early life

Tennyson was born in Somersby, Lincolnshire, England, where his father, George Clayton Tennyson, was rector of St Margaret's Church. He was the fourth of twelve children. His father was a clergyman, and Alfred was educated at home by his father before attending Louth Grammar School and then Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1827. At Cambridge he joined the secret society known as the Cambridge Apostles, where he met Arthur Henry Hallam, who became his closest friend.

Career

Tennyson's first publication was a collection jointly written with his brother Charles, Poems by Two Brothers (1827). His collection Poems (1842), in two volumes, was a critical and popular success and included "The Lady of Shalott", "Locksley Hall", "Ulysses", and "Break, Break, Break".

In 1850, after Wordsworth's death, Tennyson was appointed Poet Laureate; the same year he published In Memoriam A.H.H., a long elegy for his friend Arthur Hallam, who had died suddenly in 1833. The poem, written over seventeen years, is considered one of the great works of Victorian literature, reflecting the era's tension between religious faith and the findings of science. Also in 1850, he married Emily Sellwood, whom he had known since childhood.

Tennyson wrote "The Charge of the Light Brigade" in 1854 after reading a newspaper report of the Battle of Balaclava in the Crimean War. Among his other major works are Maud (1855), the twelve-poem Arthurian cycle Idylls of the King (published between 1859 and 1885), Enoch Arden (1864), and "Crossing the Bar" (1889), written shortly before his death.

Later life and death

In 1883, Tennyson accepted a peerage, becoming Baron Tennyson of Aldworth and Freshwater, at the urging of Queen Victoria and Prime Minister Gladstone. He took his seat in the House of Lords in March 1884. He lived at Farringford House on the Isle of Wight and at Aldworth House in Sussex. In 1889 he made phonograph recordings of his own voice reading his poems for Thomas Edison's emissary.

Tennyson died on 6 October 1892 at Aldworth House, reportedly with a copy of Shakespeare open beside him. He was buried in Poets' Corner in Westminster Abbey.

Legacy

Tennyson remains one of the most popular British poets. Phrases from his works — "'Tis better to have loved and lost / Than never to have loved at all", "Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die", "To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield" — have entered the English language. Queen Victoria was an admirer, finding In Memoriam a comfort after the death of Prince Albert.
