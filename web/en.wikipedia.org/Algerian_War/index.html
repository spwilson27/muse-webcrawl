The Algerian War (1954–1962), also known as the Algerian War of Independence, was a major armed conflict between France and the Algerian National Liberation Front (FLN). It ended more than 130 years of French colonial rule and led to the independence of Algeria, but at the cost of hundreds of thousands of deaths and lasting trauma in both countries.

Course of the war

The war began on 1 November 1954 ("Toussaint Rouge") with coordinated FLN attacks. Unlike France's other colonies, Algeria was legally part of France and home to about a million European settlers, so successive French governments committed enormous forces — eventually nearly 500,000 troops — using quadrillage, fortified frontiers (the Morice Line) and the forced resettlement of millions of Algerians. The FLN fought a guerrilla war in the countryside and an urban campaign in the capital, culminating in the Battle of Algiers (1956–57), in which French paratroopers dismantled the FLN network amid systematic torture that scandalised opinion in France itself.

The crisis in France

The war destroyed the Fourth Republic: in May 1958, fearing the government would abandon Algeria, the army and settlers in Algiers defied Paris, bringing Charles de Gaulle to power as founder of the Fifth Republic. De Gaulle gradually accepted self-determination, provoking a settler and military backlash — the "week of the barricades" (1960), a generals' putsch (1961) and the terrorist campaign of the OAS. Negotiations produced the Évian Accords of March 1962, and a referendum confirmed independence on 5 July 1962. Nearly all the pieds-noirs fled to France, and tens of thousands of harkis — Algerians who had served with the French — were massacred.

Legacy

Estimates of Algerian Muslim deaths range from several hundred thousand to the Algerian state's figure of 1.5 million. France officially recognised the conflict as a "war" only in 1999, and questions of torture, memory and apology remain politically charged in both countries.
