Octopus

Order: Octopoda
Species: ~300 known
Habitat: Oceans worldwide

An octopus (pl.: octopuses or octopodes) is a soft-bodied, eight-limbed mollusc of the order Octopoda. The order consists of some 300 species and is grouped within the class Cephalopoda with squids, cuttlefish, and nautiloids. Like other cephalopods, an octopus is bilaterally symmetric with two eyes and a beaked mouth at the centre point of the eight limbs.

Anatomy

The soft body can rapidly alter its shape, enabling octopuses to squeeze through small gaps. They trail their eight appendages behind them as they swim. The siphon is used both for respiration and for locomotion, by expelling a jet of water. Octopuses have a complex nervous system and excellent sight, and are among the most intelligent and behaviourally diverse of all invertebrates. About two-thirds of an octopus's neurons are located in its arms, which can taste and act semi-autonomously.

Behaviour and ecology

Octopuses inhabit every ocean, at depths from the intertidal zone to the abyss. Most are nocturnal predators of crabs, molluscs and fish, subduing prey with venom delivered through a chitinous beak. They are masters of camouflage, changing colour and texture through chromatophores, and eject ink to confuse predators. Most species are short-lived, growing fast and dying after one to two years; reproduction is semelparous, with males dying after mating and females after brooding their eggs.

Relationship with humans

Octopuses feature in the mythology and cuisine of many maritime cultures. They are increasingly studied for their cognition, kept in aquariums, and have prompted ethical debate over cephalopod welfare in research and farming.
