United Nations Convention against Corruption

The United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) is the only legally binding universal anti-corruption treaty. Adopted by the UN General Assembly on 31 October 2003 and in force from 14 December 2005, it has been ratified by the overwhelming majority of UN member states. The convention was negotiated in Vienna and opened for signature at a conference in Mérida, Mexico, in December 2003; it is administered by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC).

Contents

UNCAC has eight chapters and 71 articles organized around five pillars. Preventive measures require anti-corruption policies, dedicated bodies, codes of conduct for public officials, transparent procurement and public financial management. Criminalization and law enforcement oblige states to criminalize bribery of national and foreign public officials, embezzlement, trading in influence, abuse of functions and illicit enrichment (the last being optional in some constitutional systems), plus laundering of proceeds and obstruction of justice. International cooperation covers extradition, mutual legal assistance and joint investigations. Asset recovery — a fundamental principle of the convention and a first in international law — provides for the return of stolen public funds to their countries of origin. A final pillar addresses technical assistance and information exchange.

Implementation and review

A Conference of the States Parties oversees implementation, and in 2009 it established an Implementation Review Mechanism based on peer review of chapters III and IV, later extended to chapters II and V. Civil-society organizations such as Transparency International monitor compliance and criticize the review process's limited transparency and the slow pace of recovering stolen assets.
