Van Allen radiation belt

The Van Allen radiation belt is a zone of energetic charged particles, most of them originating from the solar wind, that are captured and held around a planet by its magnetic field. Earth's belts consist mostly of high-energy electrons and protons trapped in two main toroidal regions encircling the planet.

Discovery

The belts were discovered in 1958 by instruments aboard the Explorer 1 and Explorer 3 satellites, in experiments led by James Van Allen of the University of Iowa. The inner belt, lying roughly 1,000–12,000 km above the surface, contains concentrated protons; the outer belt, at roughly 13,000–60,000 km, consists mainly of high-energy electrons. A gap known as the slot region separates the two.

Effects

Spacecraft passing through the belts must be hardened against radiation, and satellites in medium Earth orbit are particularly exposed. Astronauts on Apollo missions transited the belts quickly along trajectories minimising exposure. In 2012–2013 NASA's Van Allen Probes observed a transient third belt. Similar belts exist around the giant planets, notably Jupiter.
