Tarot

Tarot (first known as tarocchi) is a pack of playing cards, used from the mid-15th century in various parts of Europe to play card games such as Tarocchini and French Tarot. From their Italian roots, tarot cards spread to much of Europe, where tarot games remain popular in countries including France, Austria and Italy. In the late 18th century, tarot packs began to be used for divination and cartomancy, a use for which they are now best known in the English-speaking world.

Structure of the pack

A traditional tarot pack has 78 cards: four suits of fourteen cards each (pip cards numbered one to ten, plus jack, knight, queen and king), a separate twenty-one-card trump suit, and a single card known as the Fool. In occult usage, the trumps and the Fool are called the Major Arcana, and the suit cards the Minor Arcana. The most influential designs include the Tarot de Marseille and the Rider–Waite tarot, published in 1909 with artwork by Pamela Colman Smith.

History

Tarot originated in northern Italy in the 1440s, probably in Milan or Ferrara, where the trump suit was added to the existing four-suited pack. The oldest surviving cards are from luxury hand-painted decks such as the Visconti-Sforza tarot. The divinatory use of tarot was popularised in France after 1781 by Court de Gébelin, who claimed (without evidence) that the cards embodied ancient Egyptian wisdom; occultists such as Eliphas Lévi and Arthur Edward Waite developed elaborate esoteric interpretations linking tarot to Kabbalah and astrology.

Divinatory use

In cartomancy, a reader lays out cards in a pattern called a spread — the Celtic Cross is among the best known — and interprets the symbolism of the cards and their positions as a guide to the querent's situation. Historians of the occult note that tarot's mystical associations date only from the late eighteenth century, not from antiquity.
