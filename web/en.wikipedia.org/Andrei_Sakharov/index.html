Andrei Sakharov

Andrei Dmitrievich Sakharov
Born: 21 May 1921, Moscow, Russian SFSR
Died: 14 December 1989 (aged 68), Moscow
Fields: Theoretical physics
Awards: Nobel Peace Prize (1975)

Andrei Dmitrievich Sakharov (21 May 1921 – 14 December 1989) was a Soviet physicist who played a central role in developing the Soviet hydrogen bomb, and who later became the USSR's most prominent dissident, campaigning for human rights, disarmament, and democratic reform. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1975.

Weapons work

Recruited to the Soviet nuclear programme in 1948, Sakharov devised the “layer cake” design for the RDS-6s test of 1953 and, with his colleagues, the radiation-implosion principle behind the true two-stage thermonuclear weapon tested in 1955. He contributed to the 50-megaton Tsar Bomba of 1961, yet growing concern about radioactive fallout—he calculated the future victims of atmospheric testing—turned him toward advocacy of test bans, contributing to the Partial Test Ban Treaty of 1963.

Dissident activity

His 1968 essay Reflections on Progress, Peaceful Coexistence, and Intellectual Freedom, circulated in samizdat and published abroad, called for convergence between socialist and capitalist systems and an end to repression. Co-founding the Moscow Human Rights Committee in 1970, he defended political prisoners and national minorities. Barred from travelling to Oslo in 1975, he had his wife, Yelena Bonner, read his Nobel lecture. After protesting the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, he was exiled to the closed city of Gorky in 1980.

Final years

Mikhail Gorbachev released Sakharov in December 1986. Elected to the Congress of People's Deputies in 1989, he became a leading voice for democratisation before his death that December. The European Parliament's Sakharov Prize for Freedom of Thought bears his name.
