Rye whiskey

American rye whiskey, known for its spicy, dry character.

Rye whiskey is a distilled spirit made primarily from rye grain. In the United States, rye whiskey must be made from a mash of at least 51 percent rye, distilled to no more than 80 percent alcohol by volume, and aged in charred new oak barrels; "straight" rye must be aged at least two years. Canadian whisky is also commonly called rye whisky, though Canadian rules permit much less rye in the mash.

History

Rye whiskey was the dominant American spirit of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, produced across Pennsylvania, Maryland, and the Monongahela valley, where rye grew well and surplus grain was distilled to make it transportable. The Whiskey Rebellion of 1791–94 arose from a federal excise tax on such distillers. Prohibition devastated the industry, and after repeal American tastes shifted to smoother bourbon and blended whiskies; rye survived mainly as a mixer until a craft-cocktail revival in the 2000s restored it to prominence, reviving classic drinks such as the Manhattan, Sazerac, and Old Fashioned.

Flavor

Rye grain gives the whiskey a characteristically spicy, fruity, and drier profile than corn-based bourbon, with notes of pepper, clove, and dill in some expressions. Canadian "rye" tends to be lighter and smoother, typically a blend of base whiskies with smaller amounts of flavorful rye whisky.
