Chemical equilibrium

A reversible reaction: forward and reverse rates equalise at equilibrium.
FieldPhysical chemistry
Key relationLaw of mass action; ΔG° = −RT ln K
Key principleLe Chatelier's principle

In a chemical reaction, chemical equilibrium is the state in which both the reactants and products are present in concentrations which have no further tendency to change with time, so that there is no observable change in the properties of the system. This state results when the forward reaction proceeds at the same rate as the reverse reaction: the reaction rates are equal, not the concentrations, which are generally far from equal. Equilibrium is therefore dynamic — reactions continue in both directions but cancel out.

The equilibrium constant

For a general reversible reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the law of mass action states that at equilibrium the ratio

K = ([C]c[D]d) / ([A]a[B]b)

is constant at a given temperature. K is the equilibrium constant, expressed in activities (dimensionless ratios to standard states); large K (>10³) indicates a reaction favouring products, small K (<10⁻³) one favouring reactants. Thermodynamics links K to the standard Gibbs energy change: ΔG° = −RT ln K, so a negative ΔG° implies K > 1. Changing temperature alters K (per the van 't Hoff equation); changing pressure or concentration alters only the position of equilibrium, not K itself.

Le Chatelier's principle

Le Chatelier's principle predicts the response to a disturbance: if a system at equilibrium is subjected to a change in concentration, temperature, or pressure, it shifts in the direction that counteracts the change. Adding a reactant drives the reaction forward; increasing pressure shifts a gas-phase equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas (as exploited in the Haber process for ammonia, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃); heating shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction. Catalysts accelerate both forward and reverse reactions equally and therefore change the time to reach equilibrium, never its position.

History and applications

Claude Louis Berthollet recognised reversibility in chemical reactions around 1803 from observations at Egyptian soda lakes, but the quantitative law of mass action was formulated by the Norwegians Cato Guldberg and Peter Waage (1864–1879), and the thermodynamic treatment by J. Willard Gibbs and van 't Hoff. Equilibrium concepts underpin the analysis of acid–base and solubility behaviour (Ka, Ksp), complex formation and binding constants in biochemistry, and the design of industrial syntheses such as ammonia production and the contact process for sulfuric acid.
