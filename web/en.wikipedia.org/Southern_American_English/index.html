Southern American English is a regional dialect — or group of dialects — of American English spoken across the Southern United States, from the Atlantic coast to as far west as Texas and Oklahoma. It is the largest regional accent group in the United States by area and is popularly recognised by the "Southern drawl".

Phonology

Modern Southern speech is defined by the Southern Vowel Shift, in which the traditional front vowels swap positions: the vowel of dress rises toward that of kit, and vice versa. Other hallmarks include the monophthongisation of /aɪ/ to [aː] (so ride sounds like "rahd"), the pen–pin merger before nasals, loss of rhoticity in older varieties, and the breaking of short front vowels into gliding syllables — the classic "drawl".

Grammar and vocabulary

Distinctive grammar includes the second-person plural pronoun y'all, the double modal constructions such as might could, and completive done ("I done told you"). Vocabulary items include buggy for a shopping cart in parts of the South, and regional terms for soft drinks (Coke, soda, pop).

History

The dialect descends from the speech of settlers from the British Isles, strongly shaped by contact with African American English, with which it shares a long intertwined history. Since the mid-twentieth century, urbanisation has produced a more uniform "mainstream" Southern accent, even as traditional rural varieties recede.
