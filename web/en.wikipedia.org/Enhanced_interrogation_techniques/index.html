Enhanced interrogation techniques

"Enhanced interrogation techniques" is a euphemism used by the United States government under President George W. Bush for a set of harsh interrogation methods applied to detainees held by the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and the military during the "war on terror" after the September 11 attacks. The techniques included waterboarding, sleep deprivation, stress positions, confinement in small boxes, slapping and "walling", and exposure to cold. Critics, including the U.S. Senate Intelligence Committee, United Nations bodies, and human rights organizations, have characterized the techniques as torture.

Background

After 2001, Justice Department Office of Legal Counsel memos (the so-called "Torture Memos") advised that certain techniques did not violate U.S. anti-torture law or the Geneva Conventions as applied to detainees designated "enemy combatants." The CIA applied the techniques at secret detention sites ("black sites") to dozens of detainees, including Khalid Sheikh Mohammed and Abu Zubaydah. Some methods were adapted from the military's SERE (Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape) training, which had been designed to prepare U.S. personnel to resist coercive interrogation.

Revelations and responses

Press reports from 2004 onward, and the Abu Ghraib scandal, brought the program to public attention. In 2009 President Barack Obama released the legal memos and banned the techniques by executive order, requiring interrogations to follow the Army Field Manual. The Senate Intelligence Committee's 2014 report concluded that the program was more brutal and extensive than the CIA had represented, and that it had not produced unique intelligence that could not have been obtained by other means — conclusions the CIA disputed. No senior officials were prosecuted for the program, though debates over its legality and morality continue.

Terminology debate

The phrase "enhanced interrogation" itself has been widely criticized as a euphemism; major news organizations have debated whether to call the techniques torture, and several adopted that term in their reporting once officials acknowledged waterboarding's use.
