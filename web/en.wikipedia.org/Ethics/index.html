Ethics

Ethics, also called moral philosophy, is the branch of philosophy that involves systematizing, defending, and recommending concepts of right and wrong conduct. The field of ethics addresses questions such as "What is the best way for people to live?" and "What actions are right or wrong in particular circumstances?" It is one of the main branches of philosophy, alongside logic, metaphysics, and epistemology.

Major branches

Meta-ethics

Meta-ethics explores the nature, scope, and meaning of moral judgment. It asks whether moral truths exist, whether they are objective or relative, and what moral language means. Positions include moral realism, error theory, expressivism, and relativism.

Normative ethics

Normative ethics investigates the standards by which actions can be judged right or wrong. Its major traditions are:

  • Virtue ethics — emphasizes the character and virtues of the moral agent, descending from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics.
  • Deontological ethics — judges actions by their conformity to duties and rules; Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative is the classic example.
  • Consequentialism — judges actions by their outcomes; utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, holds that the best action maximizes overall well-being.

Applied ethics

Applied ethics examines contentious practical issues such as abortion, animal rights, capital punishment, environmental protection, and euthanasia. Specialized fields include bioethics, business ethics, medical ethics, and the ethics of technology and artificial intelligence.

History

Ethical thought begins in ancient religious and legal codes, and becomes a philosophical discipline with Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle in Greece, and with Confucius and other thinkers in China and India. Medieval ethics was dominated by theological frameworks; the Enlightenment brought secular theories from Hume, Kant, and the utilitarians. In the 20th century, meta-ethics flourished, followed by a revival of normative and applied ethics.
