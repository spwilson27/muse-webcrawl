Clay tablet

In the Ancient Near East, clay tablets were used as a writing medium, especially for writing in cuneiform, throughout the Bronze Age and well into the Iron Age. A stylus — usually made from a cut reed — was pressed into soft, moist clay to produce wedge-shaped impressions; the tablet was then dried in the sun or baked in a kiln for permanence. Clay tablets are among the earliest writing artefacts in human history and preserve the administrative records, letters, laws, literature and scholarship of Mesopotamia and its neighbours for over three millennia.


A cuneiform clay tablet recording rations, from southern Mesopotamia.

Use and manufacture

Scribes kneaded fine, washed clay into a flat pillow-shaped tablet that fit in the palm of the hand; larger tablets served for royal inscriptions, dictionaries and epics, often ruled into registers and columns. Because wet clay could be re-kneaded, tablets were recyclable drafts; documents meant to last were baked. Confidential letters were enclosed in clay "envelopes" bearing the sender's seal, to be broken open by the recipient. The fires that destroyed palaces often baked their tablet archives hard, inadvertently preserving them.

Contents

The surviving corpus — hundreds of thousands of tablets in museums — spans:

  • Administration and law: accounts, contracts, receipts, and law codes such as the Code of Hammurabi (inscribed on stone, but copied on tablets).
  • Correspondence: royal diplomacy, as in the Amarna letters of the 14th century BC.
  • Literature: the Epic of Gilgamesh, creation myths, hymns, proverbs and lamentations.
  • Scholarship: lexical lists (the ancient world's dictionaries), mathematics (including the tablet known as Plimpton 322), astronomy and omen compendia.

Major archives

Great collections were excavated at Ebla, Mari, Hattusa (the Hittite capital, with tablets in several languages), Ugarit (whose scribes adapted cuneiform to an alphabet), and above all Nineveh, where the Library of Ashurbanipal held some 30,000 tablets and fragments. Cuneiform on clay spread to Elam, the Hurrians, Urartu and Achaemenid Persia, remaining in use for astronomical records into the 1st century AD.
