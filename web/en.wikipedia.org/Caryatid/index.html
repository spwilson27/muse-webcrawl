Caryatid

The Porch of the Maidens of the Erechtheion, Athens

A caryatid is a sculpted female figure serving as an architectural support in place of a column or pillar, bearing an entablature on her head. The Greek term karyatides means "maidens of Karyai", an ancient town in Laconia where, according to tradition, women danced in honour of Artemis Karyatis. The male counterpart of a caryatid is termed a telamon or atlas.

Ancient examples

Figures used as supports appear in earlier traditions — in archaic Greek treasuries at Delphi, such as the Siphnian Treasury (c. 525 BC), whose porch was carried by two korai. The most famous surviving examples are the six caryatids of the south porch of the Erechtheion on the Acropolis of Athens (421–406 BC), known as the Porch of the Maidens. Despite their decorative appearance the figures are structurally sophisticated: their weight is carried on one straight "engaged" leg while the other is relaxed, and the thick mass of hair at the neck helps distribute the load.

The Roman architect Vitruvius gave a hostile origin story, claiming the figures represented the women of Karyai, enslaved after their city sided with Persia, and condemned to bear burdens forever — an etymology modern scholars treat with caution.

The Elgin controversy

One of the six Erechtheion caryatids was removed by Lord Elgin's agents in the early 19th century and is now in the British Museum, while the other five originals are in the Acropolis Museum in Athens (replicas stand on the building itself). Its return is a central demand of Greece's campaign for the reunification of the Parthenon sculptures.

Later use

Caryatids returned to fashion in Renaissance and especially Neoclassical architecture — Jean Goujon's caryatids for the Louvre (1550), the figures of Inigo Jones and 18th-century country houses, and innumerable 19th-century Beaux-Arts buildings. Auguste Rodin carved a fallen caryatid crushed by her stone, and the motif remains a staple of classical revival design.
