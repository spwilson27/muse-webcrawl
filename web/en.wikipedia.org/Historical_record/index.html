The historical record is the body of evidence about the past that survives in written and other communicable form — chronicles, annals, inscriptions, letters, laws, tax registers, diaries, newspapers and official archives — together with the discipline of reconstructing the past from it. Conventionally, the beginning of the historical record marks the boundary between history proper and prehistory: events before the invention of writing (around 3400–3300 BC in Mesopotamia and Egypt) are known only through archaeology and oral tradition.

Nature of the record

The historical record is fragmentary and biased by who could write, what institutions chose to preserve, and what happened to survive. Medieval history rests disproportionately on monastic chronicles; modern history on state archives and mass media. Historians therefore apply source criticism — assessing a document's authenticity, provenance, purpose and reliability — and triangulate between independent sources. "History is written by the victors" is a common shorthand for the record's selectivity, though archival records often preserve the voices of the defeated and the marginal as well.

Forms of record

  • Annals and chronicles — year-by-year records maintained at courts and monasteries (e.g. the Chinese dynastic histories).
  • Official documents — charters, censuses, court rolls, diplomatic correspondence, government archives.
  • Personal testimony — letters, memoirs, diaries, oral histories.
  • Material texts — inscriptions, coins, and monuments carrying written evidence.
  • Modern media — newspapers, photographs, sound and film recordings, and born-digital records.

Preservation

Archives and libraries preserve records against fire, decay and deliberate destruction — from ancient damnatio memoriae to modern archival appraisal deciding what is worth keeping at all. In the digital era the record faces new risks: format obsolescence, link rot and the sheer volume of born-digital material challenge the completeness of the future historical record.
