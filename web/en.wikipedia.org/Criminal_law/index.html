Criminal law

Criminal law is the body of law that relates to crime. It prescribes conduct perceived as threatening, harmful, or otherwise endangering to the property, health, safety, and moral welfare of people inclusive of one's self. Most criminal law is established by statute, which is to say that the laws are enacted by a legislature. Criminal law includes the punishment and rehabilitation of people who violate such laws. It varies according to jurisdiction and differs from civil law, where emphasis is more on dispute resolution and victim compensation than on punishment or rehabilitation.

Criminal law concerns offences against the state or society

Elements of a crime

Most crimes require two elements: the actus reus (the prohibited act, omission, or state of affairs) and the mens rea (the culpable mental state, such as intention, knowledge, or recklessness). The prosecution must generally prove both beyond reasonable doubt. Some offences impose strict liability, requiring no proof of fault as to one or more elements. Recognised defences include insanity, automatism, intoxication in limited circumstances, duress, necessity, and self-defence.

Objectives

Criminal law is distinctive for the uniquely serious, potential consequences or sanctions for failure to abide by its rules. Its enforcement mechanisms are justified by aims including retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, and restitution to the community or victims.

Classification of offences

Offences are commonly divided into categories such as offences against the person (murder, assault), offences against property (theft, burglary, criminal damage), sexual offences, drug offences, public order offences, and offences against the state. In many jurisdictions they are also classified procedurally: in England and Wales as summary, indictable, or either-way offences; in the United States as felonies and misdemeanours.

Criminal law by tradition

In common law systems, crimes developed historically at common law but are now largely statutory; in civil law systems, criminal codes comprehensively define offences, consistent with the principle of legality (nullum crimen sine lege — no crime without law). International criminal law addresses the gravest offences of concern to the international community, prosecuted by tribunals such as the International Criminal Court.
