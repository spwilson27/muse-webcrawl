François Quesnay (4 June 1694 – 16 December 1774) was a French economist and physician, the founder of the Physiocratic school of economic thought and physician to Madame de Pompadour and Louis XV at the court of Versailles. His Tableau économique (1758) is one of the foundational works of modern economics.

Life

Born at Méré, near Montfort-l'Amaury, Quesnay trained as a surgeon and practised in Mantes before moving to Paris and then Versailles. As physician to the Marquise de Pompadour he gained a position at court, where he turned increasingly to economic questions, encouraged by the intendant Vincent de Gournay. Around him gathered the circle known as the Physiocrats, including Mirabeau the elder and Dupont de Nemours.

Economic thought

Quesnay held that land, and agriculture in particular, is the sole source of a nation's wealth — the produit net — and that manufacturing and commerce, though useful, are "sterile" in the sense of producing no net surplus. His Tableau économique depicted the circulation of income between the productive (agricultural), proprietary (landowning) and sterile classes in a zig-zag diagram, an early model of the circular flow of the economy that later influenced Marx's reproduction schemes and Leontief's input–output analysis. The Physiocrats advocated a single tax on land, free trade in grain and relief from mercantilist regulation, summed up in the motto laissez-faire, laissez-passer. Adam Smith, who visited the circle in Paris, was influenced by Quesnay and planned to dedicate The Wealth of Nations to him.
