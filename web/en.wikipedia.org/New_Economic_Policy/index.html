New Economic Policy

The New Economic Policy (NEP) (Russian: Novaya ekonomicheskaya politika) was an economic policy of the Soviet Union proposed by Vladimir Lenin in 1921 as a temporary retreat from the extreme centralisation of War Communism. Lenin described it as a form of "state capitalism", intended to revive an economy devastated by world war, civil war, drought and famine.

Provisions

The NEP replaced forced grain requisitioning (prodrazverstka) with a fixed tax in kind (prodnalog), allowing peasants to sell their surplus on the open market. Small-scale industry and retail trade were denationalised and returned to private hands, foreign concessions were permitted, and a stable currency (the chervonets) was introduced. The state retained control of the "commanding heights": heavy industry, banking, transport and foreign trade. Private traders known as "NEPmen" became a visible — and ideologically suspect — feature of Soviet cities.

Results and abandonment

The policy succeeded in restoring agricultural and industrial output roughly to pre-war levels by the mid-1920s, but it generated political tension within the Bolshevik Party and economic crises, including the 1923 "scissors crisis" between industrial and agricultural prices and recurrent grain-procurement shortfalls. After Lenin's death in 1924, debates over the NEP became entangled in the succession struggle. Joseph Stalin abandoned the policy from 1928, launching forced collectivisation of agriculture and the First Five-Year Plan, which ended the market experiment and brought mass famine in Ukraine, Kazakhstan and elsewhere. Some historians and reformers later invoked the NEP as a precedent for market-oriented reform, including during perestroika.
