Fiqh (فقه)
Literally"Understanding"
DefinitionIslamic jurisprudence: human interpretation of Sharia
SourcesQuran, Sunnah, consensus (ijma), analogy (qiyas)
SchoolsHanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali; Ja'fari (Shia)

Fiqh is the theory or philosophy of Islamic law, based on the teachings of the Quran and the traditions (hadith) of the Prophet Muhammad. In the Islamic view, fiqh is for all practical purposes Islamic jurisprudence. Whereas Sharia is the divine law itself, fiqh is the human effort to understand and apply it.

Historically, fiqh was elaborated by jurists working within legal schools (madhhabs) using methods formalised in the discipline of usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence): the Quran, the Sunnah, consensus (ijma) and analogical reasoning (qiyas), supplemented in places by juristic preference and public interest (maslaha).

History

Fiqh developed in the 7th–9th centuries CE as Muslim scholars systematised rulings for the expanding Islamic state. Figures such as Abu Hanifa (d. 767), Malik ibn Anas (d. 795), al-Shafi'i (d. 820) and Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 855) gave their names to the four surviving Sunni schools; al-Shafi'i is credited with systematising legal theory.

Sources and methods

Rulings are derived by ijtihad (independent reasoning) using the sources in hierarchy, with instruments such as analogy, consensus, and consideration of the law's purposes (maqasid al-sharia). Jurists' opinions take the form of fatwas (non-binding legal opinions) and court judgments.

Schools of law

  • Hanafi — largest school; predominant in Turkey, the Balkans, Central and South Asia
  • Maliki — predominant in North and West Africa
  • Shafi'i — predominant in Southeast Asia, Egypt, East Africa
  • Hanbali — predominant in Saudi Arabia and Qatar
  • Ja'fari — the principal Twelver Shia school

Categories of acts

Fiqh classifies human acts into five categories (al-ahkam al-khamsa): obligatory (fard/wajib), recommended (mandub), neutral (mubah), disliked (makruh) and forbidden (haram). Subject areas include ritual worship, family law, inheritance, contracts, finance, crime and governance.

Modern application

In contemporary Muslim-majority states, fiqh operates in varying combinations with codified state law, especially in personal-status matters. Islamic finance is a major modern field applying classical contract jurisprudence, and debates continue over renewal of ijtihad on new questions such as bioethics and digital assets.
