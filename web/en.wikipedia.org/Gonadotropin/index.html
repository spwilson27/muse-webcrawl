Gonadotropin

Gonadotropins are glycoprotein hormones secreted by gonadotrope cells of the anterior pituitary of vertebrates. This family includes the mammalian hormones follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH), the placental hormone human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), and a pituitary chorionic gonadotropin in horses and other equids. These hormones are central to the complex endocrine system that regulates normal growth, sexual development, and reproductive function.

Structure

Each gonadotropin is a heterodimer of two glycoprotein subunits: an alpha subunit that is identical across LH, FSH, hCG, and also thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), and a beta subunit that is unique to each hormone and confers biological specificity. The subunits are heavily glycosylated, which affects their half-life in circulation.

Function

In females, LH triggers ovulation and maintains the corpus luteum, while FSH stimulates growth of ovarian follicles. In males, LH stimulates testosterone production by Leydig cells, and FSH supports spermatogenesis via Sertoli cells. Secretion is pulsatile, driven by hypothalamic GnRH, and regulated by feedback from sex steroids and inhibin.

Clinical use

Gonadotropin preparations — purified from urine or made recombinantly — are used in fertility treatment for controlled ovarian stimulation and to treat hypogonadotropic hypogonadism. hCG, detectable in urine, is the analyte of most pregnancy tests.
