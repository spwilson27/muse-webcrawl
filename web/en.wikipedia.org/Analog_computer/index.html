Analog computer

An analog computer is a type of computer that uses continuously variable physical phenomena — such as electrical voltages, mechanical rotations, or hydraulic pressures — to model and solve problems. Unlike digital computers, which manipulate discrete numbers, an analog computer represents quantities by direct physical analogy, allowing it to solve differential equations by simulating the system they describe.

History

Early examples include the slide rule, nomograms, and the Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek device for predicting astronomical positions. In the 1930s Vannevar Bush built the differential analyser, a large mechanical analog computer for solving differential equations. Electronic analog computers using operational amplifiers flourished from the 1950s to the 1970s in aerospace simulation, process control, and research; the related Analytical Engine of the previous century, by contrast, was a digital design.

Operation

The user wires (patches) together computing elements — integrators, summers, multipliers, function generators — so that voltages in the machine mirror the variables of the problem. Solutions are read from oscilloscopes, chart recorders, or meters, often in real time or faster.

Advantages and decline

Analog computers offer inherent parallelism and speed for simulation, but their accuracy is limited by component tolerances (typically 0.1–1%), and they are difficult to program and miniaturise. From the 1970s, digital computers displaced them almost everywhere, though analog and hybrid computation continue to attract research interest for energy-efficient tasks such as neural-network inference.
