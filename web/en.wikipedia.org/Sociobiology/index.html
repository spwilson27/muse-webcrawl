Sociobiology

Ants and other eusocial insects helped motivate the study of social behaviour's evolution

Sociobiology is a field of biology that aims to explain social behaviour in all species — including humans — in terms of evolutionary advantage. It emerged as a distinct discipline with the publication of E. O. Wilson's Sociobiology: The New Synthesis in 1975, which applied principles such as natural selection, kin selection, and inclusive fitness to phenomena including altruism, aggression, parental care, and mating systems.

Foundations

Sociobiology builds on W. D. Hamilton's theory of kin selection (1964), which explained the evolution of sterile worker castes in ants, bees, and wasps through shared genes; Robert Trivers's work on reciprocal altruism and parental investment; and George C. Williams's gene-centred critique of group selection. The field frequently evaluates behaviour by asking how it affects the reproductive success of the genes underlying it.

Controversy

The final chapter of Wilson's book, extending sociobiological reasoning to human behaviour, provoked intense controversy. Critics — including Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin of the Sociobiology Study Group — charged that such explanations risked genetic determinism and could be used to justify existing social inequalities, while defenders argued that studying the biological roots of behaviour need not prescribe social policy. The debate anticipated later developments including evolutionary psychology and behavioural ecology, which largely supplanted the term "sociobiology" for research on animals, while its human applications remain contested. Wilson later helped develop multilevel selection theory and wrote on the evolution of eusociality late in his career.
