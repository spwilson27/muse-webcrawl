Late antiquity

Late antiquity is a periodization of history used by historians to describe the transition from classical antiquity to the Middle Ages in Europe, the Mediterranean world, and the Near East. It is generally dated from the late 3rd century — often from the reign of Diocletian and the Crisis of the Third Century (c. 284 AD) — to the early 7th or 8th century, ending around the early Muslim conquests. The field was popularized by historian Peter Brown, whose 1971 book The World of Late Antiquity reframed the era not as one of simple decline and fall, but of profound religious and cultural transformation.

Characteristics

Late antiquity saw the Christianization of the Roman Empire after Constantine, the institutional growth of the Church, monasticism, and the eventual dominance of Christianity, alongside the continuing vitality of pagan traditions, Judaism, and the rise of Manichaeism and, at the period's end, Islam. Politically, the Roman state became more authoritarian and bureaucratic under the Dominate; the empire split into eastern and western halves, of which the West dissolved into successor kingdoms in the 5th century while the East — the Byzantine Empire — endured and prospered.

Social and cultural hallmarks include the rise of the "holy man" as a figure of authority, pilgrimage and the cult of saints and relics, changes in urban life (varied strongly by region), the emergence of new capitals such as Constantinople and Ravenna, and distinctive late antique art and architecture, including basilicas, mosaics, and ivory carving. In the Near East, the Sasanian Empire of Persia was Rome's great rival, and their long wars of the 6th and early 7th centuries exhausted both empires on the eve of the Arab conquests.

Historiography

Earlier scholarship, following Edward Gibbon, emphasized decline. Brown and his successors instead stressed continuity and creativity: the period as a distinct civilization with its own religious ferment — the "long late antiquity" extending to c. 750 or 800 in some accounts. Other historians, such as Bryan Ward-Perkins, have pushed back, arguing that the material collapse in the West — in pottery, coinage, building, and literacy — was real and severe. The field remains one of the most active in ancient and medieval history, integrating archaeology, papyrology, and the study of Syriac, Coptic, and other non-Latin traditions.
