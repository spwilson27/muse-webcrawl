Medical imaging

Medical imaging is the technique and process of imaging the interior of a body for clinical analysis and medical intervention, as well as visual representation of the function of some organs or tissues. Medical imaging seeks to reveal internal structures hidden by the skin and bones, as well as to diagnose and treat disease. It forms a major component of the specialty of radiology and is central to modern medicine.

Modalities

  • Projectional radiography — X-rays projected through the body onto a detector; includes plain radiographs, fluoroscopy and mammography.
  • Computed tomography (CT) — rotating X-ray source reconstructs cross-sectional slices.
  • Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) — strong magnetic fields and radio waves image soft tissues without ionising radiation.
  • Medical ultrasound — high-frequency sound waves; widely used in obstetrics and bedside assessment.
  • Nuclear medicine — radiotracers imaged by gamma cameras (SPECT) or positron emission tomography (PET) to show physiology.
  • Optical and endoscopic imaging — visible-light techniques including endoscopy and optical coherence tomography.

History

Medical imaging began in 1895 with Wilhelm Röntgen's discovery of X-rays and the famous radiograph of his wife's hand. Ultrasound entered medicine in the 1950s, CT in the early 1970s (Godfrey Hounsfield and Allan Cormack shared the 1979 Nobel Prize for it), and clinical MRI in the 1980s. The DICOM standard (1993) and picture archiving and communication systems (PACS) moved imaging from film to networked digital workflows.

Clinical use and safety

Imaging guides diagnosis, staging, treatment planning and image-guided intervention. Modalities using ionising radiation entail a small stochastic cancer risk, managed under the ALARA principle; MRI avoids ionisation but has its own contraindications, such as certain implants. Contrast agents improve tissue discrimination but can cause allergic-like reactions or nephrotoxicity.
