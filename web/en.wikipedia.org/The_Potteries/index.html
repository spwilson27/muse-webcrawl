The Potteries

RegionNorth Staffordshire, England
Principal cityStoke-on-Trent
Known forIndustrial ceramics production

The Potteries is the traditional name for the industrial area in and around Stoke-on-Trent in North Staffordshire, England, which was for centuries the world centre of ceramic production. The name reflects the concentration of potteries — manufacturers of pottery — in the six towns of Burslem, Fenton, Hanley, Longton, Stoke and Tunstall, which were federated as the city of Stoke-on-Trent in 1910.

Why pottery flourished here

The area combined abundant local clays, coal for firing kilns, and, crucially, nearby supplies of the materials and the canal transport — notably the Trent and Mersey Canal — needed to bring in china clay, flint and bone ash and to ship fragile finished wares out without breakage. In the eighteenth century entrepreneurs such as Josiah Wedgwood transformed a cottage craft into a factory industry, developing creamware, jasperware and bone china and marketing them to a national and imperial market.

Famous firms and wares

Besides Wedgwood, celebrated Potteries firms include Spode (originators of bone china), Minton, Royal Doulton, Moorcroft and, in the twentieth century, Portmeirion's predecessor and Clarice Cliff's art deco ware. The region's distinctive bottle ovens — bottle-shaped brick kilns, of which some fifty survive — became its emblem.

Decline and heritage

From the later twentieth century the industry contracted sharply under competition from low-cost overseas producers; famous names failed or moved production abroad, and employment fell from tens of thousands to a few thousand. The Potteries Museum & Art Gallery in Hanley holds the Staffordshire Hoard of Anglo-Saxon gold along with a major ceramics collection, and surviving factories, factory shops and the Gladstone Pottery Museum anchor a heritage industry.

In culture

Novelist Arnold Bennett, born in Hanley, immortalised the area as the "Five Towns" in novels such as Anna of the Five Towns (1902) and Clayhanger (1910), omitting Fenton — still locally remembered as "the forgotten town."
