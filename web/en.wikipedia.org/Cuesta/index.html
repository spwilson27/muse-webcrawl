Cuesta

A cuesta is a hill or ridge with a gentle slope on one side and a steep slope on the other. In geology the term is more specifically applied to a ridge where a harder sedimentary rock overlies a softer layer, the whole being tilted at a low angle, so that the gentle slope follows the dip of the strata while the steep slope cuts across them as an escarpment.

Formation

Cuestas form where gently dipping sedimentary rock layers of differing resistance are eroded. The more resistant layer, often sandstone or limestone, protects the softer shale or clay beneath it. The dip slope, or back slope, roughly parallels the bedding plane of the resistant layer, while the scarp slope retreats through erosion and undercutting of the weaker strata.

Examples

Well-known cuestas include the Niagara Escarpment in North America, the chalk cuestas of southern England such as the North Downs and South Downs, and the cuesta landscapes of the Paris Basin in France. In the United States, cuestas are widespread in Texas, New Mexico, and the Great Plains, where tilted Cretaceous strata produce long, low ridges.

Etymology

The word comes from the Spanish cuesta, meaning "slope" or "coast". It entered geological usage through descriptions of landforms in the American Southwest.
