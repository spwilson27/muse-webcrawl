Cycling infrastructure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cycling infrastructure is the infrastructure used by cyclists, including networks of roads and paths used by cyclists as well as supporting facilities such as bicycle parking, repair stations, traffic signals dedicated to cyclists and wayfinding signs. Well-designed cycling infrastructure is associated with higher rates of cycling, better safety outcomes and lower crash severity.

Types of facility

Facilities range along a spectrum of separation from motor traffic: shared roadways and bicycle boulevards; painted bicycle lanes and buffered lanes; cycle tracks separated by kerbs, bollards or parked cars; and fully independent cycle paths and greenways. Junction design — protected intersections, advanced stop lines, bicycle-specific traffic signals and two-stage turn boxes — is critical, since most collisions occur at intersections.

Networks and supporting facilities

Advocacy and design guidance stress connected, low-stress networks rather than isolated segments: directness, coherence, safety, comfort and attractiveness are the five classic Dutch design principles. Supporting infrastructure includes secure bicycle parking and lockers, bikeshare stations, integration with public transport, showers and lockers at workplaces, and maintenance such as snow clearance and sweeping.

History and examples

The Netherlands and Denmark are the standard references: Dutch cities began systematically building separated cycle networks from the 1970s, achieving cycling shares above a quarter of all trips nationally. Copenhagen's "cycle superhighways", Seville's rapid network build-out and Paris's Plan Vélo are widely cited modern examples, and COVID-era pop-up lanes accelerated adoption in many cities.
