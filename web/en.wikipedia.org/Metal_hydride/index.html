Metal hydride

ExamplesPalladium hydride, titanium hydride, LaNi5H6, sodium hydride, lithium aluminium hydride
ApplicationsHydrogen storage, NiMH batteries, hydrogen compressors, neutron moderation

Metal hydrides are compounds formed by the reaction of hydrogen with metals or intermetallic alloys. They range from ionic (saline) hydrides of the alkali and alkaline earth metals, through covalent polymeric hydrides, to metallic interstitial hydrides in which hydrogen atoms occupy sites within a metal lattice, often with non-stoichiometric compositions.

Classes

Ionic hydrides such as sodium hydride (NaH) and calcium hydride (CaH2) contain the hydride anion H− and react vigorously with water. Metallic (interstitial) hydrides, formed by many transition metals and lanthanides, retain metallic conductivity; palladium famously absorbs up to 900 times its own volume of hydrogen. Complex hydrides such as lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH4) and sodium borohydride (NaBH4) contain covalent hydride groups bound in complex anions and are powerful reducing agents in organic chemistry.

Hydrogen storage

Intermetallic hydrides such as LaNi5 and FeTi absorb and release hydrogen reversibly near ambient temperature and pressure, making them candidates for safe solid-state hydrogen storage. Their volumetric hydrogen density exceeds that of liquid hydrogen, though gravimetric capacity is low because of the heavy host metals. Hydrogen absorption is exothermic and desorption endothermic, a property exploited in thermally driven metal hydride compressors and heat pumps.

Applications

The nickel–metal hydride (NiMH) battery uses a hydrogen-storage alloy (typically AB5 or A2B7 type) as its negative electrode and was widely used in hybrid vehicles and consumer rechargeables. Metal hydrides also serve as getters in vacuum technology, as neutron moderators (e.g. zirconium hydride in TRIGA reactors), in hydrogen purification, and as catalysts for hydrogenation reactions.
