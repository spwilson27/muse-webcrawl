New Criticism

New Criticism centred interpretation on the text itself, read closely.

The New Criticism was a formalist movement in literary theory that dominated American literary criticism in the middle decades of the 20th century, from the 1920s through the 1960s. It emphasized close reading, particularly of poetry, to discover how a work of literature functions as a self-contained, self-referential aesthetic object. The movement derived its name from John Crowe Ransom's 1941 book The New Criticism.

Principles

New Critics held that a poem's meaning resides in the text itself, not in the author's intention or the reader's emotional response — errors W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley named the intentional fallacy (1946) and the affective fallacy (1949). Critics such as Cleanth Brooks celebrated paradox, irony, ambiguity, and tension as the marks of successful poetry, and textbooks like Brooks and Robert Penn Warren's Understanding Poetry (1938) made the approach the standard pedagogy in American classrooms. Influences included I. A. Richards's practical criticism and T. S. Eliot's essays.

Decline and legacy

From the 1960s the movement was challenged by reader-response criticism, structuralism, deconstruction, and historically and politically oriented approaches such as the New Historicism, which objected to its detachment of texts from history and biography. Nevertheless, close reading remains a foundational skill in literary study, and the New Critics' vocabulary permanently shaped the discipline.
