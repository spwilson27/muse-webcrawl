Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite (also Pseudo-Denys) is the name given to the anonymous author of a body of Greek theological writings of the late 5th or early 6th century. The works purport to be written by Dionysius the Areopagite, the Athenian convert of Paul the Apostle mentioned in Acts 17, and this claimed apostolic authorship gave them enormous authority in medieval Christianity.

Works

The surviving Corpus Dionysiacum comprises four treatises — The Celestial Hierarchy, The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy, The Divine Names, and the brief Mystical Theology — together with ten letters. The writings fuse Christian theology with Neoplatonic philosophy, drawing especially on Proclus. They introduced the term "hierarchy" (from the angels down through the church) and became foundational for apophatic or negative theology: the teaching that God is known best by denying every concept of him, developed most famously in the Mystical Theology, with its image of entering the "divine darkness".

Authorship and influence

Doubts about authenticity were voiced already in the 6th century (by Hypatius of Ephesus in 533), and scholars of the Renaissance, including Lorenzo Valla and Erasmus, argued the works were later forgeries; 19th-century scholarship demonstrated the dependence on Proclus, fixing the date after 485. Despite this, the corpus profoundly shaped Byzantine and Western theology: translated into Latin by John Scotus Eriugena in the 9th century, it influenced scholastics such as Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas, and medieval mysticism generally.
