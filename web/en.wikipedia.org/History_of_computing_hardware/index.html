History of computing hardware


ENIAC, one of the first general-purpose electronic computers

The history of computing hardware covers the development of devices for calculation from ancient counting aids to modern digital computers. It is conventionally divided into generations defined by their core technology: mechanical devices, electromechanical relays, vacuum tubes, transistors, integrated circuits and microprocessors.

Early aids and mechanical calculators

Counting tools such as the abacus date back millennia. The Antikythera mechanism (c. 150–100 BCE), a geared astronomical calculator from ancient Greece, showed remarkable mechanical sophistication. In the 17th century John Napier's logarithms and "bones" (1617) and the slide rule aided calculation, while Wilhelm Schickard (1623), Blaise Pascal (1642) and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1673) built mechanical calculators performing addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. Joseph Marie Jacquard's loom (1804) used punched cards to control patterns — an idea later adopted for data processing.

The 19th century: Babbage and Hollerith

Charles Babbage designed the Difference Engine (1822) to tabulate polynomial functions and conceived the programmable Analytical Engine (1837), whose design included memory, a processor ("mill") and punched-card input; Ada Lovelace wrote an algorithm for it, often regarded as the first computer program. Herman Hollerith's punched-card tabulating machines processed the 1890 US census, founding a company that became IBM.

Electromechanical and early electronic machines

In the 1930s–40s, Konrad Zuse built binary mechanical and relay computers in Germany (Z1–Z3, 1936–41), and Howard Aiken's Harvard Mark I (1944) used electromechanical relays. The Atanasoff–Berry Computer (1942) pioneered electronic digital computation for linear equations. Britain's Colossus machines (1943–44), designed by Tommy Flowers' team at Bletchley Park, used vacuum tubes to break German Lorenz cipher traffic. ENIAC (1945), built by J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly, was the first general-purpose electronic digital computer, using some 18,000 vacuum tubes.

The stored-program era

The 1945 EDVAC report articulated the stored-program concept associated with John von Neumann. The Manchester Baby (1948) first ran a stored program; EDSAC (Cambridge, 1949) provided a practical computing service. UNIVAC I (1951) became the first commercial computer in the US. First-generation machines used vacuum tubes and machine code; the second generation, from the late 1950s, used transistors (e.g. IBM 7090), magnetic core memory and high-level languages such as FORTRAN and COBOL.

Integrated circuits and minicomputers

The integrated circuit (1958–59, Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce) enabled the third generation (e.g. IBM System/360, 1964, a compatible family of machines). DEC's PDP series made minicomputers affordable to laboratories and departments; the PDP-8 (1965) and PDP-11 (1970) were landmarks. Intel's 4004 microprocessor (1971) put a CPU on a single chip, ushering in the fourth generation.

Personal computing and beyond

Microcomputers such as the Altair 8800 (1975), Apple II, Commodore PET and TRS-80 (1977) brought computing to homes and small businesses; the IBM PC (1981) and Apple Macintosh (1984) set dominant paradigms. RISC architectures, workstations and servers grew through the 1980s–90s, while laptops, smartphones (from 2007) and cloud data centres defined the 2000s–2010s. Moore's law — the doubling of transistor counts roughly every two years — held for decades before slowing, driving interest in GPUs, specialised AI accelerators and quantum computing prototypes.
