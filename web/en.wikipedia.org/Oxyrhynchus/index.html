Oxyrhynchus (Greek: Ὀξύρρυγχος, "sharp-nosed") is a city in Upper Egypt, modern el-Bahnasa, about 160 km south-west of Cairo. In antiquity it was the capital of the 19th nome of Upper Egypt, named for the sacred Oxyrhynchus fish (a species of elephantfish) worshipped in local religion. Under Greek and Roman rule it became a prosperous provincial city of perhaps 20,000–25,000 inhabitants, with theatres, baths, temples, and a hippodrome.

The papyri

The site is famous for the Oxyrhynchus Papyri: hundreds of thousands of ancient manuscript fragments recovered from its rubbish dumps, preserved by the dry desert climate beyond the reach of the Nile flood. Excavations led by Bernard Pyne Grenfell and Arthur Surridge Hunt for the Egypt Exploration Society began in 1896 and continued, with interruptions, for a decade, recovering an estimated 500,000 fragments. Their publication in the series The Oxyrhynchus Papyri has continued since 1898.

Discoveries

The papyri transformed knowledge of the ancient world, yielding lost works by Sappho, Pindar, Menander, Sophocles (the Ichneutae), and Euripides, the Hellenica Oxyrhynchia, early Christian texts including sayings of Jesus (the Oxyrhynchus Gospels), and an unmatched archive of everyday documents — letters, tax receipts, census returns, and petitions — that illuminate daily life in Roman Egypt.
