Inanna is the ancient Mesopotamian goddess of love, sexuality, beauty, war and political power, associated with the planet Venus. One of the most important deities of the Sumerian pantheon, she was worshipped from at least the Uruk period (c. 4000–3100 BC) and was later identified with the Akkadian goddess Ishtar; through her, aspects of Inanna influenced goddesses across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean.

Character and worship

Inanna embodied apparent contradictions: she was goddess both of erotic love and of battle, of the morning and evening star. Her principal sanctuary was the Eanna temple in Uruk, where she was the city's patron deity. Kings sought her favour as the source of legitimate rule, and the sacred marriage rite — in which the king ritually united with the goddess through her priestess — features in early Sumerian hymns. The priestess and poet Enheduanna (c. 2300 BC), history's earliest named author, composed celebrated hymns exalting Inanna.

Myths

The Descent of Inanna

In the best-known myth, Inanna descends to the underworld (Kur), ruled by her sister Ereshkigal. At each of its seven gates she is stripped of an item of clothing or jewellery — the emblems of her power — until she arrives naked and is struck dead. Through the intervention of the god Enki she is revived and allowed to ascend, provided she sends a substitute. Finding her husband Dumuzid insufficiently mournful, she gives him to the demons; Dumuzid thereafter spends part of each year in the underworld, a pattern linked to the seasonal cycle.

Other tales

In "Inanna and the Huluppu Tree" and in the Epic of Gilgamesh, Inanna-Ishtar proposes marriage to the hero Gilgamesh, is scornfully refused, and in revenge unleashes the Bull of Heaven. Other myths tell how she obtained the me — the divine powers and offices of civilisation — from Enki at Eridu and brought them to Uruk.

Legacy

As Ishtar she remained a central Babylonian and Assyrian goddess for over two millennia, and her imagery and functions passed in part to Phoenician Astarte, and onward toward figures such as Aphrodite. The eight-pointed star of Inanna-Ishtar endures as a symbol of ancient Mesopotamia.
