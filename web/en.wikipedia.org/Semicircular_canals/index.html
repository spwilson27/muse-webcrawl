Semicircular canals

The semicircular canals are three semicircular interconnected tubes located in the innermost part of each ear, the inner ear. The three canals are the horizontal, superior and posterior semicircular canals. They are components of the vestibular system and detect rotational movements of the head, providing the sense of dynamic balance.

Structure

The semicircular canals are a component of the bony labyrinth filled with a fluid called perilymph, and containing a smaller membranous labyrinth filled with endolymph. Each canal is roughly two-thirds of a circle, oriented approximately at right angles to the other two, so that together they cover rotations in all three planes of space. At the base of each canal is an enlargement called the ampulla, which contains a sense organ, the crista ampullaris, with hair cells embedded in a gelatinous structure called the cupula.

Function

When the head rotates, the endolymph inside the canals lags behind due to inertia, deflecting the cupula and bending the hair cells. This generates nerve signals conveyed by the vestibulocochlear nerve to the brain, which interprets them as angular acceleration. The canals work in push-pull pairs with their counterparts on the opposite side of the head. They drive reflexes such as the vestibulo-ocular reflex, which stabilizes gaze during head movement.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the semicircular canals cause vertigo. In benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), displaced otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) enter a canal, most often the posterior canal, producing brief episodes of spinning triggered by head position changes; it is treated with repositioning maneuvers such as the Epley maneuver. The caloric test assesses canal function by irrigating the ear with warm or cool water.
