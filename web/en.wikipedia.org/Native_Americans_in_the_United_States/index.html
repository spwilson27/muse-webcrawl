Native Americans, sometimes called American Indians, are the Indigenous peoples of the contiguous United States and Alaska (where the broader term Alaska Natives is used). Their ancestors migrated from Asia into the Americas at least 15,000 years ago, and by 1492 hundreds of distinct societies — from the mound-building cultures of the Mississippi Valley to the Pueblo peoples of the Southwest and the salmon cultures of the Pacific Northwest — inhabited the continent. Today Native Americans are citizens of their tribal nations, of the United States, and of their states, with tribal sovereignty recognised under US law.

Pre-colonial history

Archaeological traditions include the Clovis culture (c. 13,000 years ago), the Archaic period, and later regional developments: the Adena and Hopewell mound builders, the Mississippian culture centred on Cahokia (whose population rivalled contemporary London), the Ancestral Puebloans of Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde, the Hohokam irrigation cultures of Arizona, and the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee) of the Northeast, whose confederal structure is sometimes cited as an influence on American political thought.

Colonisation and its consequences

European colonisation brought warfare, land dispossession, and catastrophic epidemics of Old World diseases such as smallpox and measles, to which Indigenous populations had no immunity; demographic losses in many regions reached 50–90 percent over the following centuries. The Thirteen Colonies and later the United States concluded hundreds of treaties with tribal nations, most of which were subsequently broken or unilaterally altered.

Removal, reservations, and assimilation

The Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to the forced relocation of the Cherokee, Muscogee, Seminole, Chickasaw, and Choctaw — the "Five Civilized Tribes" — to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), a journey remembered as the Trail of Tears in which thousands died. Western expansion brought further wars (including the Sioux Wars and the Apache Wars), the destruction of the bison herds, and the massacre at Wounded Knee in 1890. The Dawes Act (1887) allotted communal lands to individuals and sold "surplus" land, reducing the tribal land base from about 138 million to 48 million acres by 1934. Federal boarding schools sought to assimilate Native children by suppressing Indigenous languages and customs.

20th century to present

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment and encouraged tribal self-government. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 had earlier extended US citizenship to all Native Americans born in the country. After the "termination" policies of the 1950s, the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 ushered in the modern era of tribal sovereignty; the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988) enabled tribally operated casinos, and the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (1990) addressed the return of human remains and cultural items. Contemporary issues include the protection of treaty rights, land-back movements, language revitalisation, health and economic disparities, and the crisis of missing and murdered Indigenous women.
