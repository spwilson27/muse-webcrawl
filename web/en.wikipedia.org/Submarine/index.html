A submarine (or sub) is a watercraft capable of independent operation underwater. Unlike a surface ship, a submarine controls its buoyancy with ballast tanks, allowing it to submerge, cruise at depth, and resurface at will. Most large submarines are military vessels — attack submarines, ballistic missile submarines, and guided missile submarines — but smaller submersibles serve research, tourism, and offshore industry.

History

Early underwater craft include Cornelius Drebbel's rowed submersible of the 1620s and David Bushnell's Turtle (1775), used in the American Revolutionary War. The French Plongeur (1863) was the first mechanically powered submarine. John Philip Holland's designs, adopted by the U.S. Navy in 1900, established the classic form. In both World Wars, German U-boats waged commerce-raiding campaigns against Allied shipping, most dangerously in the Battle of the Atlantic. The nuclear era began with USS Nautilus in 1955, giving submarines effectively unlimited underwater endurance.

Technology

A submarine's double hull encloses a pressure hull housing the crew and machinery, with ballast tanks between. Diesel-electric boats must snorkel or surface to recharge batteries, while nuclear submarines generate steam for propulsion and electricity indefinitely. Modern boats use sonar as their primary sense, communicate via very-low-frequency radio or trailing buoys, and navigate by inertial systems. Armament includes torpedoes, anti-ship and land-attack cruise missiles, and, for "boomers," submarine-launched ballistic missiles forming the sea-based leg of nuclear deterrence — valued because submerged missile submarines are extremely hard to locate and destroy in a first strike.

Civilian use

Research submersibles such as Alvin have explored the deep ocean, including the wreck of the Titanic and hydrothermal vent communities. Small tourist submarines operate in resort waters, and unmanned underwater vehicles increasingly take over inspection and survey tasks.
