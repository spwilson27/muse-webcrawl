Domitian (Titus Flavius Caesar Domitianus Augustus; 24 October 51 – 18 September 96) was Roman emperor from 81 to 96. The third and last emperor of the Flavian dynasty, he was the son of Vespasian and the younger brother of Titus. His reign saw the strengthening of the imperial administration and the economy, an extensive building programme in Rome, and campaigns on the Rhine and Danube frontiers, including the conquest of the Agri Decumates.

Domitian's autocratic style, his insistence on being addressed as dominus et deus ("lord and god"), and his conflicts with the Senate brought him condemnation by senatorial historians such as Tacitus, Pliny the Younger and Suetonius, whose hostile accounts shaped his traditional reputation as a tyrant. Modern scholarship has partly revised this picture, stressing his competent, if rigid, administration.

Reign

Domitian revalued the Roman coinage, raised army pay, and pursued an aggressive foreign policy: the governor Agricola campaigned in Britain, while wars against the Chatti and the Dacians led to peace terms with the Dacian king Decebalus that many senators considered humiliating. In his later years, treason trials and executions alienated the aristocracy.

Assassination and legacy

Domitian was assassinated on 18 September 96 by court officials, in a conspiracy involving his wife Domitia and the praetorian prefects. The Senate rejoiced, ordering his memory condemned (damnatio memoriae), and chose the elderly senator Nerva as his successor, beginning the era of the "Five Good Emperors".
