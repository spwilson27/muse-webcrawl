Absolute music

Absolute music (sometimes abstract music) is music that is not explicitly "about" anything; in contrast to programme music, it is non-representational. The ideal of absolute music — music valued purely for its formal and sonic properties, independent of text, story, or image — was a central doctrine of 19th-century aesthetics, though its roots are older.

History of the idea

The term was popularised (often critically) in connection with Richard Wagner, who used "absolute music" for instrumental music limited by its separation from the other arts. The critic Eduard Hanslick, in On the Musically Beautiful (1854), became the leading champion of the idea, arguing that music's content is "tonally moving forms" (tönend bewegte Formen) and that it does not represent definite feelings. The debate framed a famous opposition between the programme music of Liszt and the New German School and the "absolute" symphonies and chamber works defended by Brahms's supporters.

Discussion

Philosophers and musicologists debate whether purely absolute music exists: works acquire associations, titles, and cultural meanings, and composers such as Beethoven (whose purely instrumental works dominate the canon) were received as expressing profound ideas despite lacking programmes. In the 20th century, formalism and Stravinsky's claim that music is "powerless to express anything at all" revived the doctrine, while later scholarship has emphasised the historical and rhetorical situatedness of even the most "abstract" works.
