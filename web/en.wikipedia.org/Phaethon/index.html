Phaethon

In Greek mythology, Phaethon (Ancient Greek: Φαέθων, "the shining one") was the son of the sun god Helios and the Oceanid Clymene, famed for his disastrous attempt to drive his father's chariot across the sky for a day, losing control and nearly setting the world ablaze before Zeus struck him down. The fullest surviving account is in Book 2 of Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Myth

Taunted by Epaphus, son of Zeus, who doubted his divine parentage, Phaethon travelled to the palace of the Sun to seek proof. Helios, having sworn by the Styx to grant whatever he asked, was bound when the boy demanded to drive the sun chariot for one day. Despite his father's warnings, Phaethon took the reins; the horses, sensing a lighter, inexperienced driver, bolted from their course, plunging toward earth and scorching it — in Ovid's telling, drying rivers, burning mountains and darkening the skin of the Ethiopians — then soaring so high the earth froze. To save the cosmos, Zeus hurled a thunderbolt, and Phaethon fell "like a falling star" into the river Eridanus (identified with the Po).

Aftermath in the myth

His sisters, the Heliades, wept over his tomb until they were transformed into poplar trees, their tears hardening into amber. His friend Cycnus, grieving, became a swan. The myth explained the origin of amber, the course of the Milky Way (the chariot's scorched track, in some versions), and features of the earth's climate zones.

Sources and legacy

The story appeared in Euripides' fragmentary Phaethon and was elaborated by later poets; Plato alludes to it in the Timaeus as an allegorised astronomical event. Phaethon became a byword for youthful overambition and rashness. The name was given to the asteroid 3200 Phaethon — parent body of the Geminid meteor shower and a "rock comet" with an unusually close approach to the Sun — and to a hypothetical planet once proposed to have existed between Mars and Jupiter.
