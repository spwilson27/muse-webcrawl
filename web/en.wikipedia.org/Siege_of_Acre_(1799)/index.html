Siege of Acre (1799)

The siege of Acre of 1799 was an unsuccessful French siege of the Ottoman city of Acre (now in Israel) during Napoleon Bonaparte's Egyptian and Syrian campaign. Lasting from 20 March to 21 May 1799, the siege ended in defeat for Napoleon, forcing him to retreat to Egypt and marking the turning point of his campaign in the Levant.

Background

After invading Egypt in 1798 and advancing into Ottoman Syria in early 1799, capturing Gaza and Jaffa, Napoleon moved against Acre, a fortified port held by the Ottoman governor Jazzar Pasha. The capture of Acre was intended to secure his northern flank and open the way toward Syria and possibly Constantinople or India.

The siege

The French siege was hampered from the start: Napoleon’s heavy siege guns, sent by sea, were captured by a British squadron under Sir Sidney Smith, who used the captured artillery to strengthen Acre's defences. Smith's ships also supplied the garrison and bombarded the French. Repeated French assaults on the walls failed against determined resistance led by Jazzar Pasha and advised by the French royalist émigré engineer Louis-Edmond Antoine Le Picard de Phélippeaux, a former classmate and rival of Napoleon. Plague spread through the French camp, further weakening the besiegers.

Aftermath

On 21 May 1799, Napoleon lifted the siege and retreated to Egypt, abandoning his sick and wounded. He is reputed to have said that if Acre had fallen, he would have conquered the East. The failure ended French ambitions in Syria; Napoleon returned to France later that year.
