Glutamate

Glutamate is the anion of glutamic acid and the brain's main excitatory neurotransmitter.

Glutamate is the carboxylate anion of glutamic acid, one of the standard proteinogenic amino acids. At physiological pH, glutamic acid exists almost entirely as glutamate. It is the most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate nervous system, a key metabolic intermediate linking carbohydrate and amino-acid metabolism, and the source of the savory taste known as umami.

Neurotransmission

Glutamate is released from presynaptic terminals at the great majority of excitatory synapses in the brain and spinal cord. It acts on ionotropic receptors (AMPA, NMDA, and kainate receptors) and metabotropic glutamate receptors. NMDA-receptor activation is central to synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory. After release, glutamate is cleared from the synapse mainly by astrocytes, which convert it to glutamine and return it to neurons (the glutamate–glutamine cycle). Excessive extracellular glutamate can overexcite neurons to death — a process called excitotoxicity implicated in stroke, trauma, and neurodegenerative disease.

Metabolism

Glutamate sits at the crossroads of nitrogen metabolism. It is formed from alpha-ketoglutarate and ammonia or by transamination, and it donates amino groups for the synthesis of other amino acids. In the liver it feeds the urea cycle; in most tissues it is a precursor of glutamine, proline, and arginine, and of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA.

Flavor

Free glutamate produces the taste of umami, identified by Kikunae Ikeda in 1908 in kelp broth. Its sodium salt, monosodium glutamate (MSG), is widely used as a flavor enhancer. Glutamate occurs naturally in tomatoes, aged cheeses, mushrooms, and fermented products.
