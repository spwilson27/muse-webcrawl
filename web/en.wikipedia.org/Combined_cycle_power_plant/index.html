Combined cycle power plant

A combined cycle power plant is an assembly of heat engines that work in tandem from the same source of heat, converting it into mechanical energy to generate electricity. The most common configuration pairs a gas turbine (operating on the Brayton cycle) with a steam turbine (operating on the Rankine cycle): the hot exhaust from the gas turbine passes through a heat recovery steam generator to raise steam, which drives the steam turbine and produces additional power without burning extra fuel.

Efficiency

By capturing waste heat that a simple-cycle plant would reject, combined cycle plants achieve thermal efficiencies of around 60 percent, with the most advanced designs exceeding 62–64 percent — the highest of any conventional power generation technology. Most plants burn natural gas, and the high efficiency translates into lower fuel costs and lower carbon dioxide emissions per unit of electricity than coal or simple-cycle gas plants.

Operation

Combined cycle plants can start relatively quickly and follow load, making them a common complement to intermittent renewable generation. Configurations are described by the number of gas and steam turbines, for example "2-on-1" (two gas turbines feeding one steam turbine).
