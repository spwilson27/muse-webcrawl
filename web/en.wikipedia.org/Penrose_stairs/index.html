Penrose stairs

The Penrose stairs or Penrose steps, also called the impossible staircase, is an impossible object: a two-dimensional depiction of a staircase in which the steps form a continuous loop, so that a person could climb them forever and never get any higher. It was devised by Lionel Penrose and his son Roger Penrose, who published it in 1959, and was popularised by M. C. Escher's lithograph Ascending and Descending (1960).

History

The illusion had appeared earlier in a 1954 work by the Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd, unknown to the Penroses. Roger Penrose encountered Escher's work at the 1954 International Congress of Mathematicians in Amsterdam and, inspired, developed impossible objects with his father; Escher in turn took the staircase (and the Penrose triangle) as subjects after seeing the Penroses' article.

Interpretations

Physically realised "impossible staircases" exist as trick sculptures that work only from a single camera viewpoint, exploiting forced perspective. The Penrose stairs appear widely in popular culture, notably in the film Inception (2010). In music, the auditory analogue is the Shepard scale, which seems to rise in pitch endlessly.
