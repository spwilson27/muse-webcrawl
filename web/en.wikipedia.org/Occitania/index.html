Occitania

Occitania (Occitan: Occitània) is the historical region in Southern Europe where the Occitan language was historically spoken and where it is still sometimes used as a second language. It encompasses most of southern France — roughly the southern third of the country — together with the Aran Valley in Spain and the Occitan Valleys of Piedmont in Italy, and Monaco. The name derives from òc, the Occitan word for "yes", in contrast to the northern French oïl (modern oui).

History

The region was Romanised early and retained a distinct Romance culture through the Middle Ages, when troubadour lyric poetry in Occitan influenced all of European literature. In the 13th century the Albigensian Crusade against the Cathars and the annexation of the County of Toulouse (1229–1271) brought the south under the French crown. Over subsequent centuries, and decisively through compulsory French-language schooling in the 19th and 20th centuries, French displaced Occitan in public life; the language is now classified as endangered, though revival movements and bilingual schools (calandretas) persist.

Geography

Occitania stretches from the Atlantic coast near Bordeaux to the Alps and the Italian frontier, and from the Loire and the Massif Central to the Pyrenees and the Mediterranean. Since 2016 most of the French part has lain within the administrative regions of Occitanie (named after the historical region) and Nouvelle-Aquitaine, plus Auvergne-Rhône-Alpes and Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur.

Culture

The region is associated with troubadour poetry, the Cathar castles of the Languedoc, a distinctive cuisine, bull traditions of the Camargue and the Landes, and rugby's heartlands in the south-west. Occitan identity is expressed in cultural festivals and the red-and-gold Occitan cross, the region's emblem.
