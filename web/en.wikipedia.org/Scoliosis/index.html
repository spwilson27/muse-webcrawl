Scoliosis

Scoliosis is a condition in which a person's spine has an abnormal curve to the side. The curve is usually "S"- or "C"-shaped in three dimensions. In some, the degree of curve is stable, while in others, it increases over time. Mild scoliosis does not typically cause problems, but more severe cases can affect breathing and movement.

Signs and symptoms

Signs of scoliosis can include uneven shoulders, one shoulder blade appearing more prominent than the other, an uneven waist, and one hip higher than the other. As the curve progresses, the spine may also rotate, causing the ribs on one side to stick out more than the other. Back pain is more common in adults with scoliosis.

Causes

In most cases the cause is unknown, a form classified as idiopathic; adolescent idiopathic scoliosis is the most common type and typically appears between the ages of 10 and 18. Other cases result from congenital vertebral abnormalities, neuromuscular conditions such as cerebral palsy and muscular dystrophy, or degenerative changes in older adults.

Diagnosis

Scoliosis is suspected when the shoulders, waist, or hips appear uneven, and can be screened with the Adam's forward bend test. Diagnosis is confirmed with X-rays of the spine, on which the severity of the curve is measured using the Cobb angle; a curve greater than 10 degrees is considered scoliosis.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the degree of curve, its location, and its cause. Small curves are usually monitored with periodic observation. Curves in growing children may be treated with bracing to prevent progression. Larger or worsening curves may require surgery, most commonly spinal fusion, to correct and stabilise the spine.

Epidemiology

Scoliosis affects approximately 2–3% of the population. It occurs most often during the growth spurt just before puberty, and girls are more likely than boys to develop curves that progress to the point of needing treatment.
