Random article

Random article is a feature of Wikipedia and many other wiki-based websites that displays a randomly selected article from the encyclopedia's database when invoked. On Wikipedia it is implemented through the special page Special:Random, and a link to it has appeared in the sidebar of the desktop interface since the project's early years. The feature serves both as a discovery tool for readers and as a quality-control aid for editors.

How it works

When a user visits Special:Random, the MediaWiki software selects a page at random from the main article namespace and redirects the browser to it. The selection is uniformly distributed over the database of articles, so obscure and little-read entries are exactly as likely to appear as famous ones. Variants of the feature allow random selection within particular categories, from a list of good or featured articles, or among pages needing cleanup. Sister projects operate equivalents, such as a random entry in Wiktionary or a random quotation in Wikiquote.

Purposes

For casual readers, the random article is a tool of serendipity: a way to encounter topics outside one's usual interests, sometimes described as a digital analogue of flipping through the volumes of a printed encyclopedia. Teachers and trivia hosts use it for impromptu exercises, and it forms the starting point of games such as Wikiracing, in which players must navigate from a randomly chosen article to a target article using only internal links.

For editors, repeatedly sampling random articles provides an unbiased snapshot of the encyclopedia's overall state. Vandalised, malformed or very short articles surface quickly this way, and maintenance communities have organised "random article patrols" on this basis. Because the sample is unbiased, the distribution of article quality encountered through Special:Random is often used informally to gauge the health of the project as a whole.

History

The feature descends from the "random page" function of early wiki software. Wikipedia's predecessor software, UseModWiki, already allowed random page display, and the function was carried into the MediaWiki platform written for Wikipedia in 2002. With the growth of the article count from thousands to millions, the probability of landing on any given well-known article became vanishingly small, which increased the feature's reputation for producing obscure results: minor villages, moth species and nineteenth-century clergymen are statistically typical draws.

In culture

The randomness of the feature has been used in webcomics, browser extensions and mobile applications that present "one random fact a day". Researchers in information science have also used random sampling of articles to study bias, coverage and readership across the encyclopedia's language editions.
