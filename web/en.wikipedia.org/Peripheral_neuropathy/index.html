Peripheral neuropathy

Peripheral neuropathy, often shortened to neuropathy, refers to damage or disease affecting the peripheral nervous system — the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord. Depending on which nerves are affected, it can cause weakness, numbness, altered sensation and pain, typically in the hands and feet, as well as autonomic dysfunction.

Classification

Neuropathies are classified by the number of nerves involved (mononeuropathy, mononeuritis multiplex, or polyneuropathy), by the function affected (motor, sensory, autonomic or mixed), and by whether the axon or the myelin sheath is primarily damaged. The most common presentation is a length-dependent, symmetrical polyneuropathy producing a "glove and stocking" pattern of sensory loss.

Causes

More than 100 causes are known. The most common worldwide is diabetes mellitus; other causes include alcohol use, vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, autoimmune conditions such as Guillain–Barré syndrome and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, infections, hereditary disorders such as Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, toxins and many chemotherapy agents. In a substantial minority of cases no cause is found.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines history, examination, blood tests, nerve conduction studies and electromyography, and occasionally nerve biopsy. Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible; pain is managed with medicines such as gabapentinoids, duloxetine, tricyclic antidepressants and topical agents, alongside physiotherapy and foot care to prevent injury.
