Hubble Ultra-Deep Field

The Hubble Ultra-Deep Field (HUDF) is an image of a small region of space in the constellation Fornax, built from Hubble Space Telescope data accumulated over hundreds of orbits in 2003–2004. It contains an estimated 10,000 galaxies and was, at its release, the deepest image of the universe ever taken in visible light.

The Ultra-Deep Field: some 10,000 galaxies in a patch of sky a tenth the Moon's apparent diameter.

Observations

Astronomers pointed Hubble at an apparently empty patch of sky — chosen to avoid bright foreground stars and the Milky Way's dust — for 800 exposures totalling about 11.3 days of exposure time. The Advanced Camera for Surveys captured optical light, while NICMOS added near-infrared data revealing still more distant objects.

Scientific results

Some galaxies in the field are seen as they were less than a billion years after the Big Bang, with redshifts around 6–7 (and some candidates higher). The image let astronomers trace galaxy assembly over most of cosmic history and estimate that the observable universe contains far more galaxies than earlier counts suggested — later analyses raised the total to hundreds of billions or more.

Legacy

The HUDF followed the 1995 Hubble Deep Field and was extended by the Ultra Deep Field infrared and the eXtreme Deep Field (2012). The James Webb Space Telescope has since imaged the region still deeper in the infrared.
