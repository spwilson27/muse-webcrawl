Victor Emmanuel II Monument

The Vittoriano dominates Piazza Venezia in central Rome.

The Victor Emmanuel II National Monument (Italian: Monumento Nazionale a Vittorio Emanuele II), also known as the Vittoriano or the Altare della Patria ("Altar of the Fatherland"), is a large monument built in Rome, Italy, to honour Victor Emmanuel II, the first king of a unified Italy. It occupies a site between the Piazza Venezia and the Capitoline Hill. Constructed of white Botticino marble, it features majestic staircases, Corinthian columns, fountains, an equestrian statue of Victor Emmanuel, and two statues of the goddess Victoria riding on quadrigas.

Designed by Giuseppe Sacconi in 1885, the monument was inaugurated in 1911 and completed in 1935. Part of the Capitoline Hill — including medieval neighbourhoods and part of the Roman Forum's backdrop — was demolished for its construction. Since 1921 it has housed the tomb of Italy's Unknown Soldier, with an eternal flame and guards, beneath the statue of the goddess Roma.

Design and symbolism

The monument is 135 metres wide and up to 81 metres high. Its neoclassical design with eclectic details was intended to symbolise national unity and liberty after the Risorgimento. The great bronze equestrian statue of Victor Emmanuel II at its centre is one of the largest in Italy; the horse's belly famously hosted a celebratory banquet for the workmen. Reliefs and statues represent Italy's regions, cities, and virtues, while the colonnade supports two bronze quadrigas bearing winged Victories.

Controversy and nicknames

Romans have long mocked the monument's conspicuous whiteness and wedding-cake profile, nicknaming it la torta nuziale ("the wedding cake"), la dentiera ("the dentures"), and "the typewriter". Critics decried the destruction of historic fabric on the Capitoline Hill. Yet the Vittoriano has become one of Rome's most recognisable landmarks, offering panoramic views from its rooftop terrace, and it houses the Central Museum of the Risorgimento and exhibition spaces.

Unknown Soldier

On 4 November 1921, the body of an unidentified Italian soldier from the First World War, chosen from eleven unknown dead by Maria Bergamas, a mother who had lost a son, was interred beneath the statue of Rome. The tomb is the focal point of Italy's national commemorations on Republic Day (2 June) and Unity Day (4 November).
