The Canaanite religion was the set of ancient Semitic religious beliefs and practices of the Canaanites of the Levant, flourishing from the Bronze Age into the first centuries of the Iron Age. It was polytheistic and centred on a pantheon headed by the creator god El, with the storm god Baal playing a leading role in myth and cult. Canaanite religion is known from archaeological remains — temples, standing stones, figurines and offering vessels — and from the cuneiform texts discovered at the city of Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra) in Syria.

Canaanite religion formed the cultural background of the Phoenicians, who carried its gods across the Mediterranean, and it strongly influenced the emerging religion of ancient Israel, which in its earliest form shared much of the Canaanite pantheon and cult vocabulary before the exclusive worship of Yahweh was established.

Pantheon

The head of the pantheon was El, the aged, wise creator and father of gods and humanity, whose consort was Asherah, mother of the gods. Their son Baal (Hadad), the storm and fertility god, battles the sea god Yam and the death god Mot in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle; his sister and consort Anat is a fierce goddess of war and hunting. Other deities include Astarte, goddess of love and war; Dagon, god of grain; Resheph, god of plague; the sun goddess Shapash; the moon god Yarikh; and the craftsman god Kothar-wa-Khasis.

Mythology

The best-preserved Canaanite myths are the Ugaritic texts written in an alphabetic cuneiform script around the 13th century BCE. The Baal Cycle recounts Baal's victory over Yam (Sea), the building of his palace on Mount Zaphon, and his descent into the underworld after defeat by Mot (Death), followed by his return with the seasonal rains. The Epic of Kirta and the Tale of Aqhat combine royal legend with divine intervention.

Worship and practice

Cult centred on temples and open-air "high places" (bamot) marked by standing stones (massebot). Worship involved animal sacrifice, libations, burnt offerings and communal feasting; temple personnel included priests (kohanim), singers and, by some accounts, cult functionaries whose exact roles remain debated. Household religion used terracotta figurines and small shrines. Funerary practices included burial with grave goods and offerings to deceased ancestors, reflecting belief in an afterlife in the underworld.

Influence and legacy

The Hebrew Bible preserves abundant evidence of engagement with Canaanite religion, frequently polemicising against the worship of Baal and Asherah while retaining Canaanite poetic forms and divine epithets — such as El Elyon and El Shaddai — for Yahweh. As Israelite monotheism developed, Canaanite practices were progressively suppressed, though they survived longer in popular religion and, in Phoenician form, at colonies such as Carthage well into the Roman period.
