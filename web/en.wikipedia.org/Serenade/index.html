Serenade

TypeMusical composition
OriginEvening song of greeting or courtship
PeriodsBaroque, Classical, Romantic, modern
Famous examplesMozart, Eine kleine Nachtmusik; Serenade for Strings (Tchaikovsky; Dvořák; Elgar)

A serenade is a musical composition or performance in someone's honour, traditionally a calm, light piece performed in the evening. The word derives from the Italian serenata, from sereno ("calm, clear"), associated with the evening sky (in folk etymology linked to sera, evening). In its oldest form, a serenade was a song sung by a lover beneath his beloved's window, accompanied by guitar or mandolin — a scene immortalised in Mozart's Don Giovanni and Rossini's The Barber of Seville.

History

In the Baroque era the serenata was a dramatic cantata performed outdoors in the evening, often for royal occasions (Handel's Acis and Galatea began as one). In the Classical era, composers such as Haydn and Mozart wrote multi-movement orchestral or chamber serenades as light entertainment for social occasions, related to the divertimento and cassation. The most famous is Mozart's Eine kleine Nachtmusik (1787). Beethoven wrote serenades for chamber groups, and Brahms's two orchestral serenades were stepping stones toward his symphonies.

Later serenades

In the Romantic and modern eras the serenade became a character piece or a work for string orchestra: well-known examples include Tchaikovsky's Serenade for Strings (1880), Dvořák's Serenade for Strings (1875), Elgar's Serenade (1892), and works by Suk, Wolf and Dag Wirén. Twentieth-century composers such as Britten (Serenade for Tenor, Horn and Strings, 1943) and Bernstein (Serenade after Plato's Symposium, 1954) revived the form. In popular culture, serenading survives in mariachi performances and in songs such as "Serenade" standards of the big-band era.
