V-2 rocket

The V-2 was the world's first long-range guided ballistic missile.

The V-2 (German: Vergeltungswaffe 2, "Retribution Weapon 2"), with the technical name Aggregat 4 (A-4), was the world's first long-range guided ballistic missile. Developed by Nazi Germany under Wernher von Braun at Peenemünde, it was the first artificial object to travel into space, and after the war became the foundation of both the American and Soviet rocket programmes.

Development

Work began in the 1930s at Kummersdorf and moved to Peenemünde on the Baltic coast. The liquid-fuelled engine burned alcohol and liquid oxygen, delivered by turbopumps, and the missile was guided by gyroscopes. The first successful test flight reached the edge of space on 3 October 1942. Production moved underground to Mittelwerk, where concentration-camp prisoners from Mittelbau-Dora built the missiles under murderous conditions — more people died manufacturing the V-2 than were killed by its use as a weapon.

Operational use

The V-2 entered service in September 1944, launched mainly against London and Antwerp from mobile batteries in the Netherlands and Germany. Because it arrived supersonically, there was no warning of its approach. Over 3,000 were launched; around 1,115 struck England, killing approximately 2,754 people in London alone, with many more killed in Belgium.

Legacy

After the war the United States (Operation Paperclip) and the Soviet Union seized V-2 hardware and personnel. Captured rockets were tested at White Sands, New Mexico, and a V-2 flight in 1946 took the first photograph of Earth from space. The V-2's design directly led to the Redstone and early Soviet R-series missiles, making it the ancestor of the vehicles that opened the Space Age.
