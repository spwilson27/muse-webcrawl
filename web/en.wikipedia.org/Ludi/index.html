Ludi (Latin plural of ludus, "game, play, sport") were public games held for the benefit and entertainment of the Roman people, typically in connection with religious festivals. Together with the gladiatorial munera, they were a central feature of Roman public life and a major arena in which politicians and emperors displayed generosity and won popularity.

Types of games

Ludi circenses were chariot races held in a circus, above all the Circus Maximus in Rome, seating perhaps 150,000 spectators; the races, usually between four factions (Reds, Blues, Greens and Whites), opened with a solemn procession (pompa circensis) carrying images of the gods. Ludi scaenici were theatrical performances — comedies, tragedies, mime and pantomime — staged in theatres or temporary wooden structures. Other festivals included athletic contests in the Greek manner, beast hunts (venationes) and, increasingly, gladiatorial combats, technically munera (obligations) rather than ludi.

Major festivals

The oldest and most important were the Ludi Romani (Roman Games) in honour of Jupiter, held each September and traced by tradition to the regal period. Others included the Ludi Plebeii (November), the Ludi Apollinares vowed in 212 BC during the Second Punic War, the Ludi Megalenses for the Magna Mater, and the Ludi Cereales for Ceres. Games might also be votivi — vowed by a general in battle — or given to celebrate triumphs, temple dedications and imperial anniversaries. By the late empire the calendar of festivals consumed a large fraction of the year.

Organisation and significance

During the Republic, games were mounted by magistrates, especially the aediles, partly from public funds and partly from their own pockets as an investment in political careers; Julius Caesar and others borrowed heavily to stage spectacular shows. Under the Empire, provision of games became largely an imperial monopoly and one element of the "bread and circuses" (panem et circenses) by which, in Juvenal's satire, emperors kept the populace content.
