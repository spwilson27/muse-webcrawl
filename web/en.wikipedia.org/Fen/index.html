Fen


A fen at Wicken Fen, England

A fen is a type of peat-accumulating wetland fed by mineral-rich ground or surface water. It is one of the main types of wetland, along with marshes, swamps, and bogs. Fens are distinguished from bogs by receiving water and nutrients from sources other than precipitation alone, which makes them less acidic and more nutrient-rich, supporting a diverse flora of sedges, grasses, and wildflowers. As organic matter accumulates faster than it decomposes, fens build up peat and can, over millennia, fill in to become carr woodland or raised bog.

Characteristics and ecology

The water chemistry of fens ranges from slightly acidic ("poor fens") to alkaline ("rich fens"), depending on the bedrock over which the groundwater has travelled; rich fens on limestone support especially diverse communities, including orchids and many rare sedges and mosses. Fens are important habitats for invertebrates such as dragonflies and fen raft spiders, and for breeding birds, and they store significant amounts of carbon in their peat soils.

Distribution and conservation

Fens occur throughout the temperate and boreal zones, notably in the lowlands of northern Europe, Russia, Canada, and the northern United States. The Fens of East Anglia in England were once a vast wetland region, mostly drained from the 17th century onward for agriculture, leaving remnants such as Wicken Fen — one of Britain's oldest nature reserves — and the Norfolk Broads. Because drainage, peat cutting, and nutrient pollution have destroyed most fenland, surviving fens are priorities for conservation and restoration, including projects that re-wet drained peatlands to reduce carbon emissions.
