Bossa nova is a style of Brazilian music developed in the late 1950s in Rio de Janeiro, fusing the rhythms of samba with the harmonic sophistication of cool jazz. Literally "new trend" or "new wave" in Portuguese, it is characterised by a gentle, understated vocal delivery, intricate jazz-influenced chords, and a distinctive syncopated guitar pattern.

Origins

The genre crystallised around composer Antônio Carlos Jobim, poet Vinícius de Moraes, and guitarist-singer João Gilberto, whose album Chega de Saudade (1959) — and the title song, released as a single in 1958 — launched the style. Gilberto's whispered singing and his batida guitar rhythm, condensing the parts of a samba ensemble into one instrument, became bossa nova's hallmarks. The music first flourished among middle-class musicians in Rio's South Zone apartments in neighbourhoods such as Copacabana and Ipanema.

International success

Bossa nova reached the United States in the early 1960s through guitarist Charlie Byrd, saxophonist Stan Getz, and the soundtrack to the film Black Orpheus (1959), with music by Jobim and Luiz Bonfá. The album Getz/Gilberto (1964), featuring João Gilberto, his wife Astrud Gilberto, and Jobim, won the Grammy for Album of the Year, and "The Girl from Ipanema" became one of the most recorded songs in history.

Musical characteristics

Bossa nova is typically in 2/4 time with a clave-like rhythmic pattern, extended and altered jazz chords, and restrained, intimate dynamics. Its Portuguese lyrics often evoke love, the sea, and Rio's landscape in a wistful tone of saudade. The style influenced generations of jazz, pop, and lounge musicians worldwide and remains a symbol of Brazilian music.
