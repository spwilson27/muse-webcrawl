Born: c. 1478, Trujillo, Spain
Died: June 26, 1541, Lima, Peru

Francisco Pizarro González (c. 1478 – June 26, 1541) was a Spanish conquistador, best known for his expeditions that led to the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire. Born in Trujillo, Spain, to a poor family, he is considered one of the most significant figures in the Spanish colonization of the Americas.

Early life and expeditions

Pizarro arrived in the New World as a young man and took part in expeditions in present-day Colombia and Panama, including Vasco Núñez de Balboa's crossing of the Isthmus of Panama in 1513. In the 1520s, in partnership with Diego de Almagro and Hernando de Luque, he organized voyages south along the Pacific coast of South America, reaching the Inca port of Tumbes and bringing back reports of great wealth.

Conquest of Peru

After securing a royal license from Charles V in the Capitulation of Toledo (1529), Pizarro returned to Peru in 1532 with fewer than 200 men. Taking advantage of a civil war between the Inca emperor Atahualpa and his brother Huáscar, Pizarro captured Atahualpa at the Battle of Cajamarca in November 1532. Atahualpa filled a room with gold and silver as ransom but was nevertheless executed in 1533. Spanish forces occupied the Inca capital of Cuzco later that year, and Pizarro founded Lima in 1535 as the capital of the new colony.

Death and legacy

Rivalry with his former partner Almagro led to civil war among the conquistadors; Almagro was defeated and executed in 1538. Three years later, on June 26, 1541, supporters of Almagro's son assassinated Pizarro in Lima. His conquest opened the way to Spanish rule over the Andes, with profound and often devastating consequences for the indigenous population through war, disease, and forced labor.
