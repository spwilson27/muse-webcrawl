Robust statistics

Robust statistics are statistics with good performance for data drawn from a wide range of probability distributions, especially for distributions that are not normal. Robust statistical methods have been developed for many common problems, such as estimating location, scale, and regression parameters. One motivation is to produce statistical methods that are not unduly affected by outliers. Another motivation is to provide methods with good performance when there are small departures from a parametric distribution; for example, the t-test works reasonably well for data far from the normal distribution, whereas some other methods do not.

Introduction

Classical statistical procedures rely heavily on assumptions that are often not met in practice — in particular the assumption that the data follow a normal distribution, at least approximately, or that the central limit theorem can be relied upon to produce normally distributed estimates. When these assumptions fail, classical estimators can perform badly. Robust estimators, by contrast, are designed so that a moderate amount of contamination or model error produces only moderate changes in the result.

Examples

The median is a robust measure of central tendency, while the mean is not: the median has a breakdown point of 50%, the highest possible, while a single outlier can move the mean arbitrarily far. Similarly, the interquartile range is a robust measure of scale, whereas the standard deviation is sensitive to extreme values. In regression, least squares estimation is highly sensitive to outliers in the response, whereas robust alternatives such as least absolute deviations regression or M-estimators limit their influence.

Key concepts

Breakdown point

The breakdown point of an estimator is the proportion of incorrect observations — for instance, arbitrarily large observations — it can handle before it produces an arbitrarily wrong result.

Influence function

The influence function, introduced by Frank Hampel, measures the effect of an infinitesimal contamination at a point on the value of an estimator. Estimators with bounded influence functions are called B-robust.

M-estimators

M-estimators generalise maximum likelihood estimation by replacing the likelihood criterion with a more general loss function. Peter Huber's foundational work in the 1960s established their theory, and Huber loss functions remain widely used. With a suitable choice of the loss function, M-estimators can achieve both high robustness and high efficiency at the model distribution.

History

Although robust ideas appear in early work — for example, the median predates the mean as a summary — the modern field grew from Huber's 1964 paper on robust estimation of a location parameter and from John Tukey's exploratory data analysis. The subject developed rapidly in the 1970s and 1980s, with contributions by Hampel, Peter Rousseeuw, and others, including high-breakdown methods such as least median of squares and S-estimators.
