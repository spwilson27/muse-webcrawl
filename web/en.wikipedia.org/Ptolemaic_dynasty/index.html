Ptolemaic dynasty

The Ptolemaic dynasty (Ancient Greek: Πτολεμαῖοι, Ptolemaioi), also known as the Lagid dynasty, was a Macedonian Greek royal dynasty which ruled Ptolemaic Egypt from 305 to 30 BC. Founded by Ptolemy I Soter, a general of Alexander the Great, the dynasty ruled for nearly 300 years and was the last dynasty of ancient Egypt, ending with the reign of Cleopatra VII.

History

After Alexander's death in 323 BC, his empire was partitioned among his generals, the Diadochi; Ptolemy secured Egypt and proclaimed himself king in 305/304 BC. His successors patronised the Library of Alexandria and the Mouseion, making Alexandria the intellectual capital of the Hellenistic world. All male rulers of the dynasty bore the name Ptolemy, while queens included Cleopatras, Berenices, and Arsinoes, with sibling marriage adopted from pharaonic custom.

Decline and fall

From the 2nd century BC the kingdom weakened through dynastic civil wars and growing Roman interference. Cleopatra VII, allied first with Julius Caesar and then with Mark Antony, attempted to restore its power; after their defeat at Actium (31 BC), Egypt was annexed by Octavian in 30 BC, ending three millennia of pharaonic rule.

Culture

The Ptolemies presented themselves as pharaohs to their Egyptian subjects — building temples such as Edfu and Philae — while ruling as Greek kings. The period produced advances in mathematics, astronomy, and medicine, and the cult of Serapis, a syncretic god promoted by Ptolemy I.
