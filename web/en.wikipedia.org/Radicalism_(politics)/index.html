Radicalism (politics)

Radicals of the nineteenth century demanded fundamental political reform.

In politics, radicalism (or radical politics) denotes the intent to transform or replace the fundamental principles of a society or political system, often through social change, structural change, revolution or radical reform. The word derives from the Latin radix, "root": a radical seeks change at the root of a system rather than merely adjusting it.

History

The term was first used politically in late 18th-century Britain, where Charles James Fox and others called for "radical reform" of Parliament in 1797. In the 19th century, Radicals formed an influential current in British and French politics, advocating universal male suffrage, the secret ballot, abolition of rotten boroughs, and free trade; in Britain they cooperated with and were eventually absorbed into the Liberal Party. Radical movements have since appeared across the spectrum, from radical liberalism and radical democracy to far-left and far-right "radical" programmes, and the label was used by parties such as the French Radical Party and Italy's Radical Party.

Meanings

Because it is defined relative to an existing order, radicalism has no fixed ideological content: it has described democrats, abolitionists, socialists and others demanding root-and-branch change, and in contemporary usage "radicalisation" often refers to adoption of extremist positions, though scholars distinguish radical views from violent extremism.
