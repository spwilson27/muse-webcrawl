Charles I (19 November 1600 – 30 January 1649) was King of England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1625 until his execution in 1649. His belief in the divine right of kings and his attempts to rule without Parliament, combined with religious policies that alienated both English Puritans and Scottish Presbyterians, led to the English Civil War. Defeated, he was tried for high treason by the Rump Parliament and beheaded — the only English monarch to be executed by his own subjects in this manner. The monarchy was abolished and a republic, the Commonwealth, proclaimed, until Charles's son Charles II was restored in 1660.

Early reign and Parliament

The second son of James VI and I, Charles became heir after the death of his elder brother Henry in 1612. As prince he accompanied the Duke of Buckingham on the abortive "Spanish Match" journey to Madrid in 1623 and returned an opponent of Spain. Succeeding in 1625, he married the French Catholic Henrietta Maria, whose religion made him suspect to Protestant opinion. Three parliaments in four years attacked his favourite Buckingham (assassinated in 1628) and his fiscal methods; after dissolving Parliament in 1629, Charles ruled alone for eleven years — the "Personal Rule" or, to critics, "eleven years' tyranny."

Personal Rule and religious policy

Without parliamentary taxes, Charles exploited prerogative revenues, most controversially Ship Money, levied on inland counties from 1635; John Hampden's narrow legal defeat over it in 1637 inflamed opinion. With Archbishop William Laud he pursued a "High Church" ceremonial uniformity ("the beauty of holiness") that Puritans equated with popery. Attempting to impose a new prayer book on Presbyterian Scotland provoked the National Covenant (1638) and the Bishops' Wars (1639–40), whose failure forced Charles to summon Parliament in 1640.

Civil War

The Long Parliament impeached his ministers Strafford (executed 1641) and Laud, abolished prerogative courts, and, after the Irish Rebellion of 1641, contested control of the militia. Charles's attempt to arrest five MPs in January 1642 destroyed remaining trust; he raised his standard at Nottingham that August. The ensuing First Civil War saw Royalist successes in 1643 matched by Parliament's alliance with the Scots and the creation of the New Model Army, which crushed the king at Naseby (1645). Charles surrendered to the Scots in 1646, was handed to Parliament, escaped to the Isle of Wight, and intrigued for a Scottish invasion — the Second Civil War of 1648, ended by Cromwell's victory at Preston.

Trial and execution

The army purged Parliament (Pride's Purge, December 1648) and the remaining Rump erected a High Court of Justice. Charles refused to plead, denying the court's legitimacy. Convicted of tyranny and treason against the people of England, he was beheaded outside the Banqueting House, Whitehall, on 30 January 1649, reportedly wearing two shirts so that shivering would not be mistaken for fear. His scaffold speech defended the people's liberties "in their persons and goods" but insisted a subject's and sovereign's rights were different. The Eikon Basilike, published as his spiritual autobiography, fostered a cult of "Charles the Martyr"; he was canonised by the Church of England in 1660, its only saint formally added after the Reformation.
