Virtual screening

Virtual screening (VS) is a computational technique used in drug discovery to search libraries of small molecules in order to identify those structures most likely to bind to a drug target, typically a protein receptor or enzyme. It is usually a computer-based "screen" of large chemical databases, from which a manageable number of candidate compounds are selected for experimental testing. Virtual screening complements high-throughput screening and has become a standard stage of early drug discovery.

Methods

Two broad strategies are used. In structure-based screening, candidate ligands are docked into the three-dimensional binding site of the target and scored for predicted affinity; the method depends on an experimental or modelled protein structure. In ligand-based screening, compounds are compared with known active molecules using molecular similarity, pharmacophore models, or machine-learning classifiers; no target structure is required. Screens usually filter libraries first for drug-likeness (for example, Lipinski's rule of five) and undesired reactive groups, including so-called PAINS patterns.

Workflow and limitations

A typical screen evaluates millions to billions of compounds — ultra-large make-on-demand libraries now exceed billions — using fast scoring, with top-ranked hits rescored by more expensive methods such as molecular dynamics and free-energy calculations. Only a small fraction of predicted hits prove active experimentally, and scoring inaccuracies, protein flexibility, and solvation effects remain major limitations; nonetheless, virtual screening has delivered numerous lead compounds, including for targets considered difficult by other means.
