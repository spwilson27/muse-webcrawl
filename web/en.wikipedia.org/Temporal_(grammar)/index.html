Temporal (grammar)

In grammar, a temporal expression, form, or construction is one that relates to time — locating an event, action, or state at a point or over a period relative to the moment of speaking or to another reference point. The chief grammatical devices for expressing time relations across languages are tense (which locates a situation in time, typically past, present, or future), aspect (which expresses how the action unfolds in time — completed, ongoing, habitual), and temporal adverbials and conjunctions ("yesterday", "before the war", "while", "until").

Tense and reference points

The philosopher Hans Reichenbach's influential analysis (1947) distinguishes three points: the speech point (S, when the utterance is made), the event point (E, when the described situation occurs), and the reference point (R, the vantage from which the event is viewed). Basic tenses follow from the ordering of these points: in the simple past ("she left"), E, R precede S; in the present perfect ("she has left"), E precedes R, and R coincides with S; in the future perfect ("she will have left"), S precedes R, which follows E. Many languages also make finer temporal distinctions than English — for example, hodiernal (today) versus hesternal (yesterday) pasts in various Bantu languages, or a remoteness distinction in the future.

Aspect and temporal adverbials

Aspect — perfective versus imperfective, progressive, habitual, inceptive, and so on — describes the internal temporal structure of a situation (in Vendler's terms, states, activities, accomplishments, and achievements). Temporal relations are further specified by adverbials of time ("in 1066"), duration ("for three hours"), and frequency ("every Tuesday"), and by temporal clauses introduced by conjunctions such as when, before, after, since, and until. Some languages, including Mandarin Chinese, lack grammatical tense altogether and rely on aspect markers and adverbials for temporal interpretation.
