Pontius Pilate (Latin: Pontius Pilatus) was the fifth governor of the Roman province of Judaea, serving under Emperor Tiberius from about 26 to 36 AD. He is best known as the official who presided over the trial of Jesus and ordered his crucifixion, as described in the Gospels, in which Pilate is portrayed as washing his hands of the decision.

Life and administration

Pilate was an equestrian appointed prefect (his title is confirmed by the Pilate Stone, a dedication inscription discovered at Caesarea Maritima in 1961). Jewish sources such as Philo and Josephus describe a harsh administration marked by provocations — introducing imperial images into Jerusalem, using Temple funds for an aqueduct — and by violent suppression of protests. After a bloody crackdown on Samaritans at Mount Gerizim, complaints to the legate of Syria led Tiberius to recall him to Rome in 36 AD; his later fate is unknown.

In the Gospels and later tradition

All four canonical Gospels depict Pilate questioning Jesus, finding no capital offence, and yielding to pressure from the Jerusalem authorities and crowd. Pilate became a complex figure in later tradition: condemned in the Nicene Creed's phrase "suffered under Pontius Pilate", yet venerated as a saint and martyr in the Ethiopian and Coptic churches. Numerous apocryphal "Acts of Pilate" and medieval legends elaborated his story.
