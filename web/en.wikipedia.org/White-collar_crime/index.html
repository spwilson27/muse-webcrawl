White-collar crime

White-collar crime is financially motivated, nonviolent crime committed by businesses and government professionals. The term was first defined by sociologist Edwin Sutherland in 1939 as "a crime committed by a person of respectability and high social status in the course of their occupation". Typical white-collar crimes include fraud, bribery, Ponzi schemes, insider trading, embezzlement, cybercrime, copyright infringement, money laundering, identity theft and forgery.

History of the term

Sutherland introduced the concept in a 1939 address to the American Sociological Association and developed it in his 1949 monograph White Collar Crime. He argued that criminology had been biased toward offences committed by lower-class offenders and that corporate and professional offenders caused enormous financial harm while rarely being prosecuted as criminals.

Types

Corporate crime is committed by or on behalf of a corporation, while occupational crime is committed by individuals against their employers or clients. Securities fraud, including insider trading, and large-scale schemes such as the Enron scandal and the fraud of Bernie Madoff are prominent examples. The financial cost of white-collar crime is generally estimated to far exceed that of conventional street crime, though enforcement has historically been lighter.
