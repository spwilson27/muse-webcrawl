Aorist

FromGreek ἀόριστος aóristos, "undefined, unbounded"
TypeGrammatical tense–aspect category
Found inAncient Greek, Sanskrit, Slavic languages, Georgian, and others

The aorist (from Greek ἀόριστος, "undefined") is a grammatical category of the verb found in certain Indo-European languages, most prominently Ancient Greek and Sanskrit, which characteristically expresses an action as a simple, complete whole — the perfective aspect — without reference to its internal duration or structure. In Ancient Greek it functions alongside the present and perfect stems as one of the three aspectual stems of the verb.

In Ancient Greek

In the Greek indicative, the aorist usually refers to the past, and is the default narrative tense for telling a story ("he did X, then Y"). In other moods — subjunctive, optative, imperative, infinitive, and participle — it carries primarily aspectual rather than temporal meaning, presenting the action as a single event, in contrast to the present stem (ongoing or repeated action) and the perfect stem (a state resulting from a completed action). Two main formations exist: the "first" or sigmatic aorist, marked by a suffix in -σ- (e.g., ἔλυσα, "I released"), and the "second" or thematic aorist, formed on a distinct stem (e.g., ἔλαβον, "I took"). Root aorists, as in ἔβην ("I went"), survive in some verbs.

In other languages

In Sanskrit the aorist was one of the past-tense systems of the verb, with several formations, later largely displaced by other past forms. In the South Slavic languages (Bulgarian, Macedonian, Serbian) an aorist tense survives as a past tense for completed actions, distinct from the imperfect. Scholars debate how far the Greek and Sanskrit aorists continue a single Proto-Indo-European category.

Semantics

Because the aorist presents an event "in summary," treating it as an indivisible whole, grammarians describe it with metaphors such as the view of a spectator who takes in the whole action at once. Contextual uses include the ingressive aorist (entry into a state, "he became king"), the gnomic aorist (general truths), and the dramatic aorist. In Koine Greek, including the language of the New Testament, the aorist is the most frequent narrative tense, and its aspectual nuances are a longstanding topic of exegetical debate.
