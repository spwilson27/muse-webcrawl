Two-sided market

A platform serves two distinct groups whose demand is interdependent
FieldEconomics, platform strategy
Key featureIndirect (cross-side) network effects

A two-sided market, also called a two-sided network, is an economic platform having two distinct user groups that provide each other with network benefits. The organization that creates value primarily by enabling direct interactions between two (or more) distinct types of affiliated customers is called a multi-sided platform. This concept of two-sided markets has been used mainly in the literature on platform economics and industrial organization.

Examples include credit card systems (cardholders and merchants), video game consoles (players and game developers), operating systems (users and application developers), marketplaces such as eBay and Amazon, and ride-hailing services (riders and drivers).

Network effects and pricing

In two-sided markets, each side's willingness to participate depends on the size and quality of the other side — a cross-side network effect. Platform pricing therefore differs from ordinary markets: a platform may rationally subsidize one side, even charging below cost or nothing at all, while earning its margin from the other side. Newspapers, for example, are often sold to readers below production cost because advertisers value access to readers.

The chicken-and-egg problem

New platforms face a start-up problem: neither side wants to join until the other is present. Strategies to overcome it include subsidizing the more price-sensitive side, securing marquee participants, and initially operating as a conventional business on one side before opening the platform.
