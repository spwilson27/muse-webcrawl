Estancia

An estancia on the Argentine pampas

An estancia is a large, private plot of land used for farming or raising cattle or sheep in South America, particularly in the southern cone countries of Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay, and southern Brazil and Chile. The term derives from the Spanish verb estar, "to be [located]", and in the colonial period referred to a station or stopping place before acquiring its sense of a landed estate.

History

During the Spanish colonial era, estancias developed as ranching outposts on the pampas, worked by gauchos and often granted to individuals or to religious orders such as the Jesuits, whose estancias in Córdoba, Argentina, are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. In the nineteenth century, estancieros became a powerful landed oligarchy, especially after the introduction of barbed wire, pedigree livestock imported from Britain, and refrigerated shipping, which turned Argentina into a major exporter of beef and wool.

Modern estancias

The traditional estancia centres on a main house (casco), with quarters for the foreman and peons, corrals, and outbuildings, often shaded by ombú trees. Many estancias have declined economically or been subdivided, but since the late twentieth century numerous estancias have opened to tourism, offering horse riding, polo, and traditional asado meals as "estancia stays" or day visits, particularly near Buenos Aires and in Patagonia.

Equivalent terms

Related forms of large rural estate in the Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking world include the hacienda in Mexico and the Andes, the fazenda in Brazil, and the fundo in Chile.
