Stamen

Stamen
PartsAnther, filament
Collective termAndroecium
FunctionPollen production (male reproduction)

The stamen (plural stamina or stamens) is the male reproductive organ of a flower. Each stamen typically consists of a stalk called the filament and a pollen-producing tip called the anther. The collection of all stamens in a flower is called the androecium.

Morphology

The anther is usually bilobed, with each lobe containing two pollen sacs (microsporangia) in which pollen grains develop by meiosis from microspore mother cells. When mature, the anther splits open (dehisces), most often along longitudinal slits, to release the pollen. The filament is a slender stalk that positions the anther to favour pollen transfer; in some flowers the filament is absent and the anther is sessile.

Variation

Stamen number and arrangement vary greatly among plant families and are important taxonomic characters. Flowers may have as few as one stamen or several hundred. Stamens may be free or fused: fusion of filaments forms a tube in the mallow family, while fusion of anthers (syngenesious) characterizes the aster family. Sterile stamens, called staminodes, occur in many flowers and may be modified into nectar-producing or petal-like structures.

Function in pollination

Stamens are arranged to place pollen on visiting pollinators or to release it to the wind. In many animal-pollinated flowers, the timing of anther dehiscence and the positioning of stamens relative to the stigma promote cross-pollination and reduce self-fertilization.
