Dime novel

The dime novel is a form of late 19th-century and early 20th-century American popular fiction issued in inexpensive paperbound editions. The term dime novel, though originally referring to a specific series produced by the publisher Beadle & Adams, came to denote any cheap, sensational story paper or pamphlet novel. Dime novels were the precursors of today's mass-market paperbacks, comic books and pulp magazines, and are generally considered the first truly mass-produced, mass-consumed American fiction.

History

The publishing house of Beadle & Adams launched "Beadle's Dime Novels" in 1860 with Ann S. Stephens's Malaeska: The Indian Wife of the White Hunter, a reprint of a serial from 1839. The distinctive orange-covered pamphlets sold for ten cents and achieved enormous print runs, selling hundreds of thousands of copies during the American Civil War to soldiers and a newly literate working-class readership. Rival series proliferated, including cheaper five-cent "nickel novels", story papers, and later the thicker "libraries" issued by Street & Smith.

Early dime novels favoured frontier and pioneer tales; later series expanded into detective fiction (the Nick Carter stories began in 1886), outlaw and western adventures featuring Buffalo Bill, Deadwood Dick and Jesse James, working-girl romances and city mysteries. Authors were paid by the story and often wrote under house names at great speed.

Cultural reception

Contemporary moralists, including Anthony Comstock, denounced dime novels as corrupting influences on youth, associating them with juvenile delinquency; they were banned from some schools and libraries. Despite — or because of — this notoriety, they remained enormously popular into the 1890s, when they were gradually displaced by pulp magazines, which offered similar fare at similar prices in a more convenient format. Many dime novel heroes and conventions passed directly into pulps, comics and early cinema.

Legacy

Dime novels are now studied as foundational texts of American popular culture and as evidence of working-class reading tastes. Major collections are held by the Library of Congress and universities such as Stanford and Northern Illinois University, whose digital collections have made thousands of titles available to researchers.
