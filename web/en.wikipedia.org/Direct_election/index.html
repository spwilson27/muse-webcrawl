Direct election

A direct election is an election in which voters directly choose the officeholders who will govern them, as opposed to an indirect election in which the voters elect an electoral college or intermediate body, which in turn elects the officeholders.

Examples

Most members of national legislatures in democratic states are directly elected. Presidents are directly elected in countries such as France, Ireland, Brazil, and Mexico. By contrast, the President of the United States is formally elected by the Electoral College, although electors are themselves chosen by popular vote in each state. In the United States, United States Senators were indirectly elected by state legislatures until the ratification of the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913, which established their direct election.

Direct democracy distinguished

Direct election should not be confused with direct democracy, in which citizens vote on policies and laws themselves rather than electing representatives. A direct election still produces representatives; what is "direct" is the link between the voter and the person elected.

Advantages and criticisms

Advocates argue direct election maximises democratic legitimacy and accountability. Critics of indirect systems note that they can insulate decision-making from popular pressure, while critics of direct election in some contexts argue that intermediaries can deliberate and filter extreme or uninformed choices.
