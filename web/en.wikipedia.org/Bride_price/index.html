Bride price

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Bridewealth gifts at a betrothal

Bride price, also called bridewealth, is money, property or another form of wealth paid by a groom or his family to the family of the bride at marriage. It is not a purchase price: anthropologists describe it as creating an alliance between families, compensating the bride's family for the loss of her labour and company, and legitimising the children of the union as members of the groom's lineage. Bride price is traditionally practised in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa, Southeast Asia, Melanesia and among various indigenous peoples.

Forms and functions

Payments may consist of livestock (such as cattle in the lobola custom of southern Africa), shell valuables (as in the bridewealth exchanges of Papua New Guinea), cash, or goods, and may be made in instalments over years. In many societies the payment is partly returned or redistributed in feasting and counter-gifts. Anthropologists contrast bridewealth with the dowry, in which wealth flows with the bride from her own family; the two systems are associated with different patterns of property, labour and inheritance.

Modern debates

Inflation of bride price demands, driven in part by cash economies and conspicuous consumption, has been criticised for delaying marriages and burdening young men, while some campaigners link very high or compulsory payments to gender inequality. Others defend the institution as honoring the bride and binding families together. Legal and customary status varies: some countries regulate or restrict payments, while in others courts enforce refund of bridewealth on divorce under customary law.
