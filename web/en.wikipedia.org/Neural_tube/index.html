The neural tube is the embryonic precursor to the central nervous system, which is made up of the brain and spinal cord. It forms during a process called neurulation, in which a flat sheet of ectodermal cells known as the neural plate folds in on itself and fuses to form a hollow tube. In humans, neural tube closure usually occurs by the fourth week of pregnancy, around day 28 after conception.

Development

Neurulation begins when signals from the underlying notochord induce the overlying ectoderm to thicken into the neural plate. The lateral edges of the plate elevate as neural folds, and a neural groove forms down the midline. The folds gradually approach one another and fuse, converting the groove into a closed tube. Fusion begins in the cervical (neck) region and proceeds both rostrally (toward the head) and caudally (toward the tail), a pattern sometimes described as closure proceeding like a zipper in two directions.

The open ends of the tube, called the anterior (cranial) and posterior (caudal) neuropores, close at approximately days 25 and 27 respectively. The lumen of the tube becomes the ventricular system of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord. Cells at the crest of the folds, the neural crest cells, detach and migrate widely through the embryo, giving rise to much of the peripheral nervous system, melanocytes, and various other structures.

Structure and derivatives

The cranial portion of the neural tube expands into the primary brain vesicles—the prosencephalon (forebrain), mesencephalon (midbrain), and rhombencephalon (hindbrain)—which subdivide further into the five secondary vesicles. The caudal portion remains more uniform in calibre and becomes the spinal cord. The wall of the tube differentiates into layers: an inner ventricular zone of proliferating cells, an intermediate mantle layer forming grey matter, and an outer marginal layer forming white matter.

Neural tube defects

Failure of the neural tube to close properly results in neural tube defects, among the most common congenital malformations. Failure of cranial closure leads to anencephaly, which is incompatible with life, while failure of caudal closure leads to spina bifida, in which the spinal cord and meninges may protrude through an opening in the vertebral column. Adequate maternal intake of folate (vitamin B9) before and during early pregnancy substantially reduces the incidence of these defects, which has led many countries to fortify staple foods with folic acid.
