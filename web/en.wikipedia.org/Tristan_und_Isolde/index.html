Tristan und Isolde


The lovers in Act 2

Tristan und Isolde (WWV 90) is an opera in three acts by Richard Wagner, with a German libretto by the composer, based largely on the medieval romance of Tristan by Gottfried von Strassburg. Composed between 1857 and 1859, it premiered at the Königliches Hof- und Nationaltheater in Munich on 10 June 1865, conducted by Hans von Bülow.

Synopsis

Tristan escorts the Irish princess Isolde to marry King Marke of Cornwall; en route they drink what they believe is a death potion but is in fact a love potion. Their adulterous passion, discovered by Marke, ends with the mortally wounded Tristan dying as Isolde arrives, and Isolde's final transfiguration in the famous Liebestod.

Music and influence

The opera is renowned for its advanced chromatic harmony, epitomized by the opening "Tristan chord", and for long-delayed resolutions that mirror the lovers' unfulfilled longing. Influenced by Schopenhauer's philosophy, the work is widely regarded as a landmark in the development of Western music, pointing toward the dissolution of traditional tonality.
