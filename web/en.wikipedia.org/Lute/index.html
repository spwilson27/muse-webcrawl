Lute

A Renaissance lute

Classification: plucked string instrument (chordophone)
Related: oud, theorbo, archlute, mandora

The lute is any plucked string instrument with a neck and a deep round back enclosing a hollow cavity, usually with a sound hole or opening in the body. In the Renaissance and Baroque eras the term referred specifically to the European lute: a half-pear-shaped body built from thin ribs of wood, a flat soundboard often decorated with a carved rose, gut strings arranged in courses, and a pegbox bent back at nearly a right angle.

History

The European lute derives from the Arab ʿūd (oud), introduced to Europe through al-Andalus and the Crusades; the very word "lute" comes from Arabic al-ʿūd, "the wood". By the sixteenth century the lute was the pre-eminent solo instrument of European courts and homes, with a vast repertoire of dances, fantasias, and song accompaniments written in tablature. Composers such as John Dowland in England and Francesco da Milano in Italy defined its golden age.

Construction and technique

Renaissance lutes typically had six to ten courses of strings; Baroque lutes, favoured in France and Germany, had eleven to thirteen courses in new tunings. Players pluck the strings with the fingertips of the right hand while the left hand stops notes on a fretted neck, the frets being made of tied gut. Extended bass strings gave rise to larger instruments such as the theorbo and archlute for continuo playing.

Decline and revival

The lute fell out of fashion in the late eighteenth century as keyboard instruments and the guitar rose in popularity. The twentieth-century early music revival, led by performers and makers such as Julian Bream and the luthiers who reconstructed historical instruments, restored the lute to concert life.
