Abolition of the han system

The abolition of the han system (Japanese: haihan-chiken, "abolish the domains, establish prefectures") was an administrative reform of the Meiji government of Japan in 1871, under which the feudal domains (han) ruled by the daimyo were abolished and replaced by prefectures governed from Tokyo. It was a decisive step in the centralization of the Meiji state.

Background

In 1869 the daimyo of the Satsuma, Chōshū, Tosa, and Hizen domains had "returned" their registers to the emperor (hanseki hōkan), and other lords followed, being reappointed as imperial governors of their former domains. The government, however, still lacked direct control over taxation and administration across most of the country.

The reform

On 29 August 1871 (14th day of the 7th month, Meiji 4), the emperor announced the abolition of the approximately 260 domains. The former daimyo were ordered to move to Tokyo with generous stipends, and the domains were converted into some 300 prefectures, quickly consolidated to 72, and then close to the modern total of 47. The reform was prepared in secrecy and backed by an imperial guard recruited from the loyalist domains, so it was carried out with little resistance.

Consequences

The reform removed the last structural remnant of feudal rule, gave the central government a national tax base and administrative chain of command, and paved the way for conscription, compulsory education, and other Meiji reforms.
