LaserDisc (LD), also known by its early brand name DiscoVision, is a home video format and the first commercial optical disc storage medium. Marketed initially by MCA DiscoVision in 1978, the format — developed by Philips and MCA — used 30 cm (12-inch) double-sided discs read by a laser beam. LaserDisc offered markedly better picture and sound quality than VHS and Betamax, along with random access and special features such as director's commentary, laying the groundwork for the DVD era.

History

Optical videodisc technology was demonstrated by David Paul Gregg in the 1960s; Philips and MCA combined their research and launched the format in Atlanta in December 1978, two years after VHS. Pioneer became the format's principal champion, buying into the business and marketing players under the LaserDisc name. Despite its technical superiority, the format remained a niche, premium product in North America and Europe — players and discs were expensive and the format could not record — though it achieved substantial mainstream success in Japan and among affluent collectors, videophiles, and institutions in North America; by the late 1980s millions of players had been sold in Japan.

Technology

A LaserDisc stores an analog composite video signal as a frequency-modulated pattern of pits of varying length, read by a focused laser; audio was originally analog FM and later supplemented by digital PCM tracks, and from the mid-1990s some discs carried Dolby Digital (AC-3) or DTS sound. Discs were recorded in two modes: CAV (constant angular velocity), holding about 30 minutes per side with perfect freeze-frame and frame-by-frame access on any player, and CLV (constant linear velocity), holding about 60 minutes per side. Disc sizes included the standard 12-inch format and smaller 8-inch singles. Most players required the disc to be flipped manually at side breaks, though some high-end models could play both sides automatically.

Legacy

LaserDisc popularized the special-edition home video release: The Criterion Collection, founded in 1984, pioneered commentary tracks, letterboxing, and supplementary materials on LaserDisc. Production of film titles ended around 2000–2001 as DVD displaced it, and Pioneer manufactured the last players in 2009. The format also saw use in arcade games such as Dragon's Lair (1983) and in computer-controlled interactive video systems.
