Solidarity (Polish trade union)

Solidarity (Polish: Solidarność, officially the Independent Self-Governing Trade Union "Solidarity", Niezależny Samorządny Związek Zawodowy „Solidarność”) is a Polish trade union founded in August 1980 at the Lenin Shipyard (now Gdańsk Shipyard) by Lech Wałęsa and others. It became the first independent trade union in a Warsaw Pact country to be recognised by the state, and grew into a broad social movement that played a central role in ending communist rule in Poland.

Origins

Solidarity emerged from the wave of strikes that swept Poland in the summer of 1980 after food price rises. The inter-factory strike committee at the Gdańsk Shipyard, led by Lech Wałęsa, formulated 21 demands including free trade unions, the right to strike and freedom of speech. The Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980 conceded these demands, and the union was legally registered on 10 November 1980. Within a year it had some 10 million members — nearly a third of Poland's working-age population.

Martial law and underground

The communist government of Wojciech Jaruzelski imposed martial law on 13 December 1981; Solidarity was suspended, then banned, and its leaders interned. The union survived underground for seven years, publishing samizdat and maintaining links with the Catholic Church and Western supporters. Wałęsa received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983.

1989 and after

Renewed strikes in 1988 forced the regime to the Round Table Talks, at which Solidarity was re-legalised. Its candidates swept the semi-free elections of 4 June 1989, and Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the Soviet bloc. Wałęsa won the presidency in 1990. Solidarity continues today as a trade union with roughly half a million members, and its red brush-stroke logo, designed by Jerzy Janiszewski, remains an iconic symbol of peaceful resistance.
