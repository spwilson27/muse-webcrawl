Streptolysin

Streptolysins are two hemolytic exotoxins produced by Streptococcus pyogenes and related streptococci: streptolysin O (SLO) and streptolysin S (SLS). Both damage cell membranes, producing the complete (beta) hemolysis seen when group A streptococci grow on blood agar.

Streptolysin O

Streptolysin O is a protein of about 69 kDa belonging to the cholesterol-dependent cytolysin family. It is oxygen-labile — its hemolytic activity is inactivated by exposure to oxygen — and strongly antigenic: infection provokes antibodies against it, and the anti-streptolysin O (ASO) titer is a standard laboratory test used to document a recent streptococcal infection, for example when evaluating suspected rheumatic fever or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. SLO binds cholesterol in host cell membranes, oligomerizes into large pores, and lyses erythrocytes, leukocytes, and other cells; it also impairs immune cells and helps the bacterium escape from phagosomes.

Streptolysin S

Streptolysin S is a small, oxygen-stable peptide (about 2.8 kDa) that is not antigenic. It is cell-associated and is chiefly responsible for the surface hemolysis visible on blood agar plates grown in air. SLS injures erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets, and tissue cells, contributing to tissue destruction in invasive infections such as necrotizing fasciitis.

Clinical significance

The distinction between the two toxins matters diagnostically: because SLO is produced mainly beneath the agar surface and is immunogenic, ASO titers rise after most infections, whereas skin infections may raise anti-DNase B titers more reliably.
