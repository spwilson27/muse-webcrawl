Ragtime

Ragtime, also spelled rag-time or rag time, is a musical style that had its peak from the 1890s to 1910s. Its cardinal trait is its syncopated or "ragged" rhythm. Ragtime was preceded by a diverse blend of folk and popular music originating from African American communities, and it was a major influence on the development of jazz.

Characteristics

Classic ragtime, written mainly for piano, typically consists of several contrasting sections or "strains", each of sixteen bars, in a form adapted from the march. The left hand plays a steady bass pattern in 2/4 time while the right hand plays a syncopated melody against it. Ragtime was primarily a composed, notated music, disseminated as sheet music rather than improvised.

History

Ragtime coalesced in the 1890s in cities such as St. Louis and Sedalia, Missouri. Scott Joplin, its most famous composer, published the "Maple Leaf Rag" in 1899, which sold widely and established the genre. Other important figures include James Scott, Joseph Lamb, Tom Turpin, and Artie Matthews. Joplin's "The Entertainer" (1902) became famous again in the 1970s through the film The Sting.

By the late 1910s ragtime was eclipsed by jazz, though its influence persisted in stride piano and novelty piano styles. A ragtime revival began in the 1940s and peaked in the 1970s, when Joplin's opera Treemonisha was finally staged in full and his music received Grammy and Pulitzer recognition.

Legacy

Ragtime compositions influenced European composers such as Debussy, Stravinsky, and Hindemith, and the style remains a foundation repertoire for pianists. The term also inspired E. L. Doctorow's 1975 novel Ragtime and the Broadway musical adapted from it.
