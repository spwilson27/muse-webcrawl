Crop rotation

Crop rotation is the practice of growing a series of different types of crops in the same area across a sequence of growing seasons. It reduces the reliance of a crop on one set of nutrients, reduces pest and weed pressure, and can improve soil structure and fertility. Crop rotation is a type of polyculture and stands in contrast to monoculture, in which the same crop is grown year after year in the same field.

History

Farmers have recognised the value of rotations since ancient times; Roman literature refers to alternating legumes with cereals. The three-field system, used in Europe from the early Middle Ages, divided land into three parts: one sown with a winter crop such as wheat or rye, one with a spring crop such as oats, barley or legumes, and one left fallow. In the 18th century the Norfolk four-course rotation — wheat, turnips, barley, and clover or ryegrass — was popularised during the British Agricultural Revolution, eliminating the fallow year while restoring nitrogen through legumes and feeding livestock on fodder crops.

Principles

A rotation is planned so that successive crops make different demands on the soil. Legumes such as clover, peas, beans and soybeans fix atmospheric nitrogen through symbiotic bacteria in their root nodules, benefiting following crops. Deep-rooted and shallow-rooted crops are alternated to exploit different soil layers, and crops with different pest and disease profiles are sequenced so that host-specific pests decline when their host is absent.

Effects

Well-designed rotations can increase yields, reduce the need for synthetic fertilisers and pesticides, interrupt cycles of weeds, insects and diseases, improve soil organic matter and structure, and reduce erosion. Rotations are a core component of organic farming and conservation agriculture, though choosing a rotation requires balancing agronomic benefits against markets, labour, and equipment for multiple crops.
