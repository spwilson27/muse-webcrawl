Crop rotation

Crop rotation is the practice of growing a series of different types of crops in the same area across a sequence of growing seasons. It forms part of good agricultural husbandry because it reduces the reliance on one set of nutrients, pest and weed pressure, and the probability of developing resistant pests and weeds. Rotations also improve soil structure and fertility by alternating deep- and shallow-rooted plants, and by including nitrogen-fixing legumes.

History

Farmers have rotated crops since antiquity: Roman writers such as Cato, Varro and Columella described alternating cereals with legumes and fallow, and the three-field system of medieval Europe rotated winter grain, spring grain and fallow. In early modern Flanders, and then in 18th-century Britain, convertible husbandry and the Norfolk four-course system — wheat, turnips, barley, and clover, popularised by Charles "Turnip" Townshend and others — eliminated the unproductive fallow year altogether. Clover fixed nitrogen in the soil while turnips provided winter fodder, allowing more livestock, more manure and higher grain yields: crop rotation was a central technology of the British Agricultural Revolution.

Rationale

Different crops draw differently on soil nutrients and leave different residues; legumes such as peas, beans, clover and soybeans host rhizobium bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen, replenishing the key nutrient consumed by cereals. Rotating crop species also breaks the life cycles of host-specific pests, pathogens and weeds, which build up under continuous monoculture: changing the crop denies them their host in the following season. Varied rooting depths exploit water and nutrients at different levels of the soil profile, and alternating residue types supports a diverse soil microbiome and reduces erosion.

Practice

A typical modern rotation might alternate maize with soybeans (the dominant pattern in the US Corn Belt), or cereals with oilseed rape, potatoes and legumes in northwestern Europe. Cover crops and green manures are slotted into gaps in the rotation to protect and enrich the soil. Although the advent of cheap synthetic fertilisers and pesticides in the 20th century reduced rotations on some industrial farms, rotation remains a core principle of sustainable and organic agriculture, and long-term experiments such as the Morrow Plots (established 1876, the oldest experimental field in the United States) continue to demonstrate its yield and soil benefits.
