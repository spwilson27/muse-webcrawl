Haber process

The Haber process, also called the Haber–Bosch process, is the main industrial procedure for the production of ammonia. It converts atmospheric nitrogen (N₂) to ammonia (NH₃) by a reaction with hydrogen (H₂) using a metal catalyst under high temperatures and pressures. The German chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch developed the process in the first decade of the 20th century; Haber demonstrated it in 1909 and BASF scaled it up industrially by 1913 at Oppau, Germany.

An ammonia plant using the Haber–Bosch process.

The reaction

The reaction, N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃, is reversible and exothermic. Because high pressure favours ammonia formation while high temperature opposes it (Le Chatelier's principle), industrial plants compromise at pressures of 150–200 bar and temperatures around 400–500 °C over an iron catalyst promoted with oxides. Unreacted gases are recycled after the ammonia is condensed out. The hydrogen is usually obtained by steam reforming of natural gas.

Significance

Synthetic ammonia underpins the production of nitrogen fertilisers, which support the food supply of roughly half the world's population; estimates credit the Haber–Bosch process with sustaining billions of people who could not be fed by pre-industrial agriculture. The process consumes on the order of 1–2% of world energy production and is a significant source of carbon emissions, motivating research into green ammonia. In the First World War, the same plants supplied nitrates for explosives, prolonging Germany's war effort after its access to Chilean saltpetre was cut.
