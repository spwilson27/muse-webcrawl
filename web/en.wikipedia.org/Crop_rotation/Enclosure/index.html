Enclosure

Enclosure or inclosure is a term, used in English landownership, that refers to the appropriation of "waste" or "common land" enclosing it and by doing so depriving commoners of their rights of access and privilege. Agreements to enclose land could be either through a formal or informal process. The process, which transformed the open-field system of medieval agriculture into individually owned, hedged or fenced farms, was carried out in England from the 12th century onward and reached its height with parliamentary enclosure in the 18th and 19th centuries.

History

Enclosure proceeded in waves. Tudor-era enclosure for sheep pasture provoked complaint and rebellion (including Kett's Rebellion of 1549), as villagers lost grazing and gleaning rights. From about 1750 to 1850, some 4,000 private Acts of Parliament, followed by the general Inclosure Acts of 1801, 1836, and 1845, enclosed roughly a fifth of England's land area. Commissioners surveyed open fields and commons and allotted consolidated plots to landowners in proportion to their existing rights.

Effects

Enclosure is credited by many historians with enabling agricultural improvement — consolidated farms could adopt crop rotation, selective breeding, and drainage more readily — and it is also central to accounts of proletarianisation: cottagers and smallholders who lost common rights often became wage labourers, supplying the workforce of the Industrial Revolution. Debate continues over how far enclosure caused rural depopulation, since many enclosed parishes grew in population.
