Organic farming

Organic farming, also known as ecological or biological farming, is an agricultural system that emphasises soil health, biodiversity and ecological processes, and prohibits most synthetic fertilisers, pesticides, genetically modified organisms and routine antibiotics. Within rotations, organic farmers rely on crop rotation, green manures, compost and biological pest control to maintain fertility and keep pests in check.

Principles and practice

Crop rotation is fundamental to organic practice: alternating cereals with legumes that fix nitrogen, and following crops with cover crops, builds soil organic matter and breaks pest and disease cycles without synthetic inputs. Livestock are integrated so that manure recycles nutrients, and animals are kept under welfare standards that restrict confinement and require access to pasture.

Regulation

Organic production is certified against legal standards in many jurisdictions, including the European Union's organic regulation and the United States Department of Agriculture's National Organic Program. The International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM) sets out the movement's principles of health, ecology, fairness and care.

Debate

Organic yields are on average lower than conventional yields, which drives debate over land use and whether organic methods could feed the world population; supporters argue that soil quality, biodiversity, water quality and reduced pollution offset the yield gap.
