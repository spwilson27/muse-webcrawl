Monoculture

Monoculture is the agricultural practice of growing a single crop species in a field or farming system at a time. It is the opposite of polyculture and of crop rotation, in which different crops follow one another in sequence. Monoculture suits mechanised farming and allows specialisation, but it depletes specific soil nutrients and leaves crops exposed to devastating outbreaks of pests and disease.

Advantages and disadvantages

Growing one crop simplifies planting, management and harvesting with specialised machinery, and concentrates expertise and infrastructure. Against this, fields planted year after year with the same species build up populations of specialist pests and soil-borne pathogens, typically requiring increasing inputs of pesticide and fertiliser. Genetic uniformity within a monoculture means a single pathogen strain can destroy an entire crop, as happened with the Gros Michel banana and the fungus Fusarium TR4's threat to its successor, the Cavendish.

Forestry and other uses

The term is also used for even-aged single-species forestry plantations and for lawns and aquaculture. Ecologists criticise monocultures for reducing biodiversity and ecosystem services such as pollination.
