Nitrogen cycle

The nitrogen cycle is the biogeochemical cycle by which nitrogen is converted into multiple chemical forms as it circulates among atmospheric, terrestrial, and marine ecosystems. Conversion of nitrogen is carried out through both biological and physical processes. Important processes in the nitrogen cycle include fixation, ammonification, nitrification, and denitrification. The majority of Earth's atmosphere (78%) is atmospheric nitrogen, making it the largest source of nitrogen; however, atmospheric nitrogen has limited availability for biological use, leading to a scarcity of usable nitrogen in many types of ecosystems.

Processes

Nitrogen fixation converts inert N₂ into ammonia or related compounds, performed naturally by diazotrophic bacteria (including rhizobia in legume root nodules) and by lightning, and industrially by the Haber process. Ammonification (mineralisation) releases ammonia from decomposing organic matter. Nitrification, carried out by specialised bacteria and archaea, oxidises ammonia to nitrite and then nitrate, the main form absorbed by plants. Denitrification returns nitrogen gas to the atmosphere from nitrate under anaerobic conditions, while anammox performs a related conversion in oceans.

Human influence

Industrial fertiliser production, cultivation of legumes, and fossil-fuel combustion have roughly doubled the rate of nitrogen fixation from natural terrestrial sources, making reactive nitrogen abundant in soils and waters. Consequences include eutrophication of lakes and coastal zones, groundwater nitrate contamination, and emissions of nitrous oxide, a potent greenhouse gas — impacts often called the "nitrogen cascade".
