Open-field system

The open-field system was the prevalent agricultural system in much of Europe during the Middle Ages and lasted into the 20th century in Russia and parts of Scotland and elsewhere. Under this system, each manor or village had two or three large fields, usually of several hundred acres each, which were divided into many narrow strips of land. The strips or selions were cultivated by individual peasants, but the crop rotation chosen for the fields was decided collectively, and livestock were grazed over the fields after harvest and on communal "waste" land.

Organization

Typically, a village's arable land was arranged in two or three great fields cropped in rotation — for example, winter grain, spring grain, and fallow in a three-field system. Each tenant's holding consisted of scattered strips distributed across all the fields, an arrangement that spread risk over soils and aspects but made individual improvement difficult. Grazing rights on the stubble and fallow, and usage of common pasture, meadow, and woodland, were regulated by manorial courts and local bylaws.

Decline

From the later Middle Ages onward, open fields were gradually replaced through enclosure — first by agreement and piecemeal consolidation, later by parliamentary Inclosure Acts in England — creating the patchwork of hedged fields familiar today. Vestiges survive in a few places, notably at Laxton in Nottinghamshire, which still operates an open-field system, and in the strip patterns preserved in the landscape as ridge and furrow.
