Three-field system

The three-field system is a regime of crop rotation used in medieval and early-modern Europe. Under this system, the arable land of a village was divided into three large fields: one sown in autumn with wheat or rye, one sown in spring with peas, beans, oats or barley, and the third left fallow to recover its fertility while livestock grazed it.

Advantages

Compared with the earlier two-field system, which left half the land fallow, the three-field system put two-thirds of the land under crops each year. The spring-sown legumes enriched the soil with nitrogen and diversified the diet, and spreading sowing between autumn and spring reduced the risk of total harvest failure from bad weather in a single season.

Spread and decline

The system spread across Europe from roughly the eighth to the eleventh century, aided by the heavy plough and the horse collar. It was gradually superseded from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries by convertible husbandry and the Norfolk four-course system, which replaced bare fallow with fodder crops and clover.
