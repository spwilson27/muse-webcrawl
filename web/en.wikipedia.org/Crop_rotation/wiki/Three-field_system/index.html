Three-field system
Era: Medieval Europe (from c. 8th–9th century)
Fields: Autumn grain / spring crop / fallow
Preceded by: Two-field system

The three-field system is a regime of crop rotation used in medieval and early modern Europe. Under it, a community's arable land was divided into three fields: one sown in autumn with wheat or rye, one sown in spring with oats, barley, peas, or beans, and one left fallow to recover fertility.

Advantages

Compared with the older two-field system, only one third of land lay fallow instead of one half, raising total output. Spring legumes added dietary protein and fixed nitrogen, and spreading sowing across two seasons reduced the risk of total crop failure.

Decline

The system persisted for centuries in open-field villages but was gradually displaced by enclosure and more flexible rotations such as the Norfolk four-course system during the agricultural revolution.
