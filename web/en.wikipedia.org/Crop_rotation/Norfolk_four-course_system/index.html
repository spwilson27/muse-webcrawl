Norfolk four-course system

The Norfolk four-course system is a method of crop rotation developed in the county of Norfolk, England, in the eighteenth century and popularised by landowners such as Charles "Turnip" Townshend of Raynham Hall. It was a cornerstone of the British Agricultural Revolution because it abolished the fallow year, keeping land in production continuously while restoring its fertility.

The rotation

The four courses were grown in sequence on each field:

  1. Wheat
  2. Turnips — fed to livestock over winter, whose manure enriched the soil
  3. Barley — often undersown with clover and ryegrass seed
  4. Clover (or grass) — a legume that fixes nitrogen and provided grazing

The rotation alternated cereals with root crops and legumes, so that no field bore the same type of crop in successive years. Turnips supplied winter fodder, allowing more animals to be kept and selectively bred; the animals' dung in turn fertilised the arable land.

Significance

Replacing the medieval three-field system, in which a third of the land lay fallow each year, the Norfolk rotation substantially raised yields and livestock numbers, helping to feed Britain's growing industrial population.
