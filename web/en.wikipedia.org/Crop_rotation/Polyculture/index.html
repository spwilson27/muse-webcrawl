Polyculture

Polyculture is the practice of growing multiple species of plants and/or animals in the same space, in imitation of the diversity of natural ecosystems — in contrast to monoculture, the practice of growing a single crop over a large area. Polyculture includes intercropping, companion planting, polyculture in aquaculture (such as fish–rice systems and integrated multi-trophic aquaculture), and diverse pasture systems. It was the traditional mode of agriculture in most of the world before the spread of industrial monoculture in the 20th century.

Intercropping: several crops share one field.

Forms

Intercropping grows two or more crops in proximity — in alternating rows, strips, or mixed plantings. Classic examples include the Mesoamerican "Three Sisters" of maize, beans, and squash, in which maize provides a trellis, beans fix nitrogen, and squash shades the soil. Companion planting pairs crops that repel pests or attract beneficial insects (such as marigolds with vegetables). In aquaculture, polyculture combines species at different trophic levels — for example carp species in Chinese ponds, or fish with seaweed and shellfish — so that wastes from one species become inputs for another.

Advantages and disadvantages

Polycultures can increase total yield per unit area (measured by the land equivalent ratio), improve pest and disease resilience by breaking up host continuity, suppress weeds, diversify farm income and diets, and enhance soil health and biodiversity. Disadvantages include greater management complexity and difficulty of mechanisation and harvesting, which has favoured monoculture in industrial agriculture. Interest in polyculture has revived within agroecology, permaculture, and regenerative agriculture.
