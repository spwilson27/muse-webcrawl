Hypoventilation

In hypoventilation, alveolar ventilation falls below metabolic demand.

Hypoventilation (also known as respiratory depression) occurs when ventilation is inadequate to perform needed respiratory gas exchange. By definition it causes an increased concentration of carbon dioxide (hypercapnia) and respiratory acidosis. Hypoventilation is the opposite of hyperventilation, in which ventilation exceeds metabolic needs.

Causes

Causes fall into several groups: central depression of the respiratory drive by drugs (opioids, sedatives), brainstem lesions, or congenital central hypoventilation syndrome ("Ondine's curse", associated with PHOX2B mutations); neuromuscular weakness (motor neurone disease, Guillain–Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis, muscular dystrophies); chest-wall and lung restriction (severe obesity—obesity hypoventilation syndrome, or Pickwick syndrome—kyphoscoliosis, advanced COPD); and sleep-disordered breathing, in which hypoventilation during sleep precedes daytime failure.

Effects

Rising CO2 causes headache, drowsiness, confusion, and eventually coma (CO2 narcosis); hypoxaemia accompanies it because alveolar oxygen falls as CO2 rises. Chronic hypoventilation leads to pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale, and compensatory renal retention of bicarbonate partially corrects the acidosis.

Treatment

Treatment targets the cause: reversal agents such as naloxone for opioid overdose, non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP) for acute and chronic ventilatory failure, weight loss for obesity hypoventilation, and in severe congenital cases lifelong ventilatory support. Respiratory stimulants such as acetazolamide have limited roles.
