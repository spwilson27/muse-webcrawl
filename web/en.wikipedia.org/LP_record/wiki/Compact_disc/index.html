The compact disc (CD) is a digital optical disc data storage format co-developed by Philips and Sony and released in 1982, initially for storing and playing digital audio (CD-DA, standardised in the "Red Book"). The 120 mm disc stores data as microscopic pits read by a laser; a standard CD holds about 74–80 minutes of uncompressed stereo audio or 650–700 MB of data.

History

Philips and Sony combined their optical disc research in 1979 and published the Red Book standard in 1980. The first commercial players and discs appeared in Japan in October 1982 and elsewhere in 1983 — among the first albums released was ABBA's The Visitors. CDs steadily displaced vinyl and cassettes through the 1980s and 1990s, dominating recorded music sales until downloads and then streaming overtook them in the 2010s.

Technology

Audio CDs encode 16-bit stereo PCM at a 44.1 kHz sampling rate. Data is written in a spiral track of pits and lands, modulated with eight-to-fourteen modulation and protected by cross-interleaved Reed–Solomon error correction, allowing playback despite scratches and dust. The related standards family includes CD-ROM (Yellow Book) for computer data, recordable CD-R and rewritable CD-RW, Video CD, and Super Audio CD. The CD's success established optical media in computing before writable CDs and DVDs became common.

Legacy

Although streaming dominates music distribution, CDs remain a niche retail and collectors' format, and hundreds of billions were sold worldwide. The format's inventors and the Red Book standard were recognised with technical awards, and the CD marked the music industry's transition from analogue to digital media.
