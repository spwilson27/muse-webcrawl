Vinyl revival

The vinyl revival, also called the vinyl resurgence, is the renewed interest in and increased sales of phonograph records, particularly the 12-inch LP, that began in the mid-2000s after the format had been largely supplanted by compact discs and then downloads and streaming. From a low point in about 2006, vinyl sales grew for well over a decade consecutively in major markets.

Growth

In the United States, vinyl LP revenues surpassed those of the CD in the early 2020s for the first time since the 1980s, and unit sales of vinyl exceeded CD units in 2022. In the United Kingdom, vinyl sales reached their highest levels since the early 1990s, with revenues overtaking the CD earlier. Record Store Day, founded in 2008, boosted independent shops with limited-edition releases. Major artists began issuing new albums on vinyl as a premium format, and supermarket and lifestyle chains resumed stocking records.

Reasons and supply

Commentators attribute the revival to nostalgia, the tactile and collectible qualities of large-format artwork, perceived warmth of analogue sound, ritual listening, and younger consumers for whom physical media are novel. Demand outstripped the capacity of the surviving pressing plants, prompting investment in new presses and factories in Europe and North America, long delivery lead times, and increased prices. The revival also boosted sales of turntables and phono equipment, including mass-produced entry-level players.

Criticism

Critics note the environmental cost of PVC manufacture and shipping, and question sound-quality claims where digital masters are used for cutting; others treat the trend as purely a collectors' market rather than a mainstream listening format, since streaming remains overwhelmingly dominant by consumption.
