45 rpm adaptation refers to the methods used to play 7-inch single records, which rotate at 45 revolutions per minute, on phonographs and turntables. The 45 rpm record, introduced by RCA Victor in 1949, has an unusually large centre hole of 1.5 inches (38 mm), designed for the fast automatic changers of RCA's dedicated players, whereas LP records and turntable spindles use a small hole of about 0.286 inches (7.26 mm). Some means of adapting the record to the spindle is therefore required.

Types of adapter

The most common solution is the spider insert, a small plastic or metal insert that snaps into the large hole and provides a small centre hole; inserts may remain in the record or be reused. Another common approach is a 45 rpm adapter placed on the turntable spindle — typically a small metal or plastic cone, disc or puck over which the record is centred; record changers and jukeboxes used their own mechanisms, jukeboxes generally handling the large hole natively. Three-pronged "spider" inserts with spring clips were widely sold for home use and became iconic shapes in record culture.

Context

The large hole was part of RCA's format war strategy against Columbia's LP: RCA's changers could drop and play a stack of singles with minimal delay. When multi-speed turntables became standard, manufacturers supplied adapters with players, and later turntables frequently included a puck-style adapter stored on the deck. Records pressed for European markets sometimes had small holes or push-out centres ("dinked" or solid centres), and jukebox operators in those regions used different mechanisms.
