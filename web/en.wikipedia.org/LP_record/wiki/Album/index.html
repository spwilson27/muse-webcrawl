An album is a collection of audio recordings, usually of music, issued as a single unit on a medium such as a vinyl record, compact disc, or digital file collection. The term derives from the bound "record albums" of the 78 rpm era, in which several shellac discs were kept in a book resembling a photograph album. With the arrival of the 12-inch long-playing (LP) record in 1948, capable of holding about twenty minutes per side, the album became the standard long-form unit of popular music.

Development

Early albums were sets of 78 rpm discs packaged in booklets, used for classical works and collections of popular songs. Columbia Records' LP, introduced in 1948, allowed a whole programme to fit on one disc in a cardboard sleeve, and album artwork and liner notes became important parts of the package. In the 1960s, artists such as the Beatles with Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967) elevated the album into a coherent artistic statement rather than a collection of singles plus filler.

Formats and industry

The album survived successive format changes: 8-track and cassette in the 1970s, the compact disc in the 1980s (which extended typical running times toward 74–80 minutes), digital downloads in the 2000s, and streaming playlists in the 2010s, where "album-equivalent units" combine streams and track sales for chart purposes. Album charts such as the Billboard 200 rank commercial performance. Album types include studio albums, live albums, compilations, concept albums, and double albums; shorter releases are classed as EPs and singles.

Cultural significance

The album era is often dated from the mid-1960s to the rise of downloads and streaming, when unbundled tracks and playlists shifted listening habits. Nevertheless the album remains the principal unit in which recording artists present substantial bodies of work, and vinyl album sales revived strongly in the 2010s and 2020s.
