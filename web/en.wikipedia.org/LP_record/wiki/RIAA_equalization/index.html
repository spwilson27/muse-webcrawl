RIAA equalization is a specification for the recording and playback of phonograph records, established by the Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA). It defines a standard equalisation curve applied when cutting a record and the complementary curve applied by the phono preamplifier on playback, so that together they yield a flat frequency response.

Purpose

When a record is cut with constant-velocity transducers, bass frequencies would produce very large groove excursions and treble frequencies very small ones, increasing playing time problems, tracking difficulty and surface noise. The RIAA curve attenuates low frequencies and boosts high frequencies during recording; on playback the reverse is applied, cutting bass boost and treble, which also reduces audible surface hiss. This permits narrower grooves, longer playing time and better signal-to-noise ratio.

The standard curve

The RIAA curve is defined by three time constants: 3180 µs (bass turnover at 50 Hz), 318 µs (500 Hz) and 75 µs (treble turnover at 2122 Hz). Before standardisation in the mid-1950s, competing labels used differing curves (such as Columbia, Decca FFRR and NAB variants), so early LPs played with the wrong curve sound tonally incorrect; some modern preamplifiers offer switchable historic curves. A later IEC amendment added a low-frequency roll-off (the "new orthophonic" modification) to reduce rumble, which many manufacturers ignore.

Modern practice

Every phono stage in turntables, amplifiers and DJ mixers implements the RIAA playback curve, typically to within a fraction of a decibel. The persistence of the standard reflects the size of the LP catalogue cut since 1954.
