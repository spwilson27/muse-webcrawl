A phonograph record (also called a gramophone record, or simply a record or vinyl) is an analogue sound storage medium in the form of a flat disc with an inscribed, modulated spiral groove. The groove is traced by a stylus as the disc rotates on a turntable, converting vibrations into sound. Records were the dominant format for recorded music for most of the twentieth century.

History

Emile Berliner introduced flat disc records in the late 1880s, competing with Edison's cylinders; discs won out for ease of manufacture and storage. Early records were made of shellac, played at about 78 rpm, and held three to five minutes per side. Columbia Records introduced the 12-inch long-playing (LP) record at 33⅓ rpm in 1948, using microgrooves and vinyl plastic; RCA Victor answered in 1949 with the 7-inch 45 rpm single. The ensuing "war of the speeds" ended with LPs for albums and 45s for singles.

Characteristics

Stereo records, introduced commercially in 1958, carry two channels in a single groove via the Westrex 45/45 system. Vinyl records are pressed from stampers made from a lacquer master; playback equalisation follows the RIAA curve. Shellac 78s are fragile and heavy, while PVC vinyl is durable and quieter. Records are specified by diameter, speed and groove type, and multi-speed turntables with idle-wheel, belt or direct drive became household fixtures.

Decline and revival

The compact disc displaced vinyl in the 1980s, and by the 1990s records were a niche medium kept alive by DJs and audiophiles. From the late 2000s vinyl underwent a major commercial revival, and in the 2020s vinyl revenues again surpassed CD revenues in several markets, with new pressing plants built to meet demand.
