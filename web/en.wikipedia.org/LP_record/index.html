LP record

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The LP (from "long playing" or "long play") is an analog sound storage medium, a phonograph record format characterized by its use of 33⅓ rpm, a microgroove, a diameter of 12 inches (300 mm), and "vinyl" (a plastic made of polyvinyl chloride) composition. Introduced by Columbia Records in 1948, it was soon adopted as a new standard by the entire US record industry. Apart from a few relatively minor refinements and the important later addition of stereophonic sound in 1957, it remained the standard format for record albums, during a period in popular music known as the album era.

Beginning in the late 1970s, LP sales began to decline because of the increasing popularity of cassette tapes and, later, compact discs. Since the first decade of the 21st century, a vinyl revival has boosted sales again; by the early 2020s vinyl LPs were outselling CDs in revenue in the United States for the first time since the 1980s.

History

The LP grew out of research at Columbia Records led by engineer Peter Goldmark. Earlier attempts at long-playing discs — including Edison's long-playing records of the 1920s and RCA Victor's 33⅓ rpm program transcription discs of 1931 — had failed commercially, chiefly because they required playback equipment the public did not own and offered poor sound. Columbia's system combined a slow speed with very fine "microgrooves" (about 224–300 grooves per inch, versus roughly 90 for 78s) and a lightweight pickup, giving each side of a 12-inch disc more than 20 minutes of playing time — enough for an entire symphony movement or a set of popular songs. Columbia unveiled the LP at the Waldorf-Astoria Hotel in New York on 21 June 1948.

RCA Victor answered in 1949 with the 7-inch 45 rpm single, and the ensuing "war of the speeds" ended with turntables that played both: LPs for albums and classical works, 45s for singles. The LP made possible the album as an artistic statement, from cast recordings and jazz albums to the concept albums of the 1960s rock era.

Format details

A typical LP has a playing time of about 22 minutes per side, though it can range from under 15 to over 30 minutes at the cost of lower volume and reduced fidelity. The grooves form a single continuous spiral from the outside edge to near the label; the signal is recorded laterally and (in stereo pressings) both laterally and vertically, encoding the sum and difference of the two channels so that mono cartridges could still play stereo records. Records are played with a stylus of diamond or sapphire tracing the groove, with tracking forces of roughly 1–3 grams for modern cartridges. Equalization follows the RIAA curve (standardized in the 1950s), which reduces bass and boosts treble during recording, reversed on playback to reduce surface noise and groove width.

LPs are commonly pressed in weights from about 120 g to audiophile 180–200 g pressings. Coloured vinyl, picture discs, and shaped discs exist as specialty items. The standard packaging — a cardboard sleeve with printed artwork — became an important medium for graphic design and liner notes.

Sound reproduction

LP fidelity improved steadily with stereo (marketed by Audio Fidelity and standardized by the Westrex 45/45 system in 1957–58), better cutting lathes, and quieter vinyl. Limitations include surface noise, inner-groove distortion as the groove speed decreases toward the centre, susceptibility to dust, scratches and warping, and wear from repeated plays. Advocates praise the format's sound and ritual; critics note that well-mastered digital formats surpass it in measurable signal-to-noise ratio and channel separation.

Decline and revival

Cassettes overtook LP sales in the early 1980s in some markets, and the compact disc (1982) displaced the LP as the mainstream album format by the late 1980s; most major labels had largely ended mass LP production by the early 1990s, though the format survived in DJ culture (especially hip hop turntablism and dance music 12-inch singles) and audiophile niches. From about 2006–2007 sales began a sustained revival, driven by younger buyers, Record Store Day (founded 2008), and marketing of vinyl as a premium, collectible artefact. In 2022, RIAA figures showed US vinyl revenue (about $1.2 billion) exceeding CD revenue for the first time since 1987, and vinyl unit sales also passed CDs.
