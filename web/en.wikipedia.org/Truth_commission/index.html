Truth commission
PurposeInvestigate past human rights abuses
FieldTransitional justice
Notable exampleSouth Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–1998)

A truth commission, or truth and reconciliation commission, is an official body established to investigate and report on a pattern of human rights abuses or violations of international humanitarian law committed over a period of time, usually under a prior regime or during an internal conflict. Truth commissions are a central mechanism of transitional justice, intended to establish an authoritative record of the past, give victims a public voice, and recommend reforms or reparations.

Characteristics

Truth commissions are typically temporary, state-sanctioned bodies focused on the past. Unlike courts, they generally do not prosecute individuals, though some—such as South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission—have had the power to grant amnesty in exchange for full disclosure. Their work usually culminates in a final report presented to the government and the public.

Examples

Dozens of truth commissions have been established worldwide. Early examples include Uganda's commissions of the 1970s and 1980s, Argentina's CONADEP (1983–1984), whose report Nunca Más documented the disappeared under military rule, and Chile's Rettig Commission (1990–1991). The South African commission, chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, became the best-known model. Later commissions operated in Peru, Sierra Leone, Liberia, Kenya, Canada (on Indian residential schools) and Colombia, among others.

Assessment

Supporters credit truth commissions with breaking official silence, validating victims' experiences and deterring future abuses. Critics note that their recommendations are often only partially implemented and that truth-telling without prosecutions may leave victims without justice.
