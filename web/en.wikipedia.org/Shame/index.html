Shame

Shame is an unpleasant, self-conscious emotion often associated with a negative evaluation of the self, withdrawal, and feelings of distress, exposure, mistrust, powerlessness, and worthlessness. Shame is a discrete, basic emotion, described as a moral or social emotion that drives people to hide or deny their wrongdoings.

Definition

Psychologists distinguish shame from guilt: guilt is typically focused on a specific behaviour ("I did a bad thing"), whereas shame is focused on the self ("I am bad"). Shame can involve a painful scrutiny of the whole self and is often accompanied by a sense of shrinking, inferiority, and a desire to disappear or hide.

Psychological research

Researchers such as June Price Tangney have studied shame-proneness and its links to depression, anxiety, addiction, and aggression. Chronic shame has been associated with low self-esteem and with defensive responses including anger and blame-shifting. In contrast, healthy shame is sometimes described as a signal that social norms have been violated, prompting reflection and reparative action.

Cultural aspects

Anthropologists have contrasted "shame cultures" and "guilt cultures", a distinction popularised by Ruth Benedict in her study of Japan, The Chrysanthemum and the Sword. In honour–shame cultures, social reputation and family honour strongly regulate behaviour. Shame is also used as a social sanction, from historical punishments such as public humiliation to modern practices of public shaming online.

Related concepts

Related states include embarrassment, humiliation, modesty, and stigma. In psychoanalysis and affect theory, writers such as Silvan Tomkins treated shame as one of the innate affects, central to identity and social bonding.
