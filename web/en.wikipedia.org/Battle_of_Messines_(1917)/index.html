Battle of Messines (1917)

The Battle of Messines (7–14 June 1917) was an attack by the British Second Army, on the Western Front, near the village of Messines (now Mesen) in West Flanders, Belgium, during the First World War. The battle began with the detonation of 19 huge mines beneath the German front position on the Messines–Wytschaete Ridge, followed by an infantry and tank assault under a creeping artillery barrage. The attack was a complete success, securing the ridge and straightening the British line south of Ypres before the Third Battle of Ypres later that summer.

Background

The Messines–Wytschaete Ridge was a natural stronghold southeast of Ypres that had been held by the Germans since 1914, giving them observation over the British salient. General Herbert Plumer, commanding the Second Army, planned a methodical, limited-objective attack. For more than a year, Royal Engineers tunnelling companies had dug deep galleries under the ridge and packed them with about 455 tonnes (1 million pounds) of ammonal explosive in 21 mines.

The attack

At 03:10 on 7 June 1917, 19 of the mines were detonated simultaneously, creating enormous craters and killing an estimated 10,000 German soldiers — the explosions were reportedly heard in London. Nine infantry divisions then advanced behind a carefully coordinated artillery barrage, supported by tanks and aircraft. By the end of the day the ridge was in British hands; fighting continued until 14 June as German counter-attacks were defeated.

Aftermath

The battle was one of the most successful British limited offensives of the war, demonstrating the effectiveness of Plumer's "bite and hold" tactics. German casualties were about 25,000 and British and Imperial casualties about 24,000. Two mines that were not fired remain in place; one exploded during a thunderstorm in 1955 without loss of life, and another still lies undiscovered beneath a farm.
