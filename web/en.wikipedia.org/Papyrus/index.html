Papyrus (plural: papyri) is a material similar to thick paper that was used in ancient times as a writing surface. It was made from the pith of the papyrus plant, Cyperus papyrus, a wetland sedge native to the Nile Delta and valley. Papyrus was first manufactured in Egypt as far back as the 4th millennium BCE and remained the standard writing material of the Mediterranean world for some three thousand years; the English word "paper" derives from its name.

Manufacture

The Roman author Pliny the Elder described the process: thin strips were sliced from the plant's triangular stem pith, laid side by side, and covered with a second layer at right angles. The two layers were moistened — by some accounts with Nile water — pressed or hammered together so that the plant's natural juices cemented them, then dried in the sun and polished smooth with a shell or ivory. Sheets were glued edge-to-edge into rolls (scrolls) of standard length, with the horizontal-fibred side (recto) used first for writing in ink applied with a reed pen.

Uses

Papyrus served for religious texts such as the Egyptian Book of the Dead, administrative records, tax receipts, letters, contracts, and literary works throughout Egypt, Greece, and the Roman Empire. The dry Egyptian climate preserved enormous quantities: the rubbish heaps of Oxyrhynchus alone yielded hundreds of thousands of fragments, recovering lost works of Sappho, Menander, and other authors, as well as early Christian texts. The Ebers Papyrus and Edwin Smith Papyrus are among the oldest medical documents in the world. The papyrus plant itself was also used for boats, sandals, rope, baskets, and mats.

Decline

Papyrus is fragile, rots in damp climates, and could only be grown in a few regions. From late antiquity it was progressively displaced by parchment, which was tougher, reusable, and producible anywhere, and then by Chinese paper transmitted through the Islamic world, which was cheaper still. Papal documents continued to be issued on papyrus until the 11th century. The study of surviving papyri — papyrology — remains a major source for the social, economic, and literary history of the ancient world.
