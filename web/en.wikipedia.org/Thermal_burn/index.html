Thermal burn

SpecialtyEmergency medicine, dermatology, plastic surgery
CausesHeat: fire, flame, hot liquids (scalds), steam, hot objects
TreatmentCooling, wound care, fluid resuscitation, analgesia, skin grafting

A thermal burn is a type of burn resulting from making contact with heated objects, such as boiling water (a scald), steam, hot cooking oil, fire, or hot surfaces. It is the most common category of burn injury, distinguished from chemical burns, electrical burns, and radiation burns.

Classification

Thermal burns are classified by depth. Superficial (first-degree) burns affect only the epidermis and present as red, painful skin, as in mild sunburn. Partial-thickness (second-degree) burns extend into the dermis and typically blister. Full-thickness (third-degree) burns destroy both layers of skin and may appear white, charred, or leathery, with loss of sensation in the centre of the wound. Fourth-degree burns extend into fat, muscle, or bone. Severity also depends on the total body surface area involved, commonly estimated by the "rule of nines" in adults.

Pathophysiology

Heat denatures proteins and kills cells in the tissue; local damage is often described in terms of zones of coagulation, stasis, and hyperaemia. Extensive burns provoke a systemic inflammatory response with capillary leak and hypovolaemic "burn shock", requiring fluid resuscitation guided by formulas such as the Parkland formula.

Treatment

First aid consists of stopping the burning process and cooling the wound with running water, while avoiding ice and hypothermia. Hospital care includes analgesia, wound cleaning and dressings, nutritional support, infection prevention, and, for deep burns, surgical excision and skin grafting. Inhalation injury from smoke and hot gases is a major determinant of mortality in flame burns.
