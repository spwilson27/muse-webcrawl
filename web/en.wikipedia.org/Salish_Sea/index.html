The Salish Sea comprises the Strait of Georgia, Puget Sound, and the Strait of Juan de Fuca.

The Salish Sea is a marginal sea of the Pacific Ocean located in the Canadian province of British Columbia and the U.S. state of Washington. It includes the Strait of Georgia, the Strait of Juan de Fuca, Puget Sound, and an intricate network of connecting channels and adjoining waters. The name recognises the Coast Salish peoples who have inhabited the region for thousands of years.

Name

The term was proposed in 1988 by marine biologist Bert Webber of Bellingham, Washington, to promote awareness of the region's waters as a single ecosystem. After campaigns on both sides of the border, the name was officially recognised in 2009 by the United States Board on Geographic Names and in 2010 by the Geographical Names Board of Canada, supplementing rather than replacing the names of the component waterways.

Geography and ecology

The Salish Sea basin covers about 17,000 square kilometres of water and a watershed of some 110,000 square kilometres. Its estuarine waters are fed by rivers including the Fraser, Skagit, and Nooksack, and are characterised by deep fjords and basins separated by shallow sills. The sea supports rich marine life, including salmon runs, shellfish, and the endangered Southern Resident killer whales, as well as major ports at Vancouver, Seattle, and Tacoma and busy ferry networks.

Environmental issues

Population growth around the basin — home to more than eight million people — has brought pollution, habitat loss, and vessel traffic pressures. Transboundary cooperation between Canada and the United States addresses declining salmon stocks, orca recovery, and the risk of oil spills from shipping.
