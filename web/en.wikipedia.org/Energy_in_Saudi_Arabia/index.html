Energy in Saudi Arabia

Proved oil reservesAbout 267 billion barrels (among the world's largest)
Oil productionRoughly 9–12 million barrels per day (varies with OPEC+ policy)
Major companySaudi Aramco
Electricity generationPredominantly oil and natural gas

Energy in Saudi Arabia is dominated by the kingdom's vast petroleum resources. Saudi Arabia holds around 17 per cent of the world's proven petroleum reserves and ranks among the largest producers and exporters of crude oil. Energy production is centred on the state-owned company Saudi Aramco, which manages the supergiant Ghawar field, the largest conventional oil field in the world.

Petroleum

Oil was first discovered in commercial quantities at Dammam in 1938. Since then, petroleum revenues have funded the kingdom's development and made it a central player in global energy markets and in OPEC, which Saudi Arabia co-founded in 1960. Production capacity has been maintained around 12 million barrels per day, giving the kingdom a unique swing-producer role.

Natural gas

Saudi Arabia also holds large reserves of natural gas, ranking among the top ten countries worldwide. Historically, most gas was associated gas produced alongside oil and was flared; the Master Gas System built in the 1970s and 1980s captured it for domestic industry and power generation. Unlike its oil, Saudi gas is consumed entirely at home rather than exported.

Electricity

Electricity demand has grown rapidly with population growth, desalination and heavy use of air conditioning. Generation is supplied almost entirely by burning crude oil, refined products and natural gas. Peak summer demand strains the system, and burning crude directly for power has been criticised as wasteful of a valuable export commodity.

Renewables and diversification

Under the Saudi Vision 2030 programme, the kingdom has set targets to generate half of its electricity from renewable sources by 2030, principally solar power, exploiting its high solar irradiance. Projects such as the Sakaka solar plant and large wind farms mark the start of this transition, alongside investments in hydrogen and carbon capture.
