Decolonization

Decolonization
DefinitionThe undoing of colonialism; the process by which colonies become independent of the colonising country
Major wavesAmericas (1776–1820s); Asia and Africa (1945–1975)
Key instrumentUN General Assembly Resolution 1514 (1960), the Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples
UN bodySpecial Committee on Decolonization (C-24), established 1961

Decolonization is the process by which colonies become independent of the ruling country. It sometimes involves negotiation and sometimes violence, and its outcomes have ranged from peaceful transfers of power to protracted wars of independence. Modern decolonization is mainly dated from the mid-twentieth century, when the European empires in Asia and Africa were dismantled within a generation.

Historical waves

The first great wave followed the American Revolutionary War (1776) and the Latin American wars of independence in the early nineteenth century, ending Spanish and Portuguese rule in most of the Americas. A second, smaller wave followed the First World War, with mandates and partial independences in the Middle East.

Post-1945 decolonization

After 1945, weakened European powers faced organised nationalist movements and pressure from both the United States and the Soviet Union. India and Pakistan gained independence from Britain in 1947, followed by Burma and Ceylon; Indonesia and Indochina fought or negotiated their way out of Dutch and French rule. In Africa, Ghana's independence in 1957 opened the floodgates: seventeen nations became independent in 1960 alone, the "Year of Africa". The Bandung Conference of 1955 and the Non-Aligned Movement gave the new states a collective voice. Portugal's African empire was the last to fall, in 1975, after the Carnation Revolution in Lisbon.

International framework

The United Nations Charter affirmed self-determination, and in 1960 the General Assembly adopted Resolution 1514, declaring that all peoples have the right to self-determination and that inadequacy of preparation should never delay independence. A Special Committee on Decolonization monitors the remaining Non-Self-Governing Territories, of which seventeen remain on the UN list.

Consequences

Decolonization redrew the world map, more than tripled UN membership, and left complex legacies: borders inherited from colonial administration, economic dependence debated under the label of neocolonialism, and continuing debates about restitution of cultural property and the cultural and intellectual dimensions of decolonization.
