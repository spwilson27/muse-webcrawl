Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 203, is a United States labor law that creates the right to a minimum wage, and "time-and-a-half" overtime pay when people work over forty hours a week. It also prohibits the employment of minors in "oppressive child labor". It applies to employees engaged in interstate commerce or employed by an enterprise engaged in commerce or in the production of goods for commerce, unless the employer can claim an exemption from coverage. The FLSA was a product of the New Deal, introduced by Senator Hugo Black and signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Provisions

The Act originally set a minimum wage of 25 cents per hour, a maximum workweek of 44 hours (falling to 40), and banned most child labour. Repeated amendments raised the minimum wage — reaching $7.25 per hour in 2009, where the federal rate has since remained, though many states and cities set higher rates — and extended coverage to most public and private employees. Overtime at one-and-a-half times the regular rate is required for hours over 40 per week for non-exempt employees; executive, administrative, professional, and certain other employees are exempt. The Act also requires recordkeeping and restricts the hours and occupations of workers aged 14–17.

History and amendments

The FLSA survived constitutional challenge in United States v. Darby Lumber Co. (1941), which upheld Congress's power under the Commerce Clause. Major amendments include the Equal Pay Act of 1963 (prohibiting sex-based wage discrimination, technically a separate statute but part of the same framework), the 1961 and 1966 extensions of coverage to retail and service workers, the 1974 extension to domestic and public employees, and the Portal-to-Portal Act of 1947, which limited claims for preliminary and travel time.

Enforcement and debates

The Wage and Hour Division investigates complaints; employees may also sue for back pay and liquidated damages. Continuing debates concern the frozen federal minimum wage, the salary threshold for the white-collar exemptions, classification of gig-economy workers as employees or independent contractors, and tipped wages, which the Act allows to be as low as $2.13 per hour in cash wages provided tips bring earnings to the minimum.
