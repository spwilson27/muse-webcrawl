The KGB (Russian: КГБ; Komitet Gosudarstvennoy Bezopasnosti, "Committee for State Security") was the main security agency of the Soviet Union from 1954 until its dissolution in 1991. It combined the functions of foreign intelligence, counterintelligence, internal security, border protection, and the suppression of political dissent, answering directly to the leadership of the Communist Party.

History

The KGB was created in March 1954 from the security ministries of the late Stalin period, in the succession of Soviet security organs descending from the Cheka (1917) through the GPU, NKVD, and MGB. After Stalin's death and the fall of Lavrentiy Beria, the new agency was placed under tighter party control and formally renounced the mass terror of its predecessors, though it continued extensive surveillance, censorship support, and persecution of dissidents, including the use of punitive psychiatry.

Under its longest-serving chairman, Yuri Andropov (1967–1982), the KGB expanded its foreign operations and its domestic campaign against dissent, and cultivated an image of relative incorruptibility. Andropov's rise to General Secretary in 1982 made him the only KGB chief to lead the USSR. In August 1991, KGB chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov was a leading figure in the failed coup against Mikhail Gorbachev; the agency was broken up in the aftermath.

Organization

The KGB's main directorates included the First (foreign intelligence), Second (counterintelligence), Third (military counterintelligence), Fifth (ideological/political security, created 1967), Seventh (surveillance), Eighth (communications and cryptography), and Ninth (guards), together with the Border Troops, which numbered in the hundreds of thousands. Total personnel, including troops, were estimated at several hundred thousand, supported by a vast network of informants.

Foreign operations

Abroad, the KGB ran espionage networks, "active measures" of disinformation and political influence, and support for allied intelligence services such as the East German Stasi. Notable episodes include the recruitment of the Cambridge Five in Britain, penetration of Western governments during the Cold War, and disinformation campaigns such as Operation INFEKTION, which spread the claim that the United States created HIV/AIDS.

Legacy

After 1991 the KGB's functions were divided among successor agencies in the former Soviet republics, principally Russia's FSB (internal security) and SVR (foreign intelligence). The agency's archives remain largely closed, and its methods and personnel have had a lasting influence on the security services of the region.
