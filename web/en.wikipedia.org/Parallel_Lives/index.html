The Parallel Lives (Greek: Βίοι Παράλληλοι, Bíoi Parállēloi) is a series of biographies of famous Greeks and Romans, arranged in pairs to illuminate their common moral virtues or failings, written by the Greek historian and biographer Plutarch of Chaeronea in the late first and early second centuries AD. The surviving text comprises 23 pairs — 46 lives — plus four unpaired biographies.

Structure and method

Each pair matches a Greek figure with a Roman one of similar career or character: Theseus with Romulus, Alexander the Great with Julius Caesar, Demosthenes with Cicero, Alcibiades with Coriolanus, and so on. Most pairs were followed by a short formal comparison (synkrisis) weighing the two men. Plutarch famously announced in the Life of Alexander that he was "writing lives, not histories", and that "a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall" — his aim was moral portraiture rather than exhaustive narrative.

Sources and survival

Plutarch drew on a vast range of earlier historians, many now lost, making the Lives an indispensable source for Greek and Roman history despite their moralising purpose. Several pairs known in antiquity are lost, including the life pair of Scipio Africanus and Epaminondas. The Lives were transmitted through Byzantine manuscripts and printed in Greek in 1517 at Florence; Jacques Amyot's French translation (1559) and Thomas North's English version from Amyot (1579) made them canonical in early modern Europe.

Influence

Shakespeare took the plots of Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra, Coriolanus and Timon of Athens largely from North's Plutarch, often following its wording closely. The Lives shaped Renaissance and Enlightenment ideas of republican virtue, were favourite reading of figures from Montaigne to Emerson, and established paired comparative biography as a literary form.
