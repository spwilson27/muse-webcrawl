Inattention blindness

Inattentional blindness is a psychological phenomenon in which a person fails to perceive an unexpected stimulus that is in plain sight, because their attention is engaged on another task, event, or object. The term was coined in 1992 by Arien Mack and Irvin Rock, whose systematic studies appeared in their 1998 book Inattentional Blindness.

The invisible gorilla

The phenomenon was popularized by Christopher Chabris and Daniel Simons in their 1999 experiment: participants asked to count basketball passes made by players in white shirts often failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene, thumping their chest, and leaving — about half of observers missed it entirely. The study, later the subject of the 2010 book The Invisible Gorilla, won an Ig Nobel Prize in 2004.

Causes and factors

Inattentional blindness reflects the limited capacity of attention: perception of unattended objects can be severely reduced, particularly when the primary task is demanding, when the unexpected object resembles distractors, or when observers hold strong expectations. Expertise does not immunize against it — in one study, most radiologists screening CT scans missed a gorilla image embedded in a scan.

Implications

The phenomenon has practical implications for driving safety (including the risks of phone use while driving), aviation, medicine, eyewitness testimony, and magic, where misdirection exploits attentional limits. It is related to, but distinct from, change blindness, in which observers fail to detect changes between views of a scene.
