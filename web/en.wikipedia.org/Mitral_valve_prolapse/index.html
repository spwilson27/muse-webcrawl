Mitral valve prolapse

Mitral valve prolapse (MVP) is a valvular heart disease characterized by displacement of an abnormally thickened mitral valve leaflet into the left atrium during systole. It is the most common cause of severe non-ischemic mitral regurgitation in developed countries and affects roughly 2–3 percent of the population.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with MVP have no symptoms and a normal life expectancy. On auscultation the classic finding is a mid-systolic click, sometimes followed by a late systolic murmur if regurgitation is present. Some patients report palpitations, chest discomfort, fatigue, or anxiety, though the strength of these associations has been debated. Complications, which are uncommon, include progressive mitral regurgitation, infective endocarditis, arrhythmias, and rarely sudden cardiac death; a subset known as arrhythmic MVP has drawn research attention.

Causes and classification

MVP is classified as primary — myxomatous degeneration with redundant, thickened leaflets, as in Barlow's disease, or thinner leaflets in fibroelastic deficiency — or secondary to connective-tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, or other cardiac conditions. There is a heritable component, with several associated genetic loci identified.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by echocardiography, showing leaflet displacement of at least 2 mm beyond the annular plane. Asymptomatic patients generally need only reassurance and periodic follow-up echocardiograms. Treatment is directed at complications: surgery (usually valve repair) for severe regurgitation, and management of arrhythmias where present. Antibiotic prophylaxis against endocarditis is no longer routinely recommended for MVP alone.
