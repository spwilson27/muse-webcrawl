Atari video game burial

The Atari video game burial was a mass burial of unsold video game cartridges, consoles, and computers in a New Mexico landfill site in 1983. Buried items included copies of E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982), one of the largest commercial failures in video gaming and often cited as one of the worst video games ever released, and the Atari 2600 port of Pac-Man (1982). The burial became a symbol of the video game crash of 1983 and for decades was treated as an urban legend.

Background

Atari, Inc. had paid a reported $20–25 million for the rights to make a game of Steven Spielberg's film E.T.; programmer Howard Scott Warshaw developed it in about five weeks for the 1982 Christmas season. Around five million cartridges were manufactured; roughly 1.5 million sold, leaving Atari with vast unsold stock amid the collapsing market. In September 1983, newspapers reported that truckloads of Atari merchandise from a warehouse in El Paso, Texas, had been crushed and buried at the Alamogordo landfill, with a layer of concrete reportedly poured over the material.

Legend status

Conflicting statements by Atari executives — some describing the site as a dump of broken and returned merchandise — led many to dismiss the story as exaggerated or mythical. The narrative nonetheless became a cautionary tale in gaming culture, representing corporate hubris and the 1983 crash.

2014 excavation

On April 26, 2014, a documentary crew led by Fuel Industries, Microsoft, and filmmaker Zak Penn excavated the site with the city's permission. Hundreds of cartridges — including E.T., Centipede, and Missile Command — were unearthed within hours, confirming the burial. Recovered games were later sold by the city of Alamogordo, with proceeds benefiting the local historical society, and examples entered museum collections including the Smithsonian.
