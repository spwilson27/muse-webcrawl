p–n junction

A p–n junction in thermal equilibrium

A p–n junction is a boundary or interface between two types of semiconductor materials, p-type and n-type, inside a single crystal of semiconductor. The "p" (positive) side contains an excess of holes, and the "n" (negative) side an excess of electrons. The p–n junction is the fundamental building block of semiconductor devices such as diodes, transistors, solar cells, LEDs and integrated circuits.

Formation and the depletion region

P-type and n-type material are produced by doping a pure semiconductor (typically silicon or germanium) with acceptor or donor impurity atoms. When the two are joined, electrons diffuse from the n-side and holes from the p-side, recombining near the interface and leaving behind a depletion region (or space-charge region) populated only by fixed ionised donors and acceptors. This creates a built-in electric field and potential barrier — about 0.7 V for silicon — that opposes further diffusion.

Biasing

Under forward bias (positive voltage to the p-side), the barrier is lowered and current flows readily once the voltage exceeds the built-in potential. Under reverse bias the barrier is raised and only a tiny saturation current flows, until reverse breakdown (Zener or avalanche) occurs. This one-way conduction makes the p–n junction diode a rectifier. The junction's properties were explained theoretically following the invention of the transistor at Bell Labs in 1947, with William Shockley's theory of the junction transistor published in 1949.

Applications

Beyond diodes, p–n junctions are the working element of photovoltaic solar cells and photodiodes, of light-emitting diodes (where forward current produces light by electroluminescence), and of the emitter–base and collector–base junctions inside every bipolar junction transistor.
