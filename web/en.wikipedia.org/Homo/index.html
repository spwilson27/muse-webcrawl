Homo is the genus comprising the human species Homo sapiens and its close extinct relatives. The genus emerged in Africa roughly 2.5–2.8 million years ago from australopithecine ancestors, and is characterised by increasing brain size, obligate bipedalism, reduced dentition, and dependence on tools and culture. The name Homo is the Latin word for "human" or "man".

Species

Species commonly assigned to the genus include H. habilis ("handy man", the earliest, associated with Oldowan tools), H. erectus (which spread from Africa across Eurasia and used fire), H. heidelbergensis, H. neanderthalensis (the Neanderthals of Europe and western Asia), the Denisovans, H. naledi, H. floresiensis and H. luzonensis (small-bodied island forms), and H. sapiens, which evolved in Africa about 300,000 years ago (as at Jebel Irhoud) and later spread worldwide.

Evolution and interbreeding

Ancient DNA research has shown that early modern humans interbred with Neanderthals and Denisovans, leaving 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry in most non-African genomes. By about 40,000 years ago H. sapiens was the only surviving member of the genus. The genus is distinguished by cumulative culture, language, and complex tool traditions — from Oldowan and Acheulean stone tools to agriculture and industry — which made humans a globally dominant species, while also driving questions about the taxonomy and boundaries of the genus itself.
