Moon in fiction

The Moon in fiction has been a destination, setting and muse for storytellers since antiquity. Imaginary voyages to the Moon are among the earliest works of science fiction: Lucian's True History (2nd century AD) sent travellers there by whirlwind, and the theme runs through Kepler's Somnium (1634), Cyrano de Bergerac's lunar voyages, and Jules Verne's From the Earth to the Moon (1865).

Verne's cannon-fired capsule, an icon of early lunar fiction.

Early lunar voyages

Before powered flight, writers reached the Moon by magic, birds, balloons, ladders of dew, and Verne's giant cannon. H. G. Wells's The First Men in the Moon (1901) imagined the insectoid Selenites living in caverns beneath the surface, fed by moon-calves. Georges Méliès's 1902 film A Trip to the Moon, with its famous image of a capsule lodged in the Moon's eye, brought the lunar voyage to cinema.

The space age

As real lunar exploration approached, fiction shifted to colonies, bases and the far side: Arthur C. Clarke's A Fall of Moondust (1961), Robert A. Heinlein's harsh lunar penal colony in The Moon Is a Harsh Mistress (1966), and films from Destination Moon (1950) to 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968). After Apollo, the Moon often served as a mining outpost, refuge or mystery — as in the film Moon (2009) and Andy Weir's Artemis (2017), set in a lunar city.

Themes

Lunar fiction explores colonisation, isolation, altered gravity, Earth seen from afar, and the Moon as a mirror for human hopes; earlier traditions also populated it with kingdoms and creatures reflecting earthly satire.
