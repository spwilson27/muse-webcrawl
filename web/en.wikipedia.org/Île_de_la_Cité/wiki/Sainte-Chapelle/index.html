The Sainte-Chapelle ("Holy Chapel") is a royal chapel in the Gothic Rayonnant style, within the medieval Palais de la Cité on the Île de la Cité in Paris. It is celebrated for its stained glass, among the most extensive and beautiful thirteenth-century glazing to survive anywhere.

History

King Louis IX commissioned the chapel to house his collection of Passion relics, above all the Crown of Thorns, which he had acquired from the Latin Emperor of Constantinople in 1239 for a sum greater than the cost of the chapel itself. Built with remarkable speed between 1242 and 1248 and consecrated on 26 April 1248, the chapel served as the king's palace chapel and a monumental reliquary in stone and glass.

The building has two storeys: a lower chapel for palace staff, dedicated to the Virgin Mary, and an upper chapel for the king and court, where the relics were displayed in a great gilded shrine. Damaged during the French Revolution — when it was used as a flour store and archive — the chapel was restored in the nineteenth century under Félix Duban and Jean-Baptiste Lassus.

The stained glass

Fifteen enormous windows, each about 15 metres high, fill the upper chapel with light. Their 1,113 glass panels narrate biblical history from Genesis to the arrival of the relics in Paris, dominated by deep blues and ruby reds. About two-thirds of the glass is original thirteenth-century work, making the chapel one of the most complete ensembles of medieval stained glass in existence. A great rose window, added in the fifteenth century in the Flamboyant style, depicts the Apocalypse.
