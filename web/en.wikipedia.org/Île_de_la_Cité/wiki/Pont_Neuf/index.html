The Pont Neuf ("New Bridge") is, despite its name, the oldest standing bridge across the river Seine in Paris, France. It stands by the western point of the Île de la Cité, the island in the middle of the river that was the heart of medieval Paris.

History

King Henry III laid the first stone in 1578, but work was interrupted by the Wars of Religion and the bridge was only completed under Henry IV, opening to traffic in 1606 and being inaugurated in 1607. The design, attributed to the royal architects including Baptiste Androuet du Cerceau, was revolutionary: unlike earlier Parisian bridges it was built of stone, carried no houses, and was fitted with pavements (trottoirs) — the first in Paris — protecting pedestrians from mud and horses. Rows of small semicircular balconies over the piers became popular spots for street performers, dentists, booksellers, and gossips, making the Pont Neuf the social centre of the city for two centuries.

Features

The bridge consists of two spans: five arches from the right bank to the island and seven from the island to the left bank, totalling 232 metres. Along its cornice run 381 stone mascarons — grotesque masks carved by Germain Pilon's workshop, restored in the nineteenth century. At the point of the island stands an equestrian statue of Henry IV; the original of 1614 was destroyed in the Revolution and replaced in 1818. Downstream of the statue, stairs lead to the Square du Vert-Galant, a small park at the island's tip.
