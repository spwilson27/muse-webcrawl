Notre-Dame de Paris ("Our Lady of Paris"), officially the Cathedral of Notre-Dame de Paris, is a medieval Catholic cathedral on the Île de la Cité in Paris, France. It is considered one of the finest examples of French Gothic architecture and among the most famous churches in the world.

History

Construction began in 1163 under Bishop Maurice de Sully and was largely complete by 1260, though modifications continued into the fourteenth century. The cathedral pioneered the use of the rib vault and flying buttress, and its enormous rose windows and sculptural decoration made it a model for Gothic building across Europe. It witnessed major events of French history, from the coronation of Napoleon in 1804 to the liberation of Paris in 1944.

Neglected and damaged during the French Revolution, the cathedral was famously rescued from obscurity by Victor Hugo's 1831 novel Notre-Dame de Paris (The Hunchback of Notre-Dame), which prompted a sweeping restoration by Eugène Viollet-le-Duc between 1844 and 1864, including the re-creation of its flèche (spire).

2019 fire and restoration

On 15 April 2019 a fire destroyed the roof and spire and caused extensive interior damage, drawing worldwide attention and pledges of roughly €1 billion. After stabilisation and a reconstruction faithful to the historic design — including the rebuilding of the oak "forest" roof frame and Viollet-le-Duc's spire — the cathedral reopened to the public in December 2024.

Architecture

The west facade, with its twin 69-metre towers, three sculpted portals, and gallery of kings, is organised according to geometric principles. Inside, the nave rises 32 metres, lit by stained glass including three rose windows. The cathedral houses relics including the Crown of Thorns and is guarded by its famous gargoyles and chimeras.
