The Île Saint-Louis is one of two natural islands in the Seine in Paris, France (the other is the Île de la Cité, to which it is connected by the Pont Saint-Louis). Named after King Louis IX, it is a quiet, largely residential enclave celebrated for its harmonious seventeenth-century streetscape.

History

Unlike the neighbouring Île de la Cité, settled since antiquity, the Île Saint-Louis was long uninhabited pasture: medieval maps show it divided by a channel into two islets, the Île Notre-Dame and the Île aux Vaches, used for grazing cattle and storing timber. In 1614 the engineer Christophe Marie began joining and developing the islands under contract with Louis XIII. Speculative plots were bought up by courtiers and financiers, who commissioned grand hôtels particuliers from architects such as Louis Le Vau.

Sights

The island's principal landmarks include the church of Saint-Louis-en-l'Île (begun 1664), the Hôtel Lambert at the eastern tip — a masterpiece of Le Vau, once home to Voltaire's circle and later to the Czartoryski princes — and the Hôtel de Lauzun, briefly home to Charles Baudelaire. The main street, Rue Saint-Louis-en-l'Île, is lined with small shops and is famous for the Berthillon ice-cream parlour, founded in 1954. Residents, nicknamed Ludovisiens, prize the island's village-like calm a few steps from Notre-Dame.

Bridges

Five bridges link the island to the rest of Paris: the Pont Saint-Louis to the Île de la Cité, the Pont de la Tournelle and Pont de Sully to the Left Bank and Right Bank, the Pont Louis-Philippe and Pont Marie to the Right Bank.
