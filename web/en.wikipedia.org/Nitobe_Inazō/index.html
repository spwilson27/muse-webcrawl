Nitobe Inazō (新渡戸 稲造, September 1, 1862 – October 15, 1933) was a Japanese educator, agricultural economist, diplomat, and Christian internationalist. He is best known as the author of Bushido: The Soul of Japan (1899), written in English, which introduced the samurai code of ethics to Western readers and remains one of the most widely read works on Japanese culture. Nitobe served as an Under Secretary-General of the League of Nations and worked to build cultural bridges between Japan and the West; his portrait appeared on the Japanese 5,000 yen banknote from 1984 to 2004.

Nitobe Inazō, author of Bushido: The Soul of Japan

Early life and education

Nitobe was born in Morioka in northern Japan, the third son of a samurai retainer of the Nanbu clan. He studied at Sapporo Agricultural College, where he converted to Christianity, and later pursued studies in the United States at Johns Hopkins University and in Germany, earning a doctorate in agricultural economics from the University of Halle.

Career

Nitobe taught at Sapporo Agricultural College and helped develop Taiwan's sugar industry as a colonial administrator. He became the first president of Tokyo Women's Christian University and an influential liberal educator. From 1920 to 1926 he served in Geneva as Under Secretary-General of the League of Nations, directing the International Bureaux Section and promoting intellectual cooperation; he was later appointed to Japan's House of Peers.

Bushido: The Soul of Japan

Written in 1899 in Pennsylvania and published in 1900, Bushido presents the unwritten samurai code — rectitude, courage, benevolence, politeness, sincerity, honor, loyalty, and self-control — as the moral foundation of Japanese society, drawing comparisons with European chivalry and Stoicism. The book shaped Western perceptions of Japan for generations.

Death and legacy

Nitobe died of pneumonia in Victoria, British Columbia, in 1933 while returning from a conference in Banff. The Nitobe Memorial Garden at the University of British Columbia honors his memory.
