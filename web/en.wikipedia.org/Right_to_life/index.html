Legal sources: Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Art. 3; ICCPR, Art. 6; ECHR, Art. 2

Right to life

The right to life is the belief and legal principle that a human being has the right to live and, in particular, should not be killed by another human being or by the state without lawful justification. It is recognised in Article 3 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 6 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, and Article 2 of the European Convention on Human Rights.

Scope and debates

The right underpins debates over capital punishment, abortion, euthanasia, self-defence, and the use of lethal force by police. International law generally prohibits the arbitrary deprivation of life while permitting exceptions that vary by jurisdiction — for example, many states retain the death penalty under the ICCPR's safeguards, while the European Convention system has moved toward abolition. Disputes often turn on when legally protected life begins and on how the right weighs against the rights of others.

Legal protection

Most national constitutions protect life, and courts such as the European Court of Human Rights have read into the right positive obligations: to investigate suspicious deaths, to protect individuals from known threats, and to regulate dangerous activities. The phrase "right to life" is also the name used by movements opposed to abortion and euthanasia.
