Universal Grammar

In modern linguistics, Universal Grammar (UG) is the theory of the innate biological component of the language faculty, usually credited to Noam Chomsky. The basic claim is that a certain set of structural rules — governing, for example, the hierarchical organization of phrases — is innate to humans and shared by all languages, so that children acquire language rapidly despite limited input.

Theory

Chomsky introduced generative grammar in the 1950s, arguing against behaviorist accounts of language learning with the "poverty of the stimulus" argument: children produce and understand sentences they have never heard, and know constraints they were never taught. In the principles and parameters framework, UG consists of universal principles plus parameters that vary between languages (such as head-initial vs. head-final order), set by exposure. Later, the Minimalist Program sought to reduce UG to simpler operations, centering on the recursive operation Merge, which Chomsky, with Marc Hauser and W. Tecumseh Fitch, proposed in 2002 as possibly the core of the narrow language faculty.

Criticism

Usage-based linguists and typologists have challenged UG, citing languages that appear to lack proposed universals, the gradient and statistical character of child learning, and the possibility that general cognitive abilities explain language acquisition. Figures such as Michael Tomasello and Daniel Everett are prominent critics, and empirical work on linguistic diversity has complicated strong versions of the innateness claim, though the debate continues.
