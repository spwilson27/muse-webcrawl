Cretan hieroglyphs

Cretan hieroglyphs appear mainly on seal stones and clay documents from Minoan Crete.

Cretan hieroglyphs are a hieroglyphic writing system used in early Bronze Age Crete, during the Minoan civilization, roughly from 2100 to 1700 BCE. The script remains undeciphered. It is one of three overlapping but distinct scripts used in the Bronze Age Aegean, alongside Linear A and the later Greek-derived Linear B.

Corpus

The known corpus, catalogued in the Corpus Hieroglyphicarum Inscriptionum Cretae (CHIC), comprises around 830 documents bearing some 3,000 sign occurrences, drawn from a signary of roughly 96 syllabic signs and numerous logograms. Most texts are short: seal stones, seal impressions on clay, medallions, and a small number of clay tablets and bars, found chiefly at Knossos, Malia, Phaistos and Petras.

Relationship to other scripts

Cretan hieroglyphs coexisted with Linear A for perhaps two centuries; some signs are shared between the two systems, leading scholars to suggest that Linear A developed partly out of the hieroglyphic tradition or that both drew on a common source. Unlike the hieroglyphs, Linear A was written with a stylus in a linear fashion better suited to clay tablets, whereas the hieroglyphic signs retained a pictorial character suited to seal carving.

Decipherment attempts

Because the underlying language — presumably the same "Minoan" language as Linear A — is unknown, and inscriptions are short and formulaic, the script has resisted decipherment. Statistical and structural analyses suggest a syllabary with values partly comparable to Linear A and the Cypro-Minoan scripts, but no proposed reading commands scholarly consensus.
