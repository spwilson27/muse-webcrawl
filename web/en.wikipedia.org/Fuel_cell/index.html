Fuel cell

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fuel cell is an electrochemical cell that converts the chemical energy of a fuel (often hydrogen) and an oxidizing agent (often oxygen) into electricity through a pair of redox reactions. Fuel cells differ from batteries in that they require a continuous external supply of fuel and oxygen to sustain the reaction, and can therefore produce electricity continuously for as long as fuel is supplied.

How it works

In a proton-exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell, hydrogen is oxidized at the anode, releasing protons and electrons. The protons pass through a polymer electrolyte membrane while the electrons travel through an external circuit, producing electric current. At the cathode, oxygen reacts with the protons and electrons to form water, the cell's only direct emission when hydrogen is the fuel.

Types

Fuel cells are classified by their electrolyte and operating temperature. Major types include polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM) cells, phosphoric acid fuel cells, solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs), molten carbonate fuel cells, and alkaline fuel cells, the type used in the Apollo and Space Shuttle programs to supply both electricity and drinking water.

Applications

Applications include fuel cell vehicles such as the Toyota Mirai and Hyundai Nexo, stationary and backup power, combined heat and power systems, forklifts, buses, and portable power. Barriers to wider adoption include the cost of catalysts such as platinum, durability, and the limited infrastructure for producing, distributing, and storing hydrogen — especially low-carbon "green" hydrogen made by electrolysis.
