Proto-Dravidian is the reconstructed common ancestor of the Dravidian languages, a family of about 86 languages spoken mainly in southern and central India, including Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam. Because no written records of the proto-language survive, its vocabulary and grammar have been recovered by the comparative method from its daughter languages.

Reconstruction

The foundational work of reconstruction is the Dravidian Etymological Dictionary by Thomas Burrow and Murray B. Emeneau, which assembled thousands of cognate sets. Scholars such as Bhadriraju Krishnamurti have reconstructed the phonology, morphology and much of the core vocabulary of the proto-language, dividing it into stages (Proto-Dravidian I and II) corresponding roughly to the periods before and after the northern branch split away.

Phonology

Proto-Dravidian is reconstructed with a five-vowel system (a, i, u, e, o), each with a long counterpart. Its consonant inventory is notable for a rich series of retroflex consonants and a three-way contrast among dental, alveolar and retroflex stops — a feature preserved in Tamil and Malayalam but lost or merged in many other daughters. It lacked the aspirated consonants of Indo-Aryan languages, though many modern Dravidian languages acquired them through contact with Sanskrit and other Indo-Aryan tongues.

Homeland and chronology

The Dravidian homeland is debated. Most linguists place the proto-language in peninsular India, in the Deccan or further south, roughly in the 4th millennium BCE. Some researchers have proposed connections with the Indus Valley Civilisation and with Elamite (the Elamo-Dravidian hypothesis), but neither link is established. Loanwords between early Dravidian and Vedic Sanskrit indicate prolonged contact in the northwest as Indo-Aryan speakers entered the subcontinent.

Vocabulary

Reconstructed roots include words for agriculture (ploughing, rice, millet), kinship terms reflecting Dravidian cross-cousin marriage systems, and a vigesimal-influenced counting vocabulary. Words for specifically northern flora and fauna are absent, supporting a southern homeland.
