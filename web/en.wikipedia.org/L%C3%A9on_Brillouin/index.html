Léon Nicolas Brillouin (7 August 1889 – 4 October 1969) was a French physicist who made fundamental contributions to quantum mechanics, solid-state physics, radio wave propagation and information theory. He was the son of the physicist Marcel Brillouin. Concepts named after him include the Brillouin zone, central to the band theory of solids, and Brillouin scattering of light by acoustic waves.

Career

Brillouin studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris and worked with leading figures of early quantum theory. In 1926, independently of Wentzel and Kramers, he developed the WKB (Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin) approximation in quantum mechanics. His 1930 book on wave propagation in periodic structures introduced the Brillouin zones of reciprocal space, fundamental to understanding electron states in crystals.

War work and emigration

Before and during the Second World War, Brillouin worked on radio broadcasting in France; after the fall of France he emigrated to the United States, where he worked on radar-related problems and taught at several universities, including a period at Harvard. He became a professor of applied mathematics at Columbia University and later worked in industry research with IBM. He became an American citizen in 1949.

Information theory

In his 1956 book Science and Information Theory, Brillouin explored the connection between information and entropy, developing the concept of "negentropy" and analysing Maxwell's demon, showing that acquiring information carries an entropic cost — an idea closely related to later work on the physics of information.
