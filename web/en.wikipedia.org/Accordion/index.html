Accordion

An accordion (from 19th-century German Akkordeon, from Akkord — "musical chord, concord of sounds") is a box-shaped musical instrument of the bellows-driven free-reed aerophone family, sometimes referred to as a squeezebox. A person who plays the accordion is called an accordionist.

The accordion is played by compressing or expanding the bellows while pressing buttons or keys, causing pallets to open, which allow air to flow across strips of brass or steel, called reeds, that vibrate to produce sound inside the body. The instrument typically has a treble side with a piano-style keyboard or rows of buttons, and a bass side with buttons that play bass notes and preset chords.

History

The basic form of the accordion was invented in Berlin in 1822 by Christian Friedrich Ludwig Buschmann, though similar instruments predate this. The accordion spread rapidly with European emigration and became central to many folk traditions: French musette, German and Alpine music, Italian folk, Irish traditional music (button accordion), Cajun and zydeco music in Louisiana, norteño and conjunto music in Mexico and Texas, and vallenato in Colombia, among others. In the 1950s it also became a popular instrument in mainstream pop music in North America.

Construction

The accordion consists of two wooden end boxes joined by pleated bellows. The right-hand (treble) side carries the melody keys or buttons and usually several register switches that change the reed combinations. The left-hand (bass) side on most modern instruments uses the Stradella bass system, with rows of buttons for counterbass, fundamental bass, and major, minor, dominant seventh and diminished chords — a full-size instrument has 120 bass buttons.

Types

Main types include the piano accordion, the chromatic button accordion (common in Europe and Russia, where it is called a bayan), the diatonic button accordion (melodeon), and the concertina, a smaller related instrument. Electronic and digital accordions also exist.
