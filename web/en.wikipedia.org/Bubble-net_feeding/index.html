Bubble-net feeding

Bubble-net feeding is a cooperative feeding behaviour engaged in by groups of humpback whales (Megaptera novaeangliae), and also observed in Bryde's whales. The whales dive beneath a school of fish or krill and blow bubbles in a spiral or ring, creating a rising "net" of foam that frightens and concentrates the prey, before lunging upward through the centre with open mouths.

Behaviour

A feeding group typically consists of a handful to as many as twenty whales with coordinated roles. One individual often leads and emits a distinctive feeding call that appears to coordinate the group and may stun or herd the prey. The bubbles form a cylindrical wall that fish are reluctant to cross, packing them into a dense bait ball near the surface. The whales then surge upward simultaneously, engulfing thousands of prey in a single lunge.

Learning and culture

The technique is not instinctive in all populations and appears to be learned and transmitted socially — an example of animal culture. Researchers in the Gulf of Maine documented a related innovation, lobtail feeding, spreading through the whale population over decades. Bubble-net feeding is best known from Alaska and western North America, and similar behaviour has been recorded in Antarctic waters.

Individual variation

Studies using suction-cup tags and aerial drones have shown that particular whales adopt specialised positions within the net, and that skilled individuals produce nets of consistent size. Modified techniques include single whales blowing nets, and in Thailand and Australia, Bryde's whales trap fish by holding their jaws open at the surface in a behaviour dubbed "trap feeding".
