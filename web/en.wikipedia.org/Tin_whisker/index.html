Tin whisker

Tin whiskers are electrically conductive, crystalline structures of tin that spontaneously grow from surfaces coated with tin, particularly tin solders and platings. They are hair-like filaments, typically a few micrometres in diameter and up to several millimetres long, that can bridge adjacent conductors and cause short circuits in electronic equipment.

Mechanism

Whisker growth is driven by compressive stress in the tin layer. As intermetallic compounds form at the interface between tin and a substrate such as copper, the resulting stress is relieved by extruding tin atoms into a filament. Unlike dendrites, which grow by electroplating in the presence of moisture and voltage, whiskers grow from solid metal without any applied field.

Problems

Tin whiskers have caused failures in satellites, military equipment, medical devices, and nuclear power plants. Notable incidents include the failures of commercial communications satellites in the late 1990s and early 2000s. The problem became more prominent after environmental regulations such as the European Restriction of Hazardous Substances Directive (RoHS) pushed manufacturers toward lead-free, pure-tin finishes; alloying tin with lead had long suppressed whisker growth.

Mitigation

Mitigation strategies include using matte rather than bright tin finishes, applying conformal coatings, adding a nickel underlayer, annealing or reflowing the coating, and avoiding pure tin in high-reliability applications.
