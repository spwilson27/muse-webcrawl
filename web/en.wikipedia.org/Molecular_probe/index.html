Molecular probe

A molecular probe is a group of atoms or molecules used in molecular biology or chemistry to study the properties of other molecules or structures. In the most common usage, a molecular probe is a labelled fragment of DNA or RNA used to detect the presence of a complementary nucleic acid sequence in a sample by hybridization. If some measurable characteristic changes in response to binding or a physical process, the probe can be used to study that process.

Nucleic acid probes

Nucleic acid probes are single-stranded sequences of DNA or RNA that are complementary to a target sequence. Because of the specificity of base pairing, a probe will hybridize only to a target containing the complementary sequence, allowing researchers to identify specific genes or transcripts among millions of unrelated molecules.

Probes are typically labelled so that the hybridized target can be detected. Historically, probes were labelled with radioisotopes such as 32P. Modern probes more often use fluorescent dyes, chemiluminescent labels, or enzymes such as horseradish peroxidase or alkaline phosphatase.

Applications

  • Southern and Northern blotting — detecting specific DNA or RNA sequences separated on membranes.
  • Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) — locating specific sequences on chromosomes within cells and tissues.
  • Microarrays — measuring the expression levels of thousands of genes simultaneously using arrays of immobilized probes.
  • Real-time PCR (qPCR) — using hydrolysis (TaqMan) or hybridization probes to quantify DNA as it is amplified.
  • Colony and plaque hybridization — screening libraries of clones for a sequence of interest.
  • Diagnostics — detecting pathogens and genetic mutations in clinical samples.

Probe design

Effective probes must be specific to the target, sensitive enough to detect low-abundance sequences, and stable under the conditions of the experiment. Length is a trade-off: longer probes are more specific but hybridize more slowly, while short oligonucleotide probes (roughly 15–30 bases) can discriminate single-nucleotide differences when conditions are carefully controlled. Melting temperature, GC content, and the avoidance of secondary structure are key design parameters.

Chemical probes

In chemistry and chemical biology, the term probe is used more broadly for small molecules that bind selectively to a protein or other target and report on its activity. Activity-based probes carry a reactive group that covalently labels active enzymes, along with a reporter tag, and are used in proteomics to profile enzyme activity across complex samples.
