Joseph Lister, 1st Baron Lister (5 April 1827 – 10 February 1912) was a British surgeon, medical scientist, experimental pathologist and pioneer of antiseptic surgery and preventative medicine. His introduction of sterile techniques dramatically reduced post-operative infections and made surgery far safer.

Early life and education

Lister was born into a prosperous Quaker family in Essex. His father, Joseph Jackson Lister, was a wine merchant and amateur optician who made important improvements to the compound microscope. Lister studied at University College London, earning a Bachelor of Arts and then a medical degree, and became a fellow of the Royal College of Surgeons in 1852. He worked in Edinburgh under the surgeon James Syme, whose daughter Agnes he married in 1856.

Antiseptic surgery

As professor of surgery at the University of Glasgow from 1860, Lister was troubled by the high death rates from sepsis, gangrene and erysipelas following operations — so common that they were described as "hospitalism". Reading Louis Pasteur's work on fermentation, he reasoned that wound infections were caused by airborne microbes settling in wounds.

From 1865 Lister began treating wounds and sterilising instruments and dressings with carbolic acid (phenol), which had been used to treat sewage in Carlisle. In 1867 he published his results in The Lancet, reporting a dramatic fall in mortality in his wards: amputation deaths fell from about 46 per cent to about 15 per cent. He later introduced the carbolic acid spray, catgut ligatures, and emphasised hand-washing and clean gloves.

Reception and legacy

Lister's methods were adopted slowly and controversially in Britain and America, but more rapidly in Germany. He became professor of surgery at King's College London in 1877, served as president of the Royal Society from 1895 to 1900, and was raised to the peerage in 1897 as Baron Lister. The bacterium genus Listeria and the mouthwash Listerine are named in his honour. His principles, developed later into aseptic technique, remain foundational to modern surgery.
