Surveillance capitalism

Surveillance capitalism is a concept in political economy describing a market-driven process in which personal data is collected, commodified and used to predict and modify human behaviour as a means of producing revenue and market control. The term was popularised by Harvard professor Shoshana Zuboff, notably in her 2019 book The Age of Surveillance Capitalism.

The concept

Zuboff argues that, beginning at Google around 2001–2002, companies discovered that data exhaust generated by users — searches, clicks, locations and interactions — could be retained as a "behavioral surplus" and fed into machine-intelligence processes to make predictions about future behaviour. These prediction products are sold to business customers, principally advertisers. Surveillance capitalists, she argues, progress from predicting behaviour to shaping it, through tuning, herding and conditioning users — an "instrumentarian" power that she contrasts with totalitarianism.

Criticism and responses

Critics have questioned the novelty of the model, pointing to older traditions of advertising, credit scoring and market research, and some economists dispute Zuboff's account of how the firms make money. Technology companies respond that data collection is disclosed in their terms and is used to fund free services and improve products. The concept has nonetheless become influential in debates over privacy regulation, antitrust, and instruments such as the EU's GDPR and Digital Markets Act.

Related ideas

Surveillance capitalism is related to, but distinct from, the state-centred mass surveillance revealed by the Snowden disclosures; Zuboff emphasises a commercial logic that governments have also come to exploit.
