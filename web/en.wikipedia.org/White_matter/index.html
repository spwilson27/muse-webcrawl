White matter

Part ofCentral nervous system
ComponentsMyelinated axons, glial cells
ContrastGrey matter

White matter refers to areas of the central nervous system (CNS) that are mainly made up of myelinated axons, also called tracts. Long thought to be passive tissue, white matter is now known to affect learning and brain functions, modulating the distribution of action potentials and coordinating communication between different brain regions.

Structure

White matter is composed chiefly of long nerve fibres (axons) wrapped in myelin, the fatty insulating sheath produced by oligodendrocytes; the myelin gives the tissue its whitish appearance in fresh specimens. It also contains glial cells including astrocytes and microglia. In the brain, white matter lies beneath the cortical grey matter, while in the spinal cord it surrounds the grey matter core.

Function

Myelin greatly increases the speed of signal conduction through saltatory conduction. The major tracts — such as the corpus callosum, internal capsule, and corticospinal tracts — connect cortical areas with each other and with the spinal cord and deep structures.

Clinical significance

Demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis destroy white matter and impair conduction. White-matter lesions seen on MRI are associated with ageing, small-vessel disease, and cognitive decline.
