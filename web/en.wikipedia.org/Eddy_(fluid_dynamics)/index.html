Eddy (fluid dynamics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Eddies shed in the wake of an obstacle, forming a vortex street.

In fluid dynamics, an eddy is the swirling of a fluid and the reverse current created when the fluid is in a turbulent flow regime. The moving fluid creates a space devoid of downstream-flowing fluid on the downstream side of an object; fluid behind the obstacle flows into the void, creating a swirl of fluid on each edge of the obstacle, followed by a short reverse flow behind it.

Formation

Eddies form wherever flow separates from a surface or boundary — behind rocks in streams, at river bends, downstream of bridge piers, and in the atmosphere behind mountain ranges. In turbulence, energy cascades from large eddies to progressively smaller ones, as described in Kolmogorov's theory, until it is dissipated by viscosity as heat at the smallest (Kolmogorov) scales.

Oceanic and atmospheric eddies

Mesoscale ocean eddies, tens to hundreds of kilometres across, transport heat, salt and nutrients and persist for months or years; rings pinched off from the Gulf Stream are prominent examples. In the atmosphere, eddies range from dust devils to the great rotating systems of cyclones and anticyclones, and the persistent eddies of the general circulation.

Applications

Engineers account for eddies in designing bridges, aircraft and pipelines; eddy currents of air behind buildings affect ventilation and pollutant dispersal, and unwanted eddy formation increases drag and noise.
