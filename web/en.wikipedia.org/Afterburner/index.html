Afterburner

An afterburner (or reheat in British English) is an additional component present in some jet engines, mostly military supersonic aircraft. Its purpose is to provide an increase in thrust, usually for supersonic flight, takeoff, and combat situations. Afterburning injects fuel into the hot exhaust gases in the jet pipe downstream of the turbine and burns it with the remaining oxygen, dramatically increasing exhaust velocity and thrust.

Operation

Because the turbine must not exceed its temperature limits, much of the oxygen entering the engine — especially in low-bypass turbofans — passes through unconsumed. The afterburner exploits this: fuel is sprayed from fuel bars into the core and bypass streams and ignited, raising gas temperature to around 1,700 °C. A variable-area convergent-divergent nozzle opens to accommodate the greater volume flow. Full afterburner typically increases thrust by 50% or more, in some engines roughly doubling it.

Disadvantages

Afterburning is extremely fuel-inefficient — specific fuel consumption may rise three- to four-fold — and produces a long, bright exhaust flame and high infrared signature. It is therefore used sparingly: for takeoff at heavy weight, transonic acceleration, supersonic dash, and combat maneuvering. Wet-rated engines allow sustained use for minutes at a time.

History

Early work was done in Britain on the Power Jets W.2/700 and in the United States during the 1940s. Afterburners became standard on supersonic fighters of the 1950s such as the F-100 Super Sabre, and on the Concorde airliner ("reheat"), which used them for takeoff and transonic acceleration. Shock diamonds are often visible in afterburner plumes.
