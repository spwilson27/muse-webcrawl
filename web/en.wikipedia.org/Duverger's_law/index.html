Duverger's law

Duverger's law is a principle in political science holding that single-member districts using plurality-rule elections (first-past-the-post) tend to favour a two-party system, while proportional representation tends to favour multi-party systems.

Explanation

The principle was formulated by French sociologist Maurice Duverger in the 1950s. Two mechanisms drive the effect: a mechanical effect, in which third parties are systematically under-represented because their votes do not translate into seats, and a psychological effect, in which voters and donors abandon smaller parties to avoid "wasting" their votes.

Examples and exceptions

The United States and United Kingdom are commonly cited as illustrating the tendency, though both have significant regional and third parties. Countries using proportional systems, such as the Netherlands and Israel, sustain many parties. Critics note that social cleavages, federalism and district magnitude also shape party systems, so the "law" is better understood as a strong tendency rather than an iron rule.
