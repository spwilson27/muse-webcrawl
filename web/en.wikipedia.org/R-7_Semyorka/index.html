The R-7 Semyorka (Russian: Р-7 "Семёрка"; GRAU index 8K71) was a Soviet missile developed during the Cold War and the world's first intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM). First successfully flown over full range in August 1957, the R-7 is more famous as the ancestor of the most successful family of space launch vehicles in history: variants launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, and Vostok 1, carrying Yuri Gagarin as the first human in space. Its descendant, the Soyuz, remained in service into the 21st century.

Design

Designed under Sergei Korolev at OKB-1 to deliver a heavy early thermonuclear warhead to American targets, the R-7 used a distinctive "stage-and-a-half" configuration: a central core (Block A), powered by an RD-108 engine, surrounded by four tapered strap-on boosters (Blocks B, V, G, D) each powered by an RD-107, all ignited together on the ground. All engines burned liquid oxygen and kerosene. At launch, twenty main thrust chambers firing at once gave the vehicle its power. The strap-ons separated about two minutes into flight, tumbling away in a pattern known as the "Korolev cross."

As a weapon the R-7 was cumbersome: it required enormous fixed launch complexes, hours of preparation, and cryogenic fuelling shortly before launch, and its accuracy was poor. Only a handful of launch pads were ever built, at Baikonur and later Plesetsk. The improved R-7A served briefly in the early 1960s before being superseded by later ICBMs.

Space launch derivatives

The R-7's real legacy is in spaceflight. A modified R-7 launched Sputnik 1 on 4 October 1957. The addition of upper stages produced the Vostok (human spaceflight, 1961), Voskhod, Molniya, and the Soyuz family. The R-7 family became the most-flown and most reliable launch system ever built, with well over 1,900 launches across its variants, carrying cosmonauts and astronauts to Soviet, Russian, and international space stations for six decades.
