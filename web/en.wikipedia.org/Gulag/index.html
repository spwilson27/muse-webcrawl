Gulag


A corrective-labour camp

The Gulag was a system of forced labour camps in the Soviet Union. The word Gulag is an acronym for Glavnoe upravlenie lagerei (Главное управление лагерей), the "Main Administration of Camps", the government agency in charge of the system. The Gulag is recognised as a major instrument of political repression in the Soviet Union, housing political prisoners and criminals in harsh conditions.

The camps were established under Vladimir Lenin and reached their peak during Joseph Stalin's rule from the 1930s to the early 1950s. Estimates of the total number of people who passed through the system range from about 14 million to more than 18 million, with deaths estimated between roughly 1.5 and 2 million, from exhaustion, starvation, disease, cold, and execution.

History

Forced labour camps had precedents in Imperial Russia's katorga system. After the October Revolution, the Cheka established camps for "class enemies"; the Solovki camp in the White Sea, founded in 1923, became a prototype. In 1930 the camps were consolidated under the Gulag administration of the OGPU, later the NKVD.

During the Great Terror of 1936–38, arrests surged and camp populations swelled with officials, military officers, intellectuals, kulaks, ethnic minorities, and ordinary citizens caught by quota-driven repression. Prisoners built canals (the White Sea–Baltic Canal), railways, mines in Kolyma and Norilsk, and much of the Soviet Union's timber, coal, and gold output.

After Stalin

Following Stalin's death in 1953, Lavrentiy Beria issued an amnesty releasing many non-political prisoners, and Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956 denounced Stalin's crimes, accelerating releases and rehabilitations. The Gulag agency was formally abolished in 1960, though forced-labour colonies continued in modified form. The system's history entered public consciousness through Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn's The Gulag Archipelago (1973), based on his own imprisonment and testimony of survivors.

Conditions

Prisoners (known as zeks) worked up to 14 hours a day in extreme climates, with rations tied to fulfilment of work norms—an arrangement that systematically starved the weakest. Camps varied widely, from relatively survivable transit camps to the lethal gold mines of Kolyma, sometimes called the "land of death".
