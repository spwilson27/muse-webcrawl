Prior restraint

Prior restraint is government action that prohibits speech or other expression before the speech happens, typically through a licensing scheme, an administrative order, or a court injunction against publication. It is distinguished from subsequent punishment, in which a speaker is punished after publication. In United States constitutional law, prior restraint is considered an especially serious infringement of the First Amendment, and government attempts at it bear a "heavy presumption" of unconstitutionality.

United States doctrine

The foundational case is Near v. Minnesota (1931), in which the Supreme Court struck down a state law allowing courts to shut down "malicious, scandalous and defamatory" newspapers as a public nuisance. Chief Justice Hughes allowed only narrow exceptions, such as preventing the publication of troop sailing dates in wartime or obscenity. In New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), the Court refused to enjoin publication of the Pentagon Papers, holding that the government had not met its burden of justification. In Nebraska Press Association v. Stuart (1976), the Court struck down a gag order on reporting about a criminal trial, setting out a demanding three-part test. Licensing schemes must also contain strict procedural safeguards, as in Freedman v. Maryland (1965).

Other jurisdictions

Most liberal democracies restrict prior restraint in some form, though the balance differs: British courts historically granted pre-publication injunctions more readily, including the controversial "super-injunctions" of the 2000s, while the European Convention on Human Rights protects expression under Article 10 subject to proportionate restrictions. Licensing of printers and pre-publication censorship were central targets of early press-freedom theorists from John Milton (Areopagitica, 1644) onward.
