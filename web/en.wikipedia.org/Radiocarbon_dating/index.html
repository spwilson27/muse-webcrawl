Radiocarbon dating

Radiocarbon dating (also referred to as carbon dating or carbon-14 dating) is a method for determining the age of an object containing organic material by using the properties of radiocarbon, a radioactive isotope of carbon.

Small samples are analysed by accelerator mass spectrometry in modern radiocarbon laboratories.

The method was developed in the late 1940s at the University of Chicago by Willard Libby, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work in 1960. It is based on the fact that radiocarbon (14C) is constantly being created in the Earth's atmosphere by the interaction of cosmic rays with atmospheric nitrogen. The resulting radiocarbon combines with atmospheric oxygen to form radioactive carbon dioxide, which is incorporated into plants by photosynthesis; animals then acquire 14C by eating the plants.

Principles

When an organism dies, it stops exchanging carbon with the biosphere, and the 14C trapped within it begins to decay to nitrogen-14 with a half-life of about 5,730 years. By measuring the remaining proportion of 14C relative to stable 12C in a sample, and comparing it with the expected level in living tissue, the time elapsed since death can be calculated.

Calibration

Because atmospheric 14C levels have varied over time — owing to changes in solar activity, the Earth's magnetic field, fossil-fuel burning (the Suess effect), and nuclear weapons testing (the bomb pulse) — raw radiocarbon ages are converted to calendar ages using calibration curves such as IntCal, built from tree rings, corals, and lake sediments.

Uses and limits

Radiocarbon dating is the principal method for dating archaeological and geological material up to about 50,000–60,000 years old. Landmark applications include dating the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Shroud of Turin, and the peopling of the Americas. Samples can be contaminated by younger or older carbon, so careful pretreatment is essential.
