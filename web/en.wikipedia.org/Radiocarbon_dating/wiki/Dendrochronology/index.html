Dendrochronology

Annual growth rings in a tree trunk form the raw material of dendrochronology.

Dendrochronology (from Greek dendron, "tree"; khronos, "time"; and logos, "study") is the scientific method of dating tree rings, also called growth rings, to the exact year they were formed. It is used to determine the age of wooden objects and buildings, to reconstruct past climates, and to calibrate the radiocarbon dating timescale.

The method rests on the observation that trees in the same region produce rings of characteristic width that vary with the growing conditions of each year: wide rings in favourable years, narrow rings in droughts or cold spells. By cross-matching overlapping ring patterns from living trees, old timbers and preserved subfossil wood, scientists extend continuous master chronologies thousands of years into the past.

History

The astronomer Andrew Ellicott Douglass, seeking evidence linking sunspots to climate, formalised crossdating in the early 20th century at the University of Arizona, where he founded the Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research. In 1929 Douglass's chronology dated the great pueblos of the American Southwest, demonstrating the power of the method.

Applications

Dendrochronology provides exact construction dates for historic buildings, panel paintings and wooden artefacts. Dendroclimatology uses ring widths and densities to reconstruct past temperature and rainfall. Tree-ring chronologies are also the backbone of the IntCal calibration curves that convert radiocarbon ages into calendar ages, and they can record the signatures of volcanic eruptions and cosmic-ray events.
