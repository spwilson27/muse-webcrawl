Willard Libby

Willard Libby developed radiocarbon dating, transforming archaeology and Quaternary science.

Willard Frank Libby (December 17, 1908 – September 8, 1980) was an American physical chemist noted for his role in the 1949 development of radiocarbon dating, a process that revolutionised archaeology and geology. For this work he was honoured with the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960.

Libby was born in Grand Valley, Colorado, and educated at the University of California, Berkeley, where he earned his doctorate in 1933. During the Second World War he worked on the Manhattan Project at Columbia University, contributing to the gaseous diffusion method for uranium enrichment. After the war he moved to the University of Chicago, where he pursued the idea that naturally occurring carbon-14 could date organic remains.

Radiocarbon dating

Building on the discovery of carbon-14 in 1940, Libby and his team, including James Arnold and Ernest Anderson, showed in 1949 that the isotope's decay could date samples of known age — ancient Egyptian wood, for example — with remarkable accuracy. Their "curve of knowns" validated the method, which was rapidly adopted worldwide. Libby published the first edition of his book Radiocarbon Dating in 1952.

Later career

From 1954 to 1959 Libby served as a commissioner on the United States Atomic Energy Commission. He then returned to academic life at the University of California, Los Angeles, where he directed the Institute of Geophysics and Planetary Physics and worked on tritium in water, fallout monitoring and environmental science.
