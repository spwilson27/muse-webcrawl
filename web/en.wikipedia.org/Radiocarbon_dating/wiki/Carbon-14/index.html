Carbon-14

Carbon-14 is formed in the upper atmosphere and decays into nitrogen-14 with a half-life of about 5,730 years.

Carbon-14 (14C) is a radioactive isotope of carbon with an atomic nucleus containing 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Its presence in organic materials is the basis of the radiocarbon dating method pioneered by Willard Libby and colleagues at the University of Chicago in the late 1940s, for dating archaeologically and geologically important carbonaceous materials up to about 60,000 years old.

Carbon-14 is produced in the upper atmosphere by the interaction of cosmic-ray neutrons with nitrogen-14. It oxidises to radioactive carbon dioxide, which is incorporated by plants through photosynthesis and passes through the food chain, so that all living organisms maintain a small, nearly constant proportion of carbon-14 relative to the stable isotopes carbon-12 and carbon-13 — about one part per trillion. When an organism dies, the carbon-14 decays by beta emission back to nitrogen-14 and is not replenished, so the declining ratio clocks the time since death.

Discovery

Carbon-14 was discovered on February 27, 1940, by Martin Kamen and Sam Ruben at the University of California Radiation Laboratory in Berkeley, who produced it by bombarding graphite in a cyclotron. Libby then recognised that the naturally occurring isotope could be used as a clock; radiocarbon dating earned him the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960.

Uses

Beyond archaeology and geology, carbon-14 is used as a radioactive tracer in medicine and biochemistry — for example, in the urea breath test for Helicobacter pylori infection — and in studies of carbon cycling in oceans and soils. Short-lived spikes in atmospheric carbon-14 from solar storms, called Miyake events, provide absolute dating markers in tree rings.
