The Spirit of Law (French: De l'esprit des lois, originally De l'esprit des loix) is a treatise on political theory and comparative law by the French philosopher Montesquieu, first published anonymously in 1748. The product of some twenty years of work, it is one of the most influential books of the Enlightenment, best known for articulating the theory of the separation of powers that underlies many modern constitutions, including that of the United States.

Argument

Montesquieu holds that laws should be adapted to the people, climate, terrain, economy, and customs of each nation — its general "spirit." He classifies governments as republics (animated by virtue), monarchies (by honor), and despotisms (by fear), and analyzes how each type tends to preserve or corrupt itself. He famously argues that commerce softens manners, that climate shapes social institutions (a theory much debated ever since), and that slavery is contrary to natural law.

Separation of powers

In Book XI, drawing on his reading of the English constitution and of John Locke, Montesquieu distinguishes the legislative, executive, and judicial powers and argues that liberty is secure only when no single person or body exercises more than one of them: "When the legislative and executive powers are united in the same person, or in the same body of magistrates, there can be no liberty." This doctrine profoundly influenced the American Founders — it is the most cited authority in The Federalist Papers after the Bible — and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man.

Reception

Published in Geneva to evade French censorship, the book was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books in 1751, prompting Montesquieu's Defense of the Spirit of Laws (1750). It ran through dozens of editions in his lifetime and shaped political thought across Europe and the Americas; Catherine the Great drew on it for her Nakaz.
