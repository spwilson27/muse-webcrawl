Sterilization

An autoclave sterilises instruments with pressurised steam.

Sterilization (or sterilisation) refers to any process that removes, kills or deactivates all forms of life — including bacteria, viruses, fungi and spores — present in or on a surface, substance or fluid. The term also has distinct meanings in medicine (surgical contraception) and economics, summarised below.

Microbiological sterilization

Sterilization differs from disinfection, which reduces but does not eliminate all viable microorganisms; bacterial endospores are the most resistant forms and serve as the benchmark for sterility assurance. Common methods include moist heat in an autoclave (typically 121 °C under pressure), dry heat, filtration, ionising radiation (gamma rays or electron beams), and chemical sterilants such as ethylene oxide. Sterile technique underpins surgery, pharmaceutical manufacture and laboratory work.

Reproductive sterilization

In medicine, sterilization also denotes procedures that permanently end fertility, such as vasectomy and tubal ligation. These are among the most widely used methods of contraception worldwide. The 20th century saw extensive programmes of compulsory sterilization under eugenic policies in several countries — practices now widely condemned, and the subject of official apologies and compensation schemes.

Economics

In monetary economics, "sterilisation" describes central-bank operations that offset the effect of foreign-exchange intervention on the domestic money supply.
