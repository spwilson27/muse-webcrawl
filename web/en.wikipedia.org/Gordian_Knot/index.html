Gordian Knot

The Gordian Knot is a legend of ancient Phrygia associated with Alexander the Great in Gordium, the capital of Phrygia. It is often used as a metaphor for an intractable problem (untying an impossibly tangled knot) solved easily by finding an approach to the problem that renders the perceived constraints of the problem moot ("cutting the Gordian knot").

The legend

According to the myth, the Phrygians, who were without a king, were told by an oracle that the next man to enter the city driving an ox-cart should become their king. A peasant farmer named Gordias drove into town on an ox-cart and was immediately declared king. In gratitude to Zeus, Gordias dedicated his ox-cart to the god, tying it up in the temple with an intricate knot of cornel bark.

An oracle declared that whoever could untie the knot would rule all of Asia. In 333 BC, Alexander the Great, wintering at Gordium, attempted the knot. In the most famous version of the story, Alexander simply cut through the knot with his sword, declaring that it did not matter how the knot was loosed. Other sources say he removed the linchpin of the yoke, exposing the ends of the cord and allowing him to untie it.

Metaphorical use

"Cutting the Gordian knot" has come to mean solving a seemingly intractable problem by bold, decisive action outside the expected rules, and the phrase is used widely in law, business, and politics.
