Murder, Inc.

Murder, Inc. was the name given by the press to an enforcement group of organized crime syndicates active in the 1930s and 1940s in New York City. It served as the enforcement arm of the National Crime Syndicate, carrying out contract killings ordered by syndicate bosses such as Louis "Lepke" Buchalter and Albert Anastasia. The group is estimated to have committed anywhere from 400 to 1,000 murders during its existence.

The killers, many drawn from Brooklyn's Jewish and Italian gangs, operated out of a candy store in Brownsville that stayed open around the clock. They used a variety of methods designed to make deaths appear accidental or to leave no traceable evidence.

Downfall

The organization unravelled beginning in 1940, when hitman Abe "Kid Twist" Reles became a government informant. His testimony sent Buchalter — the only major American mob boss ever executed by the state — and several others, including Harry "Pittsburgh Phil" Strauss and Martin "Buggsy" Goldstein, to the electric chair. Reles himself died in 1941 after falling from a window of the Half Moon Hotel in Coney Island while under police guard, an event that gave rise to the phrase "the canary who sang but couldn't fly."
