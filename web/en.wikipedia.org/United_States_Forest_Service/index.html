United States Forest Service

The United States Forest Service is an agency within the U.S. Department of Agriculture that administers the nation's 154 national forests and 20 national grasslands, covering about 193 million acres (780,000 km²) of land. Founded in 1905 under President Theodore Roosevelt, with conservationist Gifford Pinchot as its first chief, the agency manages public lands for multiple uses including timber, recreation, grazing, watershed protection, and wildlife habitat, and it is a world leader in wildland fire management and forest research.

History

Federal forest reserves were first authorised by the Forest Reserve Act of 1891 and initially administered by the Department of the Interior. In 1905 the reserves were transferred to the Department of Agriculture's Bureau of Forestry, renamed the Forest Service. Pinchot shaped its philosophy of "the greatest good for the greatest number in the long run" — conservation through wise use rather than strict preservation, a stance that put the agency at odds with preservationists such as John Muir.

Organisation and activities

The agency is organised into regions, forests, and ranger districts, with research stations and the Forest Products Laboratory. Its responsibilities include maintaining thousands of miles of trails and roads, issuing grazing and timber permits, managing wilderness areas jointly with other agencies, and suppressing wildfires — an activity that now consumes more than half its budget. Smokey Bear, created in 1944, is its famous fire-prevention mascot.

Controversies

The Forest Service has faced recurring debate over logging of old-growth forests, road building, fire policy (the long-standing practice of total suppression contributed to fuel accumulation), and the rising cost and danger of wildfire seasons lengthened by climate change.
