Anthracite, also known as hard coal and black coal, is a hard, compact variety of coal with a submetallic lustre. It has the highest carbon content, the fewest impurities and the highest energy density of all types of coal, representing the highest rank of coalification — the metamorphosed end-product of the transformation of peat through lignite and bituminous coal under prolonged heat and pressure.

Properties

Anthracite typically contains 86–97% fixed carbon and only 2–8% volatile matter. It is hard and brittle, with a conchoidal fracture, and burns slowly with a pale blue, smokeless flame and intense heat, making it historically prized for domestic heating and steam raising. Because of its low volatile content it is harder to ignite than bituminous coal and requires a strong draught.

Occurrence and production

Anthracite forms where coal seams have been subjected to intense deformation and heat, as in folded mountain belts. Historic production centres include the Coal Region of north-eastern Pennsylvania (long the world's chief source, which fuelled the industrialisation of the north-eastern United States), the South Wales Coalfield in Britain, and the Donets Basin in Ukraine. Today the largest producers include China, Russia, Vietnam, Ukraine, South Africa, Spain and the United States, though anthracite accounts for only around 1% of world coal reserves and production.

Uses

Uses include domestic and industrial heating, water filtration (crushed anthracite is a standard filter medium), electrodes and carbon products, and some metallurgical applications as a reductant and substitute for coke. In the 19th and early 20th centuries anthracite heated homes across the eastern United States and powered locomotives and warships where its smokelessness was valued. Pennsylvania's anthracite industry declined after the Second World War as oil and gas took over home heating, leaving a landscape of culm banks and former mining towns.
