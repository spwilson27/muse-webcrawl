Racemic mixture

A racemic mixture contains equal amounts of left- and right-handed enantiomers of a chiral molecule.

In chemistry, a racemic mixture, or racemate, is one that has equal amounts of left- and right-handed enantiomers of a chiral molecule or salt. Racemic mixtures are rare in nature, but many compounds are produced industrially as racemates. The first known racemic mixture was "racemic acid", which Louis Pasteur found to be a mixture of the two enantiomeric isomers of tartaric acid.

A racemic mixture is optically inactive: although the individual enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light, equal quantities of the two forms cancel each other's rotation, giving no net optical activity. A sample with only one enantiomer is said to be enantiopure or optically pure.

History

In 1848 Louis Pasteur separated the crystals of sodium ammonium tartrate into two mirror-image forms by hand, using tweezers, and showed that solutions of each rotated polarised light in opposite directions while their mixture was inactive. This experiment founded the science of stereochemistry and demonstrated the molecular basis of chirality.

Resolution

Separating a racemate into its enantiomers — a process called resolution — is important because enantiomers frequently differ in biological activity. For example, one enantiomer of thalidomide was sedative while the other was teratogenic. Resolution methods include crystallisation of diastereomeric salts with a chiral resolving agent, chiral chromatography, and kinetic resolution using enzymes or chiral catalysts.

Pharmaceutical significance

Many drugs are chiral, and regulatory agencies increasingly require that the activity of each enantiomer be characterised. Some medicines are sold as single enantiomers (such as esomeprazole, the S-enantiomer of omeprazole), a strategy known as a "chiral switch".
