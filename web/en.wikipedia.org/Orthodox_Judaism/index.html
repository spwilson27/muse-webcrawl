Orthodox Judaism

Prayer at the Western Wall, Jerusalem.

Orthodox Judaism is the collective term for the traditionalist branches of Judaism that maintain the belief that the Torah — both the Written Law and the Oral Torah as interpreted by the rabbis — was divinely revealed to Moses at Mount Sinai and must be observed as binding law (halakha). It is one of the major movements of modern Judaism, alongside Conservative, Reform, and Reconstructionist Judaism.

Beliefs and practice

Orthodox life is structured by observance of the commandments (mitzvot): the dietary laws of kashrut, the Sabbath, festivals, daily prayer, Torah study, and laws of family purity. Rabbinic authority and precedent govern interpretation, and changes to established practice are approached cautiously. Maimonides' Thirteen Principles of Faith are widely, though not universally, treated as a statement of core doctrine.

Branches

The movement ranges from Modern Orthodoxy, which seeks to combine full halakhic observance with engagement in secular education and modern society, to Haredi (often called "ultra-Orthodox") Judaism, which emphasises insulation from secular culture; Haredi Judaism includes the Hasidic dynasties and the Lithuanian (Litvish) yeshiva world. The term "Orthodox" itself arose in the nineteenth century, as traditional communities defined themselves against the emerging Reform movement in Germany.

Demographics

Orthodox Jews number roughly two to three million worldwide, concentrated in Israel and the United States. High fertility rates — Haredi families commonly have six or more children — mean the Orthodox share of world Jewry is growing rapidly, and Orthodox majorities are projected in several countries within decades.
