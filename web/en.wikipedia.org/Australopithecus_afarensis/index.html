Australopithecus afarensis

Australopithecus afarensis

Temporal range: Pliocene, c. 3.9–2.9 million years ago

Known from: Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania

Famous specimen: "Lucy" (AL 288-1), found 1974

Australopithecus afarensis is an extinct species of australopithecine that lived in East Africa about 3.9 to 2.9 million years ago, during the Pliocene. It is one of the best-known early hominins because of the abundance of its fossils, and is widely considered a likely ancestor or close relative of the genus Homo.

Discoveries

Fossils were recovered beginning in the 1930s, but the species gained prominence with finds at Hadar, Ethiopia in the 1970s, including the 40%-complete skeleton nicknamed "Lucy", discovered by Donald Johanson's team in 1974, and the "First Family" group of individuals. At Laetoli, Tanzania, Mary Leakey's team excavated footprint trails preserved in volcanic ash some 3.66 million years old, widely attributed to this species. The species was formally named in 1978 by Johanson, Tim White, and Yves Coppens, taking its name from the Afar region.

Anatomy and behaviour

A. afarensis combined ape-like features — a small braincase (about 380–550 cm³), projecting face, and curved finger bones suggesting climbing — with clear adaptations to upright walking in the pelvis, knee, and foot. Adults stood roughly 1.1–1.5 m tall, and males were considerably larger than females. The Laetoli footprints show a bipedal gait with an arched foot, though the extent of its tree-climbing remains debated.
