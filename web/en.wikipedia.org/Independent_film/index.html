Independent film

An independent film, independent movie, indie film, or indie movie is a feature film or short film that is produced outside the major film studio system in addition to being produced and distributed by independent entertainment companies (or, in some cases, distributed by major companies). Independent films are sometimes distinguishable by their content and style and by the way in which the filmmakers' personal artistic vision is realized.

History

Independent production grew out of resistance to the control exercised by the early Hollywood trusts and, later, the studio system. The 1948 United States Supreme Court decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc. weakened studio control over exhibition and helped open space for independents. Directors such as John Cassavetes pioneered a personal, low-budget mode of filmmaking in the 1950s and 1960s.

The indie boom

In the 1990s, independent cinema achieved notable commercial success: films such as sex, lies, and videotape (1989), Reservoir Dogs (1992), Clerks (1994), and Pulp Fiction (1994) demonstrated that low-budget films could reach large audiences. The Sundance Film Festival became a key marketplace and showcase. Major studios responded by creating or acquiring specialty divisions such as Miramax and Fox Searchlight.

Financing and distribution today

Advances in digital cameras and editing dramatically lowered production costs in the 2000s, while film festivals, video on demand, and streaming platforms widened distribution possibilities, even as theatrical release remained difficult for small films.
