Lincoln is a cathedral city and county town of Lincolnshire in the East Midlands of England. The city sits on the River Witham in a gap in the Lincolnshire Wolds, and grew from the Roman town of Lindum Colonia, itself established beside a legionary fortress in the 1st century AD. Its historic core is dominated by Lincoln Cathedral and Lincoln Castle, both dating from the decades after the Norman Conquest.

History

After Roman rule, Lincoln was one of the Five Boroughs of the Danelaw. William the Conqueror ordered Lincoln Castle built in 1068, and Bishop Remigius began the cathedral in 1072; the rebuilt Gothic cathedral, consecrated in its present form through the 13th century, was for a time — by some calculations, from about 1311 until the spire collapsed in 1548 — the tallest building in the world, surpassing the Great Pyramid of Giza. The city prospered on the medieval wool trade, declined, and then industrialised in the 19th century as a centre of heavy engineering: firms such as Ruston & Hornsby built steam engines and, during the First World War, some of the first tanks, earning Lincoln the nickname "Tank Town".

Landmarks

  • Lincoln Cathedral — one of Europe's finest Gothic buildings, housing a 1215 copy of Magna Carta in the adjacent castle vault.
  • Lincoln Castle — Norman castle with a Victorian prison; site of the battles of Lincoln in 1141 and 1217.
  • Steep Hill — a famously steep cobbled street of independent shops linking the cathedral quarter to the modern centre, winner of an Academy of Urbanism "Great Street" award.
  • The Brayford Pool — the old inland port, now a marina and leisure quarter beside the University of Lincoln (founded 1996).

Modern city

Lincoln is an agricultural, engineering, tourism and university city, with major employers in turbines and food processing. Its Christmas market, held around the cathedral from 1982, became one of Britain's best-known seasonal markets.
