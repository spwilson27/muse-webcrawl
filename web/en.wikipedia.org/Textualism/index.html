Textualism

Textualism is a formalist theory of statutory interpretation, holding that the ordinary meaning of a legal text should govern its interpretation, rather than inquiries into non-textual sources such as the intention of the legislature in passing the law or the problem the law was intended to remedy. A related but distinct theory, applied to constitutional interpretation, holds that a constitution should be construed according to its ordinary public meaning at the time of ratification.

Overview

Textualists argue that the words of an enacted text are the law, and that judges should not look beyond those words to legislative history, committee reports, or floor statements, which they regard as unreliable and manipulable evidence of meaning. In the United States, the theory is most closely associated with Justice Antonin Scalia, who championed it during his tenure on the Supreme Court, and with later jurists such as Justice Neil Gorsuch and Justice Amy Coney Barrett.

Principles

Core textualist principles include:

  • The ordinary meaning of words at the time of enactment controls.
  • Words must be read in context, including grammar, punctuation, and the structure of the statute as a whole (the "whole act rule").
  • Canons of construction, such as ejusdem generis and expressio unius, guide interpretation.
  • Legislative history should not be used to override or cloud a clear text.
  • Absurd results may be avoided, but only in narrow circumstances.

Textualism and originalism

Textualism is closely related to, but distinct from, originalism. Textualism focuses on the semantic meaning of the words used; originalism is a broader family of theories about fixing constitutional meaning at the time of adoption. Scalia described his own approach as "originalist textualism" or original public meaning originalism.

Criticism

Critics, including purposivists such as Justice Stephen Breyer, argue that rigid textualism can produce results divorced from legislative purpose, that words often lack a single "ordinary" meaning, and that legislative history is a legitimate aid to understanding. Textualists respond that purpose-based approaches invite judges to impose their own policy preferences.
