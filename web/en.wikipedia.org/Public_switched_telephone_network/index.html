Abbreviation: PSTN
Type: circuit-switched telephone network

Related: ISDN, SS7, VoIP

The public switched telephone network (PSTN) is the aggregate of the world's telephone networks, which are operated by national, regional, or local telephony operators. It provides infrastructure and services for public telecommunication. The PSTN consists of telephone lines, fibre-optic cables, microwave transmission links, cellular networks, communications satellites, and undersea telephone cables, all interconnected by switching centers so that most telephones can communicate with each other.

History

Commercial telephone networks began in the late 1870s following the inventions of Alexander Graham Bell and others. Early exchanges were connected manually by operators using cord boards; automatic electromechanical switches, such as the Strowger switch, progressively automated the system from the 1890s onward.

Architecture

The network is organised hierarchically. Subscribers connect to a local exchange over the local loop. Exchanges connect to a subscriber's line only for the duration of a call — the defining feature of circuit switching. Digitalisation from the 1960s onward replaced analogue trunks with digital transmission, using pulse-code modulation at a standard rate of 64 kbit/s per voice channel, and out-of-band signalling systems culminating in Signalling System No. 7 (SS7).

Regulation

In most countries a government agency regulates the PSTN, and technical standards are set largely by the ITU-T. The E.163/E.164 standard defines the international numbering plan of country codes and national numbers.

Decline

Since the early 2000s, fixed-line subscriptions have fallen in many countries as users migrate to mobile telephony and Voice over IP services. Several national operators have announced plans to switch off their legacy PSTN entirely, moving all voice services onto IP-based networks.
