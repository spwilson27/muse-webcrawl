Fideism

Fideism is the position in the philosophy of religion that religious faith is independent of, and in some accounts superior to or even hostile to, human reason. Fideists hold that reason is incapable of establishing the truth of fundamental religious doctrines, and that belief must rest on faith alone. The term derives from the Latin fides, "faith".

Forms

Moderate fideists hold only that truths of faith lie beyond the competence of reason, so that reason can neither prove nor disprove them. More radical fideists argue that reason is positively misleading in matters of religion, and that apparent conflicts between faith and reason should be resolved in favour of faith. Blaise Pascal is often associated with a fideistic outlook in his appeal to the wager and to the "reasons of the heart". Søren Kierkegaard's notion of a "leap of faith" in the face of objective uncertainty is another classic fideistic theme, although scholars debate how far these thinkers should be classified as fideists.

History and reception

Tertullian's famous paradox — "I believe because it is absurd" (as popularly paraphrased) — is an early expression of the fideistic spirit. In the Catholic tradition, fideism in its strong form was condemned in the nineteenth century, notably in the encyclicals of Gregory XVI and in the decrees of the First Vatican Council, which taught that God can be known with certainty by reason from created things. In Protestant thought, fideistic tendencies appear in some strands of Reformed epistemology and in the dialectical theology of Karl Barth.

Criticism

Critics argue that fideism cannot explain how to choose between competing faiths without some rational criteria, and that abandoning reason risks making religious belief arbitrary.
