History of architecture

The history of architecture traces the changes in architecture through various traditions, regions, overarching stylistic trends and dates. The beginnings of architecture lie in the Neolithic period, when human beings first built permanent shelters; it has developed alongside building technology, materials, religion and social organisation ever since.

Prehistoric and ancient architecture

Monumental building began with structures such as the megalithic temples of Malta and Stonehenge. Ancient Egyptian architecture produced the pyramids of Giza (c. 2600–2500 BC) and great temple complexes at Karnak and Luxor, built of massive stone with post-and-lintel construction. In Mesopotamia, mud-brick ziggurats rose over cities, while the Indus Valley produced planned cities of fired brick.

Classical antiquity

Greek architecture developed the classical orders — Doric, Ionic and Corinthian — exemplified by the Parthenon in Athens (447–432 BC), where refined proportions and optical corrections such as entasis were applied. Roman architecture combined the orders with the arch, vault and dome, using concrete to span vast interiors such as the Pantheon (c. AD 125), and producing aqueducts, baths, basilicas and amphitheatres like the Colosseum.

Byzantine and Islamic architecture

Byzantine builders crowned basilicas with domes on pendentives, most famously Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (AD 537). Islamic architecture, arising from the 7th century, developed the mosque with its dome, minarets, mihrab and geometric and calligraphic ornament, from the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem to the great mosques of Córdoba, Samarkand and Istanbul, and palaces such as the Alhambra.

Romanesque and Gothic

Romanesque architecture (c. 1000–1200) revived round arches and thick masonry walls. Gothic architecture, beginning at the abbey of Saint-Denis in the 1140s, introduced the pointed arch, ribbed vault and flying buttress, allowing taller, lighter structures filled with stained glass, as at Chartres, Reims, Salisbury and Cologne cathedrals.

Renaissance and Baroque

The Renaissance, codified by Alberti and Brunelleschi in 15th-century Florence, revived classical orders and mathematical proportion; Brunelleschi's dome for Florence Cathedral and later Palladio's villas influenced all of Europe. Baroque architecture of the 17th and 18th centuries added drama, movement and integration with painting and sculpture, seen in St Peter's Basilica and Versailles. Rococo followed with lighter, more playful ornament.

Neoclassicism and the Industrial Revolution

The 18th and 19th centuries saw Neoclassical revivals and then eclectic historicism. Iron, steel and glass transformed construction: Paxton's Crystal Palace (1851) demonstrated prefabricated iron and glass, while railway stations, bridges and markets exploited the new materials. The invention of the safety elevator and steel framing enabled the first skyscrapers in Chicago and New York.

Modernism

Modern architecture rejected historical ornament in favour of functionalism and the honest expression of materials — reinforced concrete, steel and glass. The Bauhaus, Le Corbusier (with his Five Points of a new architecture), Mies van der Rohe and Frank Lloyd Wright shaped an International Style that dominated after 1945. Brutalism followed in the 1950s–70s with monumental exposed concrete.

Postmodernism and contemporary architecture

Postmodernism, from the 1960s, reintroduced ornament, colour and historical quotation, reacting against modernist austerity. Deconstructivism, high-tech architecture and blobitecture followed. Contemporary practice is shaped by computer-aided design, parametric modelling and sustainability, producing landmarks from the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao to the Burj Khalifa, the world's tallest building (2010).
