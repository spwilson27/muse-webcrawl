Diamictite

Diamictite (from Ancient Greek dia-, "through", and miktós, "mixed") is a type of poorly sorted sedimentary rock composed of a wide range of unsorted to poorly sorted clasts, from clay to boulders, suspended in a muddy matrix. The term was proposed by Flint and others in 1960 as a purely descriptive, non-genetic term for such rocks, avoiding the genetic implications of older terms such as tillite, which implies a glacial origin.

Description

Diamictites are characterized by their very poor sorting: large clasts, often of gravel, cobble, or boulder size, are scattered through a finer-grained matrix of sand, silt, or mud. The clasts may be angular or rounded and of varied lithology. Because the term is descriptive rather than genetic, diamictites can form in many environments, including glacial, marine, lacustrine, volcanic, and tectonic settings.

Origins

Many diamictites are indeed of glacial origin, deposited by ice sheets and glaciers as till, or dropped from melting icebergs into marine or lake sediments as dropstones. Others form by submarine debris flows, slumping, or olistostromes along active continental margins. The famous Precambrian diamictites, such as those of the Marinoan and Sturtian glaciations, are key evidence for the Snowball Earth hypothesis.
