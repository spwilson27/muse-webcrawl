Fiberglass

Fiberglass (American English) or fibreglass (British English) is a common type of fiber-reinforced plastic using glass fiber. The fibers may be randomly arranged, flattened into a sheet called a chopped strand mat, or woven into glass cloth. The plastic matrix is usually a thermoset polymer such as polyester, vinyl ester or epoxy resin. The resulting material, properly known as glass-reinforced plastic (GRP), is strong, lightweight, non-conductive and relatively inexpensive.

History

Glass fibers have been produced for centuries, but mass production began in the 1930s when researchers at Owens-Illinois and Corning developed practical methods of drawing molten glass into fine filaments; their joint venture, Owens Corning, was formed in 1938 and popularised the "Fiberglas" trademark. Combined with the new synthetic resins, the material found wide use during the Second World War and afterwards.

Properties and uses

Glass fibers have high tensile strength and good resistance to heat, moisture and chemicals, though they are brittle on their own; embedded in resin they form a composite with an excellent strength-to-weight ratio. Fiberglass is used for boat hulls, car bodies, aircraft components, surfboards, swimming pools, water tanks, roofing, pipes, and wind turbine blades. In loose wool form it is a common thermal and acoustic insulation material in buildings. E-glass is the most common grade; higher-performance S-glass is used in demanding aerospace and military applications.

Health and safety

Fine glass fibers can irritate the skin, eyes and respiratory tract, so protective equipment is used when cutting or handling insulation. Modern glass wool products use fibers engineered to dissolve more readily in lung fluid, reducing biopersistence concerns.
