Cardiomyopathy

Cardiomyopathy is a group of diseases of the heart muscle (myocardium) in which the heart becomes enlarged, thickened, or stiffened, impairing its ability to pump blood. Cardiomyopathies can lead to heart failure, arrhythmias, and sudden cardiac death, and are a leading indication for heart transplantation.

Types

The main types are:

  • Dilated cardiomyopathy — the ventricles enlarge and weaken; causes include genetic mutations, alcohol, viral myocarditis, and chemotherapy.
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — abnormal thickening of the muscle, usually from sarcomere gene mutations; a common cause of sudden death in young athletes.
  • Restrictive cardiomyopathy — the walls stiffen, often from infiltrative diseases such as amyloidosis or sarcoidosis.
  • Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy — fibrofatty replacement of muscle, typically of the right ventricle, causing arrhythmias.
  • Other forms include Takotsubo ("stress") cardiomyopathy and peripartum cardiomyopathy.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on echocardiography, cardiac MRI, ECG, and increasingly genetic testing, with family screening recommended for inherited forms. Treatment addresses heart failure (with standard drug therapy), arrhythmias (sometimes with an implantable defibrillator), and the underlying cause — for example, abstinence in alcoholic cardiomyopathy or specific therapy for amyloidosis. Advanced disease may require mechanical support or transplant.
