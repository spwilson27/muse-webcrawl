Global illumination

Global illumination (GI) is a group of algorithms used in 3D computer graphics that add more realistic lighting to rendered scenes. Such algorithms take into account not only the light that comes directly from a light source (direct illumination), but also light that has been reflected by other surfaces (indirect illumination), including effects such as colour bleeding, caustics, and soft ambient occlusion.

Algorithms

The rendering equation, introduced by James Kajiya in 1986, gives the mathematical framework for global illumination. Path tracing solves it by Monte Carlo integration, tracing rays from the camera and bouncing them through the scene; bidirectional path tracing and Metropolis light transport improve convergence for difficult lighting configurations. Radiosity, developed at Cornell in 1984, is a finite-element method well suited to diffuse interreflection, while photon mapping (Henrik Wann Jensen, 1996) handles caustics efficiently by storing light particles in a photon map.

Real-time techniques

Historically, global illumination was limited to offline rendering for film because of its cost. Games approximated it with precomputed lightmaps and ambient occlusion. Modern GPUs and APIs supporting hardware ray tracing, together with techniques such as voxel cone tracing, irradiance probes, and screen-space GI, have brought forms of dynamic global illumination into real-time rendering.
