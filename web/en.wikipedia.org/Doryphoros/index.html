Doryphoros

The Doryphoros (Ancient Greek: Δορυφόρος, "Spear-Bearer") is a Greek sculpture created by Polykleitos around 440 BC. It depicts a solidly built, muscular yet athletic young nude warrior, originally holding a spear balanced on his left shoulder. It is one of the best-known classical Greek sculptures, and its central importance in Greek art derives from the fact that Polykleitos also wrote a treatise, the Canon, explaining the mathematical proportions of the ideal human body which the statue embodies.

Description

The statue stands in contrapposto: the figure's weight rests on the straight right leg, while the left leg is relaxed and trailing, its foot lifted. The pattern of tense and relaxed limbs is crossed — the engaged right leg corresponds to the relaxed right arm hanging at the side, while the bent left arm that held the spear corresponds to the free left leg. This arrangement creates a harmonious balance (Polykleitos called the principle symmetria) between rest and movement.

The Canon

In his lost treatise, Polykleitos held that beauty arises from the commensurability of all the parts of the body to one another and to the whole — famously, of finger to finger and of all of them to the hand, wrist, forearm, and upper arm. The Doryphoros, with its head one-seventh of its total height, was the practical demonstration of these ratios.

Copies

The bronze original is lost. The work survives in Roman marble copies, the finest of which was excavated at Pompeii in 1797 and is now in the National Archaeological Museum in Naples. A well-preserved copy is held by the Minneapolis Institute of Art. In 2012, an illustrated fragment of a copy of the Canon was reported found in a workshop in Pompeii.
