Catherine Susan "Kitty" Genovese (1935–1964) was a New York City bar manager who was raped and stabbed to death outside her apartment building in Kew Gardens, Queens, on 13 March 1964. Her murder became famous after a New York Times report claimed that dozens of witnesses had seen or heard the attack and done nothing, giving rise to research on the bystander effect.

The murder

Returning home from work at about 3 a.m., Genovese was attacked by Winston Moseley in the street. Her screams briefly drove him off, but he returned minutes later and attacked her again in a vestibule, where a neighbour eventually intervened and called police; she died on the way to hospital. Moseley was arrested days later and convicted; he died in prison in 2016.

The "38 witnesses" story

Two weeks after the murder, the Times reported that 38 respectable citizens had watched without calling police. The story became a parable of urban apathy, but later investigations showed it was substantially inaccurate: few witnesses saw or understood the attack, some did call police, and one man held Genovese as she died.

Legacy

The case inspired social psychologists John Darley and Bibb Latané to develop the theory of the bystander effect and diffusion of responsibility. It also contributed to the adoption of the 911 emergency telephone system in the United States, and has been repeatedly revisited in books, films and journalism correcting the original account.
