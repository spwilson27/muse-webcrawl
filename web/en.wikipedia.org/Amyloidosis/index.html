Amyloidosis

SpecialtyHaematology, cardiology, nephrology
SymptomsFatigue, weight loss, shortness of breath, swelling, numbness
TypesAL, AA, ATTR (hereditary and wild-type), others
DiagnosisTissue biopsy with Congo red staining, mass spectrometry

Amyloidosis is a group of diseases in which abnormal proteins, called amyloid fibrils, build up in tissue. The amyloid deposits are insoluble protein aggregates that disrupt the structure and function of organs, most commonly the heart, kidneys, liver, spleen, nervous system, and digestive tract.

Types and causes

More than 30 different proteins can form amyloid in humans. The most common types are:

  • AL amyloidosis (primary): caused by abnormal immunoglobulin light chains produced by clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow; the most common form in developed countries.
  • AA amyloidosis (secondary): caused by the acute-phase protein serum amyloid A in chronic inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis.
  • ATTR amyloidosis: caused by transthyretin, either hereditary (variant) or wild-type, the latter increasingly recognised in older adults with heart failure.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires biopsy — often of abdominal fat — showing amyloid deposits that stain with Congo red and display apple-green birefringence under polarised light. The precursor protein is identified by mass spectrometry or immunohistochemistry.

Treatment

Treatment aims to reduce production of the amyloid precursor protein and support affected organs: chemotherapy and stem-cell transplantation for AL, control of inflammation for AA, and transthyretin stabilisers (tafamidis) or gene-silencing therapies (patisiran, inotersen) for ATTR.

Relation to Alzheimer's disease

Amyloid-beta deposits in the brain are the hallmark of Alzheimer's disease and the subject of the amyloid hypothesis, though cerebral amyloidosis is usually treated as a separate condition from systemic amyloidosis.
