Xylem is one of the two types of transport tissue in vascular plants, the other being phloem. Its basic function is to transport water and dissolved inorganic nutrients from the roots upward to the stems and leaves, and it also provides mechanical support. The word derives from the Greek xylon, meaning "wood" — indeed, wood is mostly secondary xylem.

Structure

Xylem consists of hollow, water-conducting cells called tracheary elements: tracheids, found in nearly all vascular plants, and the more derived vessel elements characteristic of flowering plants. Both cell types are dead at maturity, with thick, lignified secondary walls. Xylem also contains fibres for support and living parenchyma cells for storage and lateral transport.

Primary and secondary xylem

Primary xylem forms during primary growth from the procambium, comprising protoxylem and metaxylem. In woody plants, the vascular cambium produces secondary xylem inward each growing season, forming growth rings used in dendrochronology. The older, darker, non-conducting heartwood contrasts with the younger conducting sapwood.

Water transport

Water moves through the xylem largely by the cohesion-tension mechanism: evaporation from leaf surfaces (transpiration) creates negative pressure that pulls continuous columns of water upward, sometimes more than 100 metres in the tallest trees. Xylem sap can also be pushed by root pressure, causing guttation.

Evolution

Xylem first appeared in early land plants more than 400 million years ago, in the Silurian–Devonian, and was a key innovation permitting tall terrestrial growth.
