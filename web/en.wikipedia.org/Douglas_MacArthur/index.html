Douglas MacArthur (26 January 1880 – 5 April 1964) was an American military leader who served as General of the Army for the United States, as well as a field marshal to the Philippine Army. He was Chief of Staff of the United States Army during the 1930s, commanded Allied forces in the Southwest Pacific in World War II, ruled occupied Japan as Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, and commanded United Nations forces in the Korean War until his relief by President Harry S. Truman in 1951.

Early career

The son of Lieutenant General Arthur MacArthur Jr., a Medal of Honor recipient, MacArthur graduated first in his West Point class of 1903. In the First World War he served with distinction as chief of staff and brigade commander of the 42nd "Rainbow" Division in France, becoming the youngest divisional commander in the AEF and one of America's most decorated soldiers. Between the wars he was Superintendent of West Point, the Army's youngest Chief of Staff (1930–35), and military adviser to the Philippine Commonwealth, where he was made field marshal.

World War II and Japan

Recalled to active duty in 1941, MacArthur commanded U.S. Army Forces in the Far East; after the fall of the Philippines he escaped to Australia vowing "I shall return". His Southwest Pacific Area command advanced from New Guinea along the north coast to liberate the Philippines in 1944–45. He accepted Japan's surrender aboard USS Missouri on 2 September 1945, then as SCAP oversaw Japan's demilitarisation, land reform, and the drafting of its new constitution during the occupation.

Korea and relief

In 1950 MacArthur took command of UN forces defending South Korea. His daring amphibious landing at Inchon in September 1950 reversed the war, but after Chinese intervention drove UN forces back, his public disagreements with Truman over expanding the war led the president to relieve him on 11 April 1951, a landmark assertion of civilian control of the military. MacArthur addressed a joint session of Congress, closing with the line "old soldiers never die; they just fade away". He spent his final years in New York as chairman of Remington Rand.
