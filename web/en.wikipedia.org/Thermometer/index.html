Thermometer

UsesMeasurement of temperature
RelatedThermoscope, thermocouple, pyrometer

A thermometer is a device that measures temperature or a temperature gradient (the degree of hotness or coldness of an object). A thermometer has two important elements: (1) a temperature sensor (e.g. the bulb of a mercury-in-glass thermometer or the pyrometric sensor in an infrared thermometer) in which some change occurs with a change in temperature; and (2) some means of converting this change into a numerical value (e.g. the visible scale that is marked on a mercury-in-glass thermometer or the digital readout on an infrared model). Thermometers are widely used in technology and industry to monitor processes, in meteorology, in medicine, and in scientific research.

History

The earliest device for gauging temperature was the thermoscope, developed around 1593 and credited to Galileo Galilei and his contemporaries; it showed changes in temperature but had no scale. Sanctorius added a scale in the early 17th century. Sealed liquid-in-glass thermometers were developed in Florence in the 1650s. Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit produced reliable mercury thermometers and the scale named after him in 1714, and Anders Celsius proposed his centigrade scale in 1742. Lord Kelvin later defined the absolute thermodynamic temperature scale.

Types

Primary thermometers determine temperature from a well-understood physical law, such as the speed of sound in a gas or the thermal noise voltage of a resistor. Secondary thermometers must be calibrated against fixed points; they include liquid-in-glass thermometers (mercury or alcohol), bimetallic strip thermometers, resistance thermometers and thermistors, thermocouples, infrared thermometers, and medical thermometers for measuring body temperature. Mercury thermometers have been phased out in many countries because of the toxicity of mercury.

Temperature scales and calibration

Thermometers are calibrated using fixed points on scales such as Celsius, Fahrenheit and kelvin, defined internationally by the International Temperature Scale of 1990 (ITS-90), which specifies reproducible phase-transition temperatures of pure substances, from the triple point of hydrogen to the freezing point of copper.
