Bacteremia is the presence of bacteria in the blood. Blood is normally a sterile environment, so detection of bacteria — usually by blood culture — is always clinically significant. Bacteremia is distinct from sepsis, which is the body's dysregulated response to infection; bacteremia may be present without sepsis, and sepsis may occur without detectable bacteremia.

Types and causes

Transient bacteremia follows everyday mucosal trauma such as toothbrushing or chewing, and after procedures such as dental extraction; the bacteria are cleared within minutes by the reticuloendothelial system in healthy people. Intermittent bacteremia occurs when bacteria periodically seed the blood from an undrained abscess or infected focus. Continuous bacteremia is characteristic of intravascular infections, above all infective endocarditis, and of infected intravascular catheters. Common organisms include Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Klebsiella species.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on blood cultures obtained from separate venepuncture sites before antibiotics are given. Finding a recognised pathogen in one or more sets is significant; skin organisms such as coagulase-negative staphylococci require careful interpretation as possible contaminants. Treatment involves prompt targeted antibiotics once the organism and susceptibilities are known, removal or drainage of the source (for example infected lines or abscesses), and evaluation for complications such as endocarditis, osteomyelitis or septic emboli. S. aureus bacteremia in particular mandates a search for deep-seated infection and a defined course of intravenous therapy.
