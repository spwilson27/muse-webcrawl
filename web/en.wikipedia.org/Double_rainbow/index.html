Double rainbow

A double rainbow over a landscape.

A double rainbow is a meteorological phenomenon that consists of two rainbows, a brighter primary rainbow and a fainter secondary rainbow, appearing simultaneously in the sky. The secondary rainbow is caused by light reflecting twice inside water droplets, which reverses the order of its colours.

Formation

Rainbows are formed when sunlight enters water droplets, is refracted, internally reflected, and refracted again on exiting. In a primary rainbow the light reflects once inside the droplet, producing an arc at roughly 40–42 degrees. When light reflects a second time, a secondary rainbow appears at about 50–53 degrees, with its colour sequence reversed: red on the inside and violet on the outside. Because each reflection loses light, the secondary bow is considerably dimmer.

Alexander's band

The region of sky between the primary and secondary bows appears noticeably darker than the surrounding sky and is known as Alexander's band, after Alexander of Aphrodisias, who described it in around 200 AD. The contrast depends on how the droplets scatter light at angles between the two bows.

Cultural significance

Double rainbows are often regarded as portents of good fortune in popular culture. They gained wide internet fame in 2010 through a widely shared video in which the narrator reacts ecstatically to a vivid double rainbow in Yosemite National Park.
