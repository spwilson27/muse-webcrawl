Garden-path sentence

A garden-path sentence is a grammatically correct sentence that begins in a way that causes the reader to interpret it according to one syntactic structure, which turns out to be wrong; the reader must then backtrack and reanalyse the sentence. The name derives from the idiom "to be led down the garden path," meaning to be deceived or misled. Garden-path sentences are studied in psycholinguistics as evidence of how humans parse sentences incrementally, word by word.

Example

The horse raced past the barn fell.

On first reading, "raced" is taken as the main verb of the sentence, making "fell" unintelligible. The sentence becomes clear when reanalysed with "raced past the barn" as a reduced relative clause: "The horse [that was] raced past the barn fell." Another famous example, devised by linguist Steven Pinker, is "The old man the boat," in which "old" is a noun and "man" a verb.

Explanation

Garden-path effects arise because the human parser commits to an analysis before the whole sentence has been heard or read, preferring simpler structures and more frequent word senses. The phenomenon was central to the garden-path theory of sentence processing proposed by Lyn Frazier and colleagues in the late 1970s, which held that the parser initially follows structural principles such as Minimal Attachment and Late Closure. Competing constraint-based models instead argue that lexical frequency, plausibility, and context all guide parsing from the start, with garden-path difficulty arising when these cues conflict.
