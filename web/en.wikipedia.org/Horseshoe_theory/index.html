Horseshoe theory

The political spectrum as a horseshoe rather than a straight line

In popular discourse, horseshoe theory asserts that, rather than the political spectrum being a straight line with the far-left and far-right at opposite ends, the two extremes more closely resemble each other than they resemble the political centre — just as the two ends of a horseshoe are closer together than the ends of a straight bar. The metaphor is often attributed to the French writer and philosopher Jean-Pierre Faye, who used it in the early 2000s.

Arguments and criticism

Proponents point to similarities between extremist movements of left and right: authoritarianism, intolerance of dissent, conspiratorial thinking and a willingness to use political violence. Critics respond that the comparison glosses over fundamental differences in goals and ideology — such as attitudes to equality and hierarchy — and argue that the theory is used rhetorically to equate movements analysts regard as incomparable. Political scientists note that empirical studies of voter attitudes generally place the far left and far right far apart on economic and many social dimensions, though some research finds shared traits such as dogmatism across extremes.
