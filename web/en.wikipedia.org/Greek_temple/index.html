Greek temple

The Parthenon on the Acropolis of Athens, a Doric temple of the 5th century BCE.

Greek temples (Greek: ναός, naos, "dwelling") were structures built within sanctuaries to house the cult statue of a deity in ancient Greek religion. Unlike places of worship in many later religions, temples were not primarily congregational spaces: sacrifices and rituals took place outdoors at an altar in front of the temple, while the temple itself served as the god's house and a treasury for votive offerings.

Architecture

The canonical Greek temple is a rectangular stone building on a raised stepped platform (the crepidoma), surrounded by columns (a peristyle) supporting an entablature and a low pitched roof, with triangular pediments decorated with sculpture. Inside stood the cella (naos proper) containing the cult image, often preceded by a porch (pronaos) and sometimes balanced by a rear chamber (opisthodomos). Temples followed the classical orders — Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian — whose proportions and ornament were refined over centuries, with subtle refinements such as entasis and corner contraction giving classical buildings their visual harmony.

Development

Monumental stone temples emerged in the 7th–6th centuries BCE, replacing earlier mud-brick and timber shrines. Early examples such as the temple of Apollo at Corinth and the Heraion of Olympia gave way to the great Doric temples of Sicily and southern Italy (Paestum, Agrigento, Segesta) and, in the 5th century BCE, to the classical perfection of the Parthenon (447–432 BCE) and its neighbours on the Athenian Acropolis. Temple building continued into the Hellenistic and Roman periods, and the form profoundly influenced Roman and later neoclassical architecture.
