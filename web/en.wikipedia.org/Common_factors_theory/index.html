Common factors theory

Common factors theory
Field: psychotherapy research
Key figures: Saul Rosenzweig, Jerome Frank, Bruce Wampold
Claim: shared elements across therapies account for most outcome variance

Common factors theory holds that different schools of psychotherapy work largely through elements they share—such as the therapeutic alliance, empathy, client expectations and a plausible healing rationale—rather than through the specific techniques that distinguish them.

Origins

In 1936 Saul Rosenzweig observed that rival therapies achieved similar results, invoking the "Dodo bird verdict" from Alice in Wonderland: "everybody has won and all must have prizes." Jerome Frank's Persuasion and Healing (1961) developed the idea into a framework of shared components.

Evidence

Meta-analyses, notably by Bruce Wampold, find small differences between bona fide therapies, while alliance–outcome correlations are consistently moderate. Estimates commonly attribute only a modest share of outcome to specific ingredients, with the largest shares to client and extratherapeutic factors.

Criticism

Critics argue that the theory underplays demonstrably specific effects, for example exposure techniques for phobias and PTSD, and that equivalence findings reflect insensitive comparisons rather than true uniformity.
