Ground glass

Ground glass
MaterialGlass with a roughened (frosted) surface
ProductionAbrasive grinding, sandblasting, acid etching
UsesFocusing screens, laboratory joints, privacy glazing, diffusers

Ground glass is glass whose surface has been roughened — by grinding with abrasives, sandblasting, or acid etching — so that it becomes translucent, scattering light passing through it. Objects behind ground glass appear blurred, a property exploited in privacy glazing, lamp shades and optical instruments.

Photography

In view cameras and many reflex cameras, a ground-glass focusing screen sits at the focal plane; the lens projects an image onto the matte surface, which the photographer inspects (often with a loupe) to focus precisely. Focusing screens frequently incorporate aids such as Fresnel lenses to brighten the image, split-image prisms and microprism collars. The dim, inverted image on the ground glass was the standard viewfinding method of early photography.

Laboratory glassware

Ground glass joints are precision-matted conical or spherical fittings on flasks, condensers and stopcocks. Their frosted surfaces form gas-tight seals when joined — usually without grease for standard taper joints — allowing complex distillation and reaction apparatus to be assembled from interchangeable parts.

Medical usage

In radiology, "ground-glass opacity" describes a hazy area of increased lung density on CT scans in which vessels remain visible; it is a finding associated with many conditions, including infections and interstitial lung diseases. The term borrows the appearance of the material.
