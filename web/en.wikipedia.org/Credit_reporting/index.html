Credit reporting

Industry: Financial information services
Major bureaus (U.S.): Experian, Equifax, TransUnion
Governed by: Fair Credit Reporting Act

Credit reporting is the collection, aggregation, and sale of information about consumers' credit histories by specialist firms called credit bureaus or consumer reporting agencies. Lenders, insurers, landlords, and employers use credit reports and the credit scores derived from them to assess risk and eligibility.

How it works

Banks, card issuers, and other furnishers report account data — balances, limits, payment timeliness, defaults — to the bureaus, which assemble it into individual files. In the United States the three nationwide bureaus are Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion; scoring models such as FICO and VantageScore convert report data into three-digit scores. Reports typically include identifying information, tradelines, public records, and inquiry history.

Regulation

The U.S. Fair Credit Reporting Act (1970) gives consumers the right to access their reports, dispute inaccuracies, and limit who may view them; most negative information must be removed after seven years (ten for bankruptcy). The Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act of 2003 entitled consumers to a free report from each bureau annually, and security freezes became free nationwide in 2018 following the 2017 Equifax data breach, which exposed the personal data of roughly 147 million people.

International practice

Systems vary widely: some countries rely on public credit registries run by central banks, others on private bureaus, and coverage ranges from "negative only" reporting (defaults) to comprehensive positive reporting of all obligations.
