Roche limit

The Roche limit, also called the Roche radius, is the distance from a celestial body within which a second body held together only by its own gravity will disintegrate because the first body's tidal forces exceed the second's self-gravitation. It is named after the French astronomer Édouard Roche, who calculated it in 1848.

Description

For a rigid satellite the limit is approximately d ≈ 1.26 R (ρM/ρm)1/3, where R is the primary's radius and ρ are the densities; for a fluid satellite the factor rises to about 2.44. Inside the Roche limit, orbiting material tends to disperse into rings rather than accrete into a moon — which is why planetary rings, such as Saturn's, lie within their planet's Roche limit for icy material.

Examples

Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 was torn into a chain of fragments by Jupiter's tides in 1992 before colliding with the planet in 1994. The Roche limit also constrains how closely stars in binary systems and planets around other stars can approach before disruption or mass transfer.
