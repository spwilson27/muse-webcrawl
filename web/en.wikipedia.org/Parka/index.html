Parka

A parka is a type of coat with a hood, often lined with fur or faux fur, designed for wear in extremely cold weather. The garment originated with the Indigenous peoples of the Arctic — including the Inuit, Yupik, and Aleut — who made waterproof parkas from caribou or seal skin, sometimes coated with fish oil, for hunting and kayaking in frigid conditions. The word "parka" is thought to derive from the Nenets language of Siberia, meaning "animal skin".

A modern parka with the characteristic fur-trimmed hood.

Traditional parkas

Traditional Arctic parkas were tailored to their purpose: a woman's parka, the amauti, incorporates a large pouch at the back in which a baby can be carried. The attached hood carries a wide ruff of long fur — traditionally wolf or wolverine — which breaks up the wearer's breath and prevents frost from forming around the face. Styles and decoration varied by region and could indicate the wearer's community and status.

Military and civilian adoption

Western militaries adopted the parka during the twentieth century. The United States Army developed the snorkel parka (the N-3B) for flight crews stationed in extreme cold during the early 1950s; its name comes from the hood, which can be zipped up leaving only a small tunnel to look through. The British fishtail parka, issued as surplus after the Second World War and the Korean War, became an emblem of the 1960s mod subculture, worn over suits to protect them while riding scooters. From the 1970s onward the parka became a mainstream fashion item, and today insulated expedition parkas filled with down or synthetic insulation are standard equipment for polar research, mountaineering, and winter wear in cold climates.
