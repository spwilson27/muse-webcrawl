Squinch

Squinches bridging the corners of a square bay to support a circular dome.

In architecture, a squinch is a structural element used to support the base of a dome over a square chamber. It fills the upper angles of a square room, bridging the corners so that a circular or polygonal dome can rest on what would otherwise be a square plan. A squinch may be formed by a small arch, a series of corbelled arches, or a half-conical surface (a trumpet arch) spanning the corner.

History

The squinch was developed in Sasanian Persia, where domes on square chambers were a hallmark of palatial and fire-temple architecture; the palace of Ardashir at Firuzabad (3rd century AD) contains some of the earliest surviving examples. The technique spread through Islamic architecture and into the medieval architecture of Europe, appearing in Romanesque and Byzantine-influenced churches.

Comparison with the pendentive

The squinch is often contrasted with the pendentive, the spherical triangular surface used in Byzantine architecture, famously in Hagia Sophia, to place a dome over a square. Whereas the pendentive forms a continuous curved surface flowing from the dome's rim down to the supporting piers, the squinch is a discrete arch or niche set into each corner, leaving the geometry of the transition visibly distinct.

Forms

Squinches take several forms: simple corner arches, stacked muqarnas-like tiers of small arches, conical half-domes, and corbelled slabs. In Ottoman and Iranian architecture they were often elaborated into decorative muqarnas vaulting. Related corner devices appear in the architecture of the Renaissance and in vernacular building across Central Asia.
