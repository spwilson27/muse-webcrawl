Sir James Brooke (29 April 1803 – 11 June 1868) was a British soldier and adventurer who founded the Raj of Sarawak in Borneo. He ruled as the first White Rajah of Sarawak from 1841 until his death in 1868. His dynasty, the Brooke family, governed Sarawak for a century, until 1946.

Early life

Brooke was born at Secrore, near Benares in British India, the son of Thomas Brooke, a judge in the East India Company's service. Educated in England, he joined the Bengal Army as a cadet and was badly wounded in the First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826), after which he resigned his commission. Following unsuccessful trading ventures in the Far East, his father's death in 1835 left him an inheritance of £30,000, with which he bought and fitted out an armed schooner, the Royalist, and sailed for Borneo in 1838.

Rajah of Sarawak

Brooke arrived at Kuching in August 1839, where the local representative of the Sultan of Brunei, Pengiran Muda Hashim, was struggling to suppress a rebellion of Malay and Bidayuh Land Dayaks. Brooke's assistance — and the firepower of the Royalist — helped settle the revolt, and in 1841 he was granted governorship of Sarawak, a title confirmed as Rajah in 1842 in return for an annual payment to the Sultan. Over the following years he expanded Sarawak's territory substantially, largely at Brunei's expense, through a series of treaties.

Rule and controversies

Brooke set about creating an administration, a legal code, and trade, and campaigned vigorously, with Royal Navy support under captains such as Henry Keppel, against piracy by Illanun and Dayak (Iban) seafarers. These anti-piracy campaigns — notably the attacks on Iban communities — proved controversial in Britain: critics including Joseph Hume MP accused Brooke of massacring indigenous people under the pretext of piracy, and a Commission of Inquiry sat in Singapore in 1854, which cleared him of the charges though his reputation and health suffered. In 1847 he was appointed British governor of Labuan and consul-general in Borneo, and knighted. He survived an assassination attempt and the Chinese miners' insurrection of 1857, when Kuching was briefly captured by Hakka gold miners from Bau.

Death and legacy

Brooke, who never married, first designated his nephew John Brooke Brooke as heir, then repudiated him in a bitter dispute, settling the succession on another nephew, Charles Brooke, who succeeded him in 1868. James Brooke died at Burrator, Devon, and is buried at Sheepstor on Dartmoor. His life partly inspired Joseph Conrad's Lord Jim and Rudyard Kipling's "The Man Who Would Be King", and he remains a debated figure: founder of a state that suppressed piracy and the slave trade and governed with an unusually light colonial footprint, but also a product of 19th-century imperial expansion whose rule rested on conquest and cession under pressure.
