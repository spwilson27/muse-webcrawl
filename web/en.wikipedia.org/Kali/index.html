Kali (Sanskrit: काली, Kālī), also known as Kālikā, is a Hindu goddess associated with time, change, creation, destruction, and power. She is one of the ten Mahavidyas and a fierce form of the supreme goddess Durga (Mahadevi). Kali's earliest appearance is as a destroyer of evil forces in the Devi Mahatmyam, where she is summoned from Durga's brow to slay the demons Chanda and Munda and, most famously, Raktabija.

Iconography

Kali is usually depicted with blue or black skin, a gaunt face, and dishevelled hair. She wears a garland of severed heads (muṇḍamālā) and a skirt of severed arms, stands upon the supine body of her consort Shiva, and brandishes a bloodied sword and a severed head. Her four arms variously hold a scimitar, a trident, a severed head, and a bowl or skull-cup catching the blood. Her long tongue lolls out, a gesture interpreted both as ferocity and, in later tradition, as embarrassment when she realises she has stepped on Shiva.

Mythology

In the battle against the demon Raktabija, every drop of whose blood spawned a duplicate of himself, Kali destroyed the clones and drank his blood before it could fall, enabling Durga to kill him. Her rampage then threatened the cosmos itself until Shiva lay down in her path; seeing her husband beneath her feet, Kali was pacified.

Worship

Kali is the chief deity of the Kalighat Temple in Kolkata and of Kali Puja, celebrated on the new moon of Kartika, coinciding with Diwali in Bengal, Assam, and Odisha. She is central to Tantric practice and to the devotional poetry of saints such as Ramprasad Sen and Ramakrishna, who emphasised her aspect as a loving, if fierce, mother.
