The Imperial House of Japan (皇室, Kōshitsu), also called the Imperial Family, is the dynasty of the Japanese imperial line and the extended family of the reigning Emperor of Japan. It is widely regarded as the oldest continuous hereditary monarchy in the world, traditionally traced to Emperor Jimmu in 660 BC.

History

According to the chronicles Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, the Imperial line descends from the sun goddess Amaterasu through her grandson Ninigi and his descendant Jimmu, the first emperor. Historically verifiable emperors begin many centuries later, but the line is notable for having persisted, under a single dynasty, through the shogunates — periods when military rulers governed while emperors reigned in largely ceremonial roles — and through the Meiji Restoration of 1868, which restored imperial rule.

Under the Meiji Constitution the emperor was sovereign and sacred; after Japan's defeat in the Second World War, the Emperor Showa renounced the claim to divinity, and the 1947 Constitution redefined the emperor as "the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people", with no powers of government. The Imperial House Law of 1947 also drastically reduced the family's size, removing most collateral branches from imperial status.

Membership and succession

Succession is governed by the Imperial House Law, which restricts the throne to male descendants in the male line. The family is administered by the Imperial Household Agency, which manages its official duties, residences and ceremonies. Members carry out extensive ceremonial, diplomatic and charitable engagements but hold no political power.

The present emperor, Naruhito, acceded to the throne on 1 May 2019 following the abdication of his father, Akihito — the first imperial abdication in over two centuries — beginning the Reiwa era.

The succession question

Because the Imperial House Law excludes women from the throne and the family has very few young male members, Japan has periodically debated reform — including proposals, seriously considered in the mid-2000s, to allow female succession. The birth of Prince Hisahito in 2006, the first male born to the family in 41 years, shelved those proposals, but the long-term future of the line remains a subject of public discussion.
