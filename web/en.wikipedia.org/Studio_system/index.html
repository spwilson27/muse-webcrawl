Studio system

A major studio lot during the Golden Age of Hollywood.

The studio system is a method of film production and distribution dominated by a small number of "major" studios in Hollywood during the period from the 1920s to the 1960s, often called the Golden Age of Hollywood. The studios produced movies primarily on their own filmmaking lots with creative personnel under often long-term contract, and dominated exhibition through vertical integration — owning the production facilities, distribution arms and chains of movie theatres.

The majors and minors

The "Big Five" majors were Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer (MGM), Paramount Pictures, Warner Bros., 20th Century Fox and RKO Radio Pictures; the "Little Three" were Universal Pictures, Columbia Pictures and United Artists. Under the system, studios controlled actors and directors through long-term contracts, manufactured star personas, and sold films to theatres in blocks ("block booking"), requiring exhibitors to take weaker pictures to obtain prestigious ones.

Decline

The system unraveled after the United States Supreme Court's 1948 decision in United States v. Paramount Pictures, Inc., which forced the majors to divest their theatre chains and outlawed block booking. The rise of television, the growing power of talent agencies such as MCA, and the shift toward independent production and location shooting further eroded the old model through the 1950s and 1960s, ending the studio system era.
