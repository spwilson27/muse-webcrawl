Caspases (cysteine-dependent aspartate-directed proteases) are a family of protease enzymes that play essential roles in programmed cell death (apoptosis) and inflammation. They are cysteine proteases that cleave their target proteins specifically after aspartate residues.

Mechanism and cascade

Caspases are synthesised as inactive zymogens (procaspases). In apoptosis, initiator caspases (such as caspase-8 and caspase-9) are activated first — by the death-receptor (extrinsic) pathway or the mitochondrial (intrinsic) pathway, respectively — and in turn cleave and activate executioner caspases (caspases 3, 6, and 7). The executioners dismantle the cell in an orderly fashion: they cleave hundreds of substrates including cytoskeletal proteins, the DNA-repair enzyme PARP, and an inhibitor of a DNase, producing the characteristic features of apoptosis — membrane blebbing, DNA fragmentation, and packaging of the cell into apoptotic bodies for phagocytosis.

Other roles

Some caspases (caspase-1, -4, -5 in humans) are inflammatory: assembled in inflammasome complexes, they process the cytokines interleukin-1β and interleukin-18 and can trigger pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of cell death. Dysregulation of caspases is implicated in cancer (failed apoptosis), neurodegenerative diseases (excessive cell death), and autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, making them targets of drug research.
