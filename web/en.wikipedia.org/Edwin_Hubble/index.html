Edwin Powell Hubble (November 20, 1889 – September 28, 1953) was an American astronomer. He played a crucial role in establishing the fields of extragalactic astronomy and observational cosmology. Hubble proved that many objects then thought to be clouds of dust and gas and classified as "nebulae" were actually galaxies beyond the Milky Way. He provided evidence that the recessional velocity of a galaxy increases with its distance from Earth, a property now called Hubble's law, implying the universe is expanding.

Early life and education

Hubble studied mathematics and astronomy at the University of Chicago, then read jurisprudence at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar. After service as an infantry officer in World War I, he completed his doctorate and joined the staff of Mount Wilson Observatory in California in 1919, where he used the 100-inch Hooker telescope, then the largest in the world.

Discoveries

In 1923–24, Hubble identified Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda "nebula," demonstrating that it lay far beyond the Milky Way and resolving the Great Debate over the scale of the universe. In 1929, using redshift data gathered largely by Milton Humason and building on work by Vesto Slipher and Georges Lemaître, he published the velocity–distance relation for galaxies. He also devised the Hubble sequence for classifying galaxies into ellipticals, spirals, and barred spirals.

Legacy

The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, is named in his honor. The rate of cosmic expansion he first measured is quantified by the Hubble constant, one of the most important numbers in cosmology.
