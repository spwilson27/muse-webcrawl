Natural selection

Natural selection is the differential survival and reproduction of individuals due to differences in phenotype. It is a key mechanism of evolution: heritable traits that improve an organism's chances of surviving and reproducing become more common in a population over generations, while disadvantageous traits become rarer.

History

The mechanism was proposed independently by Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace and presented in 1858; Darwin developed it fully in On the Origin of Species (1859). Darwin reasoned from Malthusian population pressures and the variation visible in domesticated animals: organisms produce more offspring than can survive, and those best fitted to their environment leave more descendants.

Requirements and modes

Selection requires variation, heredity, and differential fitness. It may be directional (favouring one extreme), stabilising (favouring the average), disruptive (favouring extremes), or sexual (driven by mate choice and competition). Classic documented cases include industrial melanism in the peppered moth and beak changes in Galápagos finches during droughts.

Relationship to other mechanisms

Selection acts on variation generated by mutation and recombination, and alongside genetic drift, gene flow, and non-random mating, it shapes the evolution of populations. It is the only mechanism known to produce adaptation.
