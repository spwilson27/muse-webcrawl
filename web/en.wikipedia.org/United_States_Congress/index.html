The United States Congress is the legislature of the federal government of the United States. It is bicameral, composed of a lower body, the House of Representatives, and an upper body, the Senate. Article One of the United States Constitution vests all legislative powers in Congress and enumerates its powers.

Composition

The House has 435 voting members apportioned among the states by population and elected every two years from single-member districts; six non-voting delegates represent the District of Columbia and the territories. The Senate has 100 members, two from each state, elected for staggered six-year terms. Members must meet age, citizenship and residency requirements (25 years and seven years a citizen for the House; 30 and nine years for the Senate).

Powers

Congress's enumerated powers include levying taxes, borrowing money, regulating interstate and foreign commerce, coining money, establishing post offices, declaring war, and raising and supporting armies and a navy, together with the power to make laws "necessary and proper" to execute them. Legislation requires passage by both houses and the President's signature (or a two-thirds veto override). The Senate alone confirms presidential nominees and ratifies treaties by two-thirds vote; the House alone originates revenue bills and brings impeachments, which the Senate tries.

History

Congress succeeded the unicameral Congress of the Confederation under the Articles of Confederation. The bicameral structure embodied the Connecticut Compromise of 1787 between large-state (Virginia) and small-state (New Jersey) plans. The First Congress convened in New York in 1789; the Capitol in Washington has been its seat since 1800. Landmark expansions of its role include Reconstruction legislation, the New Deal, the War Powers Resolution of 1973, and the Congressional Budget Act of 1974.

Procedure and committees

Most legislative work occurs in standing committees — such as Appropriations, Ways and Means, Judiciary and Armed Services — which hold hearings and mark up bills. Party leadership (the Speaker of the House, majority and minority leaders and whips) controls scheduling. The Senate's rules permit extended debate (the filibuster), with cloture requiring 60 votes for most legislation. Support agencies include the Congressional Budget Office, the Government Accountability Office and the Congressional Research Service.
