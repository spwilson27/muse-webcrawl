The Dome of the Rock (Arabic: Qubbat aṣ-Ṣakhra) is an Islamic shrine located at the centre of the Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif) in the Old City of Jerusalem. Completed in 691–692 CE on the orders of the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik, it is one of the oldest surviving works of Islamic architecture and one of the most recognisable landmarks of Jerusalem, its gilded dome dominating the skyline.

Architecture

The shrine is an octagonal structure crowned by a wooden dome about 20 metres in diameter, originally covered in gold and re-gilded in the twentieth century (the 1994 restoration used gold donated by King Hussein of Jordan). Inside, an arcade of piers and columns surrounds the sacred Rock — a large outcrop venerated in Islamic tradition as the site from which Muhammad ascended to heaven on the Night Journey, and in Jewish tradition associated with the Temple and the binding of Isaac. The interior is richly decorated with mosaics of vegetal motifs; Qur'anic inscriptions in mosaic, among the earliest surviving, run around the octagonal arcade and include Christological passages addressing Christians.

History

The Dome was built shortly after the Muslim conquest of Jerusalem, arguably to rival the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and to assert Islam's presence in the holy city. During the Crusades it was converted into a church, the Templum Domini, administered by Augustinian canons, before Saladin restored it to Islamic use in 1187. Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent covered the exterior with the famous coloured tiles that still adorn it. The site is administered by the Jerusalem Islamic Waqf under the sensitive "status quo" arrangements shared with the nearby Al-Aqsa Mosque compound.

Significance

The Dome of the Rock is sacred in Islam as part of the third holiest site of the faith and stands on ground holy in Judaism as the Temple Mount — making it one of the most contested religious sites in the world.
