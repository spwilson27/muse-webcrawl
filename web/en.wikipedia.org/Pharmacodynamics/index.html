Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacodynamics (sometimes abbreviated PD) is the branch of pharmacology concerned with the effects of drugs and the mechanism of their action — often summarised as "what the drug does to the body". Together with pharmacokinetics ("what the body does to the drug"), it defines the dose–response relationships that underpin rational therapeutics.

Mechanisms of action

Most drugs act by binding to specific molecular targets, principally receptors, enzymes, ion channels, and transport proteins. Drugs that activate receptors are called agonists; those that bind without activating, and thereby block agonists, are antagonists. Partial agonists produce a submaximal response even at full occupancy, while inverse agonists reduce constitutive receptor activity.

Dose–response relationships

The relationship between drug concentration and effect is usually described by the Hill equation and characterised by potency (the concentration producing a given effect, often the EC50) and efficacy (the maximal effect, Emax). The therapeutic index — the ratio of the dose causing toxicity to the dose causing the desired effect — expresses the safety margin of a drug. Interactions between drugs can be additive, synergistic, or antagonistic.

Desensitisation and tolerance

Continued exposure to a drug may reduce its effect (tolerance) through receptor down-regulation, desensitisation, or increased metabolic clearance; abrupt withdrawal can then produce rebound phenomena. Conversely, some agents produce sensitisation with repeated use.
