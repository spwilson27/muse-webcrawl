Bolivian gas conflict

The Bolivian gas conflict, also known as the Bolivian Gas War (Spanish: Guerra del Gas), was a social conflict in Bolivia during 2003 and 2005 centred on the exploitation of the country's substantial natural gas reserves. It grew out of long-standing grievances over neoliberal economic policy, the privatization of state assets in the 1990s, and anger that Bolivia's resources benefited foreign companies while most Bolivians remained poor.

Background

Bolivia holds some of South America's largest natural gas reserves. Under President Gonzalo Sánchez de Lozada, the hydrocarbons sector had been opened to foreign firms including Repsol YPF, Petrobras, and BP on terms critics considered far too generous, and the state received low royalties. A plan to export liquefied gas to the United States and Mexico through a pipeline to a Chilean port proved explosive, since Bolivia lost its coastline to Chile in the 19th century and many Bolivians resisted enriching their old adversary.

The 2003 Gas War

Protests in September and October 2003 — road blockades led by Indigenous and labor movements, miners, and the residents of El Alto — were met with military repression that killed around 60 people. Sánchez de Lozada resigned on 17 October 2003 and fled the country. His successor, Carlos Mesa, held a 2004 referendum on gas policy and passed a new hydrocarbons law raising taxes, but continuing protests forced his resignation in June 2005.

Outcome

The conflict propelled Evo Morales and the Movement for Socialism to victory in the December 2005 elections. On 1 May 2006 Morales announced the nationalization of the gas industry, placing fields under the state company YPFB and sharply raising the state's share of revenue. "Gas War" demands — nationalization and a constituent assembly — reshaped Bolivian politics for a generation.
