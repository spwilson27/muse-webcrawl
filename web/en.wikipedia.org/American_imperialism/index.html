American imperialism

A 1898 cartoon depicting the reach of the United States from the Philippines to Puerto Rico.

American imperialism is the expansion of American political, economic, cultural, and media influence beyond the boundaries of the United States. Depending on the commentator, it may include military conquest, gunboat diplomacy, unequal treaties, subsidization of preferred factions, economic penetration through private companies, and cultural penetration, or it may instead mean the absence of overt military control.

History

During the late 19th century, a new generation of American leaders, including Theodore Roosevelt, Henry Cabot Lodge, and naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan, argued that the United States had to compete with European empires for colonies and markets. The New Imperialism period saw the United States acquire overseas possessions, most dramatically following the Spanish–American War of 1898, after which Spain ceded the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico to the United States, and the independent Republic of Hawaii was annexed the same year.

Opponents organized the American Anti-Imperialist League, which included Mark Twain, Andrew Carnegie, and former president Grover Cleveland, arguing that ruling subject peoples without their consent violated American republican principles. After the First and Second World Wars, American influence expanded largely through economic power, military bases, alliances, and institutions such as the International Monetary Fund, leading historians to speak of an "informal empire" rather than a formal colonial one.

Usage of the term

The term is rarely used by supporters of American foreign policy, who often cast interventions in terms of spreading democracy and human rights. Critics — domestic and foreign — have applied it to episodes including the Philippine–American War, interventions in Latin America under the Roosevelt Corollary, the Cold War-era coups in Iran (1953) and Guatemala (1954), and the 2003 invasion of Iraq.
