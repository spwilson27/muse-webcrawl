Mortgage

A mortgage is a legal instrument of the common law which is used to create a security interest in real property held by a lender as security for a debt, usually a mortgage loan. The corresponding term in civil law jurisdictions is hypothec. A mortgage in itself is not a debt; it is the lender's security for a debt. The word derives from Law French, meaning "death pledge" — the pledge ends (dies) when the obligation is fulfilled or the property is taken through foreclosure.

Overview

The borrower (mortgagor) gives the lender (mortgagee) a security interest in real estate as collateral for a loan. If the borrower defaults, the lender may foreclose, forcing the sale of the property to recover the debt. Almost all mortgage borrowers are required to carry insurance and pay property taxes, and the property must usually be maintained to protect the collateral. Mortgages are the standard method by which individuals and businesses purchase residential and commercial real estate without paying the full value immediately.

Types

  • Fixed-rate mortgage — the interest rate remains constant for the whole term, or for a fixed period, giving predictable payments. In the United States the 30-year fixed-rate mortgage is the dominant product.
  • Adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) — the rate varies with a market index after an initial period.
  • Interest-only mortgage — payments cover only interest for a period, after which principal repayment begins or a balloon payment falls due.
  • Reverse mortgage — allows older homeowners to borrow against home equity, repaid when the borrower dies or leaves the home.

Repayment

Most mortgages are amortising: each regular payment covers interest due plus a portion of principal, so the balance declines to zero over the term (commonly 15, 20, 25 or 30 years). Early payments consist mostly of interest; later payments mostly of principal. Key measures include the loan-to-value ratio (LTV) and the borrower's credit history, which together with income determine eligibility and pricing. Private mortgage insurance (PMI) may be required where the deposit is small.

Foreclosure

Foreclosure is the legal process by which a lender recovers the outstanding balance by selling the mortgaged property after default. Depending on the jurisdiction, foreclosure may be judicial (through the courts) or non-judicial. Deficiency judgments, redemption periods and borrower protections vary widely.

History and markets

Mortgage lending developed from medieval English land law. In the twentieth century, governments promoted homeownership through institutions such as the United States Federal Housing Administration (1934) and the government-sponsored enterprises Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. Lenders frequently sell mortgages into the secondary market, where they are pooled into mortgage-backed securities. Abuses in subprime lending and securitisation were central causes of the financial crisis of 2007–2008, which led to widespread foreclosures and regulatory reform.
