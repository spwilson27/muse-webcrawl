Decree of Canopus

Date238 BC
Issued bySynod of Egyptian priests
ReignPtolemy III Euergetes
ScriptsHieroglyphs, Demotic, Greek

The Decree of Canopus is an ancient Egyptian bilingual inscription issued in 238 BC by a synod of priests assembled at Canopus in honour of Pharaoh Ptolemy III Euergetes and his wife Berenice II. Like the later Rosetta Stone, it was inscribed in three scripts: Egyptian hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Koine Greek.

Contents

The decree honoured the royal couple as benefactor gods, reorganised the temples' finances, and instituted a new priestly phyle. It also attempted a calendar reform: a sixth epagomenal day was to be added every four years — an early leap-year rule — to stop the Egyptian 365-day calendar drifting against the seasons, although the reform was not ultimately adopted.

Discovery and significance

The best-preserved copy was discovered at Tanis in 1866. Because its text was longer and better preserved than that of the Rosetta Stone, the Decree of Canopus played an important supporting role in confirming the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs.
