The Treaty of San Francisco (Japanese: Sanhuranshisuko Kōwa Jōyaku), also called the Treaty of Peace with Japan, re-established peaceful relations between Japan and the Allied Powers on behalf of the United Nations, ending the legal state of war and providing for redress for hostile actions up to and including World War II. It was officially signed by 49 nations on 8 September 1951, at the War Memorial Opera House in San Francisco, California, and entered into force on 28 April 1952.

Negotiation

The treaty was the product of negotiations led largely by U.S. diplomat John Foster Dulles, who travelled repeatedly to Japan and Allied capitals in 1950–51. It ended the Allied occupation of Japan under General Douglas MacArthur and restored full Japanese sovereignty, except for the Ryukyu Islands, which remained under U.S. administration. It came into effect the same day the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty took effect.

Terms

Under the treaty, Japan recognized the independence of Korea, and renounced all right, title and claim to Taiwan (Formosa), the Pescadores, the Kuril Islands, the portion of Sakhalin it had obtained in the Treaty of Portsmouth, the League of Nations mandate over the Pacific islands, and its claims in Antarctica and the Spratly and Paracel Islands. Japan accepted the judgments of the International Military Tribunal for the Far East and agreed to pay reparations to Allied nations whose territories it had occupied, though the burden was deliberately kept modest to aid Japanese recovery.

States not party

The Soviet Union attended the conference but refused to sign, leaving issues such as the Kuril Islands dispute unresolved. Neither Chinese government — the Republic of China nor the People's Republic of China — was invited, owing to disagreement over which represented China; Japan later concluded a separate peace treaty with the Republic of China (Treaty of Taipei, 1952). The treaty left the status of Taiwan formally undetermined, a point still debated in international law.
