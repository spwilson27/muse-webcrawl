Quasicrystal

A quasicrystal is a structure that is ordered but not periodic. A quasicrystalline pattern can continuously fill all available space, but it lacks translational symmetry. While crystals, according to the classical crystallographic restriction theorem, can possess only two-, three-, four-, and six-fold rotational symmetries, the diffraction pattern of quasicrystals shows sharp peaks with other symmetry orders, such as five-fold.

Discovery

Quasicrystals were first observed by Dan Shechtman on 8 April 1982, in electron diffraction patterns of a rapidly cooled aluminium–manganese alloy, which showed the forbidden ten-fold symmetry of an icosahedral structure. His 1984 publication with Ilan Blech, Dov Levine and John Cahn was initially controversial — Linus Pauling was a prominent sceptic — but the existence of quasicrystals became established, and Shechtman received the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The first natural quasicrystal, icosahedrite, was identified in 2009 in material from the Khatyrka meteorite.

Structure and properties

Quasicrystals are commonly described as projections of periodic structures from higher-dimensional spaces, or by aperiodic tilings such as the Penrose tiling. Most are metallic alloys. They tend to be brittle, with unusually low friction and poor thermal and electrical conductivity for metals, properties that have led to applications as non-stick coatings, thermal barriers and components in surgical instruments and LED devices.
