Desktop publishing

Desktop publishing (DTP) is the creation of documents using dedicated software on a personal ("desktop") computer. It combines text and graphics into page layouts suitable for printing or digital distribution, tasks previously performed by specialised typesetters and paste-up artists. The term was coined by Paul Brainerd, founder of the Aldus Corporation, whose PageMaker software, together with the Apple Macintosh and the Apple LaserWriter printer, launched the industry in 1985.

History

Before desktop publishing, typesetting was a dedicated trade. In the early 1980s, systems such as the Xerox Star and Interleaf demonstrated what-you-see-is-what-you-get page composition, building on laser printing research at Xerox PARC. The turning point came in 1985 with the combination of the Macintosh's graphical interface, the LaserWriter (the first widely available PostScript laser printer), and PageMaker, which let users position text and graphics on a virtual pasteboard. Adobe's PostScript page description language and, later, PDF became the standards for exchanging composed pages. Professional tools such as QuarkXPress (1987) and Adobe InDesign (1999) came to dominate magazine and book production, while Microsoft Publisher and open-source tools such as Scribus served other markets.

Workflow and concepts

DTP software works with master pages, styles, grids, and layers to maintain consistency across long documents. Text is flowed into frames; typography is controlled at the level of fonts, kerning, leading, hyphenation, and justification; images are placed, scaled, and colour-managed for print (CMYK) or screen (RGB). Preflight checks verify fonts, image resolution, and colour settings before output to print, and pages are exported as print-ready PDF files for commercial presses or sent directly to home and office printers. Desktop publishing thereby merged the roles of writer, designer, and typesetter, lowering the cost of small-run publications such as newsletters, brochures, and zines dramatically — while also producing a recognisable era of amateur work, as professional typographic conventions had to be relearned by new users.

Digital publishing

Since the 2000s, the same tools have been adapted for screen reading: ebooks, interactive PDFs, and web-oriented layouts, with InDesign and similar programs exporting EPUB and HTML. The distinctions among desktop publishing, word processing, and web design have blurred, as word processors gained layout features and DTP programs gained long-document and multimedia tools. Nonetheless, professional page-layout software remains standard in book, magazine, and marketing production because of its precise typographic and colour control.
