Cumaean Sibyl

The Cumaean Sibyl was the priestess presiding over the Apollonian oracle at Cumae, a Greek colony near Naples and the oldest Greek settlement on the Italian mainland. She was the most famous of the sibyls in Roman tradition, credited with the Sibylline Books consulted by the Roman state in times of crisis.

The Sibylline Books

According to legend, the Sibyl offered nine books of prophecy to Tarquin, king of Rome. When he refused her price, she burned three, then three more, until he bought the remaining three at the original price. The books were kept in the Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus and consulted by a priestly college on senatorial orders; they burned with the temple in 83 BC and were replaced by a collected edition.

In literature

In Book 6 of Virgil's Aeneid, the Sibyl guides Aeneas through the underworld; she warns him that the descent is easy but the return is the labour. Apollo had granted her a lifespan of as many years as grains of sand she could hold, but she forgot to ask for eternal youth, so she withered to a voice in a jar — a tale retold in Ovid and echoed in T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land, which opens with an epigraph from Petronius quoting her wish to die. Michelangelo painted her among the five sibyls on the Sistine Chapel ceiling.

The cave at Cumae

A trapezoidal gallery cut into the rock at Cumae, about 131 metres long, is traditionally identified as the Sibyl's cave, with side chambers from which her voice was said to issue.
