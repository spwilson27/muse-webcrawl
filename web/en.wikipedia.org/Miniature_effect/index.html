Miniature effect

A miniature effect is a special effect created for motion pictures and television by photographing scale models, or "miniatures", in place of full-size subjects that would be impractical, dangerous or impossible to film — exploding starships, collapsing buildings, sinking ocean liners, cityscapes and landscapes. Combined with high-speed photography, motion-control cameras, matte paintings and careful lighting, miniatures were the backbone of visual effects from the silent era until computer-generated imagery (CGI) largely displaced them in the 1990s and 2000s; they continue to be used, sometimes in hybrid with digital work, by directors who value their tangible realism.

A "bigature" — an oversized miniature — built for close-up effects filming.

Techniques

To make a model read as full-size on screen, cinematographers exploit depth of field and physics. Because water droplets, smoke and debris obey gravity at real scale, miniatures are filmed at high frame rates and played back in slow motion so that falling objects and explosions appear massive; a model shot at 96 frames per second appears four times larger than one shot at 24 fps. Cameras use small apertures and intense lighting to keep the whole model sharp, and motion-control rigs repeat precise camera moves so elements (model, background plate, pyrotechnics) can be combined optically or digitally. Oversized miniatures — nicknamed "bigatures" by Weta Workshop — may be tens of metres long for close work, as in the Lord of the Rings films. Forced perspective, hanging miniatures placed near the camera, and glass matte paintings extend sets beyond the model's edges.

History

Georges Méliès and early trick-film pioneers used models; Norman Dawn and others refined matte and miniature work in the 1910s–20s. Landmarks include the miniature New York of King Kong (1933), the spaceship effects of 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), and the motion-control revolution of Star Wars (1977), which founded Industrial Light & Magic and made miniature starships the industry standard for two decades — through Blade Runner, Aliens, Titanic (1997) and the Star Wars prequels. Companies such as ILM, the Apogee and Boss Film studios, and model shops like Grant McCune's and Greg Jein's became famous for their model units.

Decline and persistence

CGI surpassed miniatures in flexibility and fell in cost, closing many model shops in the 2000s. Yet miniatures persist where physical interaction with light, water and explosives is hard to simulate: Christopher Nolan's films (a rotating corridor and full-size sets for Inception, miniature aircraft and ships supplemented by real ones in Dunkirk), Interstellar's practical spacecraft models, Wes Anderson's stylised models in The Grand Budapest Hotel, and the Weta bigatures tradition. Stop-motion animation, though using miniatures frame-by-frame, is a distinct craft.
