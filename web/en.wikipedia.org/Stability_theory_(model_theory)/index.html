Stability theory (model theory)

Stability theory classifies theories by the number of types they realise.

In model theory, stability theory is concerned with classifying complete first-order theories according to the number and structure of their models, in particular according to how many types the theory has over sets of a given size. The field was founded by Saharon Shelah, beginning with work in the late 1960s and culminating in his 1978 book Classification Theory and the Number of Non-isomorphic Models, which resolved Morley's problem on categoricity by classifying theories according to stability.

Definitions

A complete theory T is called stable if for every model M of T and every subset A of M of cardinality κ, the number of complete types over A is at most κ for all sufficiently large κ — equivalently, if the theory does not have the order property: no formula can define a linear order on an infinite set in a model of T. Theory classes refine further into superstable, totally transcendental (ω-stable), and stable but not superstable theories, among others.

Classification theory

Shelah's program drew a dividing line through the class of all first-order theories: on one side, "classifiable" theories with a structure theory for their models (built from tree-like decompositions over independent systems), and on the other side, chaotic theories where no such structure theorem is possible. Stability is the first and most fundamental dividing line; later work identified finer properties such as simplicity, NIP (the non-independence property), and NTP2, generalising stability theory in ways important in algebra and geometry.

Applications

Methods from stability theory have been applied to algebraic geometry (through the model theory of algebraically closed fields and Zariski geometries of Hrushovski), to diophantine geometry (including Hrushovski's model-theoretic proof of the Manin–Mumford conjecture), and to valued fields, differential fields, and group theory.
