Image scanner

An image scanner—often abbreviated to just scanner—is a device that optically scans images, printed text, handwriting, or an object and converts it to a digital image. Commonly used in offices are variations of the desktop (or flatbed) scanner where the document is placed on a glass window for scanning. Hand-held scanners, using a small scanning surface passed over the object, were once popular but have largely been superseded by smartphone camera applications. Modern scanners typically use a charge-coupled device (CCD) or a contact image sensor (CIS) as the light sensor, whereas drum scanners, used for very high-resolution work, use photomultiplier tubes.

History

Scanners descend from early telephotography and fax input devices. The first image scanner developed for a computer was a drum scanner built at the U.S. National Bureau of Standards by a team led by Russell A. Kirsch in 1957; the first image ever scanned on it, a photograph of Kirsch's infant son, was a 176 × 176-pixel square that became one of the most famous images in computing history. Flatbed scanners became common office equipment in the 1980s and 1990s, and standalone consumer scanners were later partly displaced by multifunction printers combining printing, scanning, copying, and faxing.

Types

Flatbed scanners hold the document stationary on glass while a sensor array moves beneath it. Sheet-fed scanners pull the page past a fixed sensor and are common in document-management workflows, often with automatic document feeders for duplex scanning. Drum scanners mount flexible originals on a rotating acrylic cylinder and achieve the highest optical resolution and colour fidelity of any type, serving prepress and archival work. Film scanners are dedicated to slides and negatives, planetary (overhead) scanners photograph bound books from above without contact, and 3D scanners capture object geometry rather than surface appearance. Hand scanners and pen scanners form smaller niche categories.

Characteristics

Scanner quality is described by optical resolution in pixels per inch (ppi), colour bit depth (24-bit colour is standard; 48-bit is common for film work), and dynamic range or Dmax, which matters most for transparencies. Software drivers follow standards such as TWAIN, WIA, ICA, and SANE. Scanned text is typically made searchable with optical character recognition (OCR), and multi-page output is usually saved as PDF, TIFF, or JPEG files.
