An album cover (also record sleeve) is the front packaging of a commercially released studio album or other audio recording. Beyond protecting the disc, the album cover became one of the most influential forms of commercial graphic design, carrying photography, illustration and typography that visually define an artist's work.

History

Early 78 rpm records were sold in plain paper or leather-bound "albums" resembling photograph albums — the origin of the word. In 1939 Columbia Records art director Alex Steinweiss persuaded the company to print original artwork on record packaging; sales of the first illustrated releases rose dramatically, and illustrated covers became standard. The 12-inch LP (1948) gave designers a large square canvas, and in the 1950s labels such as Blue Note, with Reid Miles's modernist designs and Francis Wolff's photographs, made covers collectible art.

Golden age and iconic covers

In the 1960s and 1970s, gatefold sleeves and elaborate concepts flourished: the Beatles' Sgt. Pepper's Lonely Hearts Club Band (1967, designed by Peter Blake and Jann Hawken) and Pink Floyd's The Dark Side of the Moon (1973, by Hipgnosis) are among the most recognisable images in popular culture. Design studios and photographers became stars in their own right, and covers served as vehicles for social and artistic commentary — sometimes provoking censorship disputes and retail bans.

CD and digital eras

The compact disc shrank the canvas to 4.7 inches in the 1980s–90s, though booklets and special editions kept design ambitious. With digital downloads and streaming in the 2000s, the cover became a thumbnail image, yet it remains the primary visual identity of a release, and the vinyl revival of the 2010s renewed demand for large-format sleeve art.
