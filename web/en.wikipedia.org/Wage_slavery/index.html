Wage slavery

Wage slavery is a term used to criticize labor arrangements in which a person's livelihood depends on wages or a salary, especially when the dependence is total and the worker's choices are severely constrained. Critics argue such dependence can amount to a form of unfreedom comparable in some respects to slavery, because workers must sell their labor to survive while having little control over their working conditions.

History of the concept

Comparisons between wage labor and slavery appeared in ancient thought — Cicero wrote that a laborer's wage itself was a "pledge of slavery" — and became prominent in the 19th century. In the decades before the American Civil War, labor reformers, and notably also pro-slavery writers such as George Fitzhugh (who argued chattel slaves were better treated than Northern mill workers), used the phrase "wage slavery" or "white slavery" to describe factory conditions. Karl Marx analyzed wage labor as a system in which workers, formally free, are compelled to sell their labor power because they lack independent access to the means of production.

Criticism and debate

Socialists, anarchists, and syndicalists have used the concept to argue for worker ownership, cooperatives, or abolition of the wage system. Defenders of market economies reply that wage employment is a voluntary contract between legal equals, fundamentally different from chattel slavery, and that the comparison trivializes the latter. The term remains common in labor-movement and anti-capitalist writing, and has been applied to phenomena such as debt dependence and employer-tied health insurance or visas.
