Damnatio memoriae (Latin for "condemnation of memory") is a modern term for a form of dishonour in ancient Rome whereby a person's memory was officially erased after death. The Roman Senate could decree that the name of a condemned emperor or other public figure be removed from inscriptions and monuments, that their statues be destroyed or recarved, and that their coins be melted down. The practice was intended as the ultimate disgrace: the condemned was to be forgotten by history.

Practice

The sanctions grouped under the modern label took several forms. Names were chiselled out of public inscriptions, leaving visible gaps; portraits were smashed, defaced, or recut into the likeness of a successor; and laws or acts issued by the condemned could be annulled. Because the term itself is a modern scholarly coinage (first used in a dissertation of 1689), historians debate how formalised the procedure actually was in antiquity.

Notable victims

Among the most famous targets were the emperors Caligula (informally), Nero, Domitian, Commodus, and Geta, whose images were systematically removed after his murder by his brother Caracalla. The pharaohs Hatshepsut and Akhenaten suffered comparable erasures in ancient Egypt, although the Roman term does not strictly apply.

Effectiveness

Paradoxically, damnatio memoriae often had the opposite of its intended effect: the conspicuous gaps in inscriptions and the mutilated statues advertise the erasure itself, and literary sources usually preserved the condemned person's memory in detail. Modern scholars sometimes compare the practice to the airbrushing of photographs in twentieth-century totalitarian regimes and to contemporary debates about removing statues of controversial figures.
