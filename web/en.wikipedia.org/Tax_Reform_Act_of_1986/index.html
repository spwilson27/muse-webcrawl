Tax Reform Act of 1986

The Tax Reform Act of 1986 was a sweeping bipartisan overhaul of the US federal tax code.

The Tax Reform Act of 1986 (TRA) was passed by the 99th United States Congress and signed into law by President Ronald Reagan on October 22, 1986. It was the most sweeping reform of the US federal income tax code since the introduction of the modern income tax, and is frequently cited as an example of successful bipartisan legislation.

Provisions

The Act lowered the top marginal income tax rate from 50 percent to 28 percent — the lowest top rate among major industrial nations at the time — while broadening the tax base by eliminating or curtailing many deductions, credits, and tax shelters. It reduced the number of individual rate brackets, ultimately to two, increased the standard deduction and personal exemption (removing roughly six million low-income households from the tax rolls), repealed the deduction for state and local sales taxes, phased out the deduction for consumer interest, and ended the preferential rate for capital gains (restored later). For corporations, the top rate fell from 46 to 34 percent, offset by the elimination of preferences such as the investment tax credit.

Legislative history

The reform was developed from a 1984 Treasury Department study commissioned by Reagan, and was shepherded through Congress by Treasury Secretary James Baker, Senate Finance Committee chairman Bob Packwood, and House Ways and Means chairman Dan Rostenkowski. It was designed to be revenue-neutral. The Act also introduced the passive activity loss rules, which curtailed real-estate tax shelters, and important passive foreign investment company rules.

Legacy

The 1986 Act set the basic structure of the US tax code for the following three decades. Later legislation raised the top rate again (in 1990 and 1993) and added brackets, but the model of lower rates on a broader base remained influential in tax policy discussions around the world.
