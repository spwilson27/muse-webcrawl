1860 United States presidential election

The 1860 United States presidential election was the 19th quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, November 6, 1860. In a four-way contest dominated entirely by the issue of slavery, the Republican ticket of Abraham Lincoln and Hannibal Hamlin won a national plurality and a decisive Electoral College majority, carrying every Northern state without appearing on the ballot in most Southern states. Lincoln's victory triggered the secession of seven Southern states and the formation of the Confederate States of America, setting the stage for the American Civil War.

Background

By 1860 the sectional conflict over the expansion of slavery into the western territories had shattered the national party system. The Democratic Party split into Northern and Southern factions at its 1860 conventions: Northern Democrats nominated Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois, while Southern Democrats nominated the incumbent Vice President, John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky. Former Whigs and Know Nothings formed the Constitutional Union Party and nominated John Bell of Tennessee.

Results

Lincoln won 39.8 percent of the popular vote but 180 electoral votes, an outright majority. Douglas won 29.5 percent of the popular vote — second place — yet only 12 electoral votes, because his support was spread thinly across the country. Breckinridge carried the Deep South and 72 electoral votes; Bell carried Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee with 39.

Consequences

South Carolina seceded on December 20, 1860, followed by six more states before Lincoln's inauguration on March 4, 1861. The Confederate attack on Fort Sumter in April began the Civil War. The election is often considered the most consequential in American history, as it set the nation on the path to war and, ultimately, to the abolition of slavery.
