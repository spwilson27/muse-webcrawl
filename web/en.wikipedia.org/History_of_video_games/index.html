The history of video games began in the 1950s and 1960s as computer scientists started designing simple games and simulations on mainframe computers, and developed into a global industry that by the 2020s rivalled or exceeded film and recorded music in revenue. Video games moved from laboratories to arcades, into homes through dedicated consoles and personal computers, and onto the internet and mobile phones.

Early experiments (1947–1971)

The cathode-ray tube amusement device (patented 1947) is often cited as the earliest interactive electronic game. Academic milestones followed: Christopher Strachey's draughts program (1951), A. S. Douglas's OXO (tic-tac-toe, 1952), William Higinbotham's Tennis for Two (1958), and Spacewar! (1962), created by Steve Russell and colleagues at MIT and spread to university computers across the United States. Ralph Baer's "Brown Box" prototype of the late 1960s led to the first home console.

Arcades and the first consoles (1972–1983)

Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney's Atari released Pong (1972), igniting the arcade boom; the Magnavox Odyssey brought games home the same year, and the cartridge-based Atari 2600 (1977) created a software market. The golden age of arcade games followed with Space Invaders (1978), Pac-Man (1980), and Donkey Kong (1981). Home computers such as the Commodore 64 added another platform. Oversaturation with poor-quality games and competition from computers caused the North American video game crash of 1983, which bankrupted much of the US industry.

Recovery and console generations (1985–1999)

Nintendo revived the market with the Nintendo Entertainment System (1985) and Super Mario Bros. Console "generations" and rivalries—the Sega Genesis versus the Super NES, then Sony's PlayStation (1994) with CD-ROM media and 3D graphics—defined the era. On PCs, Doom (1993) popularised first-person shooters and online play, while real-time strategy and role-playing games flourished. Nintendo's Game Boy (1989) established handheld gaming.

Online, mobile, and modern era (2000–present)

The PlayStation 2 (2000) became the best-selling console of all time. Microsoft's Xbox Live normalised online console gaming; the Nintendo Wii (2006) expanded audiences with motion controls; and massively multiplayer games such as World of Warcraft (2004) built persistent online worlds. The iPhone-era app stores from 2008 made mobile gaming the largest segment by revenue. The 2010s and 2020s brought digital distribution (Steam), esports, streaming, indie development, free-to-play models, and virtual and augmented reality, with annual global revenues approaching $200 billion.
