Tok Pisin (from "talk pidgin") is an English-based creole language spoken throughout Papua New Guinea, where it is one of the official languages alongside English and Hiri Motu. It serves as the country's principal lingua franca, uniting speakers of more than 800 indigenous languages, and is used in parliament, broadcasting, newspapers and everyday commerce. For a growing urban population it is a first language, making it a true creole rather than merely a pidgin.

History

Tok Pisin developed during the 19th century in the Pacific labour trade, as Melanesian workers on plantations in Queensland, Samoa and elsewhere formed a contact language drawing vocabulary mainly from English, with contributions from German (during Germany's colonial rule in New Guinea), Malay and local languages such as Tolai. Plantation labourers returning to New Guinea spread the language, which expanded and stabilised during the Australian administration and the Second World War, and became a symbol of national identity at independence in 1975.

Features

The vocabulary is largely English-derived but often shifted in meaning: gras means hair as well as grass; bel (belly) is the seat of emotions. Grammar is analytic and distinct from English: pronouns distinguish inclusive and exclusive "we" (yumi vs mipela) and number, including dual and trial forms; the suffix -pela marks many adjectives; bin (from "been") marks past tense; and the predicate marker i precedes verbs. Examples include haus sik (hospital, "sick house") and maus gras (moustache, "mouth grass").

Use today

Tok Pisin is used in the National Parliament, in radio and television, and in print media such as the newspaper Wantok. A complete Tok Pisin Bible translation exists. Its status continues to evolve: some educators favour English-medium schooling, while Tok Pisin's role as the everyday language of interethnic communication and national unity remains strong, and urban varieties show increasing influence from English.
