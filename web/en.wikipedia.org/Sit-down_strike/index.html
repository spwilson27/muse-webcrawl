Sit-down strike

Sit-down strike Type: Strike action; workplace occupation
Notable example: Flint sit-down strike, 1936–37

A sit-down strike is a form of civil disobedience and strike action in which an organized group of workers, usually employed at factories or other centralised locations, take possession of the workplace by "sitting down" at their stations. By occupying the plant, workers prevent the employer from replacing them with strikebreakers or removing equipment, and so hold a strong bargaining position.

History

Occupations of factories occurred in Europe in the early twentieth century, notably in Italy during the Biennio Rosso of 1919–20 and in France during the strikes of June 1936, when workers occupied factories across the country. In the United States, smaller sit-down strikes took place among rubber workers in Akron, Ohio, in 1936.

The most famous sit-down strike in American history was the Flint sit-down strike of 1936–37, in which members of the United Automobile Workers occupied several General Motors plants in Flint, Michigan, for 44 days. The strike ended with GM recognising the UAW as the bargaining agent for its workers, a landmark victory that triggered a wave of union organising across American industry.

Legality

In the United States, the Supreme Court held in NLRB v. Fansteel Metallurgical Corp. (1939) that workers who seize and hold an employer's property during a sit-down strike may lawfully be discharged, even where the employer's unfair labour practices provoked the strike. The tactic consequently declined in the United States, though workplace occupations have recurred in other countries, including the factory occupations in Argentina after 2001.
