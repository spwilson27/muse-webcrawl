Parathyroid hormone

Source: Parathyroid glands
Target: Bone, kidney, intestine
Function: Raises blood calcium levels

Parathyroid hormone (PTH), also called parathormone or parathyrin, is a peptide hormone secreted by the parathyroid glands that regulates serum calcium concentration through its effects on bone, kidney, and the intestine. PTH is secreted in response to low blood serum calcium levels, detected by calcium-sensing receptors on the chief cells of the parathyroid glands. It is an 84-amino-acid polypeptide, synthesised as a larger precursor and stored in secretory granules.

Actions

PTH raises blood calcium in several ways. In bone, it stimulates osteoblasts, which in turn activate osteoclasts to resorb bone and release calcium and phosphate into the blood. In the kidney, it increases calcium reabsorption in the distal tubule, promotes phosphate excretion, and stimulates the production of calcitriol (active vitamin D), which increases intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate. The overall effect of PTH is to increase serum calcium while decreasing serum phosphate.

Clinical significance

Excess PTH (hyperparathyroidism), most often from a benign parathyroid adenoma, causes hypercalcemia, kidney stones, bone loss, and abdominal and neuropsychiatric symptoms — classically summarised as "stones, bones, groans, and moans". Too little PTH (hypoparathyroidism), usually following thyroid or neck surgery, causes hypocalcemia with tingling and muscle spasms. Recombinant PTH analogues such as teriparatide are used as anabolic treatments for osteoporosis, exploiting the fact that intermittent low doses build bone even though chronic elevation breaks it down.
