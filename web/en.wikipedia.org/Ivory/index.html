Ivory is a hard, white material from the tusks and teeth of animals, consisting mainly of dentine. The term traditionally refers to elephant tusks, but the teeth and tusks of other animals — walrus, narwhal, hippopotamus, sperm whale, and warthog — are also ivory, as are the fossil tusks of mammoths. Prized since prehistory for its smoothness, durability, fine grain, and ease of carving, ivory has been used for art, religious objects, piano keys, billiard balls, and countless luxury goods.

Structure and identification

Ivory is dentine: a mineralized tissue of hydroxyapatite and collagen laid down continuously as a tusk grows. In cross-section, elephant ivory displays intersecting arcs called Schreger lines; the angles of these lines (obtuse in elephants, acute in mammoths) allow experts — and customs officers — to distinguish species and to separate true ivory from bone, antler, vegetable substitutes such as tagua nut, and plastics.

Uses through history

Some of the oldest known works of art are carved ivory: the Venus of Hohle Fels (c. 40,000 years old) is mammoth ivory. Ivory was treasured in ancient Egypt, the Near East, Greece and Rome, in Byzantine and medieval Christian art (diptychs, reliquaries, croziers), in Islamic and Indian carving, in Chinese and Japanese traditions (including netsuke), and in the scrimshaw of whalers. In the 19th and early 20th centuries industrial demand for piano keys, billiard balls, cutlery handles, and combs consumed enormous quantities, driving intensive hunting of African and Asian elephants.

The ivory trade and conservation

Poaching for ivory devastated elephant populations in the 1970s and 1980s; in 1989 the parties to CITES banned international commercial trade in African elephant ivory (Appendix I listing, effective 1990), and prices and poaching initially fell. Controversy continued over one-off sales of stockpiled ivory to Japan (1999) and China (2008), and a renewed poaching crisis in the 2010s — driven largely by demand in East Asia — led China to close its domestic ivory market at the end of 2017, with the United States, the United Kingdom, and others enacting near-total domestic bans. Confiscated stockpiles have been publicly burned or crushed, most famously by Kenya. Legal trade in ancient mammoth ivory from Siberia complicates enforcement because it can serve as a cover for elephant ivory. Substitutes — bone, tagua ("vegetable ivory"), and synthetic resins — now serve most former uses.
