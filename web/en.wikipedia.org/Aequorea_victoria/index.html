Aequorea victoria

Aequorea victoria, also called the crystal jelly, is a bioluminescent hydrozoan jellyfish found off the west coast of North America, from the Bering Sea to southern California. Nearly transparent, it measures roughly 3–20 cm across and is notable in science as the source of two famous proteins: aequorin and green fluorescent protein (GFP).

Bioluminescence

Along the margin of its bell, the jellyfish carries more than a hundred small photogenic organs. When disturbed, calcium ions trigger aequorin to emit blue light, which GFP then converts to green light — a process involving Förster resonance energy transfer. Wild populations are thought to use the display to startle or confuse predators.

Scientific importance

Osamu Shimomura began collecting A. victoria at Friday Harbor, Washington, in 1961, isolating aequorin and GFP. Their development as research tools — GFP as a fluorescent tag for proteins and gene expression — earned Shimomura, Martin Chalfie, and Roger Tsien the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. The jellyfish eats small crustaceans, fish eggs, and other jellies, and is itself prey for sea birds, fish, and other jellyfish.
