Structural anthropology

Structural anthropology is a school of sociocultural anthropology based on Claude Lévi-Strauss's 1949 idea that seemingly unrelated phenomena in human culture — myths, kinship systems, rituals, and customs — are expressions of common, often unconscious, underlying structures of the human mind. The term also names his influential 1958 book, Structural Anthropology.

Theory

Drawing on structural linguistics (Saussure and Jakobson), Lévi-Strauss argued that cultural phenomena should be studied as systems of relations organized by binary oppositions — raw/cooked, nature/culture, life/death — and by rules of transformation. Kinship, he proposed, rests on exchange and the incest taboo; myths across societies are variations on shared structural templates, analysed in his four-volume Mythologiques.

Influence and criticism

Structural anthropology profoundly influenced literary theory, philosophy, and the human sciences, feeding into structuralism and post-structuralism. Critics charged it with neglecting history, agency, and material conditions, and later anthropologists, including Lévi-Strauss's students, moved toward practice theory and interpretive approaches.
