Datec. 150–100 BC
MaterialBronze gears in a wooden case

The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery (model of the Solar System), described as the oldest known example of an analogue computer. It was used to predict astronomical positions and eclipses decades in advance, and could also track the four-year cycle of the Olympic Games. The device was recovered in 1901 from the Antikythera shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera.

Discovery and study

Sponge divers found the wreck, and among statues and pottery was a lump of corroded bronze containing gears — initially overlooked. Physicist Derek de Solla Price argued in the 1970s that it was an astronomical calculator. Modern imaging, including X-ray computed tomography by the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, revealed inscriptions and at least 37 meshing bronze gears, the largest about 14 cm across, modelling the irregular motion of the Moon (via a pin-and-slot mechanism following Hipparchus's theory), the Sun, and probably the five known planets.

Function

The front dial showed the zodiac and Egyptian calendar; back dials displayed the Metonic and Saros cycles for predicting lunisolar calendars and eclipses. A crank drove the mechanism; a "user manual" inscribed on the case described the displays. Reconstructions demonstrate workmanship not matched again until medieval astronomical clocks of the 14th century.
