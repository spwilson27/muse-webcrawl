Salmon

Salmon (plural salmon) are ray-finned fish in the subfamily Salmoninae of the family Salmonidae, closely related to trout and char. The name commonly refers to species in the genera Salmo (Atlantic salmon) and Oncorhynchus (Pacific salmon). They are famous for their anadromous life cycle: born in freshwater streams, migrating to the ocean to grow, and returning to their natal rivers to spawn.

Life cycle

Female salmon lay eggs in gravel nests called redds. The young (alevin, then fry, parr and smolts) migrate downstream to the sea, where they spend one to five years feeding and growing before returning — guided by smell — to the stream of their birth. Pacific salmon die after spawning once (semelparity); Atlantic salmon may survive to spawn again. The migration, during which adults stop feeding and fight currents, falls and predators, is a keystone ecological event, feeding bears, eagles and forests with marine nutrients.

Species

Pacific species include Chinook (king), coho (silver), sockeye (red), pink (humpback), chum (dog) and masu salmon. The Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) ranges both sides of the North Atlantic.

Commercial importance and conservation

Salmon are among the world's most valuable food fish, eaten fresh, smoked and canned. Wild populations have declined from damming, habitat loss, overfishing and climate change, while aquaculture — dominated by Norway, Chile, Scotland and Canada farming Atlantic salmon — now supplies most of the market, bringing its own controversies over sea lice, escapes and disease. Salmon hold deep cultural significance for Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest.
