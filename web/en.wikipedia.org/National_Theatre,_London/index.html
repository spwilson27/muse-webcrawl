The Royal National Theatre of Great Britain, commonly known as the National Theatre (NT), is a performing arts venue and associated theatre company located on the South Bank of the River Thames in London. Founded in 1963, it is one of the United Kingdom's most prominent publicly funded performing arts venues, alongside the Royal Shakespeare Company and the Royal Opera House.

History

Proposals for a national theatre dated back to the mid-19th century, but it was not until 1963 that the company was established under its first artistic director, Laurence Olivier, initially based at the Old Vic. The company's permanent home, a landmark of Brutalist architecture designed by Denys Lasdun, opened in stages between 1976 and 1977. Prince Charles later remarked on the building's resemblance to a nuclear power station, though it has since been listed and widely admired by architects.

Venues

The building contains three auditoria: the Olivier Theatre, a large open-stage auditorium modelled on the ancient Greek theatre at Epidaurus; the Lyttelton Theatre, a more conventional proscenium-arch space; and the Dorfman Theatre (formerly the Cottesloe), a flexible studio space. The theatre also produces touring productions and broadcasts performances to cinemas worldwide through National Theatre Live.

Artistic directors

Successive artistic directors have included Peter Hall, Richard Eyre, Trevor Nunn, Nicholas Hytner, and Rufus Norris. Under Hytner, the theatre enjoyed major commercial successes such as War Horse and One Man, Two Guvnors, which transferred to the West End and Broadway.
