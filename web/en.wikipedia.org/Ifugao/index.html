Ifugao

Ifugao is a landlocked province of the Philippines in the Cordillera Administrative Region of Luzon. Its capital is Lagawe. The province is also the name of the ethnolinguistic group native to it — the Ifugao people, an Igorot group numbering roughly 170,000 — and of their Austronesian language. Ifugao is world-famous for its rice terraces, especially the clusters at Banaue, Batad, Hapao, Kiangan and Mayoyao, inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites in 1995 as the Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras.

Geography and history

The province is mountainous, cut by the Lamut and Ibulao rivers. The Ifugao resisted Spanish colonisation for most of the colonial period — Spain established missions and garrisons only in the 19th century — and the region saw heavy fighting in 1945, when General Tomoyuki Yamashita surrendered at Kiangan after the end of the Second World War. The province was created in 1966 when the old Mountain Province was divided; Kiangan, site of a national shrine to the surrender, is regarded as the cultural heartland and the likely origin of the Ifugao's epic tradition.

Culture

Ifugao culture centres on wet-rice cultivation on irrigated terraces fed by communal forests (muyong). The hudhud — epic chants sung during harvests and funerals, led by women — were proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2001. Traditional houses (bale) are elevated, windowless pyramid-roofed structures; woodcarving, notably the rice-guardian figures called bulul, and woven textiles remain important crafts. The traditional religion venerates a large pantheon of deities and ancestral spirits, officiated by priests (mumbaki), though most Ifugao today are Christian.

Economy

Agriculture — rice, sweet potatoes, vegetables, coffee — dominates livelihoods, supplemented by tourism drawn to the terraces and by remittances. The terraces face threats from abandonment as younger people leave for cities, prompting conservation programmes by government and UNESCO partners.
