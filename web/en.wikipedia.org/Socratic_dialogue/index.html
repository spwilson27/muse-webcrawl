Socratic dialogue

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Plato, author of the most famous Socratic dialogues

Socratic dialogue (Ancient Greek: Σωκρατικὸς λόγος) is a genre of literary prose developed in Greece at the turn of the fourth century BC. The dialogues are either dramatic or narrative and typically present Socrates in conversation with others, examining moral and philosophical problems through a method of questioning known as the elenchus.

Authors and works

The most famous Socratic dialogues are those of Plato, including the Apology, Crito, Euthyphro, Phaedo, Symposium, and Republic. Xenophon also wrote Socratic works, notably the Memorabilia and his own Symposium and Apology. Other members of Socrates' circle — Aeschines of Sphettus, Antisthenes, Aristippus, Euclid of Megara, and Phaedo of Elis — wrote dialogues that survive only in fragments.

Method

In the dialogues, Socrates typically professes ignorance and questions an interlocutor's confident claims about concepts such as justice, piety, courage, or knowledge. Through a series of questions, the interlocutor's definitions are shown to be inconsistent, ending often in aporia (puzzlement). Later Platonic dialogues move beyond this negative method toward positive doctrine.

Influence

The genre shaped Western philosophy and literature, influencing Cicero, Lucian, and later dialogue writers from Galileo to Hume.
