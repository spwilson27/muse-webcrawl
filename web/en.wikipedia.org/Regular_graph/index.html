Regular graph

In graph theory, a regular graph is a graph where each vertex has the same number of neighbours; that is, every vertex has the same degree. A regular graph with vertices of degree k is called a k-regular graph or a regular graph of degree k. Regular graphs of degree at most 2 are easy to classify: a 0-regular graph consists of disconnected vertices, a 1-regular graph consists of disconnected edges, and a 2-regular graph consists of a disjoint union of cycles.

Examples

3-regular graphs are known as cubic graphs; the complete graph K4 and the Petersen graph are famous examples. Complete graphs Kn are (n−1)-regular. The cycle graph Cn is 2-regular, and complete bipartite graphs Kn,n are n-regular. Platonic solids form regular graphs: the tetrahedron gives K4, the cube and octahedron give 3- and 4-regular graphs respectively, and the dodecahedron and icosahedron give 3- and 5-regular graphs.

Properties

By the handshaking lemma, a k-regular graph with an odd number of vertices must have k even. The eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix of a k-regular graph have k as the largest eigenvalue. A regular graph that is also strongly connected and whose edge connectivity equals its vertex connectivity is called super-regular. A stronger notion is that of a strongly regular graph, in which any two adjacent vertices share the same number of common neighbours and any two non-adjacent vertices also share a common number. Strongly regular graphs include the Petersen graph and the Paley graphs.

Existence

A k-regular graph on n vertices exists if and only if nk is even and k < n. Random regular graphs and expander graphs derived from them are important in computer science, coding theory and network design.
