Creed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Nicene Creed in manuscript

A creed, also known as a confession of faith, a symbol, or a statement of faith, is a statement of the shared beliefs of a community (often a religious community) in a form structured by subjects which summarise the core principles. The term comes from the Latin credo ("I believe"), the opening word of the Apostles' and Nicene creeds in Latin.

Christian creeds

The earliest Christian creeds arose in connection with baptism. The Apostles' Creed, in its received form dating from late antiquity, remains the most widely used creed in Western Christianity. The Nicene Creed was formulated at the First Council of Nicaea (325) and expanded at Constantinople (381) to define orthodox belief in the Trinity against Arianism; it is recited in the liturgy of most Christian churches. Other classical statements include the Athanasian Creed and the Chalcedonian Definition (451).

During the Reformation, Protestant traditions produced confessions such as the Augsburg Confession (1530), the Belgic Confession and the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646). Many modern denominations and movements prefer informal "statements of faith", while some traditions — such as the Quakers and many Baptists — historically resist formal creeds ("no creed but the Bible").

Other uses

Analogous short statements of belief exist in other religions, such as the Jewish Shema. The word is also used metaphorically for any set of guiding principles, as in political or professional creeds.
