Pentarchy

The Pentarchy (from Greek pente, "five", and archein, "to rule") is a model of Church organisation in Eastern Christianity in which the universal Church is governed by the heads (patriarchs) of the five major episcopal sees: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem. The theory held that decisions binding on the whole Church required the agreement of these five patriarchs, by analogy with the five senses.

Development

The three apostolic sees of Rome, Alexandria and Antioch were recognised early; Constantinople was elevated by the Council of Constantinople (381) and the Council of Chalcedon (451) as "New Rome", and Jerusalem was recognised as a patriarchate at Chalcedon. The Emperor Justinian I gave the pentarchic order legal form in his legislation in the 6th century, and the Quinisext Council (692) ratified the ranking of the five sees.

Decline

The Arab conquests of the 7th century reduced the patriarchates of Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem to small communities under Muslim rule, leaving Rome and Constantinople dominant. Growing tensions between the Latin West and the Greek East culminated in the East–West Schism of 1054, after which the pentarchic system ceased to function in practice. Within Eastern Orthodoxy the idea survived as a theory of conciliar governance, with the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople recognised as primus inter pares ("first among equals") among the patriarchs.

The title "Pentarchy" is occasionally applied by analogy to other groups of five powers, such as the concert of great powers in 19th-century Europe.
