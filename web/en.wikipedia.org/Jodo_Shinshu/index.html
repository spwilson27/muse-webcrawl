Jōdo Shinshū

Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗, "The True Essence of the Pure Land Teaching"), also known as Shin Buddhism or True Pure Land Buddhism, is a school of Pure Land Buddhism founded by the Japanese monk Shinran.

For the macron-spelled article variant, see the Japanese-script titling convention.

Jōdo Shinshū is a school of Pure Land Buddhism founded by Shinran (1173–1263), a disciple of Hōnen, the founder of the independent Japanese Pure Land school Jōdo-shū. Shin Buddhism is widely considered the most widely practiced branch of Buddhism in Japan, and its congregations through the Buddhist Churches of America and related organizations are among the oldest Buddhist communities in the United States.

Doctrine

The central practice of Jōdo Shinshū is the nembutsu, the recitation of the name of Amida Buddha: Namo Amida Butsu ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha"). Unlike earlier Pure Land interpretations, Shinran taught that the nembutsu is not a meritorious practice performed by the devotee to earn birth in the Pure Land, but rather an expression of gratitude for Amida's already-accomplished saving work — the working of the Primal Vow (hongan).

Shinran radicalized Hōnen's teaching by emphasizing shinjin (entrusting heart) and the doctrine of tariki ("other-power") over jiriki ("self-power"). In his major work, the Kyōgyōshinshō, Shinran argued that even a single utterance of the nembutsu, arising from genuine entrusting, is sufficient, because salvation depends entirely on Amida's compassion rather than human calculation (hakarai).

History

After Shinran's death, his daughter Kakushinni helped preserve his teachings, and his great-grandson Kakunyo organized the Hongan-ji lineage. Under the eighth monshu, Rennyo (1415–1499), Jōdo Shinshū expanded dramatically among peasants and provincial warriors, spreading through letters (ofumi) and confraternities. The powerful Hongan-ji later split into the Nishi (West) and Higashi (East) branches under Tokugawa pressure in 1602.

Practice and institutions

Shin temples are led by priests who, following Shinran's example, may marry and eat meat — a marked departure from monastic norms. Lay practice centers on listening to the Dharma, services chanting Shinran's hymns (wasan), and observances such as Hōonkō, the memorial of Shinran's death. The head temples are Nishi Hongan-ji and Higashi Hongan-ji in Kyoto.
