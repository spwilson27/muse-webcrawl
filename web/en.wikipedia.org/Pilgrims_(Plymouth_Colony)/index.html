Pilgrims (Plymouth Colony)

The Pilgrims, also known as the Pilgrim Fathers, were the English settlers who travelled to North America on the Mayflower and established the Plymouth Colony in Plymouth, Massachusetts. Their leadership came from the religious congregations of Brownists, or Separatist Puritans, who had fled religious persecution in England for the tolerance of 17th-century Holland in the Netherlands.

Voyage

The Mayflower set sail from Plymouth, England, in September 1620 with 102 passengers. After a stormy crossing, the ship anchored at Cape Cod in November 1620. Before going ashore, the adult male colonists signed the Mayflower Compact, an agreement to form a self-governing body politic — often cited as an early example of democratic self-government.

First years

Nearly half the colonists died during the first winter. In March 1621 the survivors made contact with the Wampanoag people; with help from Tisquantum (Squanto), who taught them to cultivate corn and fish, the colony survived, and in the autumn of 1621 celebrated a harvest feast that is remembered as the First Thanksgiving.

Legacy

The Pilgrims became central to American founding mythology; Plymouth Rock, the Mayflower Compact and Thanksgiving are enduring symbols. William Bradford's history Of Plymouth Plantation is the primary source for the colony's early years.
