The Russian Revolution was a period of political and social revolution across the territory of the Russian Empire in 1917, comprising two main events: the February Revolution, which overthrew Tsar Nicholas II and ended centuries of monarchy, and the October Revolution, in which the Bolshevik Party under Vladimir Lenin seized power from the Provisional Government. (The names follow the Julian calendar then in use in Russia.)

February Revolution

Russia's disastrous involvement in the First World War brought military defeat, food shortages, and mass discontent. In March 1917 (February, Old Style), strikes and demonstrations in Petrograd escalated into a general uprising; the garrison mutinied, and Nicholas II abdicated. Power passed uneasily between the liberal-led Provisional Government and the Petrograd Soviet of workers' and soldiers' deputies — the "dual power".

October Revolution

The Provisional Government under Alexander Kerensky continued the war and postponed land reform, losing support as the army disintegrated. Lenin's Bolsheviks, promising "peace, land, and bread" and championing the soviets, gained majorities in the key cities. On 7 November 1917 (25 October, Old Style), Bolshevik Red Guards and troops organised by Trotsky's Military Revolutionary Committee seized key points in Petrograd and stormed the Winter Palace, arresting the Provisional Government with little bloodshed.

Consequences

The Bolsheviks withdrew Russia from the war at Brest-Litovsk, nationalised industry and land, and defeated the White armies in the Russian Civil War (1917–1922), at the cost of millions of lives and a devastating famine. In December 1922 the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was proclaimed. The revolution profoundly shaped the 20th century, inspiring communist movements worldwide and defining the geopolitical order that led to the Cold War.
