Yoshino Sakuzō (1878–1933) was a Japanese academic, historian, and political thinker, widely regarded as the leading intellectual voice of the Taishō democracy movement. A professor at Tokyo Imperial University, he used the pages of general-interest magazines such as Chūōkōron to advocate democratic reform within the framework of the Meiji constitution.

Minponshugi

Yoshino is best known for his doctrine of minponshugi — often translated "people-centred politics" or "government for the people's welfare" — which he carefully distinguished from minshushugi (democracy as popular sovereignty). Because sovereignty was constitutionally vested in the emperor, Yoshino argued that democracy in Japan should be understood as government whose policies aim at the welfare of the people, with party cabinets, universal manhood suffrage, and freedom of speech as its practical expressions. In his celebrated 1916 essay "On the Meaning of Constitutional Government and the Methods by which it can be Perfected", he maintained that this reading was compatible with the imperial institution.

Life and work

Born in Miyagi Prefecture, Yoshino graduated from Tokyo Imperial University and spent several years tutoring the son of Yuan Shikai in China before studying in Germany and Britain. Returning to Japan, he became an influential commentator on politics, supported the universal suffrage movement, opposed the oppressive Peace Preservation Law, and defended academic freedom. He also helped found the Rōnōkai and intellectual societies promoting political education. His thought shaped a generation of liberal politicians and journalists, though the democratic movement he championed faltered with the rise of militarism in the early 1930s.
