Show trial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A show trial is a public trial in which the judicial authorities have already determined the guilt or innocence of the defendant. The actual trial has as its only goal the presentation of both the accusation and the verdict to the public so that they will serve as both an impressive example and a warning to other would-be dissidents or transgressors.

Show trials tend to be retributive rather than corrective, and they are also conducted for propagandistic purposes. The term was first recorded in 1928.

Purpose

Show trials are a tool of repressive regimes seeking to persecute political dissidents and opponents while presenting the proceedings as legitimate judicial measures. Unlike ordinary trials, whose outcome is in principle uncertain and decided on the evidence, a show trial's verdict is decided in advance by the political leadership. The public, choreographed confession of the accused — frequently obtained through coercion, torture, or threats against family members — is often the centrepiece of the proceedings, serving to discredit the defendants' ideas and to demonstrate the supposed vigilance of the state against internal enemies.

History

Soviet Union

The term is most closely associated with the Soviet Union under Joseph Stalin. The Moscow Trials of 1936–1938, held during the Great Purge, saw leading Old Bolsheviks such as Grigory Zinoviev, Lev Kamenev, and Nikolai Bukharin confess to fantastic conspiracies of espionage and sabotage in open court before being executed. The trials were presided over by Vasily Ulrikh and prosecuted by Andrey Vyshinsky, whose demands for the defendants to be "shot like rabid dogs" became emblematic of the proceedings. Similar trials, such as the Shakhty trial of 1928 against engineers, had preceded them.

Eastern Europe

After the Second World War, show trials on the Soviet model were staged in the new communist states of Eastern Europe, including the trial of László Rajk in Hungary (1949), the Slánský trial in Czechoslovakia (1952), and the trial of Traicho Kostov in Bulgaria (1949). These trials targeted senior party figures accused of Titoism or Zionism and typically ended in executions.

Other examples

Show trials have been identified in various other settings, including Nazi Germany's People's Court under Roland Freisler, proceedings during China's Cultural Revolution, the trials of the Khmer Rouge era in Cambodia, and various military tribunals in authoritarian states. Historians debate the application of the term to particular cases, but its defining features — predetermination, publicity, confession rituals, and propaganda intent — remain the standard criteria.

Characteristics

  • The verdict and sentence are decided before the trial begins.
  • The proceedings are highly publicized, often filmed or broadcast.
  • Defendants confess, usually after coercive interrogation, and denounce themselves and others.
  • The defence is nominal; lawyers, if present, assist the prosecution.
  • The trial functions as political theatre intended to intimidate and educate the public.
