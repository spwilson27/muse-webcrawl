Hippodamus of Miletus (Greek: Ἱππόδαμος; c. 498 – c. 408 BC) was an ancient Greek architect, urban planner, physician and philosopher, considered the "father of European urban planning". He is credited with the Hippodamian plan, the orthogonal grid layout of streets that divides a city into regular blocks.

Life and works

Aristotle, our principal source, describes Hippodamus as the inventor of the art of planning cities and the planner of the Piraeus, the port of Athens, in the mid-5th century BC. He was also associated with the planning of Thurii in southern Italy (founded 443 BC) and, according to some sources, Rhodes. Aristotle also credits him with the first theoretical treatise on the ideal constitution: Hippodamus proposed a city of 10,000 citizens divided into three classes — artisans, farmers and soldiers — with the land divided into sacred, public and private portions.

Legacy

Although grid layouts predate Hippodamus (his native Miletus was rebuilt on a grid after 479 BC), his name became attached to rational, planned urbanism, and the Hippodamian grid influenced Hellenistic and Roman town planning, from Alexandria to the Roman coloniae, and much later the cities of the Americas.
