Trigger finger, also known as stenosing tenosynovitis, is a condition in which one of the fingers or the thumb catches or locks when bent and straightened, often with a painful snap resembling the pull and release of a trigger. It is one of the most common causes of hand pain in adults.

Mechanism

The flexor tendons of the fingers glide through a series of fibrous pulleys. In trigger finger the tendon sheath at the A1 pulley (at the level of the metacarpophalangeal joint) becomes thickened and narrowed, and a nodule may form on the tendon itself. The mismatch between tendon and sheath causes the digit to hesitate and then snap as it is extended, or in advanced cases to lock in flexion so that it must be straightened with the other hand.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms are often worst on waking. They include stiffness, a popping or clicking sensation, tenderness and a palpable nodule at the base of the affected finger on the palm side. The ring finger and thumb are most frequently involved. Congenital trigger thumb occurs in infants and usually presents as a fixed flexion deformity.

Treatment

Mild cases may respond to rest, splinting in extension, and anti-inflammatory medication. Corticosteroid injection into the tendon sheath is effective in a majority of cases, though less so in people with diabetes. Refractory cases are treated by percutaneous or open surgical division of the A1 pulley, which has a high success rate.
