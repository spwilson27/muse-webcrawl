Calvert Vaux (December 20, 1824 – November 19, 1895) was an English-American architect and landscape designer, best known as the co-designer, with Frederick Law Olmsted, of New York City's Central Park and Prospect Park. Trained as an architect in London, Vaux emigrated to the United States in 1850 to work with the influential landscape designer Andrew Jackson Downing, and after Downing's death continued the practice in Newburgh, New York.

Career

In 1857 Vaux invited the relatively inexperienced Olmsted to collaborate on their "Greensward" entry in the competition for Central Park; the design won, and the two formed a partnership that lasted, with interruptions, until 1872. Together they produced Prospect Park in Brooklyn, Delaware Park in Buffalo, and the plan of Riverside, Illinois. Vaux's architectural contributions to Central Park included the Bow Bridge, Belvedere Castle, and the Bethesda Terrace arcade with its celebrated Minton tile ceiling (executed with Jacob Wrey Mould).

As an architect, Vaux designed country houses and institutions in the Gothic Revival and Stick styles, published the influential pattern book Villas and Cottages (1857), and later designed the original buildings of the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the American Museum of Natural History (with Mould). He championed the idea, which he coined, of "landscape architecture" as a profession.

Death

Vaux's later career was overshadowed by Olmsted's fame. He drowned in Gravesend Bay, Brooklyn, in November 1895, an apparent accident. His contributions to American park design have been substantially reappraised since the late 20th century.
