Yijing (635–713), formerly romanized as I-tsing or I Ching, was a Chinese Buddhist monk of the Tang dynasty, famed as a pilgrim, traveller, and translator. Inspired by earlier pilgrims such as Faxian and Xuanzang, he journeyed by sea to India to obtain authentic Buddhist texts, and his written account remains a major historical source for Buddhism in seventh-century India and Southeast Asia.

Journey

Yijing set out in 671, sailing from Guangzhou aboard a Persian vessel to the kingdom of Srivijaya (in present-day Sumatra), where he spent six months studying Sanskrit grammar and Malay. He proceeded to the east coast of India, arriving at Tamralipti, and spent roughly a decade studying at the great monastic university of Nalanda, collecting some 400 Sanskrit texts comprising about 500,000 verses. On his return voyage he stopped for several years in Srivijaya, copying and translating texts, before finally reaching China in 695 after an absence of nearly 25 years.

Writings

Yijing's principal works include A Record of the Buddhist Religion as Practised in India and the Malay Archipelago, a detailed description of monastic life, discipline (vinaya), diet, medicine, and daily customs, and the Memoirs of Eminent Monks who Visited India and Neighbouring Regions in Search of the Law during the Great Tang Dynasty, containing biographies of some sixty pilgrim monks. He also translated numerous Sanskrit texts into Chinese, particularly works of the Mūlasarvāstivāda vinaya.

Legacy

Yijing's record is valued by historians for its precise observations of India under and after the reign of Harsha, and for its unique documentation of Srivijaya as a centre of Buddhist learning. His works were translated into English by J. Takakusu in the late nineteenth century.
