Governor General of Canada


Rideau Hall, the Governor General's Ottawa residence.

The governor general of Canada (French: gouverneure générale du Canada) is the federal representative of the Canadian monarch, currently King Charles III. The governor general exercises most of the Crown's powers in Canada — including granting royal assent to legislation, summoning and dissolving Parliament, and serving as commander-in-chief of the Canadian Armed Forces — almost always on the advice of the prime minister and Cabinet.

Appointment and role

The governor general is appointed by the monarch on the advice of the Canadian prime minister, conventionally for a term of about five years. Since the Statute of Westminster 1931 the officeholder has been appointed on Canadian advice alone, and since 1952 the post has been held by Canadians, alternating traditionally between anglophone and francophone appointees. The governor general resides at Rideau Hall in Ottawa and at the Citadelle of Quebec.

Though the office is largely ceremonial, the governor general retains reserve powers used in exceptional circumstances, as in the King–Byng Affair of 1926, when Lord Byng refused Prime Minister Mackenzie King's request for a dissolution. More routinely, the governor general reads the Speech from the Throne, hosts state visits, presents honours such as the Order of Canada, and undertakes extensive ceremonial and community work across the country. Mary Simon, appointed in 2021, was the first Indigenous person to hold the office.
