Harmon Craig

Harmon Craig (March 15, 1926 – March 14, 2003) was an American geochemist who worked at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography at the University of California, San Diego. He made fundamental contributions to isotope geochemistry, including work on the stable isotope systematics of natural waters, the carbon cycle, and the use of helium isotopes to study the Earth's mantle and hydrothermal systems.

Career

Harmon Craig

Born: March 15, 1926, New York City

Died: March 14, 2003 (aged 76), San Diego, California

Fields: Geochemistry, oceanography

Institutions: University of Chicago; Scripps Institution of Oceanography

Known for: Craig–Gordon model; stable isotope geochemistry; helium-3 studies

Craig received his Ph.D. from the University of Chicago, where he worked in the circle of Harold Urey and Willard Libby during the formative years of isotope geochemistry. He later moved to Scripps Institution of Oceanography, where he spent most of his career. He participated in numerous oceanographic expeditions, collecting samples from the deep ocean, mid-ocean ridges and hydrothermal vent fields.

Scientific contributions

In 1961 Craig published a landmark paper defining the meteoric water line — the global relationship between hydrogen and oxygen isotope ratios in precipitation (δD = 8δ18O + 10) — which remains a foundational reference in hydrology and paleoclimatology. With Larry Gordon, he developed the Craig–Gordon model describing isotopic fractionation during evaporation of water bodies. Craig's measurements of helium-3 in deep ocean waters revealed the primordial helium escaping from the Earth's mantle at mid-ocean ridges, providing key evidence for mantle degassing and for the mixing of mantle and surface reservoirs. He also measured radiocarbon and stable isotopes in ocean waters during major survey programs such as GEOSECS, contributing to understanding of ocean circulation and the global carbon cycle.

Honours

Craig was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and received numerous honours, including the Vetlesen Prize (1987), often described as the equivalent of a Nobel Prize for the earth sciences, and the Balzan Prize. He shared these distinctions with the generation of geochemists who transformed the field in the post-war decades.
