Traction control system

A traction control system (TCS), also known as ASR (from the German Antriebsschlupfregelung), is a vehicle safety feature that prevents loss of traction of the driven wheels when accelerating, particularly on slippery surfaces or when excessive throttle is applied. It is generally a secondary function of the electronic stability control system and uses the same wheel-speed sensors as the anti-lock braking system (ABS).

Operation

The system continuously compares the speeds of the driven and non-driven wheels. If a driven wheel begins to spin faster than the others, the control unit intervenes by one or more of several means: applying the brake to the spinning wheel (which, through the differential, also transfers torque to the wheel with grip), reducing engine torque by retarding ignition, cutting fuel, or closing the throttle, and in some all-wheel-drive vehicles redistributing torque between axles. Interventions happen in milliseconds and are indicated to the driver by a dashboard warning light.

History and use

Mechanical predecessors, such as Buick's MaxTrac of 1971, preceded modern electronic systems, which spread through production cars in the late 1980s and 1990s (Mercedes-Benz and BMW were early adopters) before becoming standard equipment. Traction control improves acceleration and stability on rain, snow, and gravel, but it can hinder progress in deep snow or sand, so most vehicles allow it to be partially switched off. In motorsport it has often been banned — Formula One prohibited it from 2008 — to place greater emphasis on driver skill.
