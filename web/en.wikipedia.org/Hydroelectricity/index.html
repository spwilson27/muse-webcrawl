Hydroelectricity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hydroelectricity, or hydroelectric power, is electricity generated from hydropower — the energy of falling or fast-flowing water. It is the world's largest source of renewable electricity, supplying roughly 15 percent of global electricity production. Most hydroelectric power stations use the potential energy of dammed water driving a water turbine and generator.

History

Waterwheels powered mills for millennia. Hydroelectric generation began in the late nineteenth century: in 1878 a waterwheel powered lighting at Cragside in England, and by the 1880s–90s commercial plants operated in the United States and Europe, including at Niagara Falls. The twentieth century saw giant projects such as the Hoover Dam (1936), Grand Coulee Dam, and later Itaipu (Brazil–Paraguay, 14 GW) and the Three Gorges Dam in China (22.5 GW), the world's largest power station by installed capacity.

Methods

Conventional dams store river water in a reservoir released through turbines. Run-of-river plants divert part of a river's flow with little or no storage. Pumped storage — the largest form of grid energy storage — pumps water uphill when power is abundant and releases it at peak demand. Tidal power captures energy from tidal flows.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hydropower is renewable, produces very low lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions, and offers flexible, dispatchable generation and long plant lifetimes. Against this stand high construction costs, ecological disruption, changed sediment and fish migration patterns, displacement of communities by reservoirs, and vulnerability to drought.

Global capacity

China is the largest producer, followed by Brazil, Canada, the United States, and Russia. Norway, Paraguay, and several other countries obtain most of their electricity from hydropower.
