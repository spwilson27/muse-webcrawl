Plebeians

In ancient Rome, the plebeians or plebs were the general body of free Roman citizens who were not patricians, as determined by the censor. From the 4th century BC or earlier, they were among the social classes of citizens. Plebeians were, in short, all citizens who did not hold a patrician name; they comprised the majority of the population and included farmers, craftsmen, merchants and soldiers.

Conflict of the Orders

During the early Republic, plebeians were excluded from most political and religious offices, and intermarriage with patricians was forbidden. The resulting struggle, known as the Conflict of the Orders (494–287 BC), saw the plebeians use secession — mass withdrawal from the city — to win concessions, including the creation of the tribunes of the plebs, the codification of law in the Twelve Tables, and eventually access to the consulship and full legal equality under the Lex Hortensia (287 BC).

Later history

By the late Republic the distinction between patrician and plebeian had largely lost its political force, though it retained social and legal significance. Some of Rome's most powerful families, such as the plebeian gens Claudia's rivals the Julii's opponents, were plebeian. In later usage, "plebeian" came to mean a commoner or member of the lower classes in general.
