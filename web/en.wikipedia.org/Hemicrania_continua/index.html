Hemicrania continua
SpecialtyNeurology
ClassificationTrigeminal autonomic cephalalgia
Key featureContinuous one-sided headache
Diagnostic hallmarkAbsolute response to indomethacin

Hemicrania continua is a primary headache disorder characterised by a continuous, strictly one-sided headache of fluctuating intensity that is present for months or years without remission (or, in a remitting subtype, with pain-free breaks). It belongs to the group of trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias, which also includes cluster headache and paroxysmal hemicrania. The disorder was first described in 1984 by Sjaastad and Spierings.

Symptoms

The background pain is usually mild to moderate, pressing or throbbing, and affects one side of the head without switching sides. Superimposed exacerbations of severe pain may last minutes to days and are often accompanied by ipsilateral autonomic features — tearing, redness of the eye, nasal congestion or runny nose, eyelid swelling or drooping, and facial sweating — as well as restlessness, and sometimes migraine-like features such as nausea, photophobia and phonophobia during the severe phases.

Diagnosis

Under the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), hemicrania continua requires a unilateral headache present for more than three months with exacerbations, accompanied by autonomic features or restlessness, and — decisively — an absolute and complete response to therapeutic doses of the anti-inflammatory drug indomethacin. This dramatic drug response is considered a defining hallmark and part of the diagnostic criteria. Brain imaging, typically MRI, is recommended to exclude secondary causes that can mimic the disorder, such as pituitary tumours and arterial dissection.

Treatment

Indomethacin is the treatment of choice and usually abolishes the headache completely; doses are tapered to the lowest effective level, often with gastroprotection because of the drug's side effects. For patients who cannot tolerate indomethacin, alternatives reported include other NSAIDs such as celecoxib, topiramate, gabapentin, melatonin, greater occipital nerve blocks and, in refractory cases, occipital nerve stimulation. The condition is considered under-recognised and is frequently misdiagnosed for years as migraine or chronic daily headache.
