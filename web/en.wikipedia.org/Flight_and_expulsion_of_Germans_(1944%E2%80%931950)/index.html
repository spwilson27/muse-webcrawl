Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944–1950)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The flight and expulsion of Germans during and after World War II was the forced migration of ethnic Germans (Volksdeutsche) and German citizens (Reichsdeutsche) from Central and Eastern Europe between 1944 and 1950. Between 12 and 14.6 million Germans fled or were expelled from territories that became part of Poland, the Soviet Union, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, and Yugoslavia — the largest forced population movement in European history.

Phases

The migration occurred in three overlapping phases. First, organised evacuations and spontaneous flight ahead of the advancing Red Army began in mid-1944, including the evacuation of East Prussia in early 1945, in which refugee treks and sinkings such as the Wilhelm Gustloff cost many thousands of lives. Second, "wild" expulsions by local authorities and militias followed the German surrender in 1945, before the Allied powers agreed at the Potsdam Conference (Article XIII of the Potsdam Agreement) that remaining German populations should be transferred "in an orderly and humane manner." Third, organised transports moved millions from Poland's new western territories (Silesia, Pomerania, East Brandenburg) and from Czechoslovakia's Sudetenland into occupied Germany through 1950.

Deaths and estimates

Estimates of civilian deaths during flight and expulsion vary widely, from around 500,000–600,000 in modern demographic research to earlier West German official figures of about 2.2 million. Expellees arrived in a ruined, divided Germany; in West Germany they formed a major political constituency organised in the Federation of Expellees, and their integration was a central theme of postwar society. The events remain politically sensitive in German–Polish and German–Czech relations, though treaties and joint historical commissions have worked toward reconciliation.
