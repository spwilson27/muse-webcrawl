Knot theory is the branch of topology that studies mathematical knots — closed curves embedded in three-dimensional space that cannot be untangled without cutting. Unlike everyday knots with loose ends, a mathematical knot forms a closed loop; the simplest nontrivial example is the trefoil knot.

History

The subject began in the 1860s with Lord Kelvin's hypothesis that atoms were knotted vortices in the ether, which prompted Peter Guthrie Tait to tabulate knots. Although the physics was wrong, the mathematics flourished: Kurt Reidemeister showed in the 1920s that three local moves (Reidemeister moves) relate any two diagrams of the same knot.

Knot invariants

An invariant is a quantity unchanged under Reidemeister moves, used to distinguish knots. J. W. Alexander introduced the Alexander polynomial in 1928; Vaughan Jones's discovery of the Jones polynomial in 1984 — later connected to physics and quantum computing — revolutionised the field. Other tools include tricolourability, crossing number, knot groups and hyperbolic geometry of knot complements.

Applications

Knot theory applies to the study of DNA recombination, where enzymes act by cutting and rejoining strands, to molecular chemistry (synthetic knotted molecules), statistical mechanics and topological quantum computing.

Open problems

Classifying knots completely remains hard; tables now extend to knots with many crossings, and questions such as the slice-ribbon conjecture are still open.
