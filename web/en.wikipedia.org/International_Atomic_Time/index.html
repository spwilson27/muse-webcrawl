International Atomic Time

International Atomic Time (French: Temps atomique international, abbreviated TAI) is a high-precision atomic coordinate time standard based on the notional passage of proper time on Earth's geoid. TAI is a weighted average of the time kept by over 450 atomic clocks in about 80 national laboratories worldwide. It is a continuous scale of time, without leap seconds, and it is the principal realization of Terrestrial Time.

TAI was initially set approximately to Universal Time at 1 January 1958, when the two scales coincided. Since then, TAI has drifted ahead of UT1 because Earth's rotation has slowed slightly. TAI underlies Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), from which civil time zones are derived; UTC differs from TAI by an integer number of leap seconds. As of 2017, TAI is ahead of UTC by 37 seconds.

Operation

TAI is a weighted average of the output of the participating clocks, most of which are caesium fountain or hydrogen maser clocks. The International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM), based in Sèvres, France, combines the clock readings, giving most weight to the best-performing clocks. The result is published monthly in the BIPM's Circular T.

By contrast, UTC is a discontinuous timescale: it is adjusted by the insertion of leap seconds to keep it within 0.9 seconds of UT1, the time determined by Earth's rotation. Because the difference between TAI and UTC changes only when leap seconds are inserted, UTC can be obtained from TAI by subtracting the accumulated leap seconds.

Uses

TAI provides the basis for UTC and hence for civil time around the world. It is also used in scientific applications requiring a uniform time scale, including satellite navigation systems and astronomical ephemerides. The Global Positioning System time scale, for example, is a continuous scale that was synchronized to UTC in 1980 and therefore runs a constant 19 seconds behind TAI.

History

Atomic timekeeping began in the mid-1950s, when the first accurate caesium atomic clocks became available. The BIPM began coordinating atomic time scales from contributing laboratories in the late 1960s, and TAI in its modern form dates from the early 1970s. In 1988, responsibility for TAI passed to the BIPM from the Bureau International de l'Heure.
