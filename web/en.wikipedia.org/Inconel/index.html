Inconel is a family of austenitic nickel–chromium-based superalloys, registered as a trademark of Special Metals Corporation. Inconel alloys are oxidation- and corrosion-resistant materials that retain their strength over a wide temperature range, and they are used wherever extreme heat and pressure would destroy ordinary metals — in jet engines, rocket motors, gas turbines and nuclear reactors.

Properties

When heated, Inconel forms a thick, stable, passivating oxide layer that protects the surface from further attack, much as aluminium does. Unlike aluminium alloys or steels, however, nickel-based superalloys keep most of their strength at high temperature: some grades are serviceable from cryogenic temperatures up to about 982 °C (1800 °F), close to their melting range. Strengthening comes from solid-solution effects and, in grades such as 718, from precipitation of intermetallic phases (gamma prime and gamma double-prime) during age hardening. The same toughness makes Inconel notoriously difficult to machine and weld by conventional techniques, and correspondingly expensive.

Common grades

  • Inconel 600 — an early, widely used grade for furnace parts and chemical processing.
  • Inconel 625 — high strength without heat treatment, outstanding corrosion resistance; used in marine and aerospace service and for bellows and piping.
  • Inconel 718 — the most widely used superalloy of all; precipitation-hardenable, weldable, and the standard material for turbine discs, rocket engine components and cryogenic tanks.
  • Inconel X-750 — used for springs, fasteners and turbine parts requiring strength up to about 700 °C.

Uses

Inconel was developed in the 1940s in England (by the Mond Nickel Company, with the name reportedly coined from "inconel" — inconel was first used in the Whittle jet engine programme). It formed the skin of the Bell X-1's successor rocket planes and the North American X-15, is used in Space Shuttle and modern rocket engine components, in Formula One and high-performance exhaust systems, and in the pressure vessels and internals of nuclear reactors. Because it resists the corrosive gases of waste incineration and chemical plants, it is also common in pollution-control and processing equipment.
