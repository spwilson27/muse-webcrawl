Interstellar extinction is a form of extinction — the absorption and scattering of electromagnetic radiation by matter between an emitting astronomical object and the observer. In the interstellar medium the responsible particles are dust grains, roughly a tenth of a micrometre in size, made of silicates, carbonaceous material, and ices. Extinction makes distant objects appear fainter and, because it is stronger at short (blue) wavelengths, redder than they really are — an effect called interstellar reddening.

Dark nebulae such as Barnard 68 are clouds of dust extinguishing nearly all visible light behind them.

Discovery and measurement

Although early astronomers suspected absorbing material in space, the existence of general interstellar extinction was convincingly demonstrated in 1930 by Robert Julius Trumpler, who found that distant open star clusters appeared systematically fainter — hence apparently farther and larger — than their angular sizes implied. Extinction at wavelength λ is expressed in magnitudes, Aλ. The selective part is measured by the colour excess E(B−V) = AB − AV = (B−V)observed − (B−V)intrinsic, and the ratio of total to selective extinction, RV ≈ 3.1, characterises the typical grain population along a sightline.

The extinction curve

Extinction generally rises from the infrared to the ultraviolet, roughly as 1/λ, but with notable structure: a broad absorption bump near 2175 Å (attributed to graphite or polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons), a far-ultraviolet rise from very small grains, and discrete infrared features from silicates at 9.7 and 18 μm and ices in dense clouds. In the infrared, extinction is far weaker than in visible light — AK is only about a tenth of AV — which is why infrared astronomy can probe the obscured Galactic Centre and star-forming clouds.

Consequences

Extinction must be corrected to derive accurate distances, luminosities, and temperatures of stars and galaxies; failure to account for it historically distorted the perceived scale of the Milky Way. Maps of extinction derived from stellar colours and infrared surveys are used in reverse to trace the three-dimensional distribution of interstellar dust.
