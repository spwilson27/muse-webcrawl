Bitumen

Bitumen is an immensely viscous, black, sticky liquid or semi-solid form of petroleum. It may be found in natural deposits, as in the La Brea Tar Pits or the oil sands of Alberta, or obtained as the residue of crude-oil refining. In North America it is commonly called asphalt (or asphalt cement), while "asphalt" elsewhere usually denotes its mixture with mineral aggregate used for paving.

Composition and properties

Bitumen is a complex mixture of high-molecular-weight hydrocarbons — principally asphaltenes dispersed in oily maltenes. It softens when heated and hardens when cooled, and because it is thermoplastic and waterproof it has been valued as an adhesive and sealant since antiquity: the peoples of Mesopotamia used it to waterproof boats and buildings, and Egyptians used it in mummification.

Uses

The great majority of refined bitumen — roughly 85 percent — is used as the binder in asphalt concrete for road surfaces, airport runways, and car parks. Most of the remainder goes into roofing felt and shingles and other waterproofing products. Bitumen extracted from oil sands is diluted ("dilbit") or upgraded into synthetic crude oil; the largest deposits are in Alberta, Canada, and the Orinoco Belt of Venezuela.
