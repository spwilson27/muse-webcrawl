The VT100 is a video terminal, introduced in August 1978 by Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC). It was one of the first terminals to support ANSI escape codes for cursor control and other tasks, and it added a number of extended codes for special keys and character attributes. The VT100's influence on the design of later terminals and terminal emulators was so great that its control sequences remain widely supported decades later.

History

The VT100 was introduced in August 1978 as a successor to DEC's earlier VT50/VT52 family. Unlike the VT52, which used proprietary escape sequences, the VT100 implemented the ANSI X3.64 standard (later ECMA-48), making it interoperable with software written for other ANSI-compatible systems. It quickly became a de facto standard terminal, against which software for Unix systems and minicomputers was commonly tested.

Description

The VT100 featured a 12-inch cathode-ray tube display capable of showing 24 lines of 80 characters, or alternatively 14 lines of 132 characters. Text attributes included bold, underline, blinking, and reverse video. The terminal included a detachable keyboard with function keys and a numeric keypad that could operate in an "application mode", sending distinct escape sequences used by full-screen applications such as text editors.

An expansion slot allowed the addition of options such as the Advanced Video Option (AVO), which enabled additional character attributes and the 132-column by 24-line mode. Later models in the series, such as the VT101, VT102, VT125 and VT131, integrated various of these options.

Legacy

The VT100's escape sequence repertoire became the model for terminal emulators such as xterm and for the console drivers of many operating systems. The later VT200 and VT300 series extended the design, and terms such as "VT100 emulation" remained in common use long after the hardware itself was discontinued.
