Shareholder

Shareholders at an annual general meeting, where they vote on company matters.

A shareholder (also called a stockholder) is an individual or institution that legally owns one or more shares of stock in a public or private corporation. Shareholders may be referred to as members of a corporation. A shareholder is a stakeholder in the corporation, entitled to a share of its profits (through dividends) and to residual claims on its assets.

Rights

Shareholders typically have the right to vote on major corporate matters, including electing the board of directors, approving mergers, and amending the corporate charter; to receive dividends when declared; to inspect certain corporate records; to transfer their shares; and to sue directors for breach of duty. Voting power is usually proportional to share ownership, though some companies issue classes of shares with unequal voting rights. Shareholders of public companies may vote in person at the annual general meeting or by proxy.

Types

Common shareholders stand last in line for a company's assets in liquidation and bear the greatest risk and reward. Preferred shareholders hold shares with priority in dividends and liquidation proceeds but usually limited voting rights. Large shareholders include institutional investors such as pension funds, mutual funds, and insurance companies, whose activism in corporate governance has grown substantially since the late 20th century.

Role in corporate governance

Modern corporate law separates ownership (shareholders) from control (directors and managers), a division analysed by Berle and Means in 1932 and giving rise to agency theory. Debates over "shareholder primacy"—the doctrine that corporations should be run to maximise shareholder value—contrast with stakeholder theories that weigh the interests of employees, customers, and communities.
