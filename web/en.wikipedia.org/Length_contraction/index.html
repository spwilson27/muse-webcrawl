Length contraction

Length contraction is the phenomenon that a moving object's length is measured to be shorter than its proper length, which is the length as measured in the object's own rest frame. It is also known as Lorentz contraction or Lorentz–FitzGerald contraction (after Hendrik Lorentz and George Francis FitzGerald) and is usually only noticeable at a substantial fraction of the speed of light. Length contraction is only in the direction in which the body is travelling. For standard objects, this effect is negligible at everyday speeds, and can be ignored for all regular purposes, only becoming significant as the object approaches the speed of light relative to the observer.

History

Length contraction was postulated by George FitzGerald in 1889 and Hendrik Antoon Lorentz in 1892 to explain the negative outcome of the Michelson–Morley experiment and to rescue the hypothesis of the stationary aether. Although both FitzGerald and Lorentz alluded to the fact that electrostatic fields in motion were deformed, it was considered an ad hoc hypothesis, as at this time there was no sufficient reason to assume that intermolecular forces behave in the same way as electromagnetic ones. In 1905, Albert Einstein gave the phenomenon a physical interpretation in his theory of special relativity, deriving it from the two postulates of relativity and the constancy of the speed of light.

Basis in relativity

In special relativity, the length of an object moving at relativistic speed v relative to an observer is given by

L = L0 √(1 − v²/c²)

where L0 is the proper length (the length of the object in its rest frame), c is the speed of light, and the factor √(1 − v²/c²) is the reciprocal of the Lorentz factor γ. The contraction is not a material compression of the object but a consequence of how lengths are measured: a length is determined by measuring the positions of both ends simultaneously, and simultaneity is relative in Einstein's theory.

Reality of length contraction

Whether the contraction is "real" has been debated. The contraction does not arise from the object being physically squeezed; an accelerometer travelling with the object would register no stress. However, the effect has measurable consequences: for example, the increased density of charge in a moving conductor produces observable magnetic effects, and the contraction is essential to explaining how muons created in the upper atmosphere can reach the Earth's surface (in the Earth frame, the atmosphere is contracted from the muon's point of view). Terrell rotation describes the visual appearance of a rapidly moving object: it appears rotated rather than simply contracted.

Experimental verification

A direct measurement of length contraction is difficult because the effect is tiny at attainable speeds. Indirect confirmations include the behaviour of heavy ions in particle accelerators, the observed pancake shape of nuclei in relativistic collisions, and the consistency of electromagnetic phenomena with the Lorentz transformations. The daily functioning of particle accelerators such as the Large Hadron Collider presupposes relativistic kinematics of which length contraction is a part.
