Edna Lewis

Edna Lewis (13 April 1916 – 13 February 2006) was an American chef, teacher, and author celebrated as the grande dame of Southern cooking. Her cookbook The Taste of Country Cooking (1976) is credited with reviving interest in traditional Southern food prepared from fresh, seasonal, local ingredients, and with establishing it as a serious American cuisine.

Edna Lewis

Early life

Lewis was born in Freetown, Virginia, a farming community founded by her grandfather and other formerly enslaved people. She learned to cook from her mother and aunts over a wood-fired stove, absorbing a cuisine organised by the seasons — spring greens and shad, summer corn and tomatoes, autumn game and harvest celebrations, winter hog killings and preserves.

Career

After moving to New York City, Lewis became the chef at Café Nicholson on the Upper East Side in 1949, cooking for patrons including Truman Capote, William Faulkner, and Tennessee Williams. Following a broken leg that kept her from the stove, she turned to writing with encouragement from editor Judith Jones. The Edna Lewis Cookbook (1972) was followed by The Taste of Country Cooking, structured around the seasonal menus of her Virginia childhood. She later cooked at Middleton Place in Charleston and returned to New York to open Gage & Tollner in Brooklyn.

Legacy

Lewis's insistence on freshness, seasonality, and respect for African-American culinary heritage profoundly influenced American chefs. In 2014 the United States Postal Service issued a stamp in her honour as part of its Celebrity Chefs series, and she is commemorated by the Edna Lewis Foundation.
