Italian East Africa (Italian: Africa Orientale Italiana, AOI) was an Italian colony in the Horn of Africa, formed on 1 June 1936 following Italy's conquest of the Ethiopian Empire in the Second Italo-Ethiopian War. It united the pre-existing colonies of Italian Eritrea and Italian Somaliland with the newly conquered Ethiopian territories under a single administration ruled by a Viceroy of Ethiopia, resident in Addis Ababa. The colony lasted only five years before being overrun by British and Commonwealth forces during the East African campaign of the Second World War.

Conquest

Benito Mussolini's Fascist regime sought to avenge Italy's humiliating defeat by Ethiopia at the Battle of Adwa in 1896 and to expand Italy's colonial empire. After the Walwal incident of December 1934 provided a pretext, Italy invaded Ethiopia on 3 October 1935 under Generals Emilio De Bono and later Pietro Badoglio. Italian forces used their overwhelming superiority in aircraft, artillery, and armour — and employed poison gas in violation of the Geneva Protocol — to defeat the Ethiopian armies. Addis Ababa fell on 5 May 1936, Emperor Haile Selassie went into exile, and on 9 May Mussolini proclaimed King Victor Emmanuel III Emperor of Ethiopia.

Administration

The AOI was divided into six governorates: Amhara, Eritrea, Galla-Sidamo, Harar, Scioa (Shewa), and Somalia. The first viceroy, Badoglio, was succeeded by Rodolfo Graziani, whose tenure was marked by brutal repression. After an assassination attempt on Graziani in February 1937, Italian forces carried out the Yekatit 12 massacres in Addis Ababa, killing thousands of Ethiopians, and later massacred the monks of Debre Libanos monastery. Ethiopian resistance fighters, the Arbegnoch (Patriots), continued guerrilla warfare throughout the occupation.

Development and settlement

The regime undertook ambitious infrastructure projects, building thousands of kilometres of roads — notably the imperial road from Addis Ababa to Mogadishu and the routes linking Eritrea with the Ethiopian interior — as well as bridges, dams, and urban redevelopment in Addis Ababa. Tens of thousands of Italian settlers were encouraged to emigrate, though numbers remained far below fascist plans. Racial segregation was progressively institutionalised, foreshadowing the racial laws of 1938 in Italy itself.

Collapse

When Italy entered the Second World War in June 1940, the AOI was isolated from Italy by British control of the Mediterranean and the Suez Canal. Italian forces under the Duke of Aosta briefly occupied British Somaliland in August 1940, but in early 1941 British, South African, Indian, and Free French forces, together with Ethiopian Patriots, counter-attacked from Sudan and Kenya. Addis Ababa was liberated on 6 April 1941 and Haile Selassie was restored to his throne on 5 May 1941, five years to the day after the occupation. The Duke of Aosta surrendered at Amba Alagi in May; the last organised Italian resistance under General Guglielmo Nasi at Gondar held out until 27 November 1941. Italy formally renounced its African colonies in the 1947 Treaty of Paris.
