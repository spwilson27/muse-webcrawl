Cognitive anthropology

Cognitive anthropology is a subdiscipline of anthropology that studies how people in different cultures perceive, think about, and organize the material and social worlds around them. It seeks to understand the shared mental models — categories, schemas, and folk taxonomies — that underlie cultural knowledge.

History

The field emerged in the 1950s and 1960s as "ethnoscience" or the "new ethnography," with scholars such as Ward Goodenough, Floyd Lounsbury, and Harold Conklin using componential analysis to map native classification systems for kinship, colour, and plants. In the 1980s and 1990s, work by researchers including Roy D'Andrade, Naomi Quinn, and Claudia Strauss shifted focus toward cultural models and schemas, and later toward connectionist and distributed cognition.

Methods and concepts

Cognitive anthropologists use freelisting, pile sorts, triad tests, frame elicitation, and consensus analysis to infer shared cultural knowledge. Key concepts include cultural models, schemas, prototypes, and folk theories — the implicit frameworks people use to interpret experience and guide action.

Relation to other fields

The discipline overlaps with cognitive psychology, linguistics, and cognitive science, contributing an emphasis on culture and real-world context to the study of mind. Applications include education, medicine, environmental decision-making, and human–computer interaction.
