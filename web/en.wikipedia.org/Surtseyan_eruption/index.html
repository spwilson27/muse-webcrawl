Surtseyan eruption
TypePhreatomagmatic (hydromagmatic) eruption
Named afterSurtsey, Iceland (eruption of 1963–1967)
ProductsFinely fragmented ash, base surges, tuff rings and cones

A Surtseyan eruption is a type of volcanic eruption that takes place in shallow seas or lakes. It is named after the island of Surtsey off the southern coast of Iceland, which emerged from the Atlantic Ocean during an eruption lasting from 1963 to 1967. Surtseyan eruptions are phreatomagmatic: the violent interaction of magma with water fragments the magma into fine ash and drives powerful explosions.

Characteristics

When basaltic magma meets shallow water, rapid heat transfer flashes water to steam, shattering the magma. The resulting eruptions throw up dense, dark jets of ash and steam — "cock's tail" plumes — accompanied by base surges, fast-moving radial clouds of gas and ash. Because the ash is wet and fine, it often consolidates into palagonite tuff, building tuff rings and cones that can grow into permanent islands if eruption continues after the vent is sealed off from the water.

Surtsey

Surtsey's eruption began underwater on 14 November 1963 and broke the surface within days; the island grew to 2.7 km². Its formation gave scientists a pristine natural laboratory, and Surtsey — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site — remains one of the world's most closely studied sites of plant and animal colonisation, closed to all but researchers.

Other examples

  • Capelinhos, Azores (1957–1958), which added new land to Faial Island.
  • Anak Krakatau, Indonesia — repeated Surtseyan activity during its regrowth after 1883 and 2018.
  • Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai (2021–2022), which began with Surtseyan phases before its climactic January 2022 explosion.
  • Ferdinandea (Graham Island), Sicily (1831), a short-lived island claimed by several nations.
