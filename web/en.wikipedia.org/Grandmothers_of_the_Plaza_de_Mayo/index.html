The Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo (Spanish: Asociación Civil Abuelas de Plaza de Mayo) is an Argentine human rights organisation dedicated to finding the children who were stolen and illegally adopted during the country's Dirty War (1976–1983), when a military dictatorship systematically disappeared thousands of dissidents.

Background

During the dictatorship, pregnant detainees were often kept alive until they gave birth in clandestine detention centres; the mothers were then murdered and their babies handed to military or police families and raised under false identities. An estimated 500 children were taken in this way. In 1977, a group of grandmothers searching for their missing children and grandchildren began meeting and demonstrating in the Plaza de Mayo in Buenos Aires, alongside the related Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo.

Work and genetic identification

The Grandmothers campaigned for the right to identity and were instrumental in the creation of the National Genetic Data Bank (Banco Nacional de Datos Genéticos) in 1987, the first institution of its kind, which stores DNA profiles so that recovered grandchildren can be identified even after the grandmothers' deaths. Working with forensic geneticists including Mary-Claire King, the organisation pioneered "grandparenthood" DNA testing. By the mid-2020s, the Grandmothers had located and restored the identity of more than 130 grandchildren.

Recognition

The organisation has received numerous international honours, including the UNESCO Félix Houphouët-Boigny Peace Prize, and has been nominated for the Nobel Peace Prize. Its long-time president, Estela de Carlotto, was reunited with her own grandson, Guido, in 2014 after a 36-year search.
