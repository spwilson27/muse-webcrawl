Half-Way Covenant

The Half-Way Covenant was a form of partial church membership adopted by Congregational churches in colonial New England in 1662. It allowed the children of baptised but "unconverted" church members—those who had not testified to a personal conversion experience—to be baptised in turn, though half-way members could not vote in church affairs or partake of the Lord's Supper.

Background

The measure responded to a generational crisis: many second-generation Puritans had not undergone the conversion experience required for full membership, leaving their children outside baptism and the churches' rolls shrinking. A synod of ministers approved the compromise in 1662. Critics saw it as a decline from founding purity—part of the "declension" lamented in jeremiads—while supporters such as Solomon Stoddard later pushed further, opening communion itself as a converting ordinance.
