Internet Message Access Protocol

The Internet Message Access Protocol (IMAP) is an Internet standard protocol used by email clients to retrieve email messages from a mail server over a TCP/IP connection. It is one of the two most prevalent protocols for email retrieval, the other being the Post Office Protocol (POP).

IMAP
Purpose: Email retrieval and mailbox management
Port: 143; 993 (IMAPS, over TLS)
Current version: IMAP4rev2 (RFC 9051, 2021)
Original author: Mark Crispin

History

IMAP was designed by Mark Crispin at Stanford University in 1986 as a remote-access mailbox protocol. The widely deployed version, IMAP version 4 revision 1 (IMAP4rev1), was specified in RFC 3501 in 2003; an updated revision, IMAP4rev2, was published as RFC 9051 in 2021 and consolidated many extensions.

Features

Unlike POP, which traditionally downloads mail to a single device, IMAP keeps messages on the server and synchronizes their state — read, replied, flagged, deleted — across multiple clients. Users can organize mail in server-side folders, search messages on the server, fetch individual MIME body parts, and maintain persistent message identifiers. IMAP supports concurrent access to the same mailbox and an IDLE extension for push-style notification of new mail.

Operation

An IMAP session proceeds through greeting, authentication, mailbox selection, and message commands such as FETCH, STORE, SEARCH, COPY, and EXPUNGE, and ends with LOGOUT. Connections are normally encrypted with TLS (IMAPS on port 993), and authentication can use passwords or mechanisms such as OAuth.

Comparison with POP3

POP3's simpler download-and-delete model suited single-computer use, whereas IMAP's server-centric model became the default for webmail and multi-device access offered by services such as Gmail, Outlook, and iCloud Mail.
