Voice-over

Voice-over (also known as off-camera or off-stage commentary) is a production technique where a voice—that is not part of the narrative (non-diegetic)—is used in a radio, television production, filmmaking, theatre, or other presentations. The voice-over is read from a script and may be spoken by someone who appears elsewhere in the production or by a specialist voice talent. Synchronous dialogue, where the voice-over is narrating the action that is taking place at the same time, remains the most common technique in voice-overs. However, asynchronous narration, which is used in cinema, is also used.

History

The technique of voice-over began in the 1920s with early radio dramas, in which narration was used to explain events that listeners could not see. With the coming of sound film, narration became a staple of newsreels, documentaries, and animated cartoons. Walt Disney's Steamboat Willie (1928) is often cited as an early use of a character voice performed off-screen. In the 1940s, film noir made extensive use of first-person voice-over narration to convey a protagonist's thoughts and to advance the plot economically.

Techniques

Character device

In film, voice-over narration can serve as a character device, giving the audience access to a character's internal thoughts, memories, or commentary on the events shown. Classic examples include the retrospective narration in Sunset Boulevard (1950) and the multiple unreliable narrators of Rashomon (1950).

Creative device

Directors such as Stanley Kubrick used ironic voice-over narration in A Clockwork Orange (1971) and Barry Lyndon (1975), where the narrator's account sometimes conflicts with the images, creating a deliberate tension.

Educational or descriptive device

Documentaries, news reports, and instructional videos rely on voice-over to explain images, translate foreign speech, or guide the viewer through a process.

Applications

  • Advertising — radio and television commercials use voice-over to promote products and services.
  • Animation and video games — voice actors provide the voices of characters who never appear on camera as themselves.
  • Dubbing — foreign-language films and series are localized with replacement dialogue recorded by voice actors.
  • Audio description — narration tracks describe visual content for blind and visually impaired audiences.
  • News and sports — commentators speak over live or recorded footage.

Voice-over artists

Voice acting is a specialized profession. Notable voice-over artists include Mel Blanc, known as "The Man of a Thousand Voices"; Don LaFontaine, whose deep voice narrated thousands of film trailers; June Foray; and James Earl Jones, the voice of Darth Vader in Star Wars. In the digital era, demand for voice-over has expanded into podcasts, audiobooks, e-learning, and automated announcement systems, though synthesized speech increasingly competes with human performers.
