Liquidity

Deep, active markets such as major stock exchanges provide high liquidity.

In business, economics and finance, liquidity is the degree to which an asset or security can be quickly bought or sold in the market at a price reflecting its intrinsic value. Cash is the most liquid asset, while real estate, collectibles and thinly traded securities are relatively illiquid. The term is also used in accounting for a firm's ability to meet its short-term obligations.

Market liquidity

A market is liquid if participants can trade large quantities quickly with little impact on price. Measures of liquidity include trading volume, the bid–ask spread (narrower spreads indicate greater liquidity), market depth and resilience after shocks. High liquidity lowers transaction costs and is considered essential to well-functioning financial markets. A sudden drying-up of liquidity — as in the flash crash of 2010 or the financial crisis of 2007–08 — can cause severe price dislocations and failures.

Accounting liquidity

For a company, liquidity refers to its ability to pay short-term debts as they fall due. Common measures include the current ratio and the quick ratio, which compare current assets (or their most liquid components) with current liabilities. A firm can be profitable yet fail through illiquidity if it cannot convert assets into cash in time.

Funding liquidity and central banks

Funding liquidity is the ease with which institutions can raise cash or borrow. Central banks act as lenders of last resort to supply liquidity to the banking system during crises, and liquidity requirements such as the Basel III liquidity coverage ratio oblige banks to hold buffers of high-quality liquid assets.

Liquidity preference and the liquidity trap

In Keynesian economics, liquidity preference describes the demand to hold money rather than interest-bearing assets. In a liquidity trap, monetary policy becomes ineffective because interest rates are near zero and people hoard cash regardless of increases in the money supply.
