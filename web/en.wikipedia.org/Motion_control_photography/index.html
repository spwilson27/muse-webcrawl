Motion control photography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Motion control photography is a technique used in visual effects and model photography in which camera movements are precisely programmed, recorded and repeated with computer-controlled rigs. Because a movement can be duplicated exactly, take after take, the technique allows separately filmed elements — miniatures, actors, matte paintings and pyrotechnics — to be composited together with perfectly matching camera motion, and enables effects such as scaling movements between models of different sizes.

History

The technique grew out of earlier mechanically linked systems and came of age with the Dykstraflex camera system developed under John Dykstra for Star Wars (1977), which used a computer-controlled crane and camera to photograph miniatures with sweeping, repeatable moves — giving static models the apparent dynamism of full-sized craft. Motion control revolutionised effects work on films such as Close Encounters of the Third Kind, Blade Runner and 2001-influenced space pictures, and later migrated to fully digital virtual cameras.

Applications

Besides spaceship and miniature work, motion control is used for time-scaling (shooting the same move at different speeds), for "multi-pass" photography of a single element — shooting beauty, matte, and lighting passes in turn — for stop-motion and go-motion animation, and in product and food advertising where identical moves must be repeated across takes. Modern systems are driven by software such as Flair and control robotic arms and track systems with sub-millimetre repeatability.
