The Transpolar Drift (also called the Transpolar Drift Stream) is a major ocean current and sea-ice drift pattern in the Arctic Ocean that carries surface water and sea ice from the Siberian coast of Russia across the North Pole region toward the Fram Strait between Greenland and Svalbard, where the ice exits into the Atlantic Ocean. It is one of the two major systems of ice circulation in the Arctic, the other being the Beaufort Gyre in the western Arctic.

Mechanism

The drift is driven mainly by wind stress on the ice and surface water, together with input of fresh water from the great Siberian rivers, the Ob, Yenisei and Lena, which discharge into the Laptev and East Siberian Seas. Sea ice formed along the Siberian shelves in autumn and winter is carried northward and westward, crossing the central Arctic over a period of roughly two to five years before exiting through the Fram Strait as part of the East Greenland Current. Drift speeds vary with the state of the Arctic Oscillation and other atmospheric circulation patterns; in some years the drift axis shifts toward the Canadian Arctic Archipelago, while in others it runs more directly from Siberia toward the Fram Strait.

Exploration

The existence of a transpolar drift was demonstrated by Fridtjof Nansen's expedition aboard the Fram (1893–1896). After earlier evidence — including wreckage from the USS Jeannette, lost off Siberia in 1881, which washed up on the southwest coast of Greenland three years later — Nansen deliberately froze his ship into the ice near the New Siberian Islands and drifted with it across the Arctic Ocean, emerging north of Svalbard after nearly three years. The Soviet North Pole-1 drifting station of 1937, led by Ivan Papanin, repeated the demonstration, drifting from near the pole to the Greenland Sea in 274 days. Drifting ice stations, icebreakers such as the Tara (2006–2008), and the MOSAiC expedition aboard Polarstern (2019–2020) have followed the same pathway to study the central Arctic.

Significance

The Transpolar Drift is the main route by which sea ice, fresh water, nutrients, sediments and pollutants are exported from the Siberian shelves to the Atlantic. It transports river-borne material and organisms across the Arctic basin and influences the salinity and stratification of the North Atlantic. Because much of the Arctic's multiyear ice once left the ocean by this route, changes in the drift are closely watched in studies of Arctic sea-ice decline: thinner, younger ice drifts faster, and recent observations indicate that the transit time from Siberia to the Fram Strait has shortened compared with the era of Nansen's Fram.
