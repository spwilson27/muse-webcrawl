Christian cross

The Christian cross, seen as a representation of the instrument of the crucifixion of Jesus, is the best-known symbol of Christianity. It is related to the crucifix, a cross that includes a corpus (a representation of Jesus's body) and to a more general family of cross symbols, including the Latin cross (a vertical bar crossed by a shorter horizontal bar near the top) and the Greek cross (with arms of equal length).

History

Although the cross was an ancient instrument of execution, early Christians were initially cautious about using it as a devotional image, preferring symbols such as the fish (ichthys) and the Chi Rho. Following the conversion of the Roman emperor Constantine in the early fourth century, the cross became the dominant emblem of the Christian faith. Tradition holds that Constantine saw a vision of a cross or Chi Rho in the sky before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312, and that his mother Helena later discovered the True Cross in Jerusalem.

Forms

Many variants of the cross exist. The Latin cross is standard in Western Christianity, while Eastern Orthodox churches commonly use crosses with three horizontal bars, the top representing the inscription above Christ's head and the slanted lower bar representing the footrest. The Celtic cross incorporates a circle around the intersection, and the patriarchal cross carries two horizontal bars. In the twentieth century the empty cross became a common symbol in Protestant churches, emphasising the resurrection rather than the crucifixion itself.

Use

Crosses are displayed in churches, worn as jewellery, placed on graves, and used in processions and heraldry. The sign of the cross, traced by hand on one's own body, is an ancient Christian prayer gesture retained in Catholic, Orthodox and some Protestant practice.
