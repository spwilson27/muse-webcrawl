Thoracentesis (/ˌθɔːrəsɛnˈtiːsɪs/), also known as thoracocentesis, pleurocentesis, needle thoracostomy, or pleural tap, is an invasive medical procedure to remove fluid or air from the pleural space for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. A cannula, or hollow needle, is carefully introduced into the thorax, generally after administration of local anesthesia. The procedure was first described in 1852.

The recommended location varies depending upon the source. It is critical that the patient hold still, exhale, or cough at specific times during the procedure. The procedure is recommended when unexplained fluid accumulates in the pleural cavity. The plural effusion can be caused by various conditions, including infection, cancer, heart failure, and liver cirrhosis.

Indications

The diagnostic evaluation and treatment of pleural effusion is the most common indication for a thoracentesis. It may be performed on an emergency basis if the accumulation is causing respiratory distress, as in pneumothorax or tension pneumothorax, though needle decompression is usually preferred in emergency situations. A thoracentesis is also recommended to differentiate the various types of pleural effusions, which can be classified as transudates or exudates.

The fluid obtained is analyzed for chemistry (protein, lactate dehydrogenase, pH, glucose), cell counts, Gram stain and culture, and cytology. These results guide further management of the underlying condition.

Contraindications

An uncooperative patient or a coagulation disorder that cannot be corrected are absolute contraindications. Relative contraindications include cases where the site of insertion has known pleural adhesion. Other relative contraindications include mechanical ventilation, bullous emphysema, and small effusions that may not warrant the risk of the procedure.

Procedure

The patient usually sits upright with arms supported. Under ultrasound guidance, the needle is inserted into the pleural space at the mid-scapular or posterior axillary line, typically at the level of the eighth, ninth, or tenth intercostal space. The needle passes over the top of the lower rib to avoid the intercostal neurovascular bundle that runs along the bottom of each rib.

Complications

Major complications are pneumothorax (3–30%), hemothorax, laceration of the spleen or liver, and re-expansion pulmonary edema. Minor complications include dry tap (no fluid obtained), subcutaneous hematoma or seroma, anxiety and dyspnea due to vagal response, and cough.

Imaging

The use of ultrasound increases the accuracy of the procedure and reduces complications. Chest X-rays may be used to evaluate for pneumothorax after the procedure. In many cases, post-procedure imaging is unnecessary if ultrasound guidance was used and there were no complications.
