Marxism–Leninism

Marxism–Leninism is a communist political ideology that became the principal ideology of the Communist International after Joseph Stalin's formalisation of it in the 1920s. It was the state ideology of the Soviet Union, the Eastern Bloc, and various other socialist states. Its proponents assert that it represents the adaptation of Marxism to the conditions of imperialism and revolution developed by Vladimir Lenin, including the vanguard party, democratic centralism, and the dictatorship of the proletariat.

Development

The term was coined in the Soviet Union during the 1920s and codified in works such as the History of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks): Short Course (1938) and Stalin's Dialectical and Historical Materialism. Lenin's contributions held to be central include his theory of imperialism as the highest stage of capitalism, his analysis of the revolutionary party in What Is to Be Done?, and the right of nations to self-determination.

Principles

Marxism–Leninism advocates the overthrow of capitalism by a proletarian revolution led by a vanguard party, followed by a socialist state that socialises the means of production and plans the economy, with the eventual goal of a classless, stateless communist society. In foreign policy it historically supported anti-imperialist and national liberation movements.

Application and variants

The ideology governed the Soviet Union until 1991 and was adopted, in modified forms, by China (where it developed into Mao Zedong Thought and later Deng Xiaoping Theory), Cuba, Vietnam, Laos and North Korea (later replaced officially by Juche). Critics associate Marxism–Leninist states with one-party rule, political repression, and censorship, while supporters credit them with rapid industrialisation, literacy, and social welfare achievements.
