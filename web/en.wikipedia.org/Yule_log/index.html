Yule log

A traditional Yule log in the hearth

A Yule log is a specially selected log traditionally burned in the hearth at Christmas, particularly in Britain, France, and other parts of Europe. The custom is associated with the midwinter festival of Yule and winter solstice celebrations, and regional names include the Yule clog in England, the Christmas block, and the bûche de Noël in France.

Customs

The log, often the largest obtainable, was brought in with ceremony on Christmas Eve and lit with a fragment of the previous year's log, which was kept to protect the house and to kindle the next fire. The log was expected to burn through the Twelve Days of Christmas; its ashes and charcoal were credited with protective and medicinal powers, and the manner of its burning was watched for omens. In Provence the cacho fio ceremony blessed the log, and in parts of Serbia the badnjak oak log fulfils a similar role.

Origins

Folklorists have connected the custom with older Germanic and Celtic solstice fires, though the documentary record is mainly from the early modern period. Robert Herrick's poetry of the 17th century describes the Christmas log in England.

Modern forms

As open hearths declined, the log survived symbolically. The bûche de Noël became a chocolate Christmas cake shaped like a log; televised "yule log" broadcasts of a burning fireplace began with New York's WPIX in 1966 and remain a seasonal staple; and some towns hold public Yule log hunts and burnings.
