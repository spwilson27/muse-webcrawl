Classical mechanics

Classical mechanics is a physical theory describing the motion of macroscopic objects — from projectiles and machinery to planets, stars and galaxies. It is the first branch of mechanics and, more broadly, the oldest branch of physics that remains in everyday use. Its predictions are extraordinarily accurate for objects that are large compared with atoms and moving slowly compared with the speed of light; where those conditions fail, it is superseded by quantum mechanics and relativistic mechanics, of which it is the limiting case.


Orbital motion is described to high accuracy by classical mechanics.

History

The theory grew out of the astronomy of Kepler and the experiments of Galileo, and was given definitive form in Isaac Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), whose three laws of motion and law of universal gravitation unified terrestrial and celestial motion. During the 18th and 19th centuries Lagrange and Hamilton recast Newtonian mechanics into analytical forms — Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics — based on energy and variational principles, which later proved essential to quantum theory.

Description

Classical mechanics describes a system by the positions and momenta of its particles evolving in time. Its core principles include:

  • Newton's laws: inertia; force equals rate of change of momentum (F = ma for constant mass); action and reaction.
  • Conservation laws: energy, linear momentum and angular momentum are conserved in isolated systems — consequences, via Noether's theorem, of the symmetries of space and time.
  • Forces: gravity, elasticity (Hooke's law), friction and electromagnetic forces acting on charges (the Lorentz force) complete the classical picture, with fields for gravity and electromagnetism.
  • Formulations: the Newtonian (vectorial), Lagrangian and Hamiltonian approaches are mathematically equivalent for most problems but differ in convenience; Hamiltonian mechanics also underlies statistical mechanics.

Domains and branches

Subfields include statics and dynamics, kinematics, continuum mechanics (solids and fluids), celestial mechanics and relativistic mechanics; the mathematical study of nonlinear systems gave rise to chaos theory — deterministic equations whose extreme sensitivity to initial conditions limits long-term prediction, as in weather dynamics. Classical mechanics fails for very small scales (where quantum effects dominate), very high speeds or strong gravitational fields (special and general relativity), and is corrected accordingly in modern physics and engineering (for instance, in GPS satellite clock corrections).
