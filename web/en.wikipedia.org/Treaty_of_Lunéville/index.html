The Treaty of Lunéville was signed on 9 February 1801 at Lunéville between the French Republic and Francis II, Holy Roman Emperor, who signed both for the Empire and for his own Austrian hereditary lands. Joseph Bonaparte negotiated for France and Count Ludwig von Cobenzl for Austria.

Background

Austria had been defeated in 1800 at the battles of Marengo and Hohenlinden, leaving it no practical alternative to peace. Negotiations had stalled over Austria's wish to include Britain, but the armistice of Teschen and French military pressure brought Vienna to terms.

Terms

The treaty broadly reaffirmed the terms of the Treaty of Campo-Formio of 1797. France's annexation of the left bank of the Rhine was confirmed, and the princes dispossessed there were to be compensated within Germany — a process carried out by the great territorial reorganisation (the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss) of 1803. Austria recognised the French client republics: the Cisalpine, Ligurian, Batavian and Helvetic republics. The Grand Duke of Tuscany was removed, his state becoming the Kingdom of Etruria.

Significance

Lunéville effectively ended the War of the Second Coalition on the continent; Britain and France made peace a year later at Amiens. The treaty marked a decisive step in the dissolution of the old imperial order in Germany, completed when Francis II abdicated the imperial crown in 1806.
