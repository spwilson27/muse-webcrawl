Telecommunications in the United States

Telecommunications in the United States encompasses the telephone, mobile, internet, broadcasting, and cable industries of the United States, regulated principally by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). The United States has one of the world's largest telecommunications markets, historically dominated by the Bell System of AT&T until its breakup in 1984.

History

The telephone was commercialized in the United States in the late 19th century, and AT&T's Bell System grew into a regulated monopoly providing near-universal service. The antitrust breakup of AT&T in 1984 created seven regional "Baby Bell" companies and opened long-distance service to competition. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 further restructured the industry, and subsequent mergers re-consolidated many of the regional companies. Meanwhile, cable television, mobile telephony, and the internet transformed the market from the 1980s onward.

Telephone and mobile

Landline subscriptions have declined steadily as mobile service has become nearly universal. The mobile market is dominated by three national carriers—Verizon, AT&T, and T-Mobile—which operate nationwide 4G LTE and 5G networks.

Internet

Broadband internet is delivered mainly by cable and fiber-optic providers, with satellite and fixed wireless serving some rural areas. Debates over rural access, affordability, and network neutrality have been central to American telecommunications policy, with federal programs funding broadband deployment in underserved areas.

Broadcasting and cable

The United States has extensive commercial radio and television broadcasting, public broadcasting, and a large cable and satellite television sector that has been losing subscribers to streaming services.
