Hiero I (Greek: Ἱέρων, Hiéron; also Hieron) was the son of Deinomenes, the brother of Gelon and tyrant of Syracuse in Sicily, reigning from 478 to 467 BC. He succeeded his brother Gelon as tyrant of Syracuse and continued the rule of the Deinomenid dynasty. Where ancient sources remembered Gelon as comparatively mild, Hiero was characterised as a harsh and suspicious ruler; yet his court became one of the great cultural centres of the Greek world, patronised by poets such as Pindar, Bacchylides, Simonides, Aeschylus and Epicharmus, several of whom composed works in his honour.

Accession and rule

On Gelon's death in 478 BC, power passed to Hiero, while a younger brother, Polyzelus, received command of the army and Gelon's widow Damarete in marriage. Ancient accounts describe tensions between the brothers, with Polyzelus eventually fleeing to Theron, tyrant of Acragas, before a reconciliation was arranged. Hiero intervened in the affairs of other Sicilian and Italian Greek cities, defeating the people of Acragas's rivals and establishing his influence over Rhegium and Messana.

In 474 BC Hiero won his most celebrated victory when the Syracusan fleet, allied with Cumae, defeated the Etruscans at the Battle of Cumae, checking Etruscan expansion in Italy. Pindar commemorated the victory in his First Pythian Ode, comparing it with the Greek victories over Persia at Salamis and Plataea.

Founder of Aetna

Hiero expelled the inhabitants of Catana (Catania) and Naxos, deporting them to Leontini, and refounded Catana under the new name of Aetna, settling it with some ten thousand colonists of Dorian stock drawn from Syracuse and the Peloponnese. He installed his young son Deinomenes as its ruler. The refoundation was celebrated by Pindar in the First Pythian Ode and dramatised in Aeschylus's lost play The Women of Aetna, commissioned for the occasion.

Court and culture

Although his government is described as severe at home—later tradition portrayed him as avaricious and distrustful—Hiero spent lavishly on display and patronage. The lyric poets Pindar and Bacchylides wrote victory odes for his wins in the chariot and horse races at the Pythian and Olympic games; Hiero's chariot team won at Olympia in 468 BC, after his death, and his earlier victories in the horse race at Delphi and Olympia were sung by both poets. The philosopher Xenophanes and the comic playwright Epicharmus were also associated with his court, and Simonides is said to have reconciled Hiero with Theron of Acragas. Xenophon's dialogue Hiero presents a fictional conversation between the tyrant and Simonides on the unhappiness of the tyrant's life.

Death and succession

Hiero, who suffered from a painful illness (described as "the stone", probably kidney or gall stones), died at Catana/Aetna in 467 BC and received heroic honours there. He was succeeded at Syracuse by his brother Thrasybulus, whose harsh rule provoked a revolt that ended Deinomenid power within a year; the settlers of Aetna were eventually expelled and the old inhabitants of Catana restored.
