Shock (circulatory)

Shock
SpecialtyEmergency medicine, critical care
DefinitionInadequate tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery leading to cellular dysfunction and death
SymptomsHypotension, tachycardia, altered mental state, cool or mottled skin, oliguria, lactic acidosis
Main typesHypovolemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, distributive (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic)
TreatmentTreat the cause; fluids, vasopressors/inotropes, transfusion, antibiotics as indicated

Shock, in medicine, is a life-threatening state of circulatory failure in which tissue oxygen delivery is inadequate to meet metabolic demand, causing cellular hypoxia, organ dysfunction, and, if prolonged, irreversible injury and death. It is not synonymous with low blood pressure — shock may exist with normal or even elevated pressure early on — and must be distinguished from emotional "shock". Shock is a common final pathway of critical illness, complicating about a third of intensive care admissions in the distributive (septic) form alone.

Signs and pathophysiology

Compensatory sympathetic activation produces tachycardia and vasoconstriction — the skin becomes pale, cool, and sweaty (except in early distributive shock, when it may be warm). As perfusion fails, patients develop rapid breathing, thirst, confusion or agitation, falling urine output, and metabolic (lactic) acidosis. Untreated, a self-reinforcing spiral of microcirculatory failure, capillary leak, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and multi-organ dysfunction leads to death; endurance of shock correlates strongly with outcome, so minutes matter.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by vital signs, bedside ultrasound, central venous oxygen saturation, and arterial blood lactate, which quantifies tissue hypoperfusion and tracks response. Identifying the type — and the underlying cause — guides treatment; haemodynamic monitoring and echocardiography help separate cardiogenic from distributive states.

Treatment

Management follows resuscitation principles: secure airway and breathing; give intravenous crystalloid fluids (or blood products for haemorrhage, with tranexamic acid where indicated); start vasopressors — norepinephrine first-line in septic shock — to maintain a mean arterial pressure around 65 mmHg; and treat the cause without delay: antibiotics and source control in sepsis, epinephrine in anaphylaxis, reperfusion therapy in myocardial infarction, drainage in tamponade, and surgery or embolisation to stop bleeding. Early goal-directed resuscitation in the first hours is associated with improved survival.
