Philology

Philology is the study of language in oral and written historical sources; it is the intersection of textual criticism, literary criticism, history, and linguistics. Philology is more commonly defined as the study of literary texts as well as oral and written records, the establishment of their authenticity and their original form, and the determination of their meaning.

History

Classical philology developed in the Hellenistic world at Alexandria and in the Renaissance, when scholars such as Poliziano and Erasmus established critical editions of Greek and Latin texts. In the 19th century, comparative philology — pioneered by William Jones's observation of similarities between Sanskrit, Greek and Latin, and developed by Franz Bopp and Jacob Grimm — gave rise to historical linguistics and the reconstruction of Proto-Indo-European.

Branches

Branches include comparative philology, textual criticism (stemmatics), epigraphy, papyrology, and decipherment — as in Jean-François Champollion's deciphering of Egyptian hieroglyphs and Michael Ventris's deciphering of Linear B.

Modern usage

In many European universities philology remains the umbrella term for the study of a language and its literature, while in English-speaking countries "philology" often denotes the historical study of texts and languages, distinct from theoretical linguistics.
