Kepler problem in general relativity

In general relativity, planetary orbits precess, tracing a rosette rather than a closed ellipse.

The Kepler problem in general relativity concerns the motion of two bodies interacting only through gravity, within the framework of Einstein's theory of general relativity. It is the relativistic counterpart of the classical Kepler problem of Newtonian gravitation. In the usual approximation one body is taken to be so much more massive than the other that its spacetime geometry is simply the Schwarzschild metric, and the lighter body moves along a geodesic of that geometry.

Historical background

By the mid-19th century astronomers had measured an anomalous advance of the perihelion of Mercury of about 43 arcseconds per century that Newtonian perturbation theory could not explain. In November 1915 Einstein showed that general relativity accounts for exactly this residual: the relativistic correction to the effective potential adds a term proportional to 1/r4 to the Newtonian effective potential, causing the orbit's periapsis to precess by 6πGM/(c²a(1−e²)) per revolution — about 43″ per century for Mercury.

Geodesic motion in Schwarzschild spacetime

Energy and angular momentum conservation reduce the problem to one-dimensional motion in an effective potential. Unlike the Newtonian problem, the relativistic effective potential has a maximum, giving rise to an innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO) at r = 6GM/c². Orbits can be bound and periodic ("rosettes" with periapsis precession), can plunge into the central mass, or can scatter to infinity; the classification of orbits was worked out in detail by Darwin and others. The exact solution of the orbit equation is expressible in terms of elliptic functions, rather than the conic sections of the classical problem.

Applications

The same formalism underlies the prediction and measurement of gravitational redshift, the deflection and time delay of light passing near the Sun, and the dynamics of stars orbiting the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way, whose precession has been observed directly. For comparable-mass binaries the problem requires post-Newtonian expansions and numerical relativity, and its solution describes the gravitational-wave sources observed by LIGO and Virgo.
