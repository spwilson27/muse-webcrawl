Common law

Common law (also known as judicial precedent, judge-made law, or case law) is the body of law primarily developed through judicial decisions rather than statutes. It is the legal system that originated in England and is now used in many countries worldwide, especially those that were once part of the British Empire.

Unlike civil law systems, which rely mainly on comprehensive legal codes, common law places significant weight on precedent — the principle of stare decisis, under which courts are bound to follow the rulings of higher courts in similar cases.

History

The common law developed after the Norman Conquest of 1066, as royal courts gradually displaced local customs and created a single body of law "common" to all England. Judges travelled on circuit applying the king's law, and written reports of decisions accumulated into a system of precedent. Distinct from the common law courts was the Court of Chancery, which developed equity — principles of fairness, trusts, and remedies such as injunctions — to soften the rigidity of common law rules. In England and Wales the two systems were fused administratively by the Judicature Acts of 1873–75.

Features

Common law systems are characterised by:

  • Precedent (stare decisis): binding decisions of higher courts, with persuasive authority from other jurisdictions;
  • Adversarial procedure: contested proceedings before a neutral judge or jury;
  • Juries in serious criminal and some civil trials;
  • Judicial interpretation of statutes, which themselves increasingly govern fields such as criminal and commercial law;
  • Distinction between ratio decidendi (the binding reasoning of a case) and obiter dicta (persuasive remarks).

Where it is used

Common law or mixed systems prevail in England and Wales, Ireland, the United States (except Louisiana, which retains civil law influences), Canada (except Quebec), Australia, New Zealand, India, Pakistan, Hong Kong, Singapore, and many Caribbean and African jurisdictions. Scotland has a distinct mixed system.

Common law and statute

Modern common law jurisdictions are heavily statutory; legislatures can override common law rules, and courts interpret statutes using precedent about their construction. Nevertheless, whole fields — contract, tort, and property in many jurisdictions — retain a strong common law core.
