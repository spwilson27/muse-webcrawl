William Stanley Jevons

William Stanley Jevons
Born: 1 September 1835, Liverpool, England
Died: 13 August 1882, near Hastings (drowned)
Fields: Economics, logic
Known for: Marginal utility theory; Jevons paradox

William Stanley Jevons (1 September 1835 – 13 August 1882) was an English economist and logician. Irving Fisher described his book A General Mathematical Theory of Political Economy (1862) as the start of the mathematical method in economics. Jevons is one of the founders of the marginal revolution in economics, alongside Carl Menger and Léon Walras.

Economics

In The Theory of Political Economy (1871), Jevons argued that value depends entirely on utility, and that labour influences value only indirectly through its effect on the utility of the product. He formulated the theory of exchange in marginal terms: rational consumers allocate spending so that the final utility per unit of money is equalised across goods. His The Coal Question (1865) famously argued that improvements in energy efficiency tend to increase, not reduce, total consumption of a resource — the Jevons paradox. He also attempted to connect business cycles to sunspot-driven harvest fluctuations.

Logic

Jevons contributed to formal logic, developing a "logical piano" (1869), a mechanical device for evaluating logical arguments, and wrote Principles of Science (1874), a major treatise on scientific method and probability. He popularised the view that Mill's methods and Boole's algebra grounded inductive reasoning.

Life and death

Born in Liverpool, Jevons studied at University College London, spent five years in Australia as an assayer at the Sydney Mint (1854–1859), and later held professorships at Owens College, Manchester, and University College London. He drowned while swimming near Hastings in 1882, aged 46.
