July Crisis

The July Crisis was a series of interrelated diplomatic and military escalations in the summer of 1914 that led to the outbreak of the First World War. It began with the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria, heir presumptive to the Austro-Hungarian throne, and his wife Sophie by the Bosnian Serb nationalist Gavrilo Princip in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914, and ended with the great powers of Europe at war within five weeks.

Course of events

Encouraged by Germany's unconditional assurance of support — the so-called "blank cheque" of 5–6 July — Austria-Hungary resolved to crush Serbia, which it blamed for the assassination. On 23 July Vienna delivered an ultimatum designed to be rejected; Serbia accepted nearly all its demands but balked at allowing Austro-Hungarian officials to investigate on Serbian territory. Austria-Hungary broke relations and declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914.

Russia, Serbia's protector, began mobilising, first partially and then, on 30 July, generally. Germany issued ultimatums to Russia and France and, on 1 August, declared war on Russia; on 3 August it declared war on France and began implementing the Schlieffen Plan through Belgium. The violation of Belgian neutrality brought Britain into the war against Germany on 4 August. Austria-Hungary declared war on Russia on 6 August, and by mid-August all the great powers were belligerents.

Historiography

The crisis has generated an enormous literature on war guilt and responsibility. The Treaty of Versailles assigned responsibility to Germany and its allies; the German historian Fritz Fischer argued in the 1960s that Germany deliberately pursued war. More recent accounts, notably Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012), distribute responsibility among all the capitals, emphasising miscalculation, alliance rigidity, mobilisation timetables, and the Balkan rivalries of Austria-Hungary, Serbia, and Russia.
