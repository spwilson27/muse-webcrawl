The Majjhima Nikāya ("Middle-length Collection"; abbreviated MN) is a Buddhist scripture, the second of the five nikāyas, or collections, in the Sutta Pitaka of the Pali Canon, the scriptures of Theravada Buddhism. It contains 152 discourses (suttas) attributed to the Buddha and his chief disciples, of "middle length" — longer than the brief sayings of the Samyutta and Anguttara Nikayas but shorter than those of the Digha Nikaya. The Majjhima Nikaya is widely regarded as one of the richest and most important sources for early Buddhist teaching, covering doctrine, meditation, ethics, monastic discipline, and narrative episodes from the Buddha's life.

Structure

The 152 suttas are divided into three groups of fifty (paññāsa), each subdivided into five chapters (vagga) of ten:

  • Mūlapaññāsa — the "Root Fifty", opening with the Mūlapariyāya Sutta and including the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta (MN 10) on the foundations of mindfulness.
  • Majjhimapaññāsa — the "Middle Fifty", including teachings to and about the Buddha's contemporaries, princes, and householders.
  • Uparipaññāsa — the "Upper Fifty", including the Ānāpānasati Sutta (MN 118) on mindfulness of breathing and analytical expositions.

Notable suttas

  • MN 10 — Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta: the foundational discourse on mindfulness (expanded as the Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta in the Digha Nikaya).
  • MN 26 — Ariyapariyesanā Sutta: the Buddha's account of his own quest for awakening.
  • MN 36 — Mahāsaccaka Sutta: a narrative of the Buddha's ascetic struggles and enlightenment.
  • MN 63 — Cūḷamālukya Sutta: the famous parable of the poisoned arrow, on the Buddha's refusal to answer speculative questions.
  • MN 72 — Aggivacchagotta Sutta: the simile of the extinguished fire and the undeclared questions.
  • MN 141 — Saccavibhaṅga Sutta: an analysis of the Four Noble Truths.

Parallels and translations

Parallel versions of many Majjhima suttas survive in the Chinese Madhyama Āgama (translated in 397–398 CE) and in Sanskrit and Tibetan fragments, evidence of the text's transmission across early Buddhist schools. The standard modern English translation is The Middle Length Discourses of the Buddha by Bhikkhu Ñāṇamoli and Bhikkhu Bodhi (1995).
