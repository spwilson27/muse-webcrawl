Sar-e Sang is a mining settlement in the Kokcha River valley of Badakhshan Province, in the Hindu Kush of north-eastern Afghanistan. Its mines are the world's oldest and most famous source of lapis lazuli, the deep-blue stone prized since antiquity for jewellery, inlay and the pigment ultramarine. The name means "head of the stone" in Persian/Dari.

History

Archaeological evidence indicates that lapis from the Badakhshan deposits reached Mesopotamia and Egypt by at least the fourth millennium BC, and workings at Sar-e Sang are often described as among the oldest mines in the world, exploited intermittently for some 6,000 years or more. Lapis from these mines adorned the treasures of Ur, the funerary mask of Tutankhamun and, ground into ultramarine, the paintings of medieval and Renaissance Europe — a trade described by Marco Polo, who visited the region in the thirteenth century and praised its azure stone.

The mines were repeatedly fought over in the modern era. During the Soviet–Afghan War the valley lay in mujahideen-held territory, and lapis revenues helped fund the resistance of Ahmad Shah Massoud; in later decades mining has been contested among local commanders, the Afghan state and the Taliban, alongside persistent reports of illegal extraction.

Geology and mining

The lapis occurs as lenses and veins in metamorphosed limestone (marble) intruded by granites, high in the rugged Kokcha gorge. The blue mineral is lazurite, typically flecked with golden pyrite and streaked with white calcite; the finest grades are an intense, uniform violet-blue. Mining at Sar-e Sang — by shafts and adits driven into the mountainsides, historically with fire-setting and hand tools, now with explosives and jackhammers — remains small-scale and hazardous, and the rough stone is carried out by pack animal and truck to markets in Kabul, Peshawar and beyond.
