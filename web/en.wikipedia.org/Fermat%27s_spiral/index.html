Fermat's spiral

Fermat's spiral (also known as a parabolic spiral) is an Archimedean spiral first described by Pierre de Fermat in 1636. In polar coordinates it can be expressed as

r = ±a·√θ

where a is a real constant. The full spiral consists of two symmetric branches, one for each sign, mirrored about the origin. It is a special case of the parabolic spiral and a member of the family of spirals rm = amθ.

Properties

Unlike the Archimedean spiral, whose arms are equally spaced, the successive turns of Fermat's spiral grow closer together as θ increases, because r grows only with the square root of θ. The area between successive turns approaches a constant, a feature that attracted Fermat's interest in quadrature.

The curvature and arc length of Fermat's spiral can be expressed in closed form using elliptic integrals in some formulations, and the spiral is the inverse of the lituus under inversion in the unit circle.

Golden angle and phyllotaxis

When points are plotted along Fermat's spiral at increments of the golden angle (approximately 137.508°), the resulting pattern closely models phyllotaxis — the arrangement of seeds in a sunflower head, florets in a daisy, or scales on a pinecone. Because the golden angle is the most irrational rotation, the points never align into spokes, producing the familiar densely packed spiral arms whose counts are consecutive Fibonacci numbers. This construction, closely related to Vogel's model, is widely used in computer graphics to generate natural-looking distributions of points on a disk.

Applications

Beyond botanical modelling, Fermat spirals appear in the design of spiral antennas, in diffraction and Fresnel zone patterns in optics, and in algorithmic point-distribution problems where uniform coverage of a circular area is required.
