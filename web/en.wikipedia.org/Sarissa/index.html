Sarissa

The sarissa (Greek: σάρισα) was a long pike, about 4 to 6.5 metres (13–21 feet) in length, used by the armies of ancient Macedon. Introduced by Philip II of Macedon in the mid-4th century BC, it was the principal weapon of the Macedonian phalanx and a key instrument in the conquests of Philip and his son Alexander the Great.

Description

The sarissa was made of tough cornel wood and carried a leaf-shaped iron spearhead at the front and a bronze butt-spike at the rear; the butt-spike could be planted in the ground to steady the weapon against charging cavalry and served as a counterweight and a reserve point if the shaft broke. Because of its length the shaft was probably joined from two pieces with an iron coupling sleeve. At roughly 5.5 to 6.5 kilograms, it had to be wielded with both hands, so the phalangite's shield was smaller than the classical hoplite's and was slung from the neck and shoulder.

Tactics

In battle the first five ranks of the phalanx levelled their sarissas, presenting an enemy with a dense hedge of spearpoints projecting several metres ahead of the front rank, while the remaining ranks held their pikes upright or at an angle to deflect missiles. The formation was nearly invulnerable from the front on level ground, but it was unwieldy on broken terrain and vulnerable to attacks on its flanks — weaknesses exploited by the Romans, whose more flexible manipular legions defeated the Macedonian and Hellenistic phalanxes at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) and Pydna (168 BC).

History

Philip II may have adapted the idea from the long spears of the Theban general Epaminondas or from the reforms of the Athenian Iphicrates. Under the Hellenistic successor kingdoms the sarissa grew even longer — ancient writers mention pikes of up to 21 feet — before the system fell out of use with the Roman conquest of the eastern Mediterranean.
