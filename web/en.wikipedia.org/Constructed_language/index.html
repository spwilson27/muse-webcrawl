A constructed language (often shortened to conlang) is a language whose phonology, grammar and vocabulary, instead of having developed naturally over time, are consciously devised or invented by an individual or group. Constructed languages are created for many reasons: to ease international communication, for fictional worlds, for linguistic experimentation, or simply for personal enjoyment.

Types

International auxiliary languages

Auxiliary languages ("auxlangs") aim at neutral global communication. The most successful is Esperanto, published by L. L. Zamenhof in 1887, which has hundreds of thousands of speakers and even some native speakers. Earlier and later projects include Volapük, Ido, Interlingua and Novial.

Artistic languages

Artistic languages are created for fiction and art. J. R. R. Tolkien's Quenya and Sindarin for Middle-earth set the modern standard; later examples include Klingon (Star Trek), Dothraki and High Valyrian (Game of Thrones), and Na'vi (Avatar), often created by professional linguists for film and television.

Engineered and philosophical languages

Engineered languages test logical or philosophical ideas: Loglan and its descendant Lojban aim at unambiguous, logic-based grammar, while minimalist languages such as Toki Pona (created by Sonja Lang, 2001) explore how much can be expressed with a tiny vocabulary.

History

Language invention goes back at least to Hildegard of Bingen's Lingua Ignota in the 12th century and flourished in the 17th century with the "philosophical languages" of John Wilkins and others. The modern hobbyist community, or "conlanging" community, developed through mailing lists and the internet from the 1990s onward.
