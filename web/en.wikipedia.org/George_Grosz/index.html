George Grosz (born Georg Ehrenfried Groß; July 26, 1893 – July 6, 1959) was a German artist known especially for his caricatural drawings and paintings of Berlin life in the 1920s. He was a prominent member of the Berlin Dada and New Objectivity groups during the Weimar Republic. He emigrated to the United States in 1933, and became a naturalized citizen in 1938.

Grosz's savage satires targeted war profiteers, corrupt officials and the militarist establishment of Weimar Germany.

Life and work

Grosz grew up in Stolp, Pomerania, and studied art in Dresden and Berlin. He volunteered for the army in 1914 but was discharged as unfit in 1915; a second call-up in 1917 ended in hospitalisation and narrow escapes from execution for desertion. The war filled him with hatred of militarism and the bourgeoisie, which poured into drawings such as Fit for Active Service (1918). In 1919 he joined Berlin Dada and (briefly) the Communist Party; his portfolios The Face of the Ruling Class (1921) and Ecce Homo (1923) — the latter seized and prosecuted for obscenity — made him famous and repeatedly prosecuted for blasphemy and defaming the Reichswehr.

His oil paintings of the 1920s, such as The Pillars of Society (1926), are classics of the New Objectivity: cold, meticulous depictions of a corrupt society of profiteers, officers and clergy. Warned of Nazi hostility, Grosz left for New York in January 1933, just before Hitler came to power; in Germany his work was branded "degenerate art". He taught at the Art Students League and painted American subjects, though his savage energy softened. He returned to Berlin in 1959 and died there weeks later after a fall.
