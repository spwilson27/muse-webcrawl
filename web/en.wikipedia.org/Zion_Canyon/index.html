Zion Canyon (also called Little Zion, Mukuntuweap, Mu-Loon'-Tu-Weap and Straight Canyon) is a deep and narrow gorge in southwestern Utah, United States, carved by the North Fork of the Virgin River. Nearly the entire canyon lies within the western half of Zion National Park.

Geography and geology

The canyon is about 15 miles (24 km) long and up to 2,640 feet (800 m) deep. Its walls are carved from reddish and tan Navajo Sandstone, an ancient dune field lithified during the Jurassic period. At its narrowest point, the section known as The Narrows, the canyon is only 20 to 30 feet wide with walls a thousand feet tall. Prominent formations include Angels Landing, the Great White Throne, the Court of the Patriarchs and the Watchman.

History

The canyon was inhabited by Ancestral Puebloan peoples and later by Southern Paiute groups, who called the area Mukuntuweap, meaning "straight canyon". Mormon settlers arrived in the 1860s; Isaac Behunin, an early settler, named it Zion after a biblical place of refuge. The geologist-geographer John Wesley Powell surveyed the area in 1872. Mukuntuweap National Monument was proclaimed in 1909 and renamed Zion National Park in 1919.

Tourism

The Zion Canyon Scenic Drive gives access to the canyon floor, and since 2000 most private vehicles have been replaced by a shuttle system. Popular hikes include Angels Landing, The Narrows, the Emerald Pools and the Canyon Overlook Trail. Flash floods are a serious hazard in the canyon's narrow sections.
