Mass surveillance

Mass surveillance is the intricate surveillance of an entire or a substantial fraction of a population in order to monitor that group of citizens. The surveillance is often carried out by local and federal governments or governmental organisations, but it may also be carried out by corporations, on behalf of governments or at their own initiative.

Methods

Mass surveillance uses means such as CCTV networks, telephone and internet interception, communications metadata collection, biometric identification (including facial recognition), financial transaction monitoring and location tracking. The ubiquity of smartphones and digital communications has greatly expanded the data available to surveilling bodies.

Revelations and debate

The 2013 disclosures by Edward Snowden, a former contractor for the US National Security Agency, revealed global surveillance programs such as PRISM and bulk telephony metadata collection, conducted by the NSA and partner agencies in the "Five Eyes" alliance. The revelations prompted worldwide debate about privacy, civil liberties and the balance between security and freedom, as well as legal reforms such as the USA FREEDOM Act of 2015.

China's extensive surveillance system, including the social credit experiments and monitoring in Xinjiang, is frequently cited as the most comprehensive example of state mass surveillance. Critics warn of a "chilling effect" on free expression and the risk of a surveillance state; supporters argue such measures are necessary against terrorism and crime.
