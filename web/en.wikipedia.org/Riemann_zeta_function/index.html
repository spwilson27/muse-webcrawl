Riemann zeta function

Riemann zeta function
FieldAnalytic number theory
IntroducedLeonhard Euler (1730s); Bernhard Riemann (1859)
DomainComplex numbers, s ≠ 1
Key problemRiemann hypothesis

The Riemann zeta function, denoted ζ(s), is a function of a complex variable s that analytically continues the sum of the infinite series Σ 1/ns, which converges when the real part of s is greater than 1. It encodes deep information about the distribution of the prime numbers.

[Image placeholder: Colour plot of the Riemann zeta function in the complex plane]

ζ(s) = Σn=1∞ 1/ns = Πp prime 1/(1 − p−s)

Euler product

Leonhard Euler discovered the identity above linking the zeta function to all prime numbers: the sum over the integers equals a product over the primes. Euler used it to give a new proof of the infinitude of the primes and computed ζ(2) = π²/6, solving the Basel problem.

Analytic continuation and functional equation

Bernhard Riemann's 1859 paper "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" extended ζ(s) to a meromorphic function on the whole complex plane, with a single simple pole at s = 1, and established the functional equation relating ζ(s) to ζ(1 − s). The function has trivial zeros at the negative even integers −2, −4, −6, …

The Riemann hypothesis

Riemann conjectured that all non-trivial zeros of ζ(s) lie on the critical line Re(s) = ½. This statement, the Riemann hypothesis, remains unproven and is one of the Millennium Prize Problems. It is equivalent to the strongest possible error term in the prime number theorem's estimate of π(x), the count of primes below x.

Values and applications

Special values include ζ(−1) = −1/12, which appears in physics in the Casimir effect and string theory. Zeta functions and their generalisations (Dirichlet L-functions) are central tools in analytic number theory.
