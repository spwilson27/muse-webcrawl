Pulmonary fibrosis

Pulmonary fibrosis is a condition in which the lungs become scarred over time. Symptoms include shortness of breath, a dry cough, feeling tired, weight loss, and nail clubbing. Complications may include pulmonary hypertension, respiratory failure, pneumothorax, and lung cancer.

Causes

Causes of pulmonary fibrosis include environmental pollution, certain medications (such as amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, and chemotherapy agents), connective tissue diseases, infections, and interstitial lung diseases. Most cases have no known cause and are termed idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), in which the lung tissue thickens and becomes stiff in a pattern of usual interstitial pneumonia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis typically involves a medical history, imaging, and lung biopsies. A high-resolution CT scan may show characteristic honeycombing and reticulation, predominantly at the lung bases. Pulmonary function tests show a restrictive pattern with reduced diffusion capacity.

Treatment and prognosis

About 5 million people are affected globally. Treatment options are limited; the antifibrotic drugs pirfenidone and nintedanib slow the decline of lung function in IPF but do not reverse scarring. Lung transplantation is an option for some patients. New cases appear roughly 12 per 100,000 people per year, and prognosis in IPF is poor, with a median survival of about three to five years after diagnosis, although the course varies widely.
