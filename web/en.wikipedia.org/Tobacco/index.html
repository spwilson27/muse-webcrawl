Tobacco is the common name of several plants in the genus Nicotiana of the family Solanaceae, and the general term for any product prepared from the cured leaves of these plants. More than 70 species of tobacco are known, but the chief commercial crop is N. tabacum, with the more potent N. rustica also used in some countries. Tobacco contains the highly addictive stimulant alkaloid nicotine, and dried tobacco leaves are mainly used for smoking in cigarettes, cigars and pipes, or as chewing tobacco, snuff, snus and dipping tobacco.

History

Tobacco was used by Indigenous peoples of the Americas for thousands of years, in ritual, medicinal and social contexts, long before European contact. After 1492, tobacco spread rapidly around the world: it became a cornerstone crop of the colonial economy of Virginia, where John Rolfe's experiments with West Indian seed produced a mild, exportable leaf, and its cultivation was a major driver of the demand for enslaved labour in the Americas. Tobacco later became one of the most heavily traded global commodities.

Cultivation and production

Tobacco is grown from tiny seeds sown in protected beds and transplanted to fields. After harvest, leaves are cured — by air, flue, fire or sun — which develops their colour and chemistry, then aged and processed. China is the world's largest producer, followed by countries such as Brazil, India and the United States. Major types include brightleaf (Virginia), burley and Oriental tobaccos, blended in most cigarettes.

Consumption

Cigarettes account for the great majority of tobacco consumed worldwide. Other forms include cigars, pipe tobacco, hookah (water pipe) tobacco, bidis, and smokeless products such as snus, snuff and chewing tobacco. In the 20th century, per-capita cigarette consumption soared in industrialised countries before declining under the pressure of health evidence, taxation and advertising restrictions.

Health effects

Tobacco smoking is a leading preventable cause of death worldwide. Tobacco smoke contains thousands of chemicals, dozens of them known carcinogens, and smoking is causally linked to lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiovascular disease and many other illnesses; the World Health Organization attributes roughly 8 million deaths a year to tobacco use, including deaths from second-hand smoke. The 1964 report of the US Surgeon General was a landmark in establishing the evidence in public policy. The WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (2003) coordinates global measures including smoke-free laws, graphic health warnings and advertising bans.
