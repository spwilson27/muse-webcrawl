Félix Houphouët-Boigny

Félix Houphouët-Boigny (18 October 1905 – 7 December 1993), affectionately called Papa Houphouët or Le Vieux ("The Old One"), was the first president of Ivory Coast (Côte d'Ivoire), serving from independence in 1960 until his death in 1993. Before that he was a leading figure in French colonial politics and the country's first prime minister.

Early life and French politics

Born into a Baoulé chiefly family in Yamoussoukro, Houphouët-Boigny trained as a medical assistant in Dakar and worked as a doctor, planter, and union organiser — founding the African Agricultural Union (SAA) in 1944 to fight forced labour, which was abolished by the 1946 law that bears his name. Elected to the French Constituent Assembly in 1945, he founded the PDCI and the inter-territorial Rassemblement Démocratique Africain (RDA), and served as a minister in several French governments in the 1950s.

President of Ivory Coast

Houphouët-Boigny led Ivory Coast to independence on 7 August 1960. Pursuing pro-Western, capitalist policies and close ties with France ("Françafrique"), he presided over two decades of strong growth — the "Ivorian miracle" — built on cocoa and coffee exports; Ivory Coast became the world's largest cocoa producer. His rule was authoritarian but stable, with a single-party state and careful balancing of ethnic factions.

Yamoussoukro and legacy

In 1983 he moved the capital to his birthplace, Yamoussoukro, where he built the Basilica of Our Lady of Peace, consecrated in 1990 and among the largest churches in the world. The Félix Houphouët-Boigny Peace Prize, established by UNESCO in 1989, bears his name. After his death, succession disputes contributed to political instability and, eventually, civil conflict in the 2000s.
