Julius Richard Petri

Born: May 31, 1852, Barmen, Prussia
Died: December 20, 1921 (aged 69), Zeitz, Germany
Fields: Bacteriology
Known for: Inventing the Petri dish

Julius Richard Petri (May 31, 1852 – December 20, 1921) was a German microbiologist who is generally credited with inventing the device known as the Petri dish, which is named after him, while working as an assistant to bacteriologist Robert Koch.

Life and career

Petri was born in Barmen and studied medicine at the Kaiser-Wilhelm Academy for military physicians in Berlin, graduating in 1876. He served as a military doctor and from 1877 to 1879 was assigned to Robert Koch's laboratory at the Imperial Health Office in Berlin, the cradle of modern bacteriology. There he worked alongside the pioneers who were developing the techniques of growing bacteria in pure culture.

The Petri dish

At the time, bacteria were cultured on solid media in flat plates covered by bell jars, which were bulky and prone to contamination. In 1887 Petri published a short paper describing a modification of Koch's plate technique: a shallow, cylindrical glass dish with a slightly larger lid that overlapped its sides. The design — the Petri dish — kept cultures protected from airborne contamination while allowing them to be stacked, observed, and handled. Combined with agar, the medium introduced by Fanny Hesse and Walther Hesse in Koch's laboratory, it became the standard vessel of microbiology and remains in daily use in laboratories worldwide, now usually moulded in disposable plastic.

Later work

Petri went on to direct a sanatorium for tuberculosis patients at Göbersdorf in Silesia and later worked at the Museum of Hygiene in Berlin, publishing on bacteriological methods, water filtration, and public health. He died in Zeitz in 1921. Despite the universality of his dish, Petri remains far less famous than the invention that bears his name.
