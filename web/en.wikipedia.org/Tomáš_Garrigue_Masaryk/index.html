Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk

Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk (7 March 1850 – 14 September 1937), often called TGM, was a Czechoslovak statesman, philosopher and sociologist who founded the Czechoslovak Republic and served as its first president from 1918 until 1935. A professor of philosophy who became the leading advocate of Czechoslovak independence during the First World War, he is remembered in the Czech Republic as the "President Liberator" (Prezident Osvoboditel) and a symbol of democratic statehood. He took his middle name from his American wife, Charlotte Garrigue.

Life and academic career

Masaryk was born in Moravia, the son of a Slovak coachman and a German-speaking Moravian mother. Trained first as a blacksmith's apprentice and locksmith, he gained an education through patrons, studied at Vienna and Leipzig, and earned his doctorate in 1876 with a thesis on Plato. In 1882 he became professor of philosophy at the new Czech university in Prague. He founded the journal Athenaeum, intervened in the controversy over the forged Königinhof and Grünberg manuscripts by insisting they were fakes despite nationalist outrage, and defended Leopold Hilsner, a Jewish man falsely accused of ritual murder, against antisemitic agitation. As a deputy in the Austrian Reichsrat he led the small Realist Party and advocated of Czech autonomy and democratic reform within the empire.

Founding Czechoslovakia

When war broke out in 1914, Masaryk went into exile and campaigned across Europe and America for an independent Czechoslovak state, organising the Czechoslovak National Council and the Czechoslovak Legions fighting on the Allied side. His 1918 meetings with President Woodrow Wilson helped secure American recognition; the Washington Declaration of October 1918 proclaimed Czechoslovak independence. Elected president, Masaryk presided over one of interwar Europe's most durable democracies, advocating humanism, minority rights within a centralised state, and the "Castle" circle of democratic politics. He was re-elected three times and resigned for reasons of age and health in December 1935, succeeded by Edvard Beneš.

Thought and legacy

Masaryk's philosophical works include studies of suicide as a social phenomenon, of Marxism, and his wartime book The New Europe. His motto "Truth prevails" (Pravda vítězí) became the motto of Czechoslovakia and remains on the Czech presidential standard. Streets, squares, Brno's Masaryk University and the Masaryk Circuit bear his name, and his birthday is a Czech commemorative day.
