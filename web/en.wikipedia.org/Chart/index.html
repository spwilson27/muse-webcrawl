Chart

A chart (sometimes known as a graph) is a graphical representation for data visualization in which "the data is represented by symbols, such as bars in a bar chart, lines in a line chart, or slices in a pie chart." A chart can represent tabular numeric data, functions, or qualitative structures, and different combinations of these, providing meaning to the data and making patterns, trends, and comparisons easier to grasp than raw numbers.

Common types

Bar charts compare quantities across categories using bars whose lengths are proportional to value. Line charts connect data points over an interval, usually time, to show trends. Pie charts divide a circle into sectors representing parts of a whole, though statisticians caution they make precise comparisons difficult. Scatter plots show the relationship between two variables as a cloud of points. Other common forms include histograms, area charts, box plots, and heat maps. The mapping of real-world navigation documents — nautical and aeronautical charts — is an older, related usage of the word.

History and principles

Modern statistical graphics were pioneered in the late 18th century by William Playfair, whose Commercial and Political Atlas (1786) introduced bar and line charts and whose later works added the pie chart; Florence Nightingale popularized the polar area diagram in the 1850s to dramatize mortality in the Crimean War. Edward Tufte's The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (1983) codified principles of graphical integrity, including maximizing the "data-ink ratio" and avoiding "chartjunk" — decoration that obscures data.
