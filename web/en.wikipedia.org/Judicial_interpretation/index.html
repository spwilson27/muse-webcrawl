Judicial interpretation is the activity and method by which courts determine the meaning of legal texts — constitutions, statutes, regulations and precedents — in the course of deciding cases. Because legal language is often ambiguous, vague or silent on unforeseen circumstances, interpretation is a central and contested part of adjudication, raising enduring questions about the proper role of judges relative to legislatures and the drafters of legal texts.

Statutory interpretation

When interpreting legislation, courts traditionally deploy canons of construction. In the common-law tradition these include the literal rule, the golden rule, and the mischief rule (from Heydon's Case, 1584), along with maxims such as ejusdem generis and presumptions against retroactivity or absurd results. Modern courts commonly also consult legislative history — committee reports, debates and statements of purpose — though textualists argue that only the enacted text should count. Many jurisdictions have enacted interpretation statutes to guide the process.

Constitutional interpretation

Interpreting constitutions has generated distinctive schools of thought. Originalism holds that constitutional meaning is fixed at the time of adoption (distinguishing original intent from original public meaning); living constitutionalism holds that broad provisions evolve with society; textualism, structuralism and doctrinalism (adherence to precedent, or stare decisis) offer further approaches. Debate over these methods is especially prominent in the United States, where they feature in confirmation hearings for Supreme Court justices.

Other approaches

Purposivism directs judges to the purpose a law was meant to serve; pragmatism weighs consequences; comparative and international methods borrow from foreign jurisprudence. In European Union law the Court of Justice favours teleological (purposive) interpretation, and multilingual texts are interpreted by reference to all language versions. Civil-law systems historically limited judicial interpretation — the French Civil Code even forbade judges from laying down general rules — but constitutional courts in those systems now engage extensively in interpretation.

Criticism

Critics across the spectrum accuse judges of "legislating from the bench" when interpretation departs from text or original meaning, while defenders reply that faithful adjudication in hard cases necessarily involves principled construction. The debate is a central theme of legal philosophy, elaborated by theorists such as Ronald Dworkin, who likened adjudication to writing successive chapters of a "chain novel".
