Foreclosure

Foreclosure is a legal process in which a lender attempts to recover the balance of a loan from a borrower who has stopped making payments by forcing the sale of the asset used as collateral for the loan. Most commonly, foreclosure concerns real property secured by a mortgage: if the borrower defaults, the lender may take possession of the property and sell it, applying the proceeds to the debt.

Process

In the United States, foreclosure may be judicial, proceeding through the courts, or non-judicial, conducted under a power-of-sale clause in a deed of trust, depending on the state. The process typically begins with a notice of default after several missed payments, proceeds through a statutory waiting period in which the borrower may cure the default, and ends in a public auction. If the sale price is less than the debt, the lender may in some jurisdictions seek a deficiency judgment for the remainder.

Effects

Foreclosure has severe consequences for borrowers, including loss of the home, damaged credit, and potential tax liability. Concentrated foreclosures depress neighbourhood property values and municipal tax bases. The United States foreclosure crisis of 2007–2010, driven by defaults on subprime and adjustable-rate mortgages, was a central feature of the financial crisis of 2007–2008 and the Great Recession, prompting federal programmes such as the Home Affordable Modification Program to encourage loan modifications as alternatives.

Alternatives

Borrowers and lenders may avoid foreclosure through loan modification, forbearance, a short sale (selling the property for less than the balance, with the lender's consent), or a deed in lieu of foreclosure. Bankruptcy can temporarily halt foreclosure through the automatic stay.
