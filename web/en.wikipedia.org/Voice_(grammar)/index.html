Voice (grammar)

FieldLinguistics / grammar

FunctionRelates subject to action/state of the verb

In grammar, voice is the form of a verb that shows the relationship between the subject of a clause and the action or state the verb expresses — in particular, whether the subject performs the action or undergoes it. The two principal voices in English and many other languages are the active voice and the passive voice.

Active and passive

In the active voice, the subject typically corresponds to the agent: "The cat chased the mouse." In the passive voice, the subject corresponds to the patient or theme, and the agent is demoted to an optional by-phrase: "The mouse was chased (by the cat)." English forms the passive with the auxiliary be (or get) plus the past participle. The passive is used to foreground the patient, to omit an unknown or obvious agent, or for style — though style guides often caution against overuse.

Other voices

Many languages have additional voices. The middle voice (prominent in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit) indicates that the subject acts on or for itself, or is affected by the action ("The book sells well"). Other systems include the antipassive voice (in ergative languages), applicative voices that promote oblique arguments, and the elaborate symmetrical voice systems of Austronesian languages such as Tagalog, where the verb marks which participant is in focus.
