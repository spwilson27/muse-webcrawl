Émile Édouard Charles Antoine Zola (2 April 1840 – 29 September 1902) was a French novelist, journalist and playwright, the best-known practitioner of the literary school of naturalism and an important figure in the political liberalisation of France. His twenty-volume cycle Les Rougon-Macquart traced the fortunes of a family under the Second French Empire, applying his theory of heredity and environment to the novel.

Life and career

Zola was born in Paris, the son of an Italian-born engineer, and raised in Aix-en-Provence, where he befriended the painter Paul Cézanne. After failing his baccalauréat he worked as a clerk and in publishing before turning to journalism and fiction. His early novel Thérèse Raquin (1867) established his naturalist manner, and the Rougon-Macquart series (1871–1893), including L'Assommoir, Nana, Germinal and La Bête humaine, made him the most famous and best-selling French author of his day.

The Dreyfus affair

In January 1898 Zola published J'accuse…!, an open letter to President Félix Faure accusing the French military and government of obstructing justice in the case of Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish artillery officer falsely convicted of treason. Convicted of libel, Zola fled to England for nearly a year. The affair split France and is widely credited with accelerating Dreyfus's eventual exoneration.

Death and legacy

Zola died of carbon monoxide poisoning from a blocked chimney in 1902, a death some have suspected was an assassination. His ashes were transferred to the Panthéon in 1908. His experimental theory of the novel, set out in Le Roman expérimental (1880), influenced realist and naturalist fiction across Europe and America.
