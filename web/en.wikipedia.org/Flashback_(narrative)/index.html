Flashback (narrative)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A flashback, sometimes called an analepsis, is an interjected scene in a narrative that takes the reader or viewer back in time from the current point in the story. Flashbacks interrupt the chronological ordering of the plot so that earlier events, memories, or backstory can be presented. They often provide the motivation for characters' present behaviour, reveal information that changes the interpretation of events already shown, or supply exposition that would be awkward to include in the story's present tense.

Flashbacks may be external, reaching events that occurred before the story began, or internal, revisiting events that have already been shown but seen from a new perspective. Short flashbacks are sometimes called flashback in miniature or moments of retrospection, while the opposite device, jumping forward in time, is a flashforward.

History

One of the earliest examples of the device in Western literature is the Odyssey of Homer, in which Odysseus recounts his wanderings to the Phaeacians — a long flashback that fills most of the middle of the poem. Medieval frame tales such as The Canterbury Tales and The Arabian Nights embed retrospective narration inside present-tense storytelling.

In film and television

Cinema adopted the flashback early: Ferdinand Zecca's Histoire d'un crime (1901) is often cited as an early example. Directors such as Orson Welles (Citizen Kane, 1941) and Akira Kurosawa (Rashomon, 1950) used nested and contradictory flashbacks to structure entire films. Rashomon in particular demonstrated that flashbacks could be unreliable, presenting mutually inconsistent versions of the same event. Modern television dramas — from Lost to This Is Us — have used flashbacks as a central structural device.

Techniques

Flashbacks can be triggered by objects, places, sensory cues, dialogue, or dreams. In film they are conventionally introduced by dissolves, fades, or colour shifts; in prose, by tense shifts, chapter breaks, or explicitly remembered scenes. A related device is the flash-sideways, which presents an alternate timeline rather than a past one.
