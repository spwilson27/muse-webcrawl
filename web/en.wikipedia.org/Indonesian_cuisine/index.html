Indonesian cuisine

Indonesian cuisine is the diverse culinary tradition of the Indonesian archipelago, one of the richest in the world in variety of flavours and techniques. Shaped by the country's thousands of islands and hundreds of ethnic groups, it draws on indigenous Austronesian traditions together with Indian, Chinese, Arab, and European — especially Dutch — influences. Rice is the staple food for most Indonesians, often accompanied by dishes flavoured with coconut milk, peanuts, and pungent spice pastes.

Characteristics and staples

Common ingredients include rice, cassava, sweet potato, coconut, chilli peppers (introduced from the Americas), turmeric, galangal, lemongrass, and shrimp paste (terasi). The condiment sambal — a chilli-based sauce of which there are hundreds of variants — accompanies most meals. Sweet soy sauce (kecap manis) is a distinctive Indonesian invention. Because the majority of Indonesians are Muslim, pork is uncommon in most regions, though it features in the cuisines of Bali, North Sulawesi, and Chinese-Indonesian communities.

Signature dishes

Nationally celebrated dishes include nasi goreng (fried rice), rendang — the slow-braised spiced beef of the Minangkabau people of West Sumatra, repeatedly voted among the world's favourite foods — satay (skewered grilled meat with peanut sauce), gado-gado (vegetable salad with peanut dressing), and soto, a family of fragrant soups. Regional traditions are strong: Minangkabau (Padang) restaurants serve dozens of dishes at once; Javanese food tends sweeter; Balinese cuisine uses the spice mix base gede; and street vendors (kaki lima) and small eateries (warung) anchor everyday eating.

Influence

Through the Dutch colonial connection, Indonesian food — especially the rijsttafel banquet — became established in the Netherlands, and Indonesian restaurants are found worldwide. Indonesian cuisine also historically shaped and was shaped by the cooking of Malaysia and Singapore, with several shared dishes remaining subjects of friendly rivalry.
