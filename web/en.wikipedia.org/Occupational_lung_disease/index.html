Occupational lung disease

Occupational lung disease
FieldOccupational medicine, pulmonology
CausesInhalation of dusts, fumes, gases or fibres at work
Major typesPneumoconioses (silicosis, coal workers' pneumoconiosis, asbestosis), occupational asthma, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, byssinosis
PreventionExposure control, ventilation, respirators, health surveillance

Occupational lung diseases are work-related conditions caused or aggravated by materials and environments encountered in the workplace, principally through the inhalation of dusts, fibres, fumes and gases. They are among the most common occupational illnesses worldwide and are largely preventable by controlling exposure.

Major categories

The pneumoconioses are fibrotic diseases caused by inhaled mineral dusts: silicosis from crystalline silica (mining, quarrying, sandblasting, engineered-stone fabrication), coal workers' pneumoconiosis ("black lung") from coal dust, and asbestosis from asbestos fibres, which are also associated with pleural plaques, lung cancer and mesothelioma. Occupational asthma, triggered by agents such as flour dust, isocyanates and laboratory animal allergens, is the most common occupational lung disease in many industrialised countries. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis results from immune reactions to organic dusts, as in farmer's lung and bird fancier's lung. Byssinosis affects cotton textile workers, and chronic beryllium disease affects workers in aerospace and electronics industries.

Diagnosis and effects

Diagnosis rests on a careful occupational history combined with imaging, lung-function testing and sometimes biopsy. Many occupational lung diseases develop slowly over years and cause progressive breathlessness, cough and disability; silicosis also increases susceptibility to tuberculosis. Some, such as acute silicosis or chemical pneumonitis from massive exposures, can develop rapidly.

Prevention and regulation

Prevention follows a hierarchy of controls: eliminating or substituting hazardous materials, engineering controls such as ventilation and wet methods, administrative limits on exposure time, and personal respiratory protection as a last resort. Regulators such as the U.S. Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) set permissible exposure limits for silica, asbestos and other agents, and many jurisdictions provide workers' compensation schemes for affected employees.
