Diesel cycle

The Diesel cycle is a thermodynamic cycle that approximates the operation of the diesel engine, patented by Rudolf Diesel in the 1890s. In it, heat is added at constant pressure rather than at constant volume as in the Otto cycle, reflecting the compression-ignition process in which fuel is injected into hot compressed air and burns as it is added.

Pressure–volume diagram of the idealised Diesel cycle.

The four processes

  1. Isentropic compression: air is compressed, raising its temperature above the fuel's ignition point.
  2. Constant-pressure heat addition: fuel is injected and burns while the piston begins to move down, so pressure stays roughly constant.
  3. Isentropic expansion: the hot gases expand and do work on the piston.
  4. Constant-volume heat rejection: the exhaust valve opens and heat is rejected.

Efficiency

The thermal efficiency of the ideal Diesel cycle depends on the compression ratio and the cutoff ratio (the ratio of volumes at the end and start of heat addition). Diesel engines achieve high real-world efficiency—often 30–45%—because they run at much higher compression ratios than spark-ignition engines, typically 14:1 to 25:1.

Comparison with the Otto cycle

For the same compression ratio, the Otto cycle is more efficient in the ideal analysis; however, practical diesel engines use far higher compression ratios, which more than compensates and yields better fuel economy.
