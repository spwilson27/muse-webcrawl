The Pindus (Greek: Πίνδος; Aromanian: Pindu) is a mountain range in northern Greece and southern Albania, often called the "spine of Greece". It stretches for about 230 kilometres from near the Greek–Albanian border southward through Epirus, Thessaly, and central Greece, forming the watershed between the Aegean and Ionian seas. Its highest summit, Mount Smolikas (2,637 m), is the second-highest mountain in Greece after Mount Olympus.

Geology and landscape

The range is largely built of limestone, flysch, and ophiolite rocks thrust up during the Alpine orogeny as part of the Dinaric–Hellenic mountain system. Deep river gorges cut through it, most famously the Vikos Gorge in the Vikos–Aoös National Park — among the deepest gorges in the world relative to its width. Extensive forests of black pine, fir, and beech cover the slopes, and the mountains shelter some of Europe's most intact temperate ecosystems.

Nature and conservation

Two national parks — Pindus (Valia Kalda) and Vikos–Aoös — protect brown bears, wolves, lynx, chamois, and rare birds of prey. The region is a stronghold of traditional transhumant pastoralism, practised historically by Vlach (Aromanian) and Sarakatsani shepherd communities.

Communities and history

The Pindus villages — Metsovo, Syrrako, and the 46 stone-built villages of Zagori (Zagorochoria) with their arched packhorse bridges — were historically prosperous trading communities. Because of the terrain, the range guarded the pass of Metsovo across Greece's waist and long insulated Epirus from the east. During the Second World War the mountains were a theatre of the Greco-Italian War of 1940–41, when Greek forces held the Pindus sector against the Italian Julia Division.
