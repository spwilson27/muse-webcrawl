Ancien Régime

The Ancien Régime (French for "old regime") is the political and social system of the Kingdom of France from the Late Middle Ages (c. 1500) until 1789, when hereditary absolute monarchy was abolished by the French Revolution. The term also came to be applied more broadly to the comparable feudal systems of early modern Europe.

Structure of society

French society was divided into three Estates: the First Estate (clergy), the Second Estate (nobility), and the Third Estate (everyone else, from wealthy bourgeois merchants to peasants). The Third Estate bore most of the tax burden, including the taille, the gabelle (salt tax), and feudal dues, while the privileged orders enjoyed extensive exemptions.

Monarchy and administration

Power was concentrated in the king, whose authority was justified by the divine right of kings. The system reached its height under Louis XIV, the "Sun King," who built the palace of Versailles and required the great nobles to attend court. Administration was hampered by venal offices (positions sold by the crown), overlapping jurisdictions, internal customs barriers, and a patchwork of provincial privileges.

Decline and fall

By the 1780s the monarchy faced fiscal crisis, worsened by support for the American Revolutionary War. Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General in 1789 for the first time since 1614; the Third Estate proclaimed itself the National Assembly, and the Revolution abolished the privileges of the Ancien Régime on the night of 4 August 1789.
