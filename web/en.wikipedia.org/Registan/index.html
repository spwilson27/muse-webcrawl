Registan

The Registan (Persian: ریگستان) was the heart of the ancient city of Samarkand, in present-day Uzbekistan, during the Timurid Empire. Its name comes from the Persian reg ("sand") and -stan ("place"), literally "sandy place" or "desert". The Registan was a public square where people gathered to hear royal proclamations, heralded by blasts on enormous copper pipes called dzharchis, and a place of public executions.

The three madrasas

The square is framed by three monumental madrasas (Islamic schools) adorned with elaborate tilework, majolica and carved bricks:

Ulugh Beg Madrasa (1417–1420), the western building, was commissioned by the astronomer-king Ulugh Beg and is the oldest of the three. Its facade features a grand pishtaq (portal) flanked by two minarets.

Tilya-Kori Madrasa (1646–1660), the central building, served both as a school and as the city's congregational mosque; its name means "gilded", a reference to its lavish gold-leaf interior decoration.

Sher-Dor Madrasa (1619–1636), the eastern building, mirrors the Ulugh Beg Madrasa. Its portal is famous for depicting tigers (often described as lions) with suns rising from their backs — a striking departure from the Islamic prohibition on representing living beings, reflecting Persian influence.

History

Under Timur (Tamerlane) and his successors, Samarkand became a centre of Islamic civilisation, and the Registan ensemble grew into one of the most celebrated architectural complexes of Central Asia. After Samarkand declined in the 18th century, the buildings fell into ruin and were damaged by earthquakes, but extensive restoration during the Soviet period and after Uzbek independence returned them to prominence. Together with other monuments of Samarkand, the Registan is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
