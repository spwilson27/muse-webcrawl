Synthetism is a term used by Post-Impressionist artists — principally Paul Gauguin, Émile Bernard, and their circle in Brittany — to describe a style of painting that synthesized three things: the outward appearance of natural forms, the artist's feelings about the subject, and the purely aesthetic considerations of line, colour, and form. In practice it meant flat areas of bold, non-naturalistic colour bounded by strong dark outlines, and the abandonment of conventional perspective and chiaroscuro in favour of expressive, decorative pattern.

Origins

The style emerged at Pont-Aven in Brittany in 1888. Émile Bernard and Louis Anquetin developed the related technique of Cloisonnism, named after cloisonné enamelwork for its separated bands of colour. Gauguin's Vision of the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel) of 1888 — with its flat vermilion field — is generally treated as the first fully Synthetist painting. Paul Sérusier carried the approach back to Paris with his small landscape The Talisman (1888), painted under Gauguin's direction, which became a founding icon of the Nabis group.

Principles

Gauguin and Bernard articulated Synthetism in polemical opposition to Impressionism and Neo-Impressionism, whose optical naturalism they regarded as superficial. The critic Albert Aurier's 1891 Symbolist manifesto championed Gauguin and summarized the Synthetist work of art as ideational, symbolist, synthetic, subjective, and decorative. The style fed directly into Symbolism, the Nabis, and aspects of early modernism, including the expressive colour of Fauvism.
