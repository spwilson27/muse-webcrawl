Michaelis–Menten kinetics

Michaelis–Menten kinetics, sometimes called Michaelis–Menten–Henri kinetics, is one of the best-known models of enzyme kinetics. Proposed in 1913 by Leonor Michaelis and Maud Menten (building on earlier work by Victor Henri), it describes how the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction depends on the concentration of substrate.

The model

The model considers an enzyme E that binds a substrate S to form a complex ES, which then breaks down to release product P and free enzyme:

E + S ⇌ ES → E + P

Under the steady-state assumption — developed rigorous form by G. E. Briggs and J. B. S. Haldane in 1925, in which the ES concentration stays roughly constant — the initial reaction rate v is given by the Michaelis–Menten equation:

v = Vmax · [S] / (Km + [S])

where Vmax is the maximum rate when the enzyme is saturated with substrate and Km, the Michaelis constant, is the substrate concentration at which the rate is half of Vmax. A low Km indicates tight binding (high affinity); the turnover number kcat = Vmax/[E] measures how many substrate molecules each enzyme converts per second.

Interpretation and plots

The equation yields a rectangular hyperbola: at low substrate concentrations the rate is nearly proportional to [S] (first-order kinetics), while at high concentrations it plateaus at Vmax (zero-order). Historically, double-reciprocal (Lineweaver–Burk) plots were used to extract Km and Vmax by linear regression, though direct nonlinear fitting of the hyperbola is now preferred as more accurate.

Scope and limitations

The model applies to many single-substrate enzymes under initial-rate conditions, but it does not cover allosteric enzymes with cooperative binding (which give sigmoidal curves described by the Hill equation), multi-substrate reactions, or enzyme inhibition in its basic form — though extended equations handle competitive, non-competitive and uncompetitive inhibitors, of central importance in drug design. Despite its age, Michaelis–Menten kinetics remains the standard framework taught in biochemistry and used in pharmacology and systems biology.
