Yūrei

Japanese幽霊 (yūrei)
Meaning"Faint/dim spirit"
OriginJapanese folklore
AppearanceWhite burial kimono, long black hair, dangling hands

Yūrei (幽霊) are figures in Japanese folklore analogous to ghosts in Western tradition. In Shinto and Buddhist belief, the soul (reikon) normally journeys to the afterlife after proper funeral rites; a person who dies suddenly or violently, or whose rites are neglected, may instead become a yūrei — a spirit bound to the physical world by a powerful emotion such as vengeance, love, or grief until it is laid to rest.

Appearance

The classic yūrei look was codified in Edo-period theatre and art: a white burial kimono, long unkempt black hair hanging over the face, limp hands dangling from the wrists, an elongated body often shown without feet, and sometimes two floating flames (hitodama) nearby. Famous examples include Oiwa of the kabuki play Tōkaidō Yotsuya Kaidan and Okiku, the servant who haunts a well counting plates.

In popular culture

Yūrei are the archetype behind modern Japanese horror: Sadako of Ring and Kayako of Ju-On follow the traditional costume and the onryō (vengeful spirit) pattern, carrying the imagery to global audiences. Yūrei are often grouped with the broader category of yōkai.
