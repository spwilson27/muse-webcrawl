Mithra

Mithra (Avestan: Miθra; Middle Persian: Mihr) is an ancient Iranian deity of covenants, light, and the sun, associated with contracts, justice, and warfare. Mithra is a central figure in Zoroastrianism, where he is a yazata (divinity worthy of worship) who presides over oaths and the orderly passage of the sun across the sky.

Origins and Indo-Iranian background

Mithra descends from the Indo-Iranian god *Mitra, cognate with the Vedic Mitra of the Rigveda, with whom he shares the role of guardian of ṛta (truth and cosmic order). The name derives from a root meaning "contract" or "covenant," reflecting the god's role as the divine witness of agreements: to break a contract was to offend Mithra himself.

In the Avesta

The Mihr Yasht, the hymn dedicated to Mithra in the Avesta, portrays him as a mighty warrior with a thousand ears and ten thousand eyes, who rides across the sky in a chariot drawn by white horses before the sun rises. He punishes covenant-breakers (mithra-druj, "those who lie to Mithra") and rewards the faithful with prosperity, victory, and healthy livestock.

Mithra in the Roman world

In the Roman Empire, Mithra became the central figure of Mithraism, a mystery religion popular among soldiers from the 1st to the 4th century CE. Roman Mithras is depicted slaying a bull in the tauroctony scene. Although Roman Mithraism clearly drew on the Iranian god's name and some imagery, scholars debate how much of its content was a Roman creation rather than a continuation of Iranian worship.

Legacy

The Persian festival of Mehregan honours Mithra (Mehr) and remains celebrated by Zoroastrians and some Iranians. The name survives in the modern Persian word mehr, meaning love, affection, and the sun. Remnants of Mithraic temples, called mithraea, have been excavated across the former Roman Empire, from Britain to Syria.
