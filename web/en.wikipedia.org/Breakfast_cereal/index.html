A breakfast cereal is a food made from processed cereal grains, traditionally eaten at breakfast, usually with milk. It includes hot cereals such as porridge and oatmeal, but the term most often denotes ready-to-eat cold cereals — flakes, puffs, shreds and shaped pieces sold boxed in supermarkets.

History

Cold breakfast cereal began in the United States in 1863 with Granula, a dense bran-nugget product by James Caleb Jackson, and was popularised by health reformers at sanitariums in Battle Creek, Michigan. There John Harvey Kellogg, and his brother Will Keith Kellogg, developed toasted corn flakes in the 1890s; W. K. Kellogg founded the company that became Kellogg's in 1906. Charles W. Post, a former Battle Creek patient, built a rival empire with Grape-Nuts (1897) and Post Toasties. Puffed grains, and later heavily sweetened children's cereals with cartoon mascots, followed in the twentieth century.

Production and nutrition

Cereals are made by flaking, shredding, puffing or extruding cooked grains, then toasting and coating, and are usually fortified with vitamins and minerals. Plain cereals such as shredded wheat or oatmeal are high in fibre, while many sweetened brands contain large amounts of added sugar, prompting long-running public-health criticism, particularly over marketing to children. Muesli and granola occupy a parallel, "natural" niche.

Cultural impact

Breakfast cereal became a staple of American and British breakfast tables and a major advertising category, funding early radio and television programming. Box-top premiums and prizes shaped generations of childhood consumer culture.
