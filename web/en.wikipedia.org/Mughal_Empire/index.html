The Mughal Empire was an early modern empire in South Asia, founded in 1526 by Babur, a Timurid prince from Central Asia who defeated the Delhi Sultanate at the First Battle of Panipat. At its height around 1700 the empire ruled most of the Indian subcontinent, with a population estimated at over 150 million — roughly a quarter of the world's population — and one of the largest economies on Earth.

The great emperors

Babur's grandson Akbar (r. 1556–1605) consolidated the empire, creating a centralised administration, a land-revenue system, and a policy of religious accommodation that bound Hindu Rajput princes into the imperial service. His successors Jahangir and Shah Jahan presided over a golden age of art and architecture — Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal in Agra and the Red Fort in Delhi. Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707) expanded the empire to its greatest territorial extent but strained its finances and cohesion with long wars in the Deccan and stricter religious policies.

Decline and fall

After Aurangzeb's death, succession struggles, Maratha expansion, regional secession, and invasions by Nader Shah of Persia (1739) and the Afghans hollowed out the empire. From the mid-18th century the British East India Company progressively eclipsed Mughal authority. After the Indian Rebellion of 1857, in which rebels proclaimed the aged Bahadur Shah II as emperor, the British exiled him to Burma and formally abolished the empire, establishing the British Raj.

Legacy

The Mughals left an enduring mark on South Asia: monumental architecture (the Taj Mahal, Humayun's Tomb, the forts of Agra and Delhi), miniature painting, the synthesis of Indo-Persian culture, the development of Urdu and of Mughlai cuisine, and administrative frameworks later adapted by the British.
