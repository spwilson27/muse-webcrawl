Dementia with Lewy bodies

Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is a type of dementia characterized by changes in sleep, behaviour, cognition, movement, and regulation of automatic body functions. It is caused by deposits of abnormal protein clumps, called Lewy bodies, inside neurons. DLB is one of the two Lewy body dementias, along with Parkinson's disease dementia.

Lewy bodies are abnormal aggregates of alpha-synuclein protein

Signs and symptoms

Core features of DLB include fluctuating cognition with pronounced variations in attention and alertness, recurrent complex visual hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, and one or more features of parkinsonism such as slowness of movement, rigidity, and tremor. Memory loss is not always an early symptom, distinguishing DLB from Alzheimer's disease. People with DLB can also show severe sensitivity to antipsychotic medications.

Causes

Like Parkinson's disease, DLB is associated with the abnormal accumulation of the protein alpha-synuclein in the brain. The exact mechanisms are not fully understood, and both genetic and environmental factors are thought to contribute.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by biomarkers such as dopamine transporter imaging, which shows reduced uptake in DLB compared with Alzheimer's disease, and polysomnography confirming REM sleep without atonia. Definitive diagnosis requires post-mortem examination of brain tissue.

Management

There is no cure. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as rivastigmine and donepezil can improve cognition and reduce hallucinations. Levodopa may help movement symptoms but can worsen psychiatric features, so treatment requires careful balancing. Prognosis averages roughly five to eight years after diagnosis.
