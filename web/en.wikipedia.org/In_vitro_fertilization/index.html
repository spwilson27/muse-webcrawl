In vitro fertilization

In vitro fertilization (IVF) is a process of fertilization in which an egg is combined with sperm in vitro ("in glass"). The process involves monitoring and stimulating a woman's ovulatory process, removing ova from her ovaries, and letting sperm fertilise them in a culture medium in a laboratory. After the fertilised egg (zygote) undergoes embryo culture for two to six days, it is transferred into the uterus with the intention of establishing a successful pregnancy.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), a common IVF technique.

History

IVF was developed by Robert Edwards, Patrick Steptoe, and Jean Purdy in Britain. The first successful birth of a child conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, occurred on 25 July 1978 in Oldham, England. Edwards received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010. Millions of babies have since been born worldwide using IVF and related assisted reproductive technologies.

Procedure

A typical cycle includes ovarian stimulation with gonadotropins, oocyte retrieval under ultrasound guidance, fertilisation in the laboratory (conventionally or by intracytoplasmic sperm injection), embryo culture, and embryo transfer. Surplus embryos may be cryopreserved. Success rates decline with maternal age; major risks include multiple pregnancy and ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome.

Indications

IVF is used to treat infertility from tubal damage, endometriosis, ovulatory disorders, severe male-factor infertility, and unexplained infertility, and enables preimplantation genetic testing and the use of donor eggs or surrogacy.
