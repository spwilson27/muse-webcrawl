Parables of Jesus

The parables of Jesus are short stories told by Jesus of Nazareth, recorded chiefly in the three Synoptic Gospels — Matthew, Mark, and Luke — that illustrate a moral or spiritual lesson. They constitute roughly a third of Jesus's recorded teachings and are among the best-known stories in world literature, including the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Sower, the Mustard Seed, and the Talents.

Parables typically feature vivid, everyday imagery drawn from first-century Palestinian life: farmers sowing seed, shepherds searching for lost sheep, merchants, kings, weddings, and vineyards. Jesus often used them to describe the Kingdom of God and to challenge his listeners' assumptions, frequently ending with a surprising reversal in which the lowly are exalted and the self-righteous are rebuked.

Major parables

Parables found in all three Synoptic Gospels include the Sower, the Mustard Seed, the Wicked Tenants, and the Leaven. Matthew and Luke share others, such as the Lost Sheep and the Great Banquet. Some of the most famous are unique to Luke: the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Rich Fool, Lazarus and the Rich Man, and the Pharisee and the Tax Collector. Matthew alone preserves the Wise and Foolish Virgins, the Sheep and the Goats, and the Workers in the Vineyard. The Gospel of John contains extended allegorical discourses, such as the Good Shepherd, rather than parables in the Synoptic sense.

Interpretation

Early Christian interpreters often read the parables allegorically, assigning a symbolic meaning to nearly every detail. In the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, scholars such as Adolf Jülicher argued that each parable makes essentially one main point. Later scholarship has emphasized the parables' literary artistry, their roots in Jewish storytelling traditions, and their function as provocative, open-ended narratives that invite the hearer to respond.
