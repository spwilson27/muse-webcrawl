The Wounded Knee Massacre, also known as the Battle of Wounded Knee, was a massacre of nearly three hundred Lakota people by soldiers of the United States Army on 29 December 1890 near Wounded Knee Creek on the Pine Ridge Indian Reservation in South Dakota. It is often regarded as the final major armed confrontation of the American Indian Wars.

Background

Amid the spread of the Ghost Dance movement — a religious revival promising renewal of the Native world — the U.S. government feared an uprising. The killing of Sitting Bull during an attempted arrest on 15 December 1890 heightened tensions. A band of Miniconjou Lakota led by Chief Spotted Elk (Big Foot), travelling to Pine Ridge under a white flag, was intercepted by the 7th Cavalry and escorted to Wounded Knee Creek.

The massacre

On the morning of 29 December, soldiers began disarming the Lakota. A shot was fired — possibly during a struggle over a rifle — and the cavalry opened fire with rifles and rapid-fire Hotchkiss guns positioned on a hill. Lakota men, women and children were killed in the camp and as they fled up a ravine; bodies were found miles away. Twenty soldiers were later awarded the Medal of Honor for the engagement, a decision long protested by Native Americans and others.

Aftermath and legacy

The dead were buried in a mass grave on the site. Wounded Knee became a symbol of the injustices inflicted on Native peoples; in 1973 members of the American Indian Movement occupied the site for 71 days. Descendants and members of Congress have repeatedly sought the revocation of the medals awarded in 1890.
