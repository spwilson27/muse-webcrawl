Identity crisis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In psychology, an identity crisis is the failure to achieve ego identity during adolescence. The term was coined by the developmental psychologist Erik Erikson, who placed "identity versus role confusion" as the fifth of his eight stages of psychosocial development, typically occurring between about ages 12 and 18.

Erikson's concept

Erikson held that adolescents must integrate childhood identifications, abilities, and social roles into a coherent sense of self. Successful resolution yields the virtue of fidelity; failure produces role confusion, uncertainty about one's place in society, and difficulty committing to occupation, ideology, and relationships. A period of psychosocial moratorium—socially sanctioned exploration before adult commitments—is considered normal and often necessary.

Marcia's identity statuses

James Marcia expanded Erikson's model into four identity statuses defined by the presence or absence of exploration (crisis) and commitment: identity diffusion (neither), foreclosure (commitment without exploration), moratorium (exploration without commitment), and identity achievement (exploration followed by commitment). Much empirical research on adolescent identity uses this framework.

Usage beyond adolescence

The phrase has broadened in popular usage to denote any period of questioning one's self-concept, including the "midlife crisis," and is also applied metaphorically to organisations, nations, and fictional characters. Clinicians note that identity disturbance is also a feature of certain personality disorders, which is distinct from the normative developmental crisis Erikson described.
