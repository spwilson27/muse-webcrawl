Meteor shower

A meteor shower is a celestial event in which a number of meteors are observed to radiate from one point in the night sky, called the radiant. Showers occur when Earth passes through the stream of debris left behind by a comet or, in a few cases, an asteroid. The particles, typically no larger than grains of sand, burn up in the upper atmosphere at speeds of 11–72 km/s.

Naming

Showers are named after the constellation in which their radiant lies: the Perseids (Perseus, peak mid-August, debris of Comet Swift–Tuttle), the Geminids (Gemini, mid-December, debris of the asteroid 3200 Phaethon), the Leonids (Leo, mid-November, Comet Tempel–Tuttle), and the Quadrantids (early January) are among the most prominent annual showers.

Meteor storms

When rates exceed roughly 1,000 meteors per hour, the event is termed a meteor storm. The Leonids produced spectacular storms in 1833 and 1966, events that helped establish the cometary origin of showers.

Observation

Showers are best observed after midnight from a dark site, when the observer's side of Earth faces into the debris stream. Activity is quantified by the zenithal hourly rate (ZHR).
