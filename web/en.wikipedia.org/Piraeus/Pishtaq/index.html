Pishtaq

A pishtaq is the monumental rectangular frame that surrounds a recessed arched portal — an iwan — in Islamic architecture, especially in Persia and Central Asia. Rising well above the roofline of the building behind it, the pishtaq forms a grand, often richly decorated entrance screen for mosques, madrasas, caravanserais and mausoleums.

Description

The pishtaq typically consists of a tall rectangular facade pierced by a large central arch, within which a smaller doorway opens. Its surfaces are a favored field for decoration: glazed tile mosaics, calligraphic bands in Arabic script, geometric girih patterns and muqarnas in the arch's semi-dome. Flanking minaret-like turrets or engaged columns sometimes mark its corners.

History and examples

The form developed in the Persian world under the Seljuks and reached its most splendid expression under the Timurids in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries. Famous examples include the pishtaqs of the Shah-i-Zinda necropolis and the Ulugh Beg Madrasa in Samarkand, and the mosques of Isfahan. Under the Mughals the concept traveled to India, where monumental gateways such as the Buland Darwaza at Fatehpur Sikri reflect the same impulse toward a towering ceremonial entrance.
