The University of Chicago (UChicago) is a private research university in Chicago, Illinois. Founded in 1890 with funding from John D. Rockefeller and land donated by Marshall Field, it opened in 1892 under its first president, William Rainey Harper. The university is known for its rigorous Core Curriculum, the Chicago schools of economics, sociology, and law and economics, and a large number of Nobel laureates among its faculty, students, and researchers.

History

Harper modelled the new university on the German research ideal combined with an American undergraduate college. Early achievements included Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiment and the founding of the first sociology department in the United States. In 1942, beneath the university's Stagg Field, Enrico Fermi and colleagues achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction (Chicago Pile-1) as part of the Manhattan Project. President Robert Maynard Hutchins reshaped undergraduate education around the Great Books in the 1930s–40s.

Academics

The university comprises an undergraduate College and graduate divisions and professional schools, including the Booth School of Business, the Law School, the Pritzker School of Medicine, and the Harris School of Public Policy. It operates Argonne National Laboratory and Fermilab for the U.S. Department of Energy and runs the University of Chicago Press, the largest university press in the United States (founded 1890).

Campus

The 217-acre campus in Hyde Park, seven miles south of downtown Chicago, is noted for Collegiate Gothic architecture, the Robie House by Frank Lloyd Wright, and newer buildings by prominent architects. The campus was designated a botanic garden in 1997.
