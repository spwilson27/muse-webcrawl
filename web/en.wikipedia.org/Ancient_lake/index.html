Ancient lake

An ancient lake is a lake that has consistently carried water for more than one million years, far longer than the typical lifespan of a lake, which is usually ended by sediment infilling within tens of thousands of years. Most ancient lakes occupy tectonic basins—especially continental rift valleys—where subsidence continually deepens the basin faster than sediment can fill it.

Formation and persistence

Only about twenty lakes worldwide are recognised as ancient. They survive because tectonic activity maintains their depth, often in active rift systems such as the East African Rift or the Baikal Rift Zone. Their longevity also requires relatively stable inflow and, in many cases, great depth that slows ecological turnover.

Biodiversity and evolution

Ancient lakes are celebrated as natural laboratories of evolution. Isolated for millions of years, they harbour extraordinary numbers of endemic species found nowhere else: the cichlid fishes of Lakes Tanganyika and Malawi, the amphipod and seal fauna of Lake Baikal, and the gastropods of Lake Ohrid and Lake Titicaca. Their sediments preserve long, continuous archives of past climate, read by palaeolimnologists through drill cores.

Examples

The oldest and deepest is Lake Baikal in Siberia (about 25–35 million years old, 1,642 m deep). Others include Lake Tanganyika, Lake Ohrid, Lake Titicaca, the Caspian Sea, Lake Biwa, and Lake Malawi.
