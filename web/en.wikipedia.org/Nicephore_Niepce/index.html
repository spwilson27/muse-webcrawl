Nicephore Niepce (born Joseph Niepce; 7 March 1765 – 5 July 1833) was a French inventor and one of the pioneers of photography. He developed heliography, a technique he used to create the oldest surviving photograph of the real world, the View from the Window at Le Gras, usually dated to about 1826 or 1827.

Working at his estate of Le Gras near Chalon-sur-Saone, Niepce coated a pewter plate with bitumen of Judea, exposed it in a camera obscura for many hours (perhaps days), and washed away the unhardened bitumen with lavender oil and turpentine, leaving a permanent direct-positive image.

Career

With his brother Claude, Niepce also built the pyreolophore (patented 1807), one of the world's first internal combustion engines, powered by lycopodium powder, which propelled a boat on the Saone. His experiments with light-sensitive materials began as an attempt to improve lithographic printing. In 1816 he obtained negative images on paper sensitized with silver chloride but could not fix them permanently.

Partnership with Daguerre

In 1829 Niepce entered a partnership with Louis Daguerre to perfect heliography. Niepce died suddenly in 1833; Daguerre went on to develop the daguerreotype, announced in 1839. Niepce's contribution was long overshadowed, but the surviving heliograph — rediscovered in the 20th century by Helmut Gernsheim — is now recognised as the first photograph, and is preserved at the Harry Ransom Center in Austin, Texas.
