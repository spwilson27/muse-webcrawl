Michel de Montaigne

Born28 February 1533, Saint-Michel-de-Montaigne, France
Died13 September 1592 (aged 59)
OccupationWriter, philosopher, statesman
Notable workEssais (Essays)

Michel Eyquem, Seigneur de Montaigne (28 February 1533 – 13 September 1592), commonly known as Michel de Montaigne, was one of the most significant philosophers of the French Renaissance. He is known for popularising the essay as a literary genre. His work is noted for its merging of casual anecdotes and autobiography with intellectual insight, and has had a substantial influence on later writers including Shakespeare, Descartes, Pascal, Rousseau and Emerson.

Life

Montaigne was born into a wealthy family on the family estate, the Château de Montaigne, near Bordeaux. His father, Pierre Eyquem, had novel ideas about education: the infant Montaigne was sent to live with a poor peasant family to learn humility, and was then raised speaking only Latin, taught by a German tutor who knew no French. He studied law and served as a counsellor of the Parlement of Bordeaux from 1557. His close friendship with Étienne de La Boétie, author of the Discourse on Voluntary Servitude, profoundly shaped him; La Boétie's early death in 1563 left a lasting mark.

In 1571 Montaigne retired from public life to the tower of his château, where he began writing the essays that would occupy him for the rest of his life. He nevertheless served two terms as mayor of Bordeaux (1581–1585) during the French Wars of Religion, acting as a moderate mediator between Catholics and Huguenots, and travelled through Germany, Switzerland and Italy in 1580–1581.

The Essays

The Essais were first published in 1580 in two books; a third book and hundreds of additions appeared in the edition of 1588, and Montaigne continued revising until his death. The famous motto "Que sçay-je?" ("What do I know?") expresses his sceptical, self-examining method: rather than constructing a doctrine, he studied himself as a specimen of humanity.

"I study myself more than any other subject. That is my metaphysics, that is my physics."

The essays range over education, friendship, cannibals, custom, solitude, experience and death, and are deliberately digressive and self-contradictory, reflecting a mind in motion rather than a finished system.
