Huygens–Fresnel principle

Summary

Field: Wave optics, physics

Proposed by: Christiaan Huygens (1690); extended by Augustin-Jean Fresnel (1816)

Application: Diffraction and wave propagation

The Huygens–Fresnel principle is a method of analysis applied to problems of wave propagation, including light. It states that every point on a wavefront can be regarded as a source of secondary spherical wavelets, and that the wavefront at a later time is the envelope tangent to all of these wavelets.

History

Christiaan Huygens proposed the principle in his Traité de la lumière (1690) to explain the propagation, reflection, and refraction of light. His construction explained many phenomena but could not account for diffraction effects. In 1816, Augustin-Jean Fresnel extended the idea by adding the principle of interference: the secondary wavelets mutually interfere, so the amplitude at any later point is the superposition of all the wavelets reaching it. This Huygens–Fresnel theory successfully explained diffraction patterns, including the famous bright spot at the centre of a circular obstacle's shadow (the Poisson spot), which helped establish the wave theory of light.

Modern formulation

Gustav Kirchhoff later gave the principle a firmer mathematical foundation in his diffraction theory, deriving an obliquity factor that suppresses the backward-propagating wave implied by the original construction. In modern physics, the principle is consistent with the path-integral picture in quantum mechanics, in which a particle or photon explores all paths and the observed behaviour emerges from the interference of contributions from each path.
