Progressive Era

The Progressive Era (1896–1920) was a period of widespread social activism and political reform across the United States. Progressives sought to address problems caused by industrialization, urbanization, immigration, and political corruption. The movement's leaders — including presidents Theodore Roosevelt, William Howard Taft, and Woodrow Wilson — expanded the role of the federal government in regulating business, protecting consumers and workers, and conserving natural resources.

Origins and themes

Progressivism grew from the populist and reform movements of the late 19th century and from the investigative journalism of "muckrakers" such as Ida Tarbell, who exposed the Standard Oil trust; Upton Sinclair, whose novel The Jungle (1906) revealed conditions in meatpacking; and Lincoln Steffens, who documented municipal corruption. Reformers shared faith in expertise, science, and efficient government, though they differed sharply on race, immigration, and the scope of federal power.

Reforms

Major achievements included antitrust enforcement and the breakup of corporate monopolies; the Pure Food and Drug Act and Meat Inspection Act (1906); the creation of the Federal Reserve System (1913); federal regulation of railroads and communications; and conservation of public lands under Roosevelt. Four constitutional amendments date to the era: the Sixteenth (income tax, 1913), Seventeenth (direct election of senators, 1913), Eighteenth (Prohibition, 1919), and Nineteenth (women's suffrage, 1920). At state and local levels, Progressives introduced initiatives, referendums, recalls, direct primaries, city-manager government, and laws limiting child labour and improving workplace safety — spurred in part by disasters such as the Triangle Shirtwaist fire of 1911.

Limits and legacy

The movement largely excluded African Americans, whose disenfranchisement in the South intensified during the era, and many Progressives supported immigration restriction and eugenics. American entry into the First World War in 1917 diverted reform energies, and the era is generally dated to about 1920. Progressive precedents — an activist federal government, regulatory agencies, and direct-democracy mechanisms — permanently reshaped American politics and laid groundwork for the New Deal.
