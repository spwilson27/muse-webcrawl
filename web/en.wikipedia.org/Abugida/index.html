Abugida

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An abugida (from Ge'ez አቡጊዳ, ʾabugida, the first four characters of the Ethiopic script) is a segmental writing system in which consonant–vowel sequences are written as units: each sign is based on a consonant letter with an inherent vowel (usually /a/), and other vowels are indicated by systematic modifications to the sign. The term was proposed by Peter T. Daniels in 1990. Abugidas include the large Brahmic family of South and Southeast Asia (Devanagari, Bengali, Tamil, Thai, and many others), the Ethiopic script, and Canadian Aboriginal syllabics.

Structure

In an abugida, a consonant letter alone is read as the consonant plus a default vowel—typically /a/ or /ə/. To write a different vowel, the letter is modified with a diacritic (such as a stroke, hook, or surrounding mark), or the vowel sign may be placed before, after, above, or below the consonant. To write a consonant with no following vowel, most abugidas use a special "killer" mark (the virama or halant in Indic scripts) that cancels the inherent vowel; alternatively, special conjunct forms or reduced forms of the letter may be used, and final consonants may take distinct shapes, as in Tibetan or Khmer.

History

The earliest abugida is generally considered to be Brahmi, attested in India by the 3rd century BCE, from which nearly all South and Southeast Asian scripts descend. The Ethiopic (Ge'ez) script developed its vocalised form in the 4th century CE, likely under Indian influence. Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, devised in the 1840s by missionary James Evans for Ojibwe and Cree, apply the abugida principle by rotating consonant shapes to indicate vowels.

Abugida vs. alphabet and syllabary

Abugidas differ from alphabets, in which vowels have full letter status equal to consonants, and from syllabaries, in which signs for similar syllables (e.g. /ka/, /ki/, /ku/) typically show no graphic resemblance. Because the consonant sign is primary and vowels are secondary modifications, an abugida is sometimes called an "alphasyllabary."
