Geneva

Geneva (French: Genève) is the second-most populous city in Switzerland, after Zürich, and the most populous city of Romandy, the French-speaking part of the country. Situated where the Rhône exits Lake Geneva, it is the capital of the Republic and Canton of Geneva. The city has a population of about 204,000, with close to a million in its metropolitan area, which extends into neighbouring France.

International Geneva

Geneva is a global city and a centre of diplomacy. It hosts the European headquarters of the United Nations at the Palais des Nations, and numerous international organisations including the International Committee of the Red Cross (founded here in 1863), the World Health Organization, the World Trade Organization, the UN Refugee Agency, and CERN's main site nearby in Meyrin. The Geneva Conventions of 1949, which form the core of international humanitarian law, were concluded in the city.

History

Geneva was an ancient settlement of the Allobroges, taken by Rome in 121 BC. In the sixteenth century, under John Calvin, it became a centre of the Protestant Reformation — the "Protestant Rome" — attracting religious refugees whose skills seeded its watchmaking and banking industries. The Republic of Geneva joined the Swiss Confederation in 1815. Its tradition of neutrality and humanitarianism made it the chosen seat of the League of Nations after the First World War.

Landmarks and economy

Landmarks include the Jet d'Eau, a 140-metre fountain in the lake that is the city's symbol; St Pierre Cathedral, where Calvin preached; and the Old Town. Geneva is a major financial centre, strong in private banking, and a hub of the Swiss watch industry, home to firms such as Rolex and Patek Philippe. It is consistently ranked among the world's most expensive cities and highest in quality of life.
