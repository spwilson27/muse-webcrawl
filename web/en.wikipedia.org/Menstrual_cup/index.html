Menstrual cup

A menstrual cup is a menstrual hygiene device inserted into the vagina during menstruation. Its purpose is to collect menstrual fluid (blood from the uterine lining mixed with other secretions). Menstrual cups are usually made of flexible medical-grade silicone, latex, or a thermoplastic isomer. They are shaped like a bell with a stem or ring on the base.

Unlike tampons and pads, cups collect menstrual fluid rather than absorbing it. One cup may be reused for up to 12 hours, depending on the amount of flow. Most menstrual cup brands sell two sizes: a smaller one recommended for women under 30 or who have never given birth vaginally, and a larger one for women over 30, who have given birth vaginally, or who have heavy flows.

History

An early version of a bulb-shaped menstrual cup was patented in 1932 by the midwifery group of McGlasson and Perkins. Leona Chalmers patented the first usable commercial cup in 1937, made of rubber. Later versions included the Tassette and the disposable Tassaway. The Keeper, a latex rubber version, appeared in 1987, followed by the first silicone cup, the Mooncup, in 2002.

Use

The cup is folded and inserted into the vagina, where it unfolds and seals against the vaginal walls below the cervix. To remove it, the user pinches the base to break the seal, empties the contents, rinses the cup, and reinserts it. After each period it should be cleaned more thoroughly, often by boiling.

Safety and advantages

Menstrual cups are considered safe when used as directed; no major health risks related to their use have been detected in medical studies. They produce far less waste than disposable products and can be more economical over time, as a single cup can last up to ten years.
