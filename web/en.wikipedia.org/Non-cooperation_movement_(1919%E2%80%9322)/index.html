Non-cooperation movement (1919–22)

Dates1920 – February 1922
LocationBritish India
LeaderMahatma Gandhi
AimSwaraj (self-rule); redress for Punjab wrongs and Khilafat

The non-cooperation movement was a nationwide campaign of nonviolent resistance launched by Mahatma Gandhi and the Indian National Congress in 1920, the first mass movement of the Indian independence struggle on an all-India scale. It sought swaraj (self-government) through the withdrawal of cooperation from the British Raj.

Causes and programme

The movement grew out of anger at the Rowlatt Acts, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre of 1919, and the Khilafat issue concerning the fate of the Ottoman caliphate, which won the support of many Indian Muslims. Its programme included boycotting foreign cloth, government schools, courts, and titles, resigning from official posts, and promoting khadi (hand-spun cloth) and national institutions.

Course and suspension

Millions participated; imports of British cloth fell sharply and leaders including Gandhi courted arrest. In February 1922, after a mob at Chauri Chaura killed 22 policemen, Gandhi suspended the movement, judging that it was turning violent. He was arrested shortly afterwards and imprisoned for sedition. Though it did not achieve swaraj, the movement transformed Congress into a mass organization and set the pattern for later campaigns such as the civil disobedience movement.
