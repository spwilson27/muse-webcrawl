Hazing

Hazing (American English), initiation ceremonies (British English), bastonnade (French), ragging (South Asia), or devilling (as a form of initiation) refers to any activity expected of someone in joining or participating in a group that humiliates, degrades, abuses, or endangers them regardless of a person's willingness to participate. Hazing is seen in many types of social groups, including gangs, sports teams, military units, fraternities and sororities, and schools.

Scope and forms

Hazing practices range from relatively benign pranks — scavenger hunts, wearing embarrassing costumes, servitude — to severe abuse, including forced alcohol or food consumption, sleep deprivation, beatings (paddling), sexual acts, and dangerous stunts. Surveys of American students have found that roughly half of students involved in clubs, teams and organizations experience hazing, most commonly involving alcohol. In the United States, at least one hazing death per year has been recorded in most years since 1961, frequently linked to alcohol poisoning among fraternity pledges, such as the 2017 death of Timothy Piazza at Pennsylvania State University, which prompted reforms and the federal Stop Campus Hazing Act, passed in 2024.

Law and prevention

Forty-four U.S. states have anti-hazing statutes, though they vary in strength and rarely criminalize the hazers' organizations. Outside the U.S., ragging is prohibited in several South Asian countries following student deaths; in India the University Grants Commission mandates anti-ragging measures in higher education. Militaries generally prohibit hazing officially while tolerating traditions such as "blood pinning"; the Russian army's dedovshchina system of senior-on-junior abuse has been linked to suicides and desertion. Many universities and national fraternity organizations now adopt zero-tolerance policies, bystander-intervention education, and amnesty (Good Samaritan) provisions encouraging students to seek help.

Psychology and debate

Defenders frame mild initiation rites as bonding and tradition; psychologists note that severe shared hardship can increase group cohesion and loyalty (effort justification), which helps explain persistence of the practice, but research also links hazing to trauma, injury and death, and finds safer alternatives can produce comparable cohesion. Anthropologists relate hazing to rites of passage studied by Arnold van Gennep and Victor Turner, in which initiates pass through a liminal phase before incorporation into the group.
