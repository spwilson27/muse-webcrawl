Self-concept

FieldPsychology, social psychology
ComponentsSelf-image, self-esteem, ideal self
Key figuresCarl Rogers, William James, Charles Cooley

Self-concept is a person's collection of beliefs about themselves, including their attributes and who and what the self is. It is how someone perceives their behaviour, abilities and unique characteristics — a mental picture of oneself as, for example, a good friend, a competitive athlete or a shy person. Self-concept develops through childhood and adolescence, shaped by social interaction and reflected appraisal from others.

Components

Carl Rogers divided the self-concept into three components: self-image (how one sees oneself), self-esteem or self-worth (how much value one places on oneself), and the ideal self (the person one wishes to be). When the self-image and ideal self are far apart, Rogers described the state as incongruence, associated with psychological distress.

Development

Charles Cooley's "looking-glass self" (1902) proposed that self-concept arises from imagining how we appear to others and how they judge us. George Herbert Mead similarly emphasised the social origins of the self. Self-concept emerges in early childhood, becomes more abstract and multi-dimensional during adolescence, and differentiation across domains (academic, social, athletic) increases with age.

Related concepts

Self-concept is distinct from self-awareness (consciousness of one's existence) and self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to succeed at specific tasks, in Albert Bandura's theory). Academic self-concept in particular shows reciprocal effects with achievement.
