Timbuktu (French: Tombouctou) is a city in Mali, situated near the Niger River on the southern edge of the Sahara Desert. Founded as a seasonal Tuareg camp around 1100, it became a legendary centre of trans-Saharan trade and Islamic scholarship, and a byword in many languages for a remote, exotic place.

Golden age

Under the Mali Empire — especially after Mansa Musa's pilgrimage of 1324 — and then the Songhai Empire, Timbuktu flourished as an entrepôt where gold from the south was exchanged for salt from the northern desert. Its Sankore University and some 180 Quranic schools taught thousands of students, and its libraries accumulated hundreds of thousands of manuscripts on astronomy, law, medicine and history. The mud-brick Djinguereber and Sankore mosques date from this era.

Decline and rediscovery

A Moroccan invasion in 1591 shattered Songhai power, exiled many scholars and began the city's long decline, worsened as trade shifted to Atlantic ports. Europeans reached the city only in the 19th century — Alexander Gordon Laing in 1826 (murdered on departure) and René Caillié in 1828, who survived to report back. France occupied Timbuktu in 1893–94.

Modern city

Today Timbuktu is the capital of Mali's Tombouctou Region, threatened by desertification and, in 2012, by occupation by armed Islamist groups who destroyed Sufi shrines — war crimes later prosecuted at the International Criminal Court. Efforts by local families and the Ahmed Baba Institute to preserve the city's manuscripts continue.
