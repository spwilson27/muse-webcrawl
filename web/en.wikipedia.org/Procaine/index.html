Procaine is a local anesthetic drug of the amino ester group. It is most commonly used in dental procedures to numb the area around a tooth and is also used to reduce the pain of intramuscular injection of penicillin. Owing largely to the ubiquity of the trade name Novocain (without the final "e"), procaine is sometimes referred to generically as novocaine or novocain. It acts mainly as a sodium channel blocker.

History

Procaine was first synthesized in 1905 by the German chemist Alfred Einhorn, who sought a safer synthetic substitute for cocaine, then widely used as a local anaesthetic but toxic and addictive. Einhorn gave the compound the trade name Novocain, from Latin novus ("new") and "caine", the suffix common to alkaloid anaesthetics. It was introduced into medical use by the surgeon Heinrich Braun, who combined it with adrenaline to prolong its effect. Procaine became the standard dental anaesthetic for decades before being largely superseded by lidocaine and other amide anaesthetics introduced from the 1940s onwards.

Pharmacology

Like other local anaesthetics, procaine blocks nerve conduction by reversibly binding to voltage-gated sodium channels in neuronal membranes, preventing the initiation and propagation of action potentials. It has a relatively slow onset and short duration of action. Procaine is rapidly hydrolysed by plasma cholinesterase to para-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) and diethylaminoethanol; PABA is responsible for a higher rate of allergic reactions than seen with amide-type anaesthetics.

Adverse effects

Adverse effects are uncommon but may include allergic reactions, nervousness, dizziness, convulsions and, with systemic toxicity, cardiovascular effects. People deficient in pseudocholinesterase may metabolise procaine more slowly.
