James Joseph Sylvester FRS (3 September 1814 – 15 March 1897) was an English mathematician. He made fundamental contributions to matrix theory, invariant theory, number theory, partition theory and combinatorics, and played a leading role in the development of American mathematics in the later nineteenth century. A prolific coiner of mathematical terms, he introduced words such as "matrix," "invariant," "discriminant" and "totient" into the mathematical vocabulary.

Early life and education

Sylvester was born in London to a Jewish family; his birth name was James Joseph. He studied at St John's College, Cambridge, finishing as second wrangler in 1837, but as a Jew he could not then take his degree or compete for the Smith's Prizes, receiving his degrees instead from Trinity College Dublin. Religious tests also barred him from a Cambridge fellowship, shaping a career spent largely outside Oxbridge until late in life.

Career

In 1841 Sylvester briefly became professor of mathematics at the University of Virginia — an episode that ended after a dispute with a student — then returned to England, worked as an actuary and studied law. From 1855 to 1869 he was professor at the Royal Military Academy, Woolwich. His most fruitful period began in 1876, when he became the first professor of mathematics at the new Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore. There he founded the American Journal of Mathematics in 1878, the first mathematics research journal in the United States, and supervised a generation of American researchers. In 1883 he returned to England as Savilian Professor of Geometry at Oxford.

Mathematical work

With his friend Arthur Cayley, Sylvester created the algebraic theory of invariants, studying quantities associated with polynomial forms that remain unchanged under linear transformations. He proved the law of inertia for quadratic forms (1852), which bears his name, and developed the theory of elementary divisors and canonical forms with Cayley and Hermite. In number theory he worked on partitions and the theory of coin denominations: with Frobenius, his name attaches to the result that the largest integer not representable as a combination of two coprime coin values a and b is ab − a − b. The Sylvester sequence and the concept of a Sylvester matrix (resultant matrix) also commemorate him.

Legacy

Sylvester was also an amateur poet who published The Laws of Verse (1870). The Royal Society's Sylvester Medal for mathematical research was instituted in his memory in 1901. He died in London in 1897.
