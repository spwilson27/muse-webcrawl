Neal Leon Cassady (February 8, 1926 – February 4, 1968) was an American figure of the Beat Generation of the 1950s and the psychedelic and counterculture movements of the 1960s. He was the model for the character Dean Moriarty in Jack Kerouac's novel On the Road (1957), and served as the archetype for the free-spirited, fast-talking hero of the road in American literature. His energetic, stream-of-consciousness letters to Kerouac helped inspire the spontaneous prose style of the Beats.

Early life

Cassady was born in Salt Lake City, Utah, and raised largely in Denver, Colorado, by an alcoholic father, spending part of his youth in flophouses on Denver's skid row and in reform school after arrests for car theft. Largely self-educated, he became an avid reader and letter writer.

Beat Generation

In 1946 Cassady traveled to New York City, where he met Kerouac, Allen Ginsberg, and other members of the emerging Beat circle at Columbia University. His cross-country journeys with Kerouac in the late 1940s formed the basis of On the Road. Cassady's long, unpunctuated "Joan Anderson letter" of December 1950 profoundly influenced Kerouac's writing method. His own autobiographical novel, The First Third, was published posthumously in 1971.

Cassady worked for years as a brakeman for the Southern Pacific Railroad. His personal life — including marriages to LuAnne Henderson and Carolyn Cassady, and a bigamy scandal in 1958 that led to imprisonment at San Quentin for a narcotics offense — became part of Beat legend; Carolyn Cassady's memoir Off the Road (1990) documents the period.

1960s and death

In the 1960s Cassady became the driver of Furthur, the psychedelic bus of Ken Kesey and the Merry Pranksters, linking the Beat Generation to the hippie movement; the journeys are chronicled in Tom Wolfe's The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test (1968). Under the name "Cowboy Neal," he was later immortalized in the Grateful Dead song "That's It for the Other One."

Cassady died on February 4, 1968, near San Miguel de Allende, Mexico, four days before his 42nd birthday, after collapsing beside railroad tracks following a wedding party; the cause of death was attributed to exposure and the effects of drugs and alcohol.
