Apostille Convention

The Apostille Convention, formally the Hague Convention Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents, is an international treaty concluded on 5 October 1961 under the Hague Conference on Private International Law. It simplifies the authentication of public documents — such as birth certificates, court orders and notarised papers — for use in other member states.

How it works

Under the convention, a document issued in one member state can be certified for legal use in another by a designated competent authority, which attaches a standardised certificate called an apostille. The apostille confirms the authenticity of the signature, the capacity of the signer and the seal or stamp on the document. It replaces the older, slower chain of consular legalisation.

Membership

More than 120 states are party to the convention, including the United States, the United Kingdom, most European states, India and China (which acceded in 2023). States periodically adopt electronic apostille (e-Apostille) systems under the e-APP programme.
