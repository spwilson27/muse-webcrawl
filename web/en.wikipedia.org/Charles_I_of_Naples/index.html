Charles I (1226/27–1285), also known as Charles of Anjou, was the youngest son of King Louis VIII of France and brother of Louis IX. As Count of Anjou and of Provence, he built a Mediterranean power base, and in 1266, invested by the Pope, he conquered the Hohenstaufen Kingdom of Sicily, founding the Angevin dynasty that long ruled Naples.

Conquest of Sicily

Pope Clement IV enlisted Charles against Manfred of Sicily, offering him the crown as a papal vassal. Charles's army defeated and killed Manfred at Benevento (1266) and destroyed the last Hohenstaufen claimant, Conradin, at Tagliacozzo (1268); Conradin was beheaded at Naples. Charles established his capital at Naples and ruled Sicily and southern Italy with a French-dominated administration whose taxes and officials bred resentment.

Mediterranean ambitions

Charles acquired claims and territories across the Mediterranean: he bought the title King of Jerusalem (1277), became Prince of Achaea in Greece, intervened in Albania and Tunisia (where his brother Louis IX died on crusade in 1270), and by the Treaty of Viterbo (1267) planned the reconquest of Constantinople from the Byzantines. His ambitions alarmed Michael VIII Palaiologos, who subsidised his enemies.

The Sicilian Vespers

On Easter Monday 1282 a revolt — the Sicilian Vespers — erupted in Palermo and massacred the French on the island. Peter III of Aragon intervened and was crowned king of Sicily; despite the loss of the island, Charles and his Angevin successors retained the mainland kingdom, still called the Kingdom of Sicily (or Naples), for generations. Dante placed Charles in Purgatory and remembered his dynasty's wars as a curse on Italy. Charles died at Foggia in January 1285 while preparing a counterattack.
