Population growth is the increase in the number of people in a population or dispersed group. Global human population growth amounts to around 75–80 million annually, or about 1 percent per year, though the growth rate has been declining since its peak of roughly 2.1 percent per year in the late 1960s. The world population grew from 1 billion in 1800 to 8 billion in 2022.

Components

Population change is determined by births, deaths, and (for sub-global units) migration. The rate of natural increase is the crude birth rate minus the crude death rate. Historically, growth accelerated when mortality fell — first through improved food supply, sanitation and medicine — while fertility remained high, a stage of the demographic transition. Fertility subsequently fell in most regions, slowing growth.

Projections

United Nations projections indicate that world population will continue to grow for several decades, peaking at around 10.3–10.4 billion in the 2080s before slowly declining. Most future growth is concentrated in sub-Saharan Africa, while populations in Europe and East Asia are shrinking. Global fertility is now near 2.3 children per woman, and more than half of all countries have below-replacement fertility.

Consequences and concerns

Rapid population growth strains resources, infrastructure and services, and was the focus of Malthusian and neo-Malthusian concern, famously in Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb (1968). Conversely, slowing growth and population decline raise concerns about ageing populations, pension sustainability and labour shortages. The environmental impact of population is mediated by consumption and technology, summarized in the I = PAT identity (Impact = Population × Affluence × Technology).
