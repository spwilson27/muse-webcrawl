Captive breeding

Captive breeding, also known as captive propagation, is the process of maintaining plants or animals in controlled environments, such as wildlife reserves, zoos, botanical gardens, and conservation facilities. For endangered species it is used to build up populations for reintroduction into the wild and to maintain a genetic insurance population against extinction.

Methods and challenges

Programs use studbooks and cooperative breeding plans (such as the Association of Zoos and Aquariums' Species Survival Plans) to manage genetic diversity and avoid inbreeding. Difficulties include captive animals' adaptation to captivity, loss of learned behaviors needed in the wild, disease, and high costs. Successful reintroductions are often paired with habitat protection.

Successes

Notable successes include the California condor, reduced to 27 birds in 1987 and recovered to several hundred; the Arabian oryx, extinct in the wild in 1972 and reintroduced from zoo stock; the black-footed ferret; the golden lion tamarin; and Przewalski's horse.
