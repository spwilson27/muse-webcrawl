Hagia Sophia (Greek: Ἁγία Σοφία, "Holy Wisdom"; Turkish: Ayasofya) is a monumental structure in Istanbul, Turkey. Built in 532–537 CE under the Byzantine emperor Justinian I, it was the largest cathedral in the world for nearly a thousand years and is considered the epitome of Byzantine architecture, famous for its massive dome set on pendentives—a revolutionary structure that seemed, contemporaries said, to hang suspended from heaven.

History

The present church was the third on the site, its two predecessors destroyed by riots. Designed by the geometers Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, it was completed in under six years; its dome partially collapsed after earthquakes and was rebuilt higher in 562. Hagia Sophia served as the cathedral of Constantinople and the seat of the Ecumenical Patriarch, the setting for imperial coronations, and the ceremonial heart of the empire until the Ottoman conquest in 1453, when Mehmed II converted it into a mosque. Minarets, a mihrab, and great calligraphic roundels were added; its figural mosaics were plastered over.

Museum and reconversion

In 1935 the Turkish Republic under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk secularised the building as a museum, and restorers uncovered celebrated mosaics, including the Deësis and the apse Virgin and Child. It forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the Historic Areas of Istanbul. In July 2020 a Turkish court annulled the 1934 museum decree and presidential decree reclassified it as a mosque, whereupon it reopened for Muslim worship while remaining open to visitors; the move drew international debate about heritage stewardship.

Architecture

The nave is crowned by a dome 31 metres across, ringed at its base by forty windows that flood the interior with light and dematerialise the structure's mass. The design—longitudinal basilica fused with a central dome—influenced Ottoman mosque architects, above all Sinan, whose mosques across Istanbul answer it across the centuries.
