Slab serif

In typography, a slab serif (also called a mechanistic, square serif, or Egyptian typeface) is a style of serif typeface characterized by thick, block-like serifs, often as thick as the letter strokes themselves, with little or no bracketing where serif meets stem.

History

Slab serifs emerged in early nineteenth-century Britain amid the explosion of advertising and display printing; the earliest dated example is Vincent Figgins's “Antique” of about 1815. Their bold forms were designed to shout from posters. The style called “Egyptian” reflected the era's fascination with Egypt after Napoleon's campaigns. The “Clarendon” model (1845) added bracketing and became a staple of wood type and wanted posters in the American West.

Varieties and use

Twentieth-century designers created text-weight slab serifs and geometric variants such as Memphis, Rockwell, and Courier (a monospaced slab serif long standard for typewriters and screenplays). Humanist examples include PMN Caecilia and Chaparral. Slab serifs are used in logos, headlines, and branding to convey sturdiness, and in long-form digital reading interfaces, where fonts like Roboto Slab are popular.
