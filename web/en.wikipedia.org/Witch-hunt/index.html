A witch-hunt, or witch purge, is a search for people believed to be witches or to practise witchcraft, often accompanied by moral panic and followed by trials and punishment. Historically, the term refers to the witch trials of early modern Europe and colonial America; in modern usage it also denotes figuratively any campaign of harassment against a targeted group, such as the anti-communist investigations associated with McCarthyism.

Early modern witch trials

Between about 1450 and 1750, tens of thousands of people — the majority of them women — were executed for witchcraft in Europe, with the most intense persecutions occurring during the confessional conflicts of the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries. Accusations typically involved maleficium (harmful magic) and, increasingly, an alleged pact with the Devil. Treatises such as the Malleus Maleficarum (1486) shaped prosecutions. In colonial New England, the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 led to twenty executions.

Causes and end

Historians link the hunts to religious conflict, social and economic stress, climate deterioration during the Little Ice Age, and legal systems that admitted torture and spectral evidence. Prosecutions declined as judicial standards tightened, scepticism spread among elites, and states centralised control of the courts; the last European executions for witchcraft occurred in the late eighteenth century.

Modern witch-hunts

Accusations of witchcraft and related violence persist in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, where thousands of people — often elderly women — have been attacked or killed in recent decades. Figuratively, "witch-hunt" is widely used to describe politically motivated persecution.
