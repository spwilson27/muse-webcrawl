Form of government

States of the world colour-coded by their form of government.

A form of government (or the structure of government, in some contexts system of government) is the system or group of systems a state uses to organize its political authority — the set of political institutions by which a government of a state is organized. The term refers principally to the constitutional character of a state: who holds power, how leaders are selected, and the relationship between the executive, legislature and judiciary.

Classical classifications

Aristotle classified constitutions by the number of rulers — one, few or many — and by whether they ruled in the common interest or their own: kingship versus tyranny, aristocracy versus oligarchy, and polity versus democracy. Modern classifications usually start from two axes: the source of legitimacy and head of state (republic or monarchy), and the territorial distribution of power (unitary or federal).

Common forms

  • Republic — head of state is not a hereditary monarch; includes presidential, semi-presidential and parliamentary republics.
  • Monarchy — a monarch serves as head of state, either with substantial power (absolute monarchy) or largely ceremonial authority (constitutional monarchy).
  • Democracy — political power rests with the people, exercised directly or through elected representatives, usually contrasted with authoritarianism and totalitarianism.
  • Federation and unitary state — describing respectively divided and centralized territorial power.
  • Theocracy — rule by religious authority, as in Iran or Vatican City.
  • One-party state — a single political party monopolizes government, as in China.

Systems of government

Within republics and constitutional monarchies, the relationship between executive and legislature gives rise to presidential systems, in which the executive is separately elected and survives independently of the legislature; parliamentary systems, in which the government depends on legislative confidence; and semi-presidential systems, which combine a popularly elected president with a prime minister responsible to parliament.

De facto versus de jure

The formal designation of a state often diverges from practice: many states with "democratic" or "people's" in their official names are not democracies in practice, and some constitutional monarchies function as parliamentary democracies in all but name. Classification therefore distinguishes the de jure form from the de facto regime type.
