Ieoh Ming Pei (26 April 1917 – 16 May 2019), commonly known as I. M. Pei, was a Chinese-American architect. Born in Guangzhou and raised in Hong Kong and Shanghai, Pei drew early inspiration from the garden villas at Suzhou, the traditional retreat of his family. He moved to the United States in 1935 to study architecture, earning degrees from the University of Pennsylvania, MIT, and Harvard's Graduate School of Design, where he studied under Walter Gropius.

Pei was regarded as one of the masters of modern architecture. His work is characterized by abstract form, geometric design, and the use of materials such as stone, concrete, glass, and steel, often combining traditional architectural elements with progressive designs based on simple geometric patterns.

Major works

  • Louvre Pyramid (1989), Paris — the glass-and-metal pyramid entrance to the Louvre Museum, initially controversial but now an iconic landmark.
  • Bank of China Tower (1990), Hong Kong — one of the most recognizable skyscrapers in Asia, with its distinctive triangular framework.
  • John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum (1979), Boston.
  • East Building of the National Gallery of Art (1978), Washington, D.C.
  • Rock and Roll Hall of Fame (1995), Cleveland.
  • Museum of Islamic Art (2008), Doha, Qatar.
  • Miho Museum (1997), Japan.

Awards and legacy

Pei won the Pritzker Architecture Prize in 1983, often called the Nobel Prize of architecture. He also received the AIA Gold Medal (1979), the Presidential Medal of Freedom (1992), and the Royal Gold Medal (2010). His firm, Pei Cobb Freed & Partners, completed projects across the world. Pei died in New York City in May 2019 at the age of 102.
