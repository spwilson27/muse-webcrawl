Conventicle Act 1664

The Conventicle Act 1664 was an Act of the Parliament of England (16 Charles II c. 4) that forbade religious assemblies — conventicles — of more than five people outside the household who met for worship outside the forms of the Church of England. It formed part of the Clarendon Code, the series of statutes by which the restored monarchy of Charles II sought to enforce Anglican conformity and suppress Protestant Nonconformity and Catholic recusancy after the English Civil War and the Commonwealth.

Provisions

The Act made it an offence for any person aged sixteen or over to attend a religious meeting at which five or more people, beyond the members of the household, were present, unless the worship followed the Book of Common Prayer. Penalties escalated sharply: a fine or up to three months' imprisonment for a first offence, heavier fines or six months for a second, and for a third offence transportation for seven years or a fine of £100. Justices of the peace could act on the oath of a single witness, and informers were rewarded with a share of the fines.

Enforcement and effects

The Act fell hardest on Presbyterians, Independents, Baptists and Quakers who had been ejected from their livings by the Act of Uniformity 1662. Thousands of Nonconformists were imprisoned under it; the Quaker leader and the diarist Samuel Pepys both record the disruption of meetings. The statute was temporary, expiring in 1668, but a second, harsher Conventicle Act followed in 1670.

Repeal

The Act's practical force faded after the Toleration Act 1689 granted freedom of worship to Protestant dissenters, and its remaining provisions were formally repealed by the Statute Law Revision Act 1948.
