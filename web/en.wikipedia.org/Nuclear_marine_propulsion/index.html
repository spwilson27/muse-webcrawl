Nuclear marine propulsion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nuclear marine propulsion is propulsion of a ship or submarine with heat provided by a nuclear reactor. The plant heats water to produce steam for a turbine used to turn the vessel's propeller through a gearbox or through an electric generator and motor. Naval nuclear propulsion offers advantages over diesel or fossil-fuelled plants: greatly extended range and endurance, since the fuel is extraordinarily energy-dense and no air is needed for combustion, and the ability to sustain high speeds indefinitely.

Technology

Most naval reactors are pressurised water reactors (PWRs) using enriched uranium fuel. The reactor heats a primary coolant loop; heat is transferred to a secondary circuit that generates steam, driving propulsion turbines and turbo-generators for ship's electricity. Because the core contains enough fuel for years or decades, some modern cores are designed to last the life of the ship. Reactors are heavily shielded and operated under strict safety regimes developed under Admiral Hyman G. Rickover's direction of the US Naval Reactors programme.

History

The first nuclear-powered vessel was the US Navy submarine USS Nautilus, which went to sea in 1955 with the famous signal "Underway on nuclear power." The Soviet Union followed with the submarine K-3 Leninsky Komsomol and the icebreaker Lenin (1957), the first nuclear surface ship and the first civilian nuclear vessel. The United States commissioned the cruiser Long Beach and the carrier Enterprise in 1961, the latter with eight reactors.

Nuclear propulsion transformed submarine warfare: a nuclear boat can remain submerged for months, limited mainly by crew endurance and food supplies. Aircraft carriers gain unlimited range and the ability to generate large amounts of electrical power for catapults, sensors, and aircraft operations.

Applications

Today nuclear propulsion is used by the navies of the United States, Russia, France, the United Kingdom, China, and India — chiefly in ballistic-missile and attack submarines and some aircraft carriers — and by Russia in its fleet of nuclear icebreakers. Civilian nuclear cargo ships, such as the American NS Savannah (1962), proved technically feasible but commercially unsuccessful. Costs, waste disposal, decommissioning, and public acceptance remain significant challenges.
