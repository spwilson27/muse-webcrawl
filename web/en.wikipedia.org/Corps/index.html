Corps

A corps (/kɔːr/; plural corps /kɔːrz/; from French corps, from Latin corpus "body") is a large military formation. It is usually composed of two or more divisions, together with corps troops such as artillery, engineers, logistics and communications units, and numbers typically between 20,000 and 50,000 soldiers. A corps is commanded by a lieutenant general or equivalent rank and sits between a division and a field army in the chain of command.

History

The corps as a self-contained combined-arms formation was developed by Napoleon Bonaparte in the early 1800s. His corps d'armée — each a miniature army with its own infantry, cavalry, artillery and staff — could march separately and fight independently for a day or more, enabling the dispersing and concentrating manoeuvres characteristic of the Napoleonic Wars. The system was widely copied, and corps were the principal large formations of the American Civil War and both World Wars. Famous corps include the German Afrika Korps of the Second World War and the numbered army corps of modern NATO forces.

Other uses

The term is also used for specialised military branches organised as corps, such as the United States Marine Corps, medical corps, and signal corps, and for non-military uniformed or organised bodies: a diplomatic corps, a press corps, service organisations such as the Peace Corps and AmeriCorps, and ensembles such as a corps de ballet. In student culture in several European countries, Corps is the name of one of the oldest types of student fraternity.
