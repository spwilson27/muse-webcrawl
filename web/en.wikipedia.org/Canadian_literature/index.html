Canadian literature is the literature of Canada, written in English and French (the latter often treated separately as Quebec literature), as well as the oral and written traditions of Canada's Indigenous peoples. Recurring themes include nature, the frontier, survival in a harsh environment, and Canada's position in relation to Britain, France and the United States.

History

Early Canadian writing consisted of exploration accounts, settler narratives such as Susanna Moodie's Roughing It in the Bush (1852), and the confederation-era poetry of the 1880s. Lucy Maud Montgomery's Anne of Green Gables (1908) became a worldwide success. A distinct modern literature emerged after the Second World War with writers such as Hugh MacLennan, Robertson Davies and Mavis Gallant.

International recognition

From the 1960s, Canadian authors achieved global prominence: Margaret Atwood (The Handmaid's Tale), Alice Munro — who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2013 for her short stories — Michael Ondaatje (The English Patient), Yann Martel (Life of Pi) and Margaret Laurence are among the most widely read. Indigenous writers including Thomas King, Eden Robinson and Tanya Tagaq have reshaped the canon.
