Forest Preserve (New York)

The New York Forest Preserve is the name given to state-owned lands in the Adirondack and Catskill parks of New York State that are required by the state constitution to be kept "forever wild." The preserve was established by legislation in 1885 and given constitutional protection in 1894 by Article XIV of the New York State Constitution, which provides that the lands "shall be forever kept as wild forest lands" and that timber on them may not be sold, removed, or destroyed.

Forest Preserve lands in the Adirondacks are constitutionally "forever wild."

History

Large areas of the Adirondacks and Catskills came into state hands through tax forfeitures in the 1870s and 1880s, just as concern grew that commercial logging was destroying watersheds that fed the Erie Canal and New York City's water supply. The 1885 Forest Preserve Act created the preserve and a Forest Commission to administer it. Fears that the legislature might sell the land led to the 1894 constitutional convention adopting the "forever wild" clause, among the strongest land-protection provisions in the world. It was championed by figures including David McClure and the young lawyer Louis Marshall.

Extent and management

Today the preserve comprises roughly 2.6 million acres in the Adirondack Park and about 287,000 acres in the Catskill Park, interspersed with private land within the parks' "Blue Line" boundaries. It is managed by the Department of Environmental Conservation, and specific exceptions (for highways, ski centres, and cemeteries, among others) have required constitutional amendments approved by voters—occasionally granted, often after bitter campaigns.
