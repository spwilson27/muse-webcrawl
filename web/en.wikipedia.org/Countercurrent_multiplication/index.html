Countercurrent multiplication

Countercurrent multiplication
SystemRenal (kidney)
StructureLoop of Henle
FunctionBuilds medullary osmotic gradient for concentrating urine

Countercurrent multiplication is the physiological process by which the loop of Henle in the kidney creates a gradient of increasing osmolarity in the interstitial fluid of the renal medulla. This gradient allows the kidney to produce urine more concentrated than blood plasma, conserving water.

Mechanism

Tubular fluid flows in opposite directions in the descending and ascending limbs of the loop of Henle. The ascending limb actively pumps sodium and chloride ions out into the interstitium but is impermeable to water, while the descending limb is permeable to water but not to salt. Working together, these properties "multiply" a small transverse gradient into a large longitudinal one along the medulla.

Role in urine concentration

The medullary gradient is exploited by the collecting duct: in the presence of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), water leaves the duct into the salty medulla, concentrating the urine. The vasa recta blood vessels act as countercurrent exchangers that preserve the gradient.

Significance

The mechanism, proposed in the early 1950s by Werner Kuhn and colleagues, explains how terrestrial mammals — especially desert species with long loops of Henle — can conserve water so effectively.
