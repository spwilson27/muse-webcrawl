Experience machine

Proposed byRobert Nozick
WorkAnarchy, State, and Utopia (1974)
FieldEthics, philosophy of well-being
TargetHedonism

The experience machine is a thought experiment proposed by philosopher Robert Nozick in his 1974 book Anarchy, State, and Utopia. Nozick asks the reader to imagine a machine, built by super-duper neuropsychologists, that can give a person any experiences they desire: while floating in a tank with electrodes attached to the brain, one would believe one was writing a great novel, making friends, or reading an interesting book — all without knowing the experiences were simulated. Nozick asks: would you plug in for life?

The argument

Nozick's point is directed against hedonism, the view that pleasure (or desirable experience) is all that matters for well-being. Most people, he contends, would hesitate to plug in, and their reasons reveal values beyond experience: we want actually to do certain things, not merely have the experience of doing them; we want to be a certain sort of person, not an "indeterminate blob" in a tank; and we want contact with a deeper reality, not only a man-made simulation. He concludes that what matters to us in addition to experience is not exhausted by how our lives feel from the inside.

Responses and debate

Hedonists have replied that reluctance to plug in may reflect status quo bias: if you learned your current life was already in a machine, you might refuse to be unplugged. Experimental philosophers have tested the intuition with varied scenarios, finding that framing matters — people are more willing to enter a machine described from inside a disappointing life than from a satisfying one. Nozick himself later revisited the puzzle, and the thought experiment has become a standard touchstone in debates about well-being, virtual reality, and the value of authenticity, often compared with the brain-in-a-vat scenario and the plot of The Matrix.
