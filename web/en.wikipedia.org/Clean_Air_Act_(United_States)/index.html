Clean Air Act (United States)

The Clean Air Act (CAA) is the United States' primary federal air quality law, intended to reduce and control air pollution nationwide. Initially enacted in 1963 and substantially amended in 1970, 1977 and 1990, it is administered by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in coordination with state, local and tribal governments.

Major provisions

The Act directs the EPA to set National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for widespread pollutants from numerous sources, including particulate matter, ground-level ozone, carbon monoxide, sulphur oxides, nitrogen oxides and lead. States must develop State Implementation Plans to attain the standards. Separate programmes regulate emissions from motor vehicles, hazardous air pollutants from industrial sources, and new or modified stationary sources under New Source Performance Standards.

The 1990 amendments

Signed by President George H. W. Bush, the 1990 amendments created an acid rain programme using a cap-and-trade system for sulphur dioxide — an early and widely cited success of emissions trading — strengthened controls on toxic air pollutants, and addressed stratospheric ozone depletion in line with the Montreal Protocol.

Impact and litigation

The EPA credits the Act with large reductions in common pollutants and substantial public-health benefits. Its scope has been tested in the courts, notably in Massachusetts v. EPA (2007), which held that greenhouse gases are "air pollutants" under the Act, and West Virginia v. EPA (2022), which limited the agency's authority to restructure the power sector under one provision.
