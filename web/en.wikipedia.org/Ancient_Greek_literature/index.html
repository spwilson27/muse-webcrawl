Ancient Greek literature

Ancient Greek literature comprises works written in the Ancient Greek language from the earliest surviving texts, the Homeric epics of the 8th century BC, through the literature of the Roman and Byzantine worlds. It founded many of the genres of Western literature, including epic, lyric poetry, tragedy, comedy, history, philosophy, and the novel.

Epic and early poetry

The Iliad and Odyssey attributed to Homer stand at the head of the tradition, composed in dactylic hexameter in an artificial poetic dialect. Hesiod's Theogony and Works and Days joined myth with didactic reflection. Lyric poets such as Sappho, Alcaeus, Pindar, and Simonides explored personal emotion and public celebration in varied metres.

Classical drama and prose

Fifth-century Athens produced the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides and the comedies of Aristophanes, performed at civic festivals such as the Dionysia. Prose flowered in the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides, the philosophy of Plato and Aristotle, and the oratory of Demosthenes and Lysias.

Hellenistic and later literature

After Alexander, Alexandria's scholars—Callimachus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Theocritus—cultivated learned, polished verse, while Menander reshaped comedy. Under Rome, Plutarch wrote biography and essays, Lucian satire, and Longus and others the early novel. Greek literature continued unbroken into the Byzantine millennium.
