Multistability

Multistability is the property of a system that possesses more than one stable state (attractor) for a given set of conditions. Bistability, with exactly two stable states, is the simplest and most studied case, and forms the basis of one-bit memory in electronics and of many switches in cell biology.

Which stable state the system occupies depends on its history, so multistable systems exhibit hysteresis: the state cannot be inferred from the present conditions alone.

Examples

In perception, ambiguous figures such as the Necker cube and Rubin's vase flip between two stable interpretations — an example of perceptual multistability. In neuroscience, bistable dynamics appear in models of binocular rivalry and in the switching behaviour of neural networks. In physics, examples include optical bistability in lasers and the two stable magnetisation directions of a ferromagnetic element; in chemistry, bistable reaction networks underpin models of cellular differentiation.

Significance

Multistability is central to memory and decision-making in biology, where networks of genes or neurons settle into alternative stable configurations, and to climate science, where the Earth system may possess alternative stable states such as glacial and interglacial conditions. Near a tipping point, small perturbations or slow parameter changes can drive abrupt transitions between states.
