Weddell seal

The Weddell seal (Leptonychotes weddellii) is a relatively large and abundant true seal (family Phocidae) with a circumpolar distribution around the coast of Antarctica. It is the most southerly distributed mammal in the world, inhabiting fast ice up to 78°S. The species is named for the British sealing captain James Weddell, who encountered it in the Weddell Sea in the 1820s.

Description

Kingdom: Animalia
Order: Carnivora
Family: Phocidae
Genus: Leptonychotes
Species: L. weddellii
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)

Adults measure about 2.5–3.5 metres and weigh 400–600 kg. They are grey, mottled with lighter patches, with a characteristically small head and a mouth that curves upward at the corners, giving a cat-like, "smiling" appearance. Weddell seals are expert divers, reaching depths of over 600 metres and remaining submerged for more than an hour while hunting fish, krill, and squid beneath the ice. They maintain breathing holes in fast ice, sawing the edges with their teeth—a habit that wears the teeth down and limits lifespan in older animals.

Life history

Females give birth to usually a single pup on the surface of fast ice in the austral spring (September–November) and nurse it for about six to seven weeks. Males defend underwater territories near the breathing holes during the breeding season and produce elaborate descending trills and other vocalizations that carry through the ice.

Research

Because they are approachable and abundant near Antarctic research stations, especially in McMurdo Sound, Weddell seals have been studied continuously since the 1960s in one of the longest-running mark–recapture programs for any wild mammal. The population is estimated in the hundreds of thousands and is not considered threatened.
