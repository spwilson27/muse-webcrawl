Theatre of Dionysus

The Theatre of Dionysus is an ancient Greek theatre in Athens. It is built into the southern slope of the Athenian Acropolis and is part of the temenos of Dionysus Eleuthereus, the sanctuary dedicated to the god of wine and theatre. The theatre is considered the cradle of Greek drama: the premieres of the works of Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes, and Menander were mounted here during the festival of the City Dionysia.

The Theatre of Dionysus seen from the Acropolis above.

History

Performances in the sanctuary began in the 6th century BC, when the tyrant Peisistratus is said to have established the Great Dionysia; tradition credits Thespis with staging the first tragedy there around 534 BC. The early theatre was built of wood. After a collapse of the wooden seating in the 4th century BC, it was rebuilt in stone under the statesman Lycurgus, reaching a capacity of some 17,000 spectators. In the Roman period the theatre was remodelled, with a marble stage building and reliefs, and gladiatorial contests and water spectacles were held in the waterproofed orchestra.

Layout

The theatre consists of the semicircular orchestra where the chorus performed, the skene or stage building behind it, and the tiered cavea (auditorium) cut into the hillside, divided by horizontal walkways. The front row holds 67 marble thrones for priests and officials, the central one reserved for the priest of Dionysus Eleuthereus.

Legacy

Excavated in the 19th century, the ruins remain a major archaeological site and a key piece of evidence for the staging of classical drama and the origins of Western theatre.
