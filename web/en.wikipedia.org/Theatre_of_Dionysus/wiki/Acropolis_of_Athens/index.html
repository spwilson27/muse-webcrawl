The Acropolis of Athens is an ancient citadel located on a rocky outcrop above the city of Athens, Greece, containing the remains of several ancient buildings of great architectural and historical significance, the most famous being the Parthenon. The word acropolis comes from the Greek words ákron ("highest point") and pólis ("city").

History

The hill was inhabited from Neolithic times and fortified in the Mycenaean period. After the Persian destruction of 480 BC, Pericles initiated an ambitious building program in the mid-fifth century BC, executed by the architects Ictinus, Callicrates, and Mnesicles and the sculptor Phidias. The resulting monuments — the Parthenon (447–432 BC), the Propylaea, the Erechtheion, and the Temple of Athena Nike — form the supreme expression of classical Greek architecture.

On its southern slope stands the Theatre of Dionysus, where the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides were first performed during the City Dionysia festival, and the later Odeon of Herodes Atticus. Over the centuries the buildings served as temples, churches, a mosque, and a fortress; an explosion in 1687, when a Venetian shell ignited an Ottoman gunpowder store in the Parthenon, severely damaged the site. Systematic excavation and restoration have proceeded since Greek independence; the Acropolis was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.
