Greek tragedy is a form of theatre from Ancient Greece and Greek-inhabited Anatolia. It reached its most significant form in Athens in the 5th century BC, and its works have been traditionally attributed to Thespis, regarded as the first actor. Greek tragedy developed out of the dithyramb, a choral hymn sung in honor of Dionysus, and was performed at the City Dionysia festival.

Structure and performance

A typical tragedy alternated spoken episodes between actors — all male, wearing masks, at most three speaking actors on stage — with choral odes (stasima) sung and danced by a chorus of twelve or fifteen in the orchestra. The play opened with a prologue, followed by the chorus's entrance song (parodos), and closed with the exodos. Plots were drawn almost entirely from Greek mythology and heroic legend, allowing playwrights to explore questions of justice, fate, and the gods through familiar stories.

The tragedians

Of the hundreds of tragedies performed, only 32 complete plays survive: seven each by Aeschylus (including the Oresteia) and Sophocles (Oedipus Rex, Antigone), and eighteen by Euripides (Medea, The Bacchae). Aristotle's Poetics analyzes the genre, defining tragedy as an imitation of a serious action that effects a catharsis of pity and fear, and identifying elements such as hamartia, anagnorisis (recognition), and peripeteia (reversal).

Influence

Greek tragedy profoundly shaped Western drama and literary theory, influencing Seneca, Renaissance tragedy, Shakespeare, Racine, and modern playwrights, and providing enduring subjects for opera, film, and psychoanalytic thought.
