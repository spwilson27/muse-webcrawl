The Dionysia was a large festival in ancient Athens in honor of the god Dionysus, the central event of which was the performance of tragedies and, from 486 BC, comedies. It was the second-most important festival after the Panathenaia. The Dionysia actually consisted of two related festivals: the Rural Dionysia in winter and the City Dionysia (or Great Dionysia) in spring, held in the month of Elaphebolion (roughly late March).

The City Dionysia

The festival began with a grand procession (the pompē) that carried a wooden statue of Dionysus and sacrificial animals to the Theatre of Dionysus on the south slope of the Acropolis. There followed the singing of dithyrambs by tribal choruses, and then the dramatic competitions: three tragedians each presented three tragedies and a satyr play over successive days, while comic poets presented individual comedies. Wealthy citizens called chorēgoí funded the productions as a public duty, and prizes were awarded by judges drawn by lot.

Cultural significance

The City Dionysia was the occasion for the premieres of the surviving tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides and the comedies of Aristophanes. Held when the sailing season had opened, it was attended by visitors from across the Greek world; Athens used the occasion to display its wealth and power, parading tribute from its allies and honoring benefactors before the performances. The festival is thus central to the history of Western theatre.
