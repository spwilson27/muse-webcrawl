Decoupling (environmental)


Absolute decoupling: growth with falling emissions

In environmental economics, decoupling refers to the ability of an economy to grow without corresponding increases in environmental pressure such as resource use, pollution, or greenhouse gas emissions. Two forms are distinguished: relative decoupling, where environmental impact grows more slowly than the economy, and absolute decoupling, where impact falls in absolute terms while the economy grows.

Measurement

Decoupling is commonly assessed by comparing trends in GDP with indicators such as domestic material consumption or carbon dioxide emissions. Some wealthy countries have achieved absolute decoupling of territorial CO2 emissions, though critics note that consumption-based accounting, which attributes emissions embedded in imports to the consuming country, often shows smaller gains.

Debate

The concept underpins strategies such as green growth promoted by the OECD and the World Bank. Critics argue that sustained, global, absolute decoupling at the speed required to meet climate targets has not been demonstrated, and that rebound effects (the Jevons paradox) can offset efficiency gains. The related idea of eco-economic decoupling extends the analysis to resource productivity over whole economies.
