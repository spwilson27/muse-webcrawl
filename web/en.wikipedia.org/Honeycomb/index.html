Honeycomb

A honeycomb is a mass of hexagonal prismatic cells built from beeswax by honey bees in their nests to contain their larvae and stores of honey and pollen. Beekeepers may remove the entire honeycomb to harvest honey, or return it to the hive after extracting the honey by centrifuge, thereby reducing the work the bees must do to rebuild comb.

Geometry

The hexagonal cross-section of the cells is famously efficient: the honeycomb conjecture, an assertion dating to antiquity and proved by Thomas Hales in 1999, states that a hexagonal grid partitions a plane into regions of equal area with the least total perimeter, and hence the least wax for a given storage volume. Each cell is a hexagonal prism capped with a trihedral (three-rhombus) base, an arrangement Charles Darwin called "absolutely perfect in economising labour and wax". The cells are also tilted slightly upward to keep honey from running out.

Construction and use

Worker bees secrete wax flakes from glands on their abdomens, chew and mold them, and build comb cooperatively, using their bodies to gauge cell size. Brood cells rear young bees; slightly larger drone cells rear males; honey and pollen are stored in others. Beyond the hive, the strength-to-weight efficiency of hexagonal structures has inspired engineered "honeycomb" panels used in aerospace, architecture, and packaging.
