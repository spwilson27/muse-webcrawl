Visual acuity

Visual acuity (VA) commonly refers to the clarity of vision, but technically rates an animal's ability to recognize small details with precision. Visual acuity depends on optical and neural factors: the sharpness of the retinal focus within the eye, the health and functioning of the retina, and the sensitivity of the interpretative faculty of the brain.

Measurement

The most common clinical measure is the Snellen chart, developed in 1862 by Dutch ophthalmologist Herman Snellen. Acuity is expressed as a fraction such as 20/20 (6/6 in metric): the numerator is the test distance and the denominator the distance at which a person with standard vision could read the same line. 20/20 is regarded as normal acuity. LogMAR charts, scaling letters by equal logarithmic steps, are preferred in research for their greater precision.

Physiology

Acuity is limited by the optics of the eye (diffraction, aberrations, and refractive errors such as myopia and astigmatism), the spacing of photoreceptor cells in the fovea, and neural processing. With an optimal pupil and healthy eye, the limit is about 30 cycles per degree. Acuity is greatest at the centre of gaze and falls steeply toward the periphery.

Abnormalities

Reduced acuity may result from uncorrected refractive error, cataract, macular degeneration, amblyopia, or damage to the visual pathways. Corrective lenses, surgery, or treatment of the underlying cause may restore it.
