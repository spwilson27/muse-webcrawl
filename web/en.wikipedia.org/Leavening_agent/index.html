Leavening agent

A leavening agent (or raising agent) is a substance used in doughs and batters that causes a foaming action — producing gas bubbles — that lightens and softens the mixture. The gas, usually carbon dioxide, expands during baking and creates the porous texture of breads, cakes and other baked goods.

Biological leavening uses yeasts, which ferment sugars to carbon dioxide and ethanol, and in sourdough also lactic and acetic acid bacteria. Chemical leaveners include baking soda (sodium bicarbonate), which releases carbon dioxide when combined with an acid and moisture or heat, and baking powder, a complete mixture of bicarbonate with acid salts that may be single- or double-acting. Mechanical leavening incorporates air or steam by creaming, whisking egg whites, or lamination, as in puff pastry.

The choice of leavener affects flavour, texture, colour and the timing of mixing and baking.
