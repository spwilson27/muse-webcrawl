Bayer process

InventorKarl Josef Bayer
Year1888
FeedstockBauxite
ProductAlumina (aluminium oxide)

The Bayer process is the principal industrial means of refining bauxite to produce alumina (aluminium oxide). Developed by the Austrian chemist Karl Josef Bayer and patented in 1888, it remains the dominant route to alumina, which is then smelted into aluminium metal by the Hall–Héroult process.

Process

Bauxite, containing 30–60% aluminium oxide together with silica, iron oxides and titanium dioxide, is crushed and digested in a concentrated solution of sodium hydroxide at temperatures of roughly 140–265 °C. The alumina dissolves as sodium aluminate while impurities form an insoluble residue known as red mud, which is separated and washed. The clarified liquor is cooled and seeded with aluminium hydroxide crystals, precipitating pure aluminium hydroxide. After filtration and washing, the hydroxide is calcined at about 1,000 °C to drive off water and yield anhydrous alumina, a white powder.

Economics and environment

Typically about two tonnes of bauxite yield one tonne of alumina, and two tonnes of alumina yield one tonne of aluminium. The process generates large volumes of highly alkaline red mud — roughly one to 1.5 tonnes per tonne of alumina — whose storage in impoundments is a significant environmental concern, as demonstrated by the 2010 Ajka alumina plant accident in Hungary. Efforts continue to find uses for red mud in construction and metal recovery. See also Bauxite and Aluminium.
