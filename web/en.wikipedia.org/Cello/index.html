Cello

The cello, or violoncello, is a bowed (and occasionally plucked) string instrument of the violin family. Its four strings are normally tuned in perfect fifths (C2, G2, D3, A3) an octave below the viola. A person who plays the cello is called a cellist.

History

The cello emerged in northern Italy in the early 16th century from earlier bowed instruments. Early makers included Andrea Amati; the smaller, standardised form was established by Antonio Stradivari in the early 18th century. Initially used mainly as a basso continuo instrument, the cello gained prominence as a solo instrument through composers such as Boccherini and Haydn.

Repertoire and use

The cello is a core member of the symphony orchestra and string quartet. Famous solo repertoire includes Bach's six Cello Suites, concertos by Haydn, Dvořák, Elgar, and Shostakovich, and works written for cellists such as Pablo Casals and Mstislav Rostropovich. The instrument is also used in folk, jazz, and popular music.

Construction

The cello is made of spruce and maple, supported between the player's knees and by a retractable endpin. Its sound is produced by bowing, plucking (pizzicato), or striking (col legno) strings stretched over a bridge that transmits vibration to the hollow body.
