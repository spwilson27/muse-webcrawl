The Macintosh, often called the Mac, is a family of personal computers designed and marketed by Apple Inc. since 1984. The original Macintosh was the first commercially successful personal computer to feature a graphical user interface (GUI) built into the screen and operated by a mouse, technologies demonstrated earlier at Xerox PARC and first brought to market by Apple in the Lisa (1983) and Macintosh lines.

History

The Macintosh project began in 1979 under Jef Raskin, who envisioned an easy-to-use, low-cost computer for the average consumer; after Steve Jobs took over the project in 1981, it absorbed the GUI innovations of the Lisa team. The original Macintosh 128k was launched on 24 January 1984, promoted by the celebrated "1984" television advertisement directed by Ridley Scott. Though initially hampered by modest memory, little software and a high price of US$2,495, the Mac found a strong market in desktop publishing after the introduction of the LaserWriter printer and Aldus PageMaker in 1985.

Transitions

The Mac platform has undergone three major processor transitions: from Motorola 68000-series processors to PowerPC in 1994; from PowerPC to Intel x86 processors in 2006; and from Intel to Apple's own ARM-based Apple silicon chips, beginning with the M1 in 2020. Its operating system likewise evolved from the classic Mac OS (1984–2001) to the Unix-based Mac OS X, now macOS, which shares foundations with iOS.

Current lineup

Current Macs include the MacBook Air and MacBook Pro laptops, the iMac all-in-one, the Mac mini and Mac Studio desktops, and the Mac Pro workstation. The product line is sold through Apple's retail stores and the Mac App Store distributes much of its software. The name derives from the McIntosh apple cultivar, slightly respelled to avoid a conflict with audio manufacturer McIntosh Laboratory.
