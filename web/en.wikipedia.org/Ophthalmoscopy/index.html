Ophthalmoscopy

Ophthalmoscopy, also called funduscopy, is a test that allows a health professional to see inside the fundus of the eye and other structures using an ophthalmoscope. It is a key part of the eye examination and is important in determining the health of the retina, optic disc and vitreous humor, as well as revealing signs of systemic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes.

Types

Direct ophthalmoscopy uses a handheld instrument to shine light into the eye while the examiner looks through a peephole, producing an upright, magnified view (about 15×) of a small area of fundus; it can be performed through an undilated pupil. Indirect ophthalmoscopy uses a condensing lens held before the eye and a head-mounted light, giving a wider, binocular, inverted view that is well suited to the peripheral retina, usually after pupil dilation. Slit-lamp examination with auxiliary lenses offers another magnified, stereoscopic view.

History

The invention of the ophthalmoscope is credited to Hermann von Helmholtz, who demonstrated his Augenspiegel in 1851, building on earlier work by Charles Babbage. For the first time clinicians could inspect the living retina, transforming ophthalmology and making possible the recognition of conditions such as glaucoma-related disc cupping and hypertensive retinopathy.

Findings

Examination assesses the optic disc (colour, cupping, swelling), the retinal vessels (calibre, crossings, haemorrhages), the macula and the background retina for exudates, pigmentation or detachment. Fundus photography and optical coherence tomography now complement the technique for documentation and screening, including in diabetic retinopathy programmes.
