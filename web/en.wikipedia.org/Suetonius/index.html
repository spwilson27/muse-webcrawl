Suetonius

Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus (c. AD 69 – after AD 122), commonly referred to in English as Suetonius, was a Roman historian who wrote during the early Imperial era of the Roman Empire. His most important surviving work is De vita Caesarum, commonly known as The Twelve Caesars: a set of biographies of the first twelve Roman rulers, from Julius Caesar to Domitian. Other works by Suetonius concern the daily life of Rome, politics, oratory, and the lives of famous writers, including poets, historians, and grammarians.

Suetonius had access to the imperial archives through his posts in the imperial administration, serving as secretary (ab epistulis) to the emperor Hadrian until he was dismissed around AD 122, reportedly for a breach of court etiquette involving the empress Sabina.

Life

Suetonius was born to an equestrian family; his father, Suetonius Laetus, was a military tribune who fought at the Battle of Bedriacum in AD 69. Suetonius practised as an advocate and was a protégé of Pliny the Younger, whose letters commend him and record the purchase of a small estate for him. He held a succession of posts in the imperial secretariat: a studiis, in charge of archives; a bibliothecis, supervisor of libraries; and finally ab epistulis, Hadrian's correspondence secretary. His dismissal around AD 122 ended his access to the archives.

The Twelve Caesars

De vita Caesarum presents the lives of Julius Caesar and the eleven emperors from Augustus to Domitian in a largely topical rather than strictly chronological arrangement, with rubrics covering ancestry, public career, personal habits, appearance, omens and death. Suetonius mixes sober documentation with gossip and scandal, quoting documents and reporting conflicting traditions. The work is an indispensable source for the early Principate, and its anecdotal style shaped later biography from Einhard's Life of Charlemagne onwards.

Other works

His collection De viris illustribus ("On Illustrious Men") covered poets, orators, historians, philosophers, grammarians and rhetoricians; the lives of grammarians and rhetoricians survive, along with fragments of the poets (including the life of Virgil). A vast encyclopaedic work, the Prata, is lost.
