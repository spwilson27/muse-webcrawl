Schema (psychology)

In psychology and cognitive science, a schema (plural: schemata or schemas) describes a pattern of thought or behavior that organizes categories of information and the relationships among them. It can also be described as a mental structure of preconceived ideas, a framework representing some aspect of the world, or a system of organizing and perceiving new information. Schemata influence attention and the absorption of new knowledge: people are more likely to notice things that fit into their schema, while re-interpreting contradictions to the schema as exceptions or distorting them to fit. Schemata have a tendency to remain unchanged, even in the face of contradictory information.

Schemata are capable of changing but often only in response to a great deal of contradictory information, through a process Jean Piaget called assimilation and accommodation.

History

The psychological term was popularized by the British psychologist Frederic Bartlett, who described it in his 1932 book Remembering. Bartlett proposed that people operate on the basis of schemas — stored knowledge about the world — and that memory is largely a reconstructive process shaped by those schemas. The Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget used the concept extensively in his theory of cognitive development, describing how children build and revise schemas as they grow.

Types of schema

Researchers have described several kinds of schema, including person schemas (knowledge about types of people), social schemas (knowledge about social roles and events), event schemas or scripts (knowledge about sequences of actions, such as eating at a restaurant), and self-schemas (knowledge about the self). Schemas for oneself are called self-schemas; a person who holds a positive self-schema about some trait tends to notice and remember information consistent with it.

Schemas and memory

Schemas guide encoding and retrieval of memories. Information consistent with an active schema is often remembered better, but schema-inconsistent information can also be distinctive and memorable. Bartlett's famous "War of the Ghosts" experiment, in which British participants retold a Native American folk tale, showed how retellings were progressively reshaped to fit the participants' cultural expectations.

Schema therapy

In clinical psychology, schema therapy, developed by Jeffrey Young, targets early maladaptive schemas — self-defeating emotional and cognitive patterns that begin early in development and repeat throughout life. Schema therapy integrates elements of cognitive therapy, attachment theory, and Gestalt therapy.
