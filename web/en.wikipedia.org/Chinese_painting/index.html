Chinese painting

Chinese painting (中國畫, zhōngguó huà) is one of the oldest continuous artistic traditions in the world. Traditional painting involves essentially the same techniques as calligraphy — brush, ink and water-based colour on paper or silk — and is deeply connected with Chinese philosophy, poetry and seal carving.

Genres and formats

The major genres are landscape (shanshui, "mountain-water"), figure painting, and bird-and-flower painting. Works take the form of hanging scrolls, handscrolls unrolled section by section, album leaves and fans. Landscape became the most esteemed genre: monumental Northern Song vistas by masters such as Fan Kuan and Guo Xi gave way to the intimate, expressive styles of the Yuan dynasty and the literati (scholar-amateur) ideal, in which painting was a vehicle for personal cultivation rather than professional craft.

Principles and history

Xie He's Six Principles (6th century) — headed by "spirit resonance" — guided criticism for centuries. The Tang dynasty excelled in figure painting (Yan Liben, Wu Daozi); the Song in landscape and bird-and-flower studies; the Yuan masters (Zhao Mengfu, Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan, Wang Meng) defined the literati canon. Ming schools (Wu School, Zhe School) and Qing individualists such as Shitao carried the tradition forward. In the 20th century, artists including Xu Beihong and Qi Baishi combined Chinese media with Western techniques.

Techniques

Painters use graded ink washes, dry-brush texture strokes (cun), and mineral and vegetable pigments, often leaving silk or paper unpainted as mist or water. Inscriptions and red seal impressions form part of the composition.
