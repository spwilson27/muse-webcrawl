Amedeo Avogadro

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Lorenzo Romano Amedeo Carlo Avogadro (1776–1856)

Lorenzo Romano Amedeo Carlo Avogadro, Count of Quaregna and Cerreto (9 August 1776 – 9 July 1856) was an Italian scientist, most noted for his contribution to molecular theory now known as Avogadro's law, which states that equal volumes of gases under the same conditions of temperature and pressure will contain equal numbers of molecules.

In tribute to him, the ratio of the number of elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions or other particles) in a substance to its amount of substance (the Avogadro constant), 6.02214076×1023 mol−1, is now called the Avogadro number, one of the seven SI defining constants.

Biography

Avogadro was born in Turin to a noble family of Piedmont, Italy, in 1776. He graduated in ecclesiastical law at the early age of 16, and took his doctorate in law at 20. He devoted himself to a successful legal career until 1800, when he began private studies of mathematics and physics.

In 1809 he began teaching mathematics and physics at a lycée in Vercelli. In 1820 he became professor of physics at the University of Turin, though political upheaval interrupted his tenure; he was restored in 1834 and remained until his retirement in 1850.

Avogadro's hypothesis

In 1811 Avogadro published an article, "Essay on a Manner of Determining the Relative Masses of the Elementary Molecules of Bodies", in which he proposed that elementary gases consist of molecules made of two or more atoms, and that equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain the same number of molecules. The hypothesis was largely ignored for decades until Stanislao Cannizzaro championed it at the Karlsruhe Congress of 1860, after which it became foundational for determining atomic and molecular weights.

Legacy

The mineral avogadrite and a lunar crater are named after him, and the Avogadro constant was designated one of the defining constants of the SI in 2019.
