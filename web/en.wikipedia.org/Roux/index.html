Roux


Roux darkens as it cooks, from white through blond to brown

Roux (pronounced "roo") is a cooked mixture of flour and fat — commonly butter — used as a thickening agent for sauces, soups and stews. It is one of the fundamental components of French cuisine and of Louisiana Creole and Cajun cooking.

Preparation

Fat is heated in a pan, flour is whisked in — typically in roughly equal parts by weight — and the mixture is cooked while stirring constantly. Cooking first removes the raw taste of the flour and gelatinises its starch when liquid is later added, giving sauces a smooth, non-gummy body. The starch also coats the fat particles, preventing lumps when the roux is combined with liquid, provided the two are at contrasting temperatures (cold roux into hot liquid, or vice versa).

Types

Roux is classified by colour, which deepens the longer it cooks:

  • White roux — cooked only until the raw smell disappears; used for béchamel and other white sauces.
  • Blond roux — cooked to a pale golden colour with a slightly nutty aroma; used for velouté.
  • Brown roux — cooked to a deep brown; used for espagnole and, in Louisiana cooking, for gumbo, where oil or lard replaces butter and the roux may be cooked to a chocolate or near-black colour.

The darker the roux, the richer and more toasted its flavour but the less thickening power it retains, because prolonged heat breaks down the starch; a dark gumbo roux can require three or four times the quantity of a white roux to achieve the same body.

History

Roux became standard in French kitchens in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, replacing earlier thickeners such as bread and egg yolks, and was codified by chefs including François Pierre de La Varenne and, later, Auguste Escoffier as the base of the mother sauces. Variants exist worldwide: Japanese curry uses a spiced roux, and Hungarian rántás is often flavoured with paprika.
