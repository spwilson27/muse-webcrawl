The Carolingian Empire was a large Frankish-dominated empire in Western and Central Europe during the early Middle Ages, ruled by the Carolingian dynasty. It was founded on Christmas Day 800, when Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne "Emperor of the Romans" in Rome, and it covered much of modern France, Germany, the Low Countries, Austria, Switzerland, and northern Italy.

Rise

The Carolingians rose as mayors of the palace of the Merovingian kings; Pepin the Short deposed the last Merovingian in 751 and founded the dynasty. His son Charlemagne (r. 768–814) conquered Lombard Italy, Saxony, Bavaria, and the Spanish March, and forcibly Christianised subject peoples. His government, centred on Aachen, issued capitularies, standardised coinage and writing (Carolingian minuscule), and sponsored the Carolingian Renaissance of learning led by scholars such as Alcuin of York.

Division

Charlemagne's sole surviving son, Louis the Pious (r. 814–840), struggled to hold the empire together amid his sons' rebellions. After civil war, the Treaty of Verdun (843) divided the empire into three kingdoms: West Francia for Charles the Bald, East Francia for Louis the German, and Middle Francia for Lothair I. Later partitions (Prüm 855, Meerssen 870, Ribemont 880) fragmented it further, and the last Carolingian emperor, Charles the Fat, was deposed in 887.

Legacy

The Carolingian Empire laid foundations for the later kingdoms of France and the Holy Roman Empire, spread a standardised Latin Christian culture, and preserved classical texts through its monastic scriptoria — most surviving copies of Roman literature descend from Carolingian manuscripts. Charlemagne is often called the "Father of Europe."
