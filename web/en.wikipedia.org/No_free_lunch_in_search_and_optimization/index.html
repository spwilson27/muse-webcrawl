No free lunch in search and optimization

In computational complexity and optimization, the no free lunch theorem is a result that states that for certain types of mathematical problems, the computational cost of finding a solution, averaged over all problems in the class, is the same for any solution method. In other words, no algorithm offers a shortcut: a solution method that works well on one class of problems necessarily performs worse on another. The phrase alludes to the saying "there ain't no such thing as a free lunch".

Overview

The theorems were published by David Wolpert and William Macready in 1995 and 1997. In informal terms, the results state that when a problem is drawn uniformly at random from all possible problems of a certain type, any two optimisation algorithms perform identically on average. A hill-climbing algorithm that exploits smooth landscapes does correspondingly badly on rugged ones, while a random search is never better or worse in expectation.

Implications

Practically, the theorem means that good performance on real-world problems comes from incorporating problem-specific knowledge — assumptions about the structure of likely problems — rather than from a universally superior optimiser. It undercuts claims of general-purpose "black box" optimisation and is frequently invoked in debates about machine learning, where it implies that no single learning algorithm is best for every possible data distribution.

Criticism and qualifications

Critics point out that uniform averaging over all problems is dominated by problems that are essentially random and unstructured, whereas practical problems lie in a tiny, highly structured subset. Sharper versions of the theorem therefore examine under what conditions free lunches can or cannot exist.
