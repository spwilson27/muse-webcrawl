Granite is a coarse-grained (phaneritic) intrusive igneous rock composed mostly of quartz, alkali feldspar, and plagioclase. It forms from magma with a high content of silica and alkali metal oxides that slowly cools and solidifies underground. Granite is typical of continental crust and is the most common plutonic rock of the Earth's crust, forming large batholiths and stocks beneath mountain ranges. It is strong, hard and durable, and has been used widely throughout history as a dimension stone for buildings, monuments, paving and sculpture—from the obelisks of ancient Egypt to modern countertops.

Mineralogy

On the QAPF classification, granite contains between 20 and 60 per cent quartz and a plagioclase content of 10 to 65 per cent of total feldspar. Accessory minerals commonly include biotite and muscovite mica, hornblende, and traces of magnetite, zircon and apatite. The interlocking crystal texture, visible to the naked eye, results from slow cooling at depth, which allows large crystals to grow.

Formation

Granitic magmas are generated mainly by partial melting of the lower continental crust, often during orogeny (mountain building), or by extensive fractional crystallization of basaltic magma. The long-running debate over "granitization" versus magmatic origins was largely settled in favour of melting by the experimental petrology of Tuttle and Bowen in the 1950s. Because granitic magma is viscous and buoyant, it rises slowly and commonly stalls in the crust, solidifying as plutons.

Radiation

Like many natural stones, granite contains trace amounts of uranium, thorium and potassium-40 and can emit small quantities of radon gas; measured exposures from granite surfaces are typically comparable to ordinary background levels.
