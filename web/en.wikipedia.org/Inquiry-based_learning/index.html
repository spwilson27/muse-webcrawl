Inquiry-based learning

Inquiry-based learning
FieldEducation
ApproachStudent-centred, question-driven
Related methodsProblem-based learning, discovery learning, project-based learning

Inquiry-based learning (also enquiry-based learning in British English) is a form of active learning that starts by posing questions, problems, or scenarios rather than simply presenting established facts. Students identify and research issues, formulate hypotheses, gather and analyse evidence, and present explanations, with the teacher acting primarily as a facilitator.

Origins

The approach draws on constructivist learning theory and the work of educators such as John Dewey, who argued that learning arises from experience and reflection. It gained prominence in science education in the 1960s, notably through Joseph Schwab and the scientist-inquiry movement, and was later promoted by curriculum reforms in many countries.

Levels of inquiry

Educators commonly describe a spectrum from structured inquiry, in which the teacher supplies the question and procedure, through guided inquiry, to open inquiry, in which students formulate their own questions and methods. The appropriate level depends on learners' experience and the subject matter.

Evidence and criticism

Research reviews find that guided inquiry can improve achievement and engagement compared with purely expository teaching, but minimally guided discovery can be ineffective for novices, who may benefit from explicit instruction before open investigation. Effective implementation therefore usually blends direct teaching with inquiry activities.
