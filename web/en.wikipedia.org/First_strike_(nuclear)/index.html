First strike (nuclear)

In nuclear strategy, a first strike is a surprise nuclear attack launched against an adversary with the intention of destroying most or all of that adversary's nuclear forces before they can be used, thereby reducing the victim's ability to retaliate. The feasibility of a successful counterforce first strike has been a central preoccupation of nuclear strategists since the late 1940s, and it underpins debates over missile defence, submarine-launched weapons, and crisis stability.

Concept

A first strike is directed primarily at counterforce targets: the enemy's intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) silos, bomber bases, ballistic missile submarine ports, and command-and-control facilities. Its strategic aim is "damage limitation" — to so weaken the opponent's arsenal that any retaliatory second strike would be tolerable, or so terrorise the opponent's leadership that it would not retaliate at all. Analysts distinguish a true bolt-from-the-blue first strike from a pre-emptive strike launched in the belief that an enemy attack is imminent.

History

In the late 1940s and 1950s, when the United States held a large lead in deliverable nuclear weapons, some planners discussed preventive war against the Soviet Union, though such proposals were rejected at the political level. The introduction of survivable forces — hardened silos, mobile missiles, and above all nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs) at sea — made a disarming first strike progressively less plausible. The prospect that MIRVed (multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicle) missiles could destroy several enemy warheads with one missile created fears of a "window of vulnerability" in the late 1970s.

Stability implications

First-strike capability is widely regarded as destabilising. Forces that are powerful but vulnerable create "use-or-lose" incentives: in a crisis, each side may fear the other will strike first, producing pressure to pre-empt. Arms control agreements such as the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty and various strategic arms limitation talks were shaped partly by efforts to reduce first-strike incentives and preserve assured retaliation.
