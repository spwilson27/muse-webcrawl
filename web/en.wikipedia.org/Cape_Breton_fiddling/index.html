Cape Breton fiddling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cape Breton fiddling is a regional style of violin music developed on Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia, Canada, by the Scottish Gaelic-speaking settlers who arrived there from the Highlands and Islands of Scotland in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. It is considered one of the most faithful survivals of the 18th-century Scottish fiddle tradition, particularly the style associated with composers such as Niel Gow and the Gow family.

Characteristics

The Cape Breton style is characterized by heavy use of ornamentation derived from bagpipe music—cuts, crans, and drones—as well as distinctive bowing patterns, "up-driven" bow strokes, and the accompaniment of a piano providing syncopated chords and bass runs. Tunes typically include strathspeys played with the dotted "Scottish snap," marches, reels, jigs, and slow airs. Dancing, especially step dancing and square sets, is integral to the tradition, and fiddlers traditionally adjust their rhythm to the dancers' feet.

History and notable musicians

Because Cape Breton's Gaelic communities were relatively isolated, their music preserved older Scottish forms even as styles in Scotland itself changed. The tradition gained wider fame through performers such as Winston "Scotty" Fitzgerald, Angus Chisholm, Buddy MacMaster, and later Natalie MacMaster and Ashley MacIsaac, who brought Cape Breton fiddling to international audiences. The annual Celtic Colours festival and community events such as the Broad Cove Scottish Concert sustain the tradition, which is also taught through recordings and fiddle camps.
