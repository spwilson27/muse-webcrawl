Class-switch recombination

Class-switch recombination (CSR), also called isotype switching, is a biological process by which a B cell changes the class (isotype) of antibody it produces—from IgM to IgG, IgA, or IgE—while retaining the same antigen specificity. It allows the immune system to tailor the effector functions of an antibody to different tissues and threats.

Mechanism

CSR occurs in activated B cells in germinal centres, driven by cytokines from T follicular helper cells and signals through CD40. The enzyme activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) deaminates cytidines in repetitive "switch" (S) regions upstream of the heavy-chain constant-region genes, generating DNA double-strand breaks. Repair by non-homologous end joining deletes the intervening DNA as an episomal circle and juxtaposes the variable region with a new constant region. Switch transcription through the target S region, promoted by cytokine-specific germline transcripts, directs where switching occurs.

Significance

Different isotypes mediate distinct functions: IgG opsonizes and crosses the placenta, IgA guards mucosal surfaces, and IgE defends against helminths but also drives allergy. Defects in CSR cause hyper-IgM syndromes, in which patients make only IgM and suffer recurrent infections; mutations in AID or in CD40/CD40L are the classic causes.
