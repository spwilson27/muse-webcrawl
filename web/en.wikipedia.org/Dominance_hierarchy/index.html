Dominance hierarchy

In biology and anthropology, a dominance hierarchy, also called a pecking order, is a type of social hierarchy that arises when members of animal social groups interact, creating a ranking system. A dominant individual is one with social status at the head of the hierarchy, subordinate to none; subordinates are subject to the social control of dominants.

Origin of the term

The term "pecking order" derives from studies of domestic chickens in the 1920s by the Norwegian zoologist Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe, who observed that hens establish a linear hierarchy determining access to food, enforced by pecking.

Functions

Dominance hierarchies can reduce the intensity and frequency of conflict within a group by settling disputes over resources, mates, and territory through ritualized displays rather than fighting. In many primate species, rank affects stress, health, and reproductive success, and hierarchies may be maintained by coalitions and grooming as much as by force.
