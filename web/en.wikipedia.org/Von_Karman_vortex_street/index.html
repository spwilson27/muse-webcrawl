Von Karman vortex street

In fluid dynamics, a von Karman vortex street is a repeating pattern of swirling vortices caused by the unsteady separation of flow of a fluid around blunt (bluff) bodies. It is named after the engineer and fluid dynamicist Theodore von Kármán, who described the stability conditions of the pattern in 1911–1912. The vortices are shed alternately from opposite sides of the body, arranging themselves into two staggered rows in the wake.

Description

When fluid flows past a bluff body such as a circular cylinder, the boundary layers on either side separate and roll up into vortices that are shed alternately. Von Kármán showed that of the possible arrangements of the two vortex rows, only the staggered arrangement — in which the vortices of one row sit midway between those of the other — is stable, and only for a specific ratio of row spacing to vortex spacing (approximately 0.281).

The shedding frequency is characterised by the Strouhal number, which for a cylinder over a wide range of Reynolds numbers is about 0.2. The alternating side forces produced by shedding can excite vibration of the structure itself, a phenomenon known as vortex-induced vibration; it contributed to the collapse of the original Tacoma Narrows Bridge's aerodynamic instability concerns and explains the humming of power lines and the behaviour of flag flutter. In nature, vortex streets appear on scales from insects' wings to cloud patterns stretching hundreds of kilometres behind oceanic islands.
