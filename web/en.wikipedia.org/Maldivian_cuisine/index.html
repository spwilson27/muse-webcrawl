Maldivian cuisine

Maldivian cuisine, also called Dhivehi cuisine, is the traditional food of the Maldives and of Minicoy, India. It is built on three principal staples long available on the low coral atolls: coconut, fish (especially tuna) and starches such as rice and taro. Because the islands produce little, the cuisine has absorbed influences from neighbouring Sri Lanka and southern India, and more recently from Arab, East Asian and Western cooking brought by trade and tourism.

Staples and ingredients

The coconut palm is used in every form: grated, as coconut milk, and as coconut oil. Fish — skipjack tuna, yellowfin tuna, frigate tuna and wahoo — is eaten fresh, boiled, smoked, sun-dried or processed into rihaakuru, a thick fish paste, and into Maldives fish (hikimas), a cured product similar to Japanese bonito flakes that also forms a traditional export. Starches include rice, now mostly imported, and traditional taro, sweet potato, cassava and breadfruit. Curry leaves, chillies, lime and onions season most dishes.

Main dishes

  • Mas huni — a breakfast dish of shredded smoked tuna mixed with grated coconut, onion, chilli and lime, eaten with flatbread (roshi) and tea.
  • Garudhiya — a clear fish broth of tuna cooked in water with salt, served over rice with lime, chilli and onion.
  • Rihaakuru — the intensely flavoured paste left after cooking garudhiya down, eaten with rice or roshi.
  • Fihunu mas — grilled or barbecued fish basted with chilli paste.
  • Bis keemiya — pastries filled with tuna or egg and cabbage.
  • Hedhikaa — the range of fried "short eats" snacks, including gulha (fish-and-coconut balls) and keemia, served with tea in the afternoon.

Beverages and sweets

Black tea (sai) accompanies meals and snacks, while raa, toddy tapped from palm flowers, was traditionally drunk fresh. Desserts use coconut, sugar and flour, such as bondibaiy, a sweet rice pudding, and are prepared for festivals and Ramadan, when a fast-breaking meal features an expanded range of dishes.

Modern cuisine

Resort islands serve international menus, while cafés (hotaa) in Malé and on inhabited islands serve both traditional short eats and adapted Indian, Chinese and Western dishes. Imported rice, flour and processed foods have become dietary staples, prompting public-health concern about rising diabetes.
