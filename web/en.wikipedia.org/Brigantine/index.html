Brigantine

A brigantine is a two-masted sailing vessel with the foremast square-rigged and the mainmast rigged with a fore-and-aft mainsail, topsail and topgallant sails. It was a popular rig from the 17th to the 19th centuries, used for merchantmen, privateers, naval dispatch vessels and small warships.

Description

The brigantine is defined by its rig: the forward (fore) mast carries square sails like a brig, while the aft (main) mast carries a large gaff-rigged fore-and-aft mainsail, above which square topsails may be set on some variants. This combination gave good performance both running before the wind and sailing to windward, and required a smaller crew than a fully square-rigged vessel of similar size.

History

The name derives from the Italian brigantino, originally a small vessel favoured by brigands and pirates in the Mediterranean — the word is related to "brigand". Early brigantines of the 13th to 15th centuries were lateen-rigged and sometimes rowed. In northern European waters the type evolved through the "brig" of the 17th century into the classic brigantine, sometimes called a "hermaphrodite brig" because it mixed the rigs of a brig and a schooner.

Brigantines were widely used in Atlantic and coastal trade, in the Royal Navy as sloops and advice boats, and by privateers during the American Revolutionary War and the War of 1812. Famous examples include the American privateer brigantines of the early republic. By the late 19th century the rig was largely superseded by schooners and steam vessels, though brigantines survive today as sail-training and charter vessels — the American brigantine Irving Johnson being a well-known 20th-century example.
