Biodiversity loss

Biodiversity loss is the decline in the variety of life on Earth, including the extinction of species, the reduction of populations and genetic diversity, and the degradation of ecosystems. Scientists estimate that current extinction rates are tens to hundreds of times higher than the natural background rate, leading many to describe the present era as a sixth mass extinction — the Holocene extinction.

Causes

The 2019 global assessment by the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES) identified five direct drivers of biodiversity loss, in order of impact: changes in land and sea use (habitat destruction and fragmentation), direct exploitation of organisms (hunting, fishing, logging), climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species. Underlying these are indirect drivers such as human population growth, consumption patterns, and economic systems that externalize environmental costs.

Scale

The IPBES report concluded that around one million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction, many within decades. Monitored populations of wild vertebrates declined by an average of roughly 69 percent between 1970 and 2018 according to the WWF Living Planet Index. Insect declines, amphibian losses to chytrid fungus, and coral bleaching events are among the most dramatic documented trends.

Consequences

Biodiversity underpins ecosystem services on which humanity depends: pollination of crops, water purification, soil formation, fisheries, and regulation of disease and climate. Its loss reduces ecosystem resilience and threatens food security, livelihoods, and the discovery of new medicines.

Responses

International efforts include the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022), whose targets include protecting 30 percent of land and sea by 2030 ("30 by 30"), restoring degraded ecosystems, and reducing harmful subsidies.
