Eihei-ji

Location: Eiheiji, Fukui Prefecture, Japan

Denomination: Sōtō Zen Buddhism

Founded: 1244

Founder: Dōgen Zenji

Status: Head temple (honzan) of the Sōtō school

Eihei-ji (永平寺) is one of two main temples of the Sōtō school of Zen Buddhism, the largest single religious denomination in Japan, the other being Sōji-ji in Yokohama. Its founder was the thirteenth-century Zen master Dōgen, who brought Sōtō Zen from China to Japan. Eihei-ji is situated in the mountains near the city of Fukui in Fukui Prefecture and remains an active training monastery where some 150 monks practise according to the regimen Dōgen prescribed.

History

Dōgen founded the temple in 1244, initially naming it Daibutsuji; in 1246 he renamed it Eihei-ji, "Temple of Eternal Peace", taking the name from the era when Buddhism is traditionally said to have reached China. After Dōgen's death in 1253, his successors — notably Keizan Jōkin — spread the school across Japan. The temple was destroyed by fire during the conflicts of the sixteenth century, when warlord forces burned it, and was rebuilt thereafter; most of the present buildings date from the Edo period and later.

The monastery

The complex comprises some seventy buildings spread over a forested hillside, connected by covered walkways. The seven principal structures of a Zen monastery (shichidō garan) include the Sanmon gate, the Butsuden (Buddha hall), the Hōjō (abbot's quarters), the Sōdō (monks' hall, where monks sleep, eat, and sit zazen on a single tatami-sized space), the kitchen (Kuin), bathhouse (Yokushitsu), and lavatory (Tōshō) — each treated in Dōgen's writings as a place of practice in its own right. Daily life follows his strict rules: rising before dawn, periods of seated meditation (zazen), work practice (samu), communal meals eaten in formal ōryōki style, and study of Dōgen's masterwork, the Shōbōgenzō.

Visiting

Eihei-ji receives large numbers of visitors and pilgrims, who may tour designated areas; laypeople may also apply to stay overnight and join the monks' schedule in an introductory practice programme (sanzen). The temple is surrounded by ancient cedar trees, some several centuries old, and by moss gardens characteristic of the Hokuriku region's wet climate.
