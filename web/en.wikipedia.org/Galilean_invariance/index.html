Galilean invariance or Galilean relativity states that the laws of motion are the same in all inertial frames of reference. Galileo Galilei first described this principle in 1632 in his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems using the example of a ship travelling at constant velocity, without rocking, on a calm sea: an observer below deck cannot tell, from any mechanical experiment — dripping water, jumping, throwing objects, or butterflies in flight — whether the ship is moving or standing still.

Formulation

In Newtonian mechanics, coordinates in two inertial frames moving at relative velocity v are related by the Galilean transformations:

x′ = x − vt,   y′ = y,   z′ = z,   t′ = t

Time is universal, distances between simultaneous events are the same in all frames, and velocities simply add. Because acceleration is unchanged by the transformation, Newton's second law has the same form in every inertial frame, so no mechanical experiment can detect absolute rest.

Application and limits

Galilean invariance is the symmetry underlying Newtonian dynamics; the transformations form the Galilean group of space translations, rotations, time translations and boosts. Its assumption of instantaneous action and universal time, however, conflicts with Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism, under which the speed of light is constant in all frames. Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905) replaced Galilean transformations with Lorentz transformations, for which Galilean invariance is recovered as an approximation when speeds are much smaller than the speed of light.
