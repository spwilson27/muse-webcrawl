Women in Christianity

Women in Christianity have played diverse roles throughout the history of the Christian faith, as disciples, martyrs, mystics, theologians, missionaries, and, in many modern denominations, ordained clergy. The place of women in church leadership remains one of the most debated questions within contemporary Christianity.

New Testament

The Gospels depict women as prominent followers of Jesus, including Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of Jesus, and the sisters Mary and Martha of Bethany. Women were the first witnesses to the resurrection in all four Gospel accounts. In the letters of Paul, women such as Phoebe, Priscilla, and Junia are named as co-workers, though passages such as 1 Timothy 2 have been interpreted by some as restricting women's teaching authority.

Early and medieval church

Women served as deaconesses in the early church and were prominent among the martyrs, including Perpetua and Felicity. In the medieval period, female monastics such as Hildegard of Bingen, Julian of Norwich, and Catherine of Siena exercised considerable spiritual and intellectual authority.

Modern debates

Many Protestant denominations, including most Methodist, Lutheran, and Anglican churches, ordain women as priests and bishops. The Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church restrict priestly ordination to men, while affirming what they describe as the complementary dignity of women.
