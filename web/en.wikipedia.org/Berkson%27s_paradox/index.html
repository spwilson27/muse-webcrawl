Berkson's paradox

Berkson's paradox, also called Berkson's bias or the Berkson fallacy, is a counterintuitive result in probability and statistics: two variables that are independent (or positively associated) in a population can appear negatively correlated within a selected sample, when selection into the sample depends on both variables. It is a form of selection bias, and in causal-graph terms it arises from conditioning on a collider.

Original example

Joseph Berkson described the paradox in 1946 in the context of hospital-based case–control studies. If two diseases each increase the chance of being admitted to hospital, then among hospital patients the presence of one disease makes the other less likely to be present — because a patient without the first disease is more likely to have been admitted for the second — producing a spurious negative association between the two diseases in the hospital sample.

Explanation

Conditioning on a common effect of two variables induces dependence between them. The same mechanism explains everyday puzzles such as why, among famous actors, talent and attractiveness may seem negatively related: selection into fame requires a high value of the sum of qualities, so those low on one are disproportionately high on the other.
