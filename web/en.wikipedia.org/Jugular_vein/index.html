Jugular vein

The jugular veins are the veins that carry deoxygenated blood from the head and neck back to the heart via the superior vena cava. On each side of the neck there are two main jugular veins: the internal jugular vein, which drains the brain, face, and much of the neck, and the external jugular vein, which drains the exterior of the cranium and the deep parts of the face and runs superficially down the neck. Smaller anterior jugular veins also contribute to drainage.

Structure

The internal jugular vein begins at the jugular foramen in the skull, where it is continuous with the sigmoid sinus, and descends within the carotid sheath alongside the internal and common carotid arteries and the vagus nerve. It joins the subclavian vein to form the brachiocephalic vein. The external jugular vein is formed behind the angle of the jaw, crosses the sternocleidomastoid muscle superficially — making it visible in the neck when pressure rises — and empties into the subclavian vein. Both veins contain valves near their terminations.

Clinical significance

Assessment of the jugular venous pulse and pressure (JVP) is a standard part of the cardiovascular examination: an elevated JVP suggests right-sided heart failure, fluid overload, or obstruction of the superior vena cava, and the waveform's components reflect events in the right atrium. Because of its size and direct route to the heart, the right internal jugular vein is a common site for central venous catheterisation, used for administering drugs and fluids and for measuring central venous pressure. The veins may be damaged in neck trauma; pressure on them is also relevant in first aid and in certain medical procedures. The name derives from the Latin jugulum, "throat" or "collarbone".
