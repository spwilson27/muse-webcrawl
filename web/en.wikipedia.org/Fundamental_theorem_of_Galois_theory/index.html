Fundamental theorem of Galois theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the fundamental theorem of Galois theory is a result that describes the structure of certain field extensions in terms of groups. It establishes a one-to-one correspondence (the Galois correspondence) between the intermediate fields of a finite Galois extension E/F and the subgroups of its Galois group Gal(E/F).

Statement

Let E/F be a finite Galois extension with Galois group G. Then there is an inclusion-reversing bijection between the intermediate fields K (with F ⊆ K ⊆ E) and the subgroups H of G: to each K one assigns the subgroup Gal(E/K) of automorphisms fixing K, and to each H one assigns the fixed field EH of elements fixed by every automorphism in H. The correspondence reverses inclusions: larger subgroups correspond to smaller fields.

Moreover, the degree of the extension satisfies [E : K] = |H| and [K : F] = [G : H]; the intermediate field K is itself Galois over F if and only if H is a normal subgroup of G, in which case Gal(K/F) ≅ G/H. Galois theory thereby reduces questions about field extensions to questions about finite groups.

Example

The splitting field of x4 − 2 over the rationals has Galois group the dihedral group D4 of order 8. Its subgroups correspond to intermediate fields such as Q(√2), Q(i), and Q(4√2); the lattice of subgroups of D4, turned upside down, is exactly the lattice of intermediate fields.

Applications

The theorem underlies Galois's criterion that a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is a solvable group, which explains why there is no general quintic formula. It also gives elegant proofs that angle trisection and doubling the cube are impossible with straightedge and compass, and it leads to the classification of finite extensions and to results on inverse Galois theory.

History

Évariste Galois introduced the group-theoretic viewpoint around 1830, in manuscripts published posthumously in 1846. The modern statement in terms of field extensions and fixed fields was developed by Richard Dedekind, Leopold Kronecker, and Emil Artin in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
