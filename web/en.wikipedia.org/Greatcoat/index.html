Greatcoat

A greatcoat is a large, heavy overcoat worn by men and, in military contexts, by soldiers, typically made of thick wool designed for warmth and protection against the weather. Greatcoats were standard issue in armies from the 17th century through the mid-20th century and remain in ceremonial and cold-weather use.

Description

The garment is commonly double-breasted with large buttons, a wide collar and lapels that can be turned up against wind, deep pockets, and a half-belt or martingale at the back. Military greatcoats often doubled as bedding for soldiers in the field. Colors were typically the service color of the army—grey for German and Russian forces, khaki or dark blue in other services.

History

Greatcoats evolved from the heavy watch coats and "surtouts" of the 17th and 18th centuries. They became emblematic of the Napoleonic Wars and both World Wars, when troops wore them in trenches and winter campaigns. The garment also appears in civilian fashion and literature—Nikolai Gogol's 1842 short story "The Overcoat" (Shinel) centers on a Russian official's greatcoat. After World War II, lighter waterproof garments largely replaced the greatcoat for field use, but it survives in dress uniforms and as a civilian fashion staple.
