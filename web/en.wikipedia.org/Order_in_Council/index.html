Order in Council

Order in Council
TypeSubordinate legislation / executive instrument
Made byThe monarch (or governor general) on the advice of the Privy Council
JurisdictionsUnited Kingdom, Canada, and other Commonwealth realms
BasisRoyal prerogative, or authority granted by an Act of Parliament

An Order in Council is a type of legislation in many Commonwealth realms, formally made by the sovereign (or the governor general, in realms outside the United Kingdom) "in Council" — that is, with the advice of the Privy Council — but in practice drafted and decided by the government of the day. Orders in Council are used for a wide range of executive purposes, from making appointments and approving university statutes to enacting detailed secondary legislation under powers delegated by Parliament.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, Orders in Council are approved at meetings of the Privy Council, where a minimum of two or three counsellors and the Lord President attend the monarch; the procedure is formal and orders are not debated. They are made either under the royal prerogative or under statute. Statutory Orders in Council are a form of secondary legislation, some subject to parliamentary annulment or affirmative approval. Well-known uses include governing British Overseas Territories, giving effect to certain international obligations, approving the constitutions and by-laws of chartered bodies such as universities and professional institutions, and issuing emergency regulations — historically, the controversial Orders in Council of 1807 during the Napoleonic Wars, which restricted neutral trade and helped precipitate the War of 1812.

Canada

In Canada, an Order in Council is made by the Governor General on the recommendation of the King's Privy Council for Canada (effectively the Cabinet) and is the standard instrument for executive decisions: appointments of judges, senators, ambassadors and heads of agencies; approval of regulations; land transactions; and proclamations bringing legislation into force. Federal Orders in Council are numbered (e.g., P.C. 2024-0001) and are publicly searchable. Provincial equivalents are made by the lieutenant governor in council.

Judicial review and controversy

Although Orders in Council are acts of the Crown on ministerial advice, they are subject to judicial review: in the GCHQ case (Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service, 1985), the House of Lords confirmed that prerogative powers exercised through Orders in Council could be reviewed by the courts. Orders made under the prerogative were also central to the Chagos Archipelago litigation (R (Bancoult) v Secretary of State, 2008), concerning the removal of the Chagossians from their homeland.
