Treaty port

Treaty ports were port cities in China, Japan, and Korea opened to foreign trade and residence by treaties between those states and Western powers in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Within the treaty ports, foreigners typically enjoyed extraterritorial privileges, and some cities developed distinctive foreign concession districts.

China

The first five Chinese treaty ports — Canton (Guangzhou), Amoy (Xiamen), Foochow (Fuzhou), Ningpo (Ningbo), and Shanghai — were opened by the Treaty of Nanking in 1842 after the First Opium War. Later treaties, including the Treaties of Tientsin (1858) and the Convention of Peking (1860), opened many more, and by the early 20th century there were dozens of treaty ports along the coast and the Yangtze River. Shanghai grew into one of Asia's great commercial cities, with British, French, and international concessions administered separately from Chinese authority.

Japan and Korea

Japan opened ports such as Shimoda, Hakodate, Nagasaki, and Yokohama under the treaties of the 1850s, beginning with the Convention of Kanagawa in 1854. The Japanese treaty ports were reintegrated and extraterritoriality ended by 1899 as Japan renegotiated the treaties from a position of growing strength. Korea opened ports including Busan and Incheon after the Japan–Korea Treaty of 1876.

Decline

In China the treaty port system eroded in the early 20th century; extraterritorial rights were surrendered by most powers in 1943, and the system ended with the establishment of the People's Republic of China in 1949.
