Lancelet

The lancelets, also known as amphioxi (singular amphioxus), consist of some 30 species of "fish-like" benthic filter feeding chordates in the subphylum Cephalochordata, class Leptocardii, and family Branchiostomatidae.

Lancelets are the modern representatives of the subphylum Cephalochordata. While occasionally eaten by humans and other animals, they are important to zoology because their anatomy provides insights into the evolutionary origin of vertebrates.

Anatomy

Adults are typically 5–8 cm long, with a translucent, laterally compressed body. They possess the five defining chordate features throughout life: a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits, an endostyle and a post-anal tail. They lack a true brain, paired sense organs, heart and mineralised skeleton; blood circulates through pulsating vessels without a central pump. Muscles are arranged in V-shaped segments (myomeres), and feeding is by filtering plankton with mucus secreted by the endostyle.

Habitat and behaviour

Lancelets live in shallow sandy marine waters worldwide, usually buried in coarse sand with only the anterior end exposed, though they can swim briefly. The best-known genus is Branchiostoma, especially Branchiostoma lanceolatum and the Florida species B. floridae, widely used as laboratory model organisms in evolutionary developmental biology.

Evolutionary significance

Because their body plan preserves many features believed ancestral for chordates, and their genome retains a pre-duplication arrangement of Hox genes, lancelets anchor comparative studies of vertebrate evolution.
