Glutamic acid (symbol Glu or E) is an α-amino acid used in the biosynthesis of proteins. Its side chain contains a carboxylic acid group that is deprotonated and negatively charged at physiological pH, making it an acidic, hydrophilic amino acid. In humans it is non-essential, being readily synthesised from α-ketoglutarate.

Flavour

Glutamate, the ionised form of glutamic acid, is responsible for umami, the savoury "fifth taste". In 1908 the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda isolated glutamic acid from kelp broth and identified it as the source of umami, leading to the commercial production of its sodium salt, monosodium glutamate (MSG), a widely used flavour enhancer. Glutamate occurs naturally in tomatoes, cheese, mushrooms and fermented products.

Neurotransmitter role

Glutamate is the most abundant excitatory neurotransmitter in the vertebrate central nervous system, acting at ionotropic receptors (NMDA, AMPA and kainate) and metabotropic receptors. It is central to synaptic plasticity, learning and memory; excessive release, by contrast, causes excitotoxic neuronal damage. Glutamate is also the precursor of the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA.

Industry

Glutamic acid and MSG are produced industrially by bacterial fermentation, chiefly with Corynebacterium glutamicum, in quantities of millions of tonnes per year.
