Multi-messenger astronomy

Multi-messenger astronomy is the coordinated observation and interpretation of messages from outer space carried by disparate physical processes: electromagnetic radiation, gravitational waves, neutrinos, and cosmic rays. Each of these carriers conveys different information about the astrophysical sources that produce them, so combining them yields a more complete picture of extreme events such as merging neutron stars, supernovae, and active galactic nuclei than any single channel could provide.

The four cosmic messengers: photons, gravitational waves, neutrinos, and cosmic rays.

Messengers

Electromagnetic radiation — light across the spectrum from radio waves to gamma rays — has been the workhorse of astronomy for centuries and remains the most precisely localized messenger. Gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime first directly detected by LIGO in 2015, reveal the motion of compact massive objects. High-energy neutrinos, detected by observatories such as IceCube at the South Pole, point back to hadronic processes in cosmic accelerators because they travel almost unimpeded. Cosmic rays, charged particles deflected by magnetic fields, are the hardest to trace to their sources but carry direct samples of accelerated matter.

Landmark events

The field's defining moment came on 17 August 2017 with GW170817, the merger of two neutron stars detected in gravitational waves by LIGO and Virgo, accompanied by a short gamma-ray burst seen by the Fermi and INTEGRAL satellites and a kilonova observed by telescopes worldwide. The event confirmed that neutron star mergers produce heavy elements such as gold and platinum and provided an independent measurement of the expansion rate of the universe.

Earlier, the nearby supernova SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud was observed both in light and in a burst of neutrinos recorded hours before the optical brightening — an early, unplanned example of multi-messenger astronomy. In 2017–2018, a high-energy neutrino detected by IceCube was associated with a flaring blazar, TXS 0506+056, giving the first strong evidence that blazars accelerate cosmic rays.

Observatories and coordination

Multi-messenger astronomy depends on rapid alert networks that distribute candidate detections within seconds so that telescopes can slew to the right patch of sky. Networks such as NASA's Gamma-ray Coordinates Network and the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA alert system underpin this follow-up, while brokers sift the enormous data streams of surveys such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory.
