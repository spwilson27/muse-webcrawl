The Sinai and Palestine campaign was part of the Middle Eastern theatre of the First World War, fought between the British Empire (with French and Arab allies) and the Ottoman Empire supported by Germany. It began with an Ottoman attempt to raid the Suez Canal in January 1915 and ended with the Armistice of Mudros in October 1918, by which time Allied forces had captured Palestine, Jerusalem, Damascus, and Aleppo.

Background

The Suez Canal was a vital artery of the British Empire, linking Britain with India and the Far East. Ottoman forces, encouraged by their German allies, attempted to seize or block the canal in February 1915 but were repulsed. British forces then advanced across the Sinai Peninsula, building a railway and water pipeline, and defeated the Ottomans at Romani in August 1916.

Advance into Palestine

After capturing Rafa and winning at Magdhaba, the Egyptian Expeditionary Force under General Edmund Allenby attacked Gaza. Two attempts in early 1917 failed, but Allenby's forces broke through at Beersheba on 31 October 1917 — a battle famous for the charge of the Australian Light Horse — and captured Jerusalem in December 1917. Meanwhile, the Arab Revolt, supported by T. E. Lawrence, harassed Ottoman forces in the Hejaz and Transjordan.

Final offensive

In September 1918 Allenby launched the Battle of Megiddo, one of the most complete victories of the war. Cavalry exploited the breakthrough, encircling and destroying the Ottoman Seventh and Eighth Armies. Damascus fell on 1 October and Aleppo later that month. The Ottoman Empire signed the Armistice of Mudros on 30 October 1918.

Consequences

The campaign led to the British Mandate for Palestine and the French Mandate for Syria and Lebanon under the League of Nations, reshaping the political map of the Middle East. It remains notable for the last great cavalry operations in warfare and for the role of the Arab Revolt.
