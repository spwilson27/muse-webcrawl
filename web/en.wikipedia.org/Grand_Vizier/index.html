Grand Vizier

An Ottoman grand vizier, the sultan's chief minister

Grand Vizier (Persian: وزيرِ اعظم vazîr-e a'zam; Ottoman Turkish: صدر اعظم sadr-ı a'zam) was the title of the effective head of government of many sovereign states in the Islamic world, above all the Ottoman Empire. The grand vizier was the monarch's chief minister and plenipotentiary, second in power only to the ruler himself.

Origins

The office derives from the vizier (wazir) of the Abbasid Caliphate, who administered the empire on behalf of the caliph. Similar offices existed in Persia, Mughal India (where the equivalent was often styled Vakil or Peshwa), and other Islamic states. In Ottoman usage, the grand vizier was distinguished from lesser viziers of the Imperial Council (Divan) by possession of the imperial seal.

Ottoman grand viziers

From the mid-15th century until the abolition of the office in 1922, more than 200 men held the post. The grand vizier presided over the Divan at the Sublime Porte, commanded armies in the sultan's stead, and supervised the entire administration. During the Köprülü era (1656–1703), a dynasty of grand viziers restored Ottoman fortunes. Notable holders include Çandarlı Ibrahim Pasha, Sokollu Mehmed Pasha, who served three sultans, and reformers such as Midhat Pasha. The office was dangerous: many grand viziers were dismissed and some, like Kara Mustafa Pasha after the failed siege of Vienna in 1683, were executed.

Modern usage

The term "grand vizier" is sometimes applied retrospectively or analogically to chief ministers in other Islamic polities, and it survives in translation for the prime minister (wazir-e-azam) of Pakistan.
