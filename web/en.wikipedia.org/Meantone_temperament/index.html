Meantone temperament

Meantone temperaments are a family of musical tuning systems that temper the perfect fifth slightly flat so that major thirds sound pure (or nearly so). They were the dominant keyboard tunings of Western music from the early 16th century until the spread of well temperaments in the 18th century.

Principle

In just intonation, four perfect fifths (3:2) overshoot two octaves plus a just major third (5:4) by the syntonic comma (81:80). Meantone tuning distributes this comma across the fifths: each fifth is narrowed by a fraction of the comma, so that major thirds built from four fifths come out pure. In the most common form, quarter-comma meantone, each fifth is flattened by one quarter of the syntonic comma, yielding perfectly just major thirds and a fifth of about 696.6 cents.

Consequences

Meantone gives sweet, stable thirds in keys near C major but leaves one unusable "wolf" fifth (usually G♯–E♭) and restricts modulation: distant keys sound harsh. This shaped Renaissance and early Baroque harmony, favouring a limited palette of keys. Third-comma and sixth-comma variants trade third purity for more usable keys.

History

Pietro Aron described quarter-comma meantone in 1523; it became standard for organs, harpsichords, and fretted instruments across Europe. Its decline followed the rise of tonal music demanding freer modulation, leading to the well temperaments and eventually equal temperament.
