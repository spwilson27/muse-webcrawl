Euclidean vector

In mathematics, physics and engineering, a Euclidean vector (sometimes a geometric or spatial vector) is a geometric object that has magnitude (length) and direction. It can be represented by an arrow from an initial point to a terminal point, and it can be added to other vectors and multiplied by scalars according to vector algebra.

Basic concepts

A vector is defined by its magnitude and direction, independent of its position: vectors with the same magnitude and direction are considered equal even if their starting points differ. In coordinates, a vector in n-dimensional Euclidean space is given by an n-tuple of components relative to a basis.

Operations

Vector addition is performed componentwise or geometrically by the parallelogram law. Key products include the dot product, giving a scalar related to the angle between vectors and to projections, and, in three dimensions, the cross product, giving a perpendicular vector used for torque and angular momentum. The length follows from the Pythagorean theorem.

Applications

Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force and electric and magnetic fields are vector quantities, contrasted with scalars such as mass and temperature. The concept generalises in linear algebra to elements of any vector space. See also Vector space, Dot product and Cross product.
