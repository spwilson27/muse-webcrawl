The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) was one of the most destructive conflicts in European history, fought primarily in Central Europe. It began as a struggle between Catholic and Protestant states within the Holy Roman Empire and gradually drew in most of the great powers of Europe, becoming as much a contest over political dominance — particularly between France and the Habsburgs — as a war of religion.

Course of the war

The war opened with the Defenestration of Prague in 1618, when Protestant Bohemian nobles revolted against the Habsburg emperor. Historians conventionally divide the conflict into Bohemian, Danish, Swedish and French phases, named for the principal external interveners. Commanders such as Wallenstein, Tilly and Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden (killed at Lützen in 1632) led armies that lived off the land, devastating large parts of Germany through battle, plunder, famine and disease; some regions lost a third or more of their population.

Peace of Westphalia

The treaties of Osnabrück and Münster in 1648 ended the war, confirmed the independence of the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederation, strengthened the autonomy of the imperial estates, and recognised Calvinism alongside Catholicism and Lutheranism. The settlement is regarded as a founding moment of the modern system of sovereign states.
