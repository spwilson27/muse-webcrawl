The Corpus Juris Civilis ("Body of Civil Law") is the modern name for the collection of fundamental works in jurisprudence issued from 529 to 534 CE by order of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I. Produced under the direction of the jurist Tribonian, it systematised nearly a thousand years of Roman law and became the foundation of the civil law tradition that underlies the legal systems of much of Europe and, through them, much of the world.

Contents

Codex Justinianus

The Code (529, revised 534) gathered and harmonised the imperial constitutions (statutes) from the time of Hadrian onward, resolving contradictions and repealing obsolete provisions.

Digest

The Digest, or Pandects (533), excerpted and systematised the writings of the great classical jurists — Ulpian, Papinian, Paulus, Gaius and others — compressing some three million words of legal literature into about 800,000. It carried the force of law and is the principal surviving source of classical Roman jurisprudence.

Institutes and Novellae

The Institutes (533), modelled on Gaius's textbook, were an official textbook for law students that itself had legal force, organising law under persons, things and actions. The Novellae ("New Constitutions") collected Justinian's later legislation, much of it in Greek.

Influence

Largely lost to legal practice in the West after the empire's collapse, the Corpus was rediscovered in Italy in the late eleventh century — a famous manuscript of the Digest resurfaced at Pisa — and studied at the University of Bologna by Irnerius and the glossators. The "received" Roman law shaped canon law and the continental ius commune, and through codifications such as the Napoleonic Code influenced legal systems across Europe, Latin America, Africa and Asia. In the East it remained the basis of Byzantine law, later restated in the Basilika.
