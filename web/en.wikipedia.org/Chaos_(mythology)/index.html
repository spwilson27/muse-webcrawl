Chaos (Ancient Greek: χάος, kháos) is the mythological void state preceding the creation of the universe (the cosmos) in Greek creation myths. In Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BC), Chaos is the very first thing to exist: "In truth at first Chaos came to be," followed by Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, and Eros. The word derives from the verb meaning "to gape open"; Chaos is thus a yawning chasm or empty space rather than "disorder" in the modern sense — the popular meaning of chaos as confusion developed later.

In classical sources

From Chaos came Erebus (the darkness of the underworld) and Nyx (Night); their union produced Aether (brightness) and Hemera (Day). In Roman retellings, notably Ovid's Metamorphoses, Chaos became a rude, unformed mass of conflicting elements ("rudis indigestaque moles") that a god later separated into the ordered world — an interpretation that shaped later Western usage. Orphic cosmogonies gave Chaos a different genealogy, placing it among the offspring of Chronos (Time).

Comparative mythology and philosophy

Primordial waters or voids precede creation in many traditions — the Egyptian Nun, the Babylonian Tiamat and Apsu, and the Norse Ginnungagap — and scholars compare these "chaoskampf" and emergence themes across cultures. Greek philosophers later treated chaos in more abstract senses; the atomists and Stoics used related concepts for unformed matter or cosmic cycles.

Legacy

The modern everyday sense of "chaos" (utter disorder) comes via the Ovidian notion of formless confusion. In science the word was borrowed for the study of deterministic disorder in chaos theory, and "chaos" names concepts from mathematics to fantasy literature, often trading on its primordial, pre-cosmic associations.
