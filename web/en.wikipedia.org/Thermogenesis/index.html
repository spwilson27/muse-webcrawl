Thermogenesis

Thermogenesis is the process of heat production in organisms. It occurs in all warm-blooded animals, and also in a few species of thermogenic plants such as the Eastern skunk cabbage, the voodoo lily, and the giant water lilies of the genus Victoria. In animals it is a central part of thermoregulation and a major component of the basal metabolic rate.

Types

Shivering thermogenesis generates heat through rapid involuntary contractions of skeletal muscle. Non-shivering thermogenesis occurs mainly in brown adipose tissue, where the mitochondrial protein uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1, or thermogenin) short-circuits the proton gradient so that energy from oxidation is released as heat rather than stored as ATP. Exercise-associated thermogenesis and diet-induced thermogenesis (the thermic effect of food) also contribute to total heat production.

Control and significance

Thermogenesis is controlled by the hypothalamus and the sympathetic nervous system, and is influenced by hormones including thyroid hormones and catecholamines. In small mammals, hibernators, and human infants, brown-fat thermogenesis is essential for maintaining body temperature in the cold. Because it expends energy, thermogenic capacity has been studied in relation to obesity and metabolic disease.

In plants

Thermogenic flowers raise their temperature above ambient, often to volatilise odours that attract pollinators; the sacred lotus can regulate its floral temperature within a narrow range.
