Gyrocompass

TypeNon-magnetic compass / navigation instrument
PrincipleGyroscopic precession aligning with Earth's rotation axis
InventorsHermann Anschütz-Kaempfe; Elmer Ambrose Sperry (early 1900s)
AdvantageFinds true north; unaffected by ship's magnetism

A gyrocompass is a type of non-magnetic compass based on a fast-spinning disc and the rotation of the Earth (or another rotating body) to find geographical direction automatically. Unlike a magnetic compass, it points to true north rather than magnetic north, and it is unaffected by ferromagnetic materials such as a ship's steel hull, making it essential aboard ships and submarines.

Principle of operation

A gyroscope free to rotate maintains its axis orientation in space. By constraining the gyroscope with gravity-dependent control mechanisms, the interaction between the spinning wheel and the Earth's rotation produces precession that drives the axis to align with the meridian — pointing true north. The instrument requires electrical power and takes some time (often a few hours) to "settle" after start-up.

History

Following early experiments by Léon Foucault with gyroscopes in the 1850s, the first practical marine gyrocompasses were developed independently by Hermann Anschütz-Kaempfe in Germany (patented 1904, at sea in 1908) and Elmer Ambrose Sperry in the United States (1911). The two firms engaged in famous patent litigation. Gyrocompasses became standard on naval and merchant vessels during the First World War.

Errors and modern successors

Gyrocompass readings are subject to errors from the vessel's speed and latitude and from rapid manoeuvres; these are corrected mechanically or electronically. Modern navigation also uses ring-laser and fibre-optic gyroscopes and satellite compasses, but the classic spinning gyrocompass remains in widespread service.
