Refsum disease

Refsum disease is an autosomal recessive neurological disease that results from the over-accumulation of phytanic acid in cells and tissues. It is named after the Norwegian neurologist Sigvald Bernhard Refsum (1907–1991), who first described it in 1946. It is one of several disorders known as leukodystrophies and belongs to the group of peroxisomal disorders.

Specialty: Neurology, medical genetics
Inheritance: Autosomal recessive
Gene: PHYH (most cases); PEX7
Onset: Childhood to early adulthood

Cause

In most cases Refsum disease is caused by mutations in the PHYH gene, which encodes phytanoyl-CoA hydroxylase, the peroxisomal enzyme that performs the first step of alpha-oxidation of phytanic acid. A minority of cases result from mutations in PEX7, which encodes a receptor needed to import the enzyme into peroxisomes. Because humans cannot synthesise pathways to break down phytanic acid when this step is blocked, the fatty acid — obtained entirely from the diet — accumulates in blood, fat, and nervous tissue.

Symptoms

Classic features include retinitis pigmentosa (often the earliest sign, causing night blindness and progressive visual loss), peripheral polyneuropathy, cerebellar ataxia, ichthyosis, anosmia, deafness, and elevated protein in the cerebrospinal fluid without an increase in cells. Cardiac arrhythmias can be life-threatening during acute exacerbations, which may be triggered by rapid weight loss or illness that mobilises stored phytanic acid.

Treatment

Treatment centres on dietary restriction of phytanic acid, avoiding foods such as dairy products, beef, lamb, and certain fish. Such a diet lowers plasma phytanic acid levels and can stabilise or partially reverse neuropathy and skin changes, though established visual loss is generally irreversible. Plasmapheresis or lipid apheresis may be used in acute presentations. Patients are advised to maintain adequate caloric intake to avoid catabolism of fat stores.
