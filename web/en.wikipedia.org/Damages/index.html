Damages

At common law, damages are a form of compensation obtainable by a claimant who has suffered a loss or detriment as a result of the defendant's wrongful acts or omissions. Damages are usually a monetary payment awarded in a civil action; unlike a criminal fine, damages are paid to the injured party rather than to the state. The primary purpose of damages in tort and contract is to place the claimant, so far as money can, in the position they would have been in had the wrong not occurred.

Categories

Compensatory damages

Compensatory damages are divided into special damages, which compensate measurable monetary losses (such as medical bills and lost wages), and general damages, which compensate non-monetary harms such as pain and suffering, loss of amenity, and emotional distress. In contract cases, they include expectation (loss of bargain), reliance, and consequential damages that were foreseeable under the rule in Hadley v Baxendale.

Punitive damages

Punitive (or exemplary) damages are awarded not to compensate but to punish particularly egregious conduct and deter others. They are common in the United States but restricted or unavailable in many other common-law jurisdictions, and are subject to constitutional limits on gross excessiveness.

Nominal and liquidated damages

Nominal damages are a small token sum awarded where a right has been violated but no substantial loss is proven. Liquidated damages are a sum fixed by the parties in the contract itself as a genuine pre-estimate of loss; clauses that amount to a penalty may be unenforceable. Statutory damages are amounts prescribed by legislation, notably in copyright law.

Assessment

Damages must generally be proven with reasonable certainty, are subject to the duty to mitigate, and may be reduced for contributory negligence. Interest may be added from the date of loss to judgment. Awards for future losses raise questions of discount rates and periodical payments.
