Mechanical engineering

Mechanical engineering is the study and application of physics, mathematics, and materials science to design, analyse, manufacture, and maintain mechanical systems. It is one of the oldest and broadest of the engineering disciplines, concerned with everything from microscopic sensors to spacecraft, power plants, vehicles, and manufacturing machinery.

Core areas

The discipline rests on mechanics (statics and dynamics), kinematics, thermodynamics and heat transfer, fluid mechanics, materials science and strength of materials, and control theory. Mechanical engineers design engines and turbines, heating and cooling systems, industrial equipment, robotics, medical devices, and transportation systems. Modern practice relies heavily on computer-aided design (CAD), finite element analysis (FEA), computational fluid dynamics (CFD), and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM).

History

Mechanical devices date to antiquity — the machines of Archimedes, Hero of Alexandria's aeolipile, and medieval clockwork — but mechanical engineering emerged as a distinct profession during the Industrial Revolution, driven by the steam engine of Watt and others and the machine tools of the 18th and 19th centuries. Professional institutions include the Institution of Mechanical Engineers (founded 1847 in Britain) and the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME, founded 1880), which publish influential codes and standards such as the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code.

Education and practice

Mechanical engineers typically hold a bachelor's degree covering calculus, physics, thermodynamics, fluid and solid mechanics, materials, and design, followed in many jurisdictions by professional licensure. Subfields include automotive, aerospace (shared with aerospace engineering), biomechanics, mechatronics, HVAC, energy systems, and manufacturing engineering. The field continues to evolve with advances in additive manufacturing, composites, nanotechnology, and autonomous systems.
