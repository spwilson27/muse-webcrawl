Louis-Honoré Fréchette

Louis-Honoré Fréchette (16 November 1839 – 31 May 1908) was a Canadian poet, playwright, short story writer and politician, often called French Canada's national poet. Writing in French from Quebec, he became the first Canadian to win a prize from the Académie française, for his poetry collection Les fleurs boréales, les oiseaux de neige (1879).

Life

Born at Lévis, Fréchette studied law at Université Laval and was called to the bar of Lower Canada, but his liberal (rouge) politics and journalism led to conflict with conservative clergy; he spent several years in exile in Chicago, where he worked on railways and wrote satirical papers. Returning to Canada, he sat as a Liberal in the House of Commons from 1874 to 1878 and later served as clerk of the Legislative Council of Quebec.

Works

Fréchette's verse epic La Légende d'un peuple (1887) retells the history of French Canada in verse, from Jacques Cartier to the nineteenth century. His other works include the poetry collection Les fleurs boréales (1879), plays such as Le retour de l'exilé and Papineau, and prose tales of old Quebec. His poetry was strongly influenced by Victor Hugo, and he championed a distinctly French-Canadian national literature.

Legacy

A highly public figure, Fréchette feuded famously with rival writers and critics and was celebrated in his lifetime as the laureate of French Canada. His influence diminished in the twentieth century, but he remains a central figure in the history of Quebec literature, and schools, streets, and a literary prize bear his name.
