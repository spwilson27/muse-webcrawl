Sedevacantism

Sedevacantism (from Latin sede vacante, "the see being vacant") is a theological position held by a minority of traditionalist Catholics who believe that the Holy See has been vacant since the death of a mid-twentieth-century pope — most commonly Pope Pius XII in 1958, though some hold later dates — and that his successors have been heretics who therefore cannot validly hold the papal office. Sedevacantists reject the popes since the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), whose reforms they regard as a departure from Catholic doctrine.

Beliefs and arguments

Sedevacantists argue chiefly from canon law and papal teaching — for example Pope Paul IV's 1559 bull Cum ex apostolatus officio — that a public heretic cannot be a member of the Church or hold ecclesiastical office, and they hold that post-conciliar popes have taught heresy on religious liberty, ecumenism, and the liturgy. A related but distinct position, sedeprivationism, holds that recent popes are popes elect but not fully in office. Most sedevacantists celebrate the Tridentine Mass and maintain their own clergy, bishops, and seminaries, sometimes consecrating bishops without papal mandate.

History

The position emerged in the 1970s among traditionalists who concluded that resistance within the Church was insufficient; early figures included priests and lay theologians in Mexico, the United States, and France. Groups and congregations associated with sedevacantism include the Society of Saint Pius V and the Istituto Mater Boni Consilii, while the larger traditionalist Society of Saint Pius X rejects the position. Adherents are a small minority, numbering in the tens of thousands worldwide.

Response of the Church

The Catholic Church regards post-conciliar popes as legitimate and treats unauthorized episcopal consecrations performed by sedevacantist clergy as grave canonical offences; persons who consecrate or receive such consecrations incur excommunication under canon law.
