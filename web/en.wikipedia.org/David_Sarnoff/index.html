David Sarnoff

David Sarnoff (27 February 1891 – 12 December 1971) was a Russian-born American businessman and pioneer of American radio and television. As the long-time head of the Radio Corporation of America (RCA), he built one of the largest communications empires of the twentieth century and founded the broadcasting network NBC.

Early life

Sarnoff was born in Uzlyany, in the Russian Empire (now Belarus), and emigrated with his family to New York City in 1900. He worked as an office boy and then as a wireless telegraph operator for the Marconi Wireless Telegraph Company of America. Popular legend, promoted by Sarnoff himself, held that he was the operator who received the distress signals from the sinking Titanic in 1912 and relayed news of the disaster, though historians regard the story as greatly embellished.

RCA and broadcasting

After the First World War, American Marconi's assets were folded into the newly formed RCA, and Sarnoff rose rapidly through the company. He championed the idea of radio as a household medium for music and entertainment rather than merely a point-to-point service, and in 1926 RCA launched NBC, the first major American radio network. Sarnoff became president of RCA in 1930 and dominated the company for four decades.

Television

Sarnoff committed RCA to the development of electronic television, backing the work of Vladimir Zworykin and fighting a bitter patent and publicity battle with inventor Philo Farnsworth. RCA demonstrated television at the 1939 New York World's Fair, and after the Second World War Sarnoff pushed the adoption of the compatible colour television system that became the American standard. He died in New York in 1971.
