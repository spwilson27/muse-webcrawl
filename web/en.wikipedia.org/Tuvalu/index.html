Tuvalu

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tuvalu is an island country in the Polynesian subregion of Oceania in the Pacific Ocean, about midway between Hawaii and Australia. It lies east-northeast of the Santa Cruz Islands, northeast of Vanuatu, southeast of Nauru and south of Kiribati. Its nine small atolls and reef islands have a total land area of only about 26 km², making it the fourth-smallest country in the world, and its population of roughly 11,000 makes it the third-least populous sovereign state.

History

Tuvalu was settled by Polynesian voyagers centuries before Europeans first sighted the islands in 1568 (Álvaro de Mendaña). As the Ellice Islands it became a British protectorate in 1892 and part of the Gilbert and Ellice Islands colony. In a 1974 referendum the Ellice Islanders voted for separation from the Gilbert Islands (Kiribati), and Tuvalu became independent in 1978, retaining Queen Elizabeth II — and now Charles III — as head of state.

Geography and climate change

The islands are extremely low-lying, with a maximum elevation of about 4.6 metres, leaving Tuvalu acutely vulnerable to sea-level rise. Tuvalu has become a leading voice in international climate diplomacy; in 2023 it signed the Falepili Union treaty with Australia, providing a migration pathway for Tuvaluans, and it has pursued a "digital nation" project to preserve its statehood and culture even if its land becomes uninhabitable.

Economy

Tuvalu's economy depends on fishing licences, remittances, foreign aid and the Tuvalu Trust Fund. Unusually, it earns substantial revenue from leasing its internet country-code domain .tv to streaming and media companies, and from postage stamp sales.
