Human experimentation

Human experimentation is the use of human beings as subjects in scientific and medical research. Legitimate human-subject research, conducted with informed consent and ethical oversight, is essential to modern medicine, underpinning clinical trials of drugs, vaccines and procedures. The term is also applied, pejoratively, to research conducted without consent or in violation of ethical norms — unethical human experimentation — which has been documented in many countries and eras and which led directly to the modern codes of research ethics.

Human-subject research, ethically conducted, underlies clinical medicine; its abuse produced modern ethics codes.

Ethical frameworks

The modern framework arose from the atrocities of the Second World War. The Nuremberg Code (1947), formulated during the Doctors' Trial, made voluntary informed consent "absolutely essential". The World Medical Association's Declaration of Helsinki (1964, repeatedly revised) extended ethical principles to all medical research involving humans. In the United States, the exposure of abuses such as the Tuskegee syphilis study led to the Belmont Report (1979) and federal regulations administered through institutional review boards (IRBs).

Notorious cases

  • Nazi experimentation — hypothermia, high-altitude, infectious-disease and sterilisation experiments performed on concentration-camp prisoners by physicians including Josef Mengele.
  • Unit 731 — the Imperial Japanese Army's biological-warfare unit, which conducted lethal experiments on prisoners in Manchuria.
  • Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972) — the U.S. Public Health Service observed untreated syphilis in African American men without their informed consent, withholding treatment even after penicillin became standard.
  • Guatemala experiments (1946–1948) — U.S. researchers deliberately infected Guatemalan subjects with sexually transmitted diseases.
  • MKUltra — CIA mind-control research, including unwitting administration of LSD.
  • Willowbrook and other institutional studies — hepatitis experiments on institutionalised children, criticised for exploitation of vulnerable populations.

Modern regulation

Today, human research in most jurisdictions requires prior ethics-committee approval, documented informed consent, risk minimisation, and special protections for children, prisoners and other vulnerable groups. Drug approval regimes require phased clinical trials, and data and safety monitoring boards oversee large studies. Debates continue over placebo use in low-income countries, research on human embryos, challenge trials, and the ethics of paying research subjects.
