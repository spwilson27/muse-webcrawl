Five-year plans of the Soviet Union

The five-year plans (Russian: pyatiletki) were a series of nationwide centralised economic plans in the Soviet Union, beginning in 1928 under Joseph Stalin. Devised by the state planning committee Gosplan, they set production targets for every sector of the economy over five-year periods. Thirteen plans were implemented before the dissolution of the USSR in 1991, and the model was widely imitated by other socialist states.

The first plan (1928–1932)

The first five-year plan pursued breakneck industrialisation and the collectivisation of agriculture. Investment poured into steel, coal, electricity and machine building — emblematic projects included the Magnitogorsk steel complex, the Dnieper hydroelectric dam and new tractor plants. Output of heavy industry rose substantially, but the human cost was enormous: collectivisation and dekulakisation contributed to famine, most catastrophically in Ukraine (the Holodomor) and Kazakhstan. The plan was declared fulfilled ahead of schedule in four years and three months.

Later plans

The second plan (1933–1937) continued industrial expansion with somewhat more attention to transport and consumer goods. The third was cut short by the German invasion of 1941. Post-war plans rebuilt the devastated economy, and later plans under Khrushchev and Brezhnev sought — with diminishing success — to raise living standards and shift toward intensive growth. The system of central planning grew rigid and wasteful, and by the 1980s the plans could not correct chronic shortages of consumer goods.

Legacy

The five-year plans transformed a largely agrarian country into an industrial superpower capable of defeating Nazi Germany and launching the space age, but at immense social cost. Five-year planning was adopted, with varying fidelity, by China, India (in a mixed-economy form) and numerous other states.
