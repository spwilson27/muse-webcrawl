Community organizing
DefinitionBuilding power through collective action by community members
Key figureSaul Alinsky (Rules for Radicals, 1971)
OrganizationsIndustrial Areas Foundation, ACORN, faith-based networks
MethodsOne-to-one meetings, house meetings, issue campaigns, accountability actions

Community organizing is a process by which people come together into an organization that acts in their shared self-interest. Unlike direct service provision or advocacy by professionals, community organizing builds the collective power of ordinary people, who identify common problems, develop leaders from within the community, and take coordinated action to win concrete changes.

History

The practice has roots in 19th-century labor and settlement-house movements. Saul Alinsky, organizing in Chicago's Back of the Yards neighborhood from 1939, founded the Industrial Areas Foundation (IAF) in 1940 and codified the approach in Reveille for Radicals (1946) and Rules for Radicals (1971). His successors, including Ed Chambers and Ernesto Cortés Jr., developed the broad-based model organizing through congregations and other mediating institutions. Fred Ross trained organizers including Cesar Chavez, whose farmworker movement applied organizing methods at national scale.

Models

Broad-based organizing builds coalitions of institutions — congregations, schools, unions — through relational meetings. Faith-based organizing networks such as the Gamaliel Foundation and Faith in Action grew from this tradition. Issue-based and neighborhood organizing, exemplified by ACORN (1970–2010), recruited individual members around housing, wages, and banking practices. Transformative organizing explicitly links local campaigns to broader social and economic critique.

Methods

Organizers recruit through one-to-one relational meetings, identify issues through house meetings and listening campaigns, research targets, and stage public "actions" — accountability sessions, rallies, and negotiations — designed to win specific, winnable demands while developing leaders. Organizing is distinguished from mobilizing: it seeks durable organizations rather than one-off turnout.
