Jack Northrop

Born: 10 November 1895, Newark, New Jersey, U.S.
Died: 18 February 1981, Glendale, California, U.S.
Known for: Flying-wing aircraft; founder of Northrop Corporation

John Knudsen "Jack" Northrop (1895–1981) was an American aircraft designer and industrialist, founder of the Northrop Corporation. A self-taught engineer, he was responsible for a series of influential all-metal aircraft designs and devoted much of his career to his vision of the pure "flying wing".

Early career

Northrop learned drafting and mechanics in California and in 1916 joined the infant Loughead Aircraft company (later Lockheed), where he helped design the F-1 flying boat and the S-1 sport biplane, notable for its monocoque wooden fuselage. In the 1920s he worked for Douglas, designing fuel systems, and then returned to Lockheed, where he was chief engineer of the revolutionary Vega (1927), whose streamlined monocoque body made it a favourite of record-breaking pilots including Amelia Earhart. In 1928 he founded his first company, the Avion Corporation, and produced the Alpha — a low-wing, all-metal monoplane with multicellular stressed-skin wing construction that influenced a generation of airliners — followed by the Beta, Gamma and Delta series.

Northrop Corporation and the flying wings

After a period within Douglas (whose DC series benefited from his wing designs), Northrop founded the independent Northrop Corporation in Hawthorne, California, in 1939. There he pursued the flying wing: the piston-engined N-1M testbed (1940), the N-9M scale models, and the giant XB-35 propeller-driven bomber, which flew in 1946; its jet-powered derivative, the YB-49, flew in 1947. The flying wings promised lower drag and greater efficiency, but suffered stability and political difficulties, and the B-49 programme was cancelled in 1949–50 — a blow from which Northrop never fully recovered professionally. The company nevertheless produced successful conventional aircraft, including the P-61 Black Widow night fighter and the F-89 Scorpion interceptor.

Legacy and vindication

Northrop retired in 1952, embittered by the flying wing's cancellation. Shortly before his death in 1981, he was cleared to see classified models of the B-2 Spirit stealth bomber — a flying wing with exactly the same 172-foot (52.4 m) wingspan as his YB-49 — and is reported to have said that he now knew why God had kept him alive. Engineers credit modern fly-by-wire controls with solving the stability problems that had doomed his designs in the era of manual flight controls.
