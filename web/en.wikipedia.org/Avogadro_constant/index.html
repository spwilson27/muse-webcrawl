Avogadro constant

Value
6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1 (exact, since 2019)
Named after
Amedeo Avogadro

The Avogadro constant, commonly denoted NA or L, is an SI defining constant with an exact value of 6.02214076 × 1023 mol−1 (reciprocal moles). It is defined as the number of constituent particles (usually molecules, atoms, ions, or electrons) per mole. The number of particles in a sample can be determined by multiplying the amount of substance in moles by the Avogadro constant.

Definition and role in the SI

Since the 2019 revision of the International System of Units, the Avogadro constant has an exactly defined value, and the mole is defined as exactly this number of elementary entities. Historically, the constant was defined so that one mole of carbon-12 atoms had a mass of exactly 12 grams.

History

The constant is named after the Italian scientist Amedeo Avogadro, who proposed in 1811 that the volume of a gas is proportional to the number of atoms or molecules in it (see Avogadro's law). Avogadro himself never estimated the number; the first estimates came decades later from work by Josef Loschmidt on the kinetic theory of gases. Jean Perrin's experiments on Brownian motion in the early 20th century gave values close to the modern one, and he proposed naming the constant in Avogadro's honour.

Measurement

Before its exact definition in 2019, the constant was measured by several independent methods, including electrolysis (via the Faraday constant), X-ray crystallography of silicon spheres in the International Avogadro Coordination project, and counting atoms in highly enriched silicon-28 crystals.
