Peculiar velocity

In physical cosmology, peculiar velocity refers to the velocity of an object (usually a galaxy) relative to the cosmic rest frame — that is, the component of its motion that deviates from the smooth expansion of the universe described by Hubble's law.

Concept

The observed redshift of a galaxy combines the cosmological redshift due to expansion with a Doppler shift from its peculiar velocity. Typical peculiar velocities are a few hundred kilometres per second; galaxies in clusters may move at over 1,000 km/s relative to their cluster's centre of mass. The Milky Way and the Local Group move at roughly 600 km/s relative to the cosmic microwave background rest frame, in the direction of the constellation Hydra–Centaurus, an infall pattern associated with the Great Attractor and the Shapley Supercluster.

Uses

Because peculiar velocities are caused by gravitational attraction toward overdense regions, mapping them — by comparing redshift-independent distance estimates (for example from the Tully–Fisher relation or Type Ia supernovae) with redshifts — allows astronomers to trace the distribution of matter, including dark matter, in the local universe. Bulk flow surveys measure the average motion of large volumes, testing predictions of the Lambda-CDM model.
