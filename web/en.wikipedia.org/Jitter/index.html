Jitter

[Image placeholder: signal waveform exhibiting jitter]

Jitter is the deviation from true periodicity of a presumably periodic signal, often in relation to a reference clock signal. In clock recovery applications it is called timing jitter. Jitter is a significant, and usually undesired, factor in the design of almost all communications links.

In computer networking, jitter — more precisely packet delay variation — is the variation in the time delay between when a signal is transmitted and when it is received over a network connection. High jitter can degrade the quality of voice and video communications, causing choppy audio or uneven playback.

Types of jitter

Jitter can be characterised in several ways. Random jitter is unpredictable and generally follows a Gaussian distribution, arising from thermal noise and other random processes. Deterministic jitter has identifiable causes, such as crosstalk, electromagnetic interference, and the effects of data-dependent signal distortion. Related concepts include phase noise in oscillators and wander, which refers to slow, long-term variations in clock timing.

Measurement

Jitter is typically measured in units of time — picoseconds or nanoseconds for high-speed digital signals — or in unit intervals (UI), where one UI is the duration of one symbol period. In packet networks it is commonly measured in milliseconds. Test equipment such as oscilloscopes with eye-diagram analysis is used to visualise and quantify jitter in serial data links.

Mitigation

Techniques to reduce jitter include the use of phase-locked loops and jitter buffers, careful circuit-board layout, proper termination and shielding, and in streaming applications, buffering that trades a small amount of additional latency for smoother playback.
