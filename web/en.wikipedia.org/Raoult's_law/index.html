Raoult's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Raoult's law is a law of thermodynamics concerning the vapour pressure of a solution, established by the French chemist François-Marie Raoult in the 1880s. It states that the partial vapour pressure of each component of an ideal mixture of liquids is equal to the vapour pressure of the pure component multiplied by its mole fraction in the mixture:

pi = pi* · xi

where pi is the partial pressure of component i, pi* is the vapour pressure of the pure component, and xi its mole fraction.

Consequences

For a non-volatile solute dissolved in a solvent, Raoult's law predicts that the vapour pressure of the solution is lower than that of the pure solvent, the lowering being proportional to the mole fraction of the solute. This vapour-pressure lowering is one of the colligative properties of solutions and underlies the related phenomena of boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression, from which molar masses can be determined.

Deviations and ideal solutions

Raoult's law is exact only for ideal solutions, in which interactions between unlike molecules are the same as between like molecules. Real solutions deviate: stronger attractions between unlike molecules produce negative deviations (lower vapour pressure than predicted, as in acetone–chloroform), while weaker attractions produce positive deviations (as in ethanol–water). Large deviations lead to azeotropes, mixtures that boil at constant composition. At the dilute limit, the solvent obeys Raoult's law while the solute obeys the related Henry's law. Corrections for non-ideality are made using activity coefficients.
