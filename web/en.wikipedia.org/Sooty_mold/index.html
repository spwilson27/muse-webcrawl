Sooty mold

TypeFungal growth on plant surfaces
Causal fungiCapnodium, Fumago, Scorias and others (Ascomycota)
SubstrateHoneydew from sap-sucking insects
Main harmBlocks light, reducing photosynthesis

Sooty mold (also spelled sooty mould) is a collective term for several species of fungi that grow on plants and other surfaces, forming a black, soot-like coating. The fungi are not usually parasitic on the plant itself; instead they grow on honeydew, the sugary excretion of sap-sucking insects such as aphids, scale insects, whiteflies, and mealybugs, or on sugary substances the plant itself exudes.

Description

The dark mycelium of genera such as Capnodium, Fumago, and Scorias coats leaves, stems, and fruit, and can also cover nearby objects — garden furniture, cars, and pavements beneath infested trees. A heavy coating shades the leaf and can reduce photosynthesis and stunt growth, and it spoils the appearance of fruit and ornamentals, though the fungus does not infect plant tissue.

Control

Management focuses on controlling the honeydew-producing insects and on washing the mold from leaves with water or mild soapy solutions; once the honeydew supply stops, the mold weathers away. Improving air circulation and avoiding overhead conditions that favour the insects also helps.

Ecology

Sooty molds are part of a wider ecological web: ants often farm honeydew insects and defend them from predators, indirectly promoting the mold.
