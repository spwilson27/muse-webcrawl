A farmers market (or farmers' market) is a physical retail marketplace intended to sell foods directly from farmers to consumers. Farmers markets may be outdoors or indoors, and typically consist of booths, tables, or stands where farmers sell fruits, vegetables, meats, cheeses, and sometimes prepared foods and beverages. By cutting out intermediaries, they allow producers to retain a larger share of the food dollar and give consumers access to fresh, seasonal, and locally grown produce.

History

Markets where growers sell their own produce are as old as urban civilization, but the modern farmers market movement in North America revived the form in the late 20th century amid interest in local food. In the United States, the number of markets listed in the USDA National Farmers Market Directory grew from about 1,755 in 1994 to more than 8,700 by 2019. Many markets now accept nutrition benefits such as SNAP/EBT and operate incentive programs doubling the value of benefits spent on produce.

Rules and character

Many markets enforce "producer-only" rules requiring vendors to sell what they themselves grow or make, distinguishing them from flea markets or wholesale resellers; standards and enforcement vary widely, and debates arise over reselling and the definition of "local". Markets typically run on a fixed weekly schedule, seasonally in cold climates and year-round in mild ones. Beyond commerce, they function as community gathering places, venues for food education, and entry points for beginning farmers and food artisans.

Worldwide

Equivalent direct-sale markets exist globally: wet markets in Asia, weekly Wochenmärkte in Germany, marchés in France, and the tianguis tradition in Mexico. In the United Kingdom, dedicated farmers' markets revived from 1997 (Bath) under certification schemes such as FARMA.
