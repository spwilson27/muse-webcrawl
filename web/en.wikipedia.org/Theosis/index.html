Theosis

Theosis (Greek: θέωσις), also called deification or divinization, is a transformative process whose goal is likeness to or union with God, as taught by the Eastern Orthodox Church, the Oriental Orthodox Churches, and the Eastern Catholic Churches, and discussed in Roman Catholic and some Protestant theology. According to the Orthodox teaching, theosis is the purpose of human life: human beings are created to become "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4) by grace, without ceasing to be creatures or becoming God by essence.

Doctrine

The classic formula is that of Athanasius of Alexandria: "He was made man, that we men might become gods." Orthodox theology safeguards the doctrine with the distinction, articulated by Gregory Palamas in the fourteenth-century Hesychast controversy, between God's inaccessible essence and his uncreated energies, in which humans can genuinely participate. Deification is thus not pantheism: the human person shares God's life and energies by grace — adoption, not identity of substance.

Stages and practice

Following a scheme associated with Maximus the Confessor and later ascetic writers, the path of theosis is often described in three stages: purification (katharsis), through repentance and keeping the commandments; illumination (theoria), the contemplation of God and the vision of the uncreated light of Tabor; and union or deification (theosis) itself. The process is ecclesial and sacramental, centred on baptism and the Eucharist, and ascetic, pursued through prayer — especially the Jesus Prayer of the Hesychast tradition — fasting, almsgiving, and obedience. It is corporate as well as individual: salvation is participation in the life of the Church, the Body of Christ.

In other traditions

Roman Catholic teaching speaks of divinization as participation in the divine nature through sanctifying grace, citing the same patristic sources, and the Catechism quotes Athanasius's formula. Some Lutheran scholars, notably the Finnish school of Tuomo Mannermaa, have argued that Martin Luther's doctrine of justification contains a real participation in Christ akin to theosis; the theme has become important in Orthodox–Lutheran ecumenical dialogue.
