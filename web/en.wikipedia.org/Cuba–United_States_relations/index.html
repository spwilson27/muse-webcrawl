Cuba–United States relations are the bilateral relations between the Republic of Cuba and the United States of America. Relations began in the early 19th century, when Cuba was a Spanish colony and the United States took a growing interest in the island, located only 90 miles (145 km) south of Florida. After the Spanish–American War of 1898, Cuba became a de facto U.S. protectorate under the Platt Amendment, and American economic influence over the island deepened for decades.

Relations deteriorated sharply after the Cuban Revolution of 1959 brought Fidel Castro to power. The United States imposed a trade embargo, broke diplomatic relations in January 1961, and backed the failed Bay of Pigs Invasion that April. The Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962 brought the two countries to the brink of nuclear war. For more than five decades afterward, relations remained hostile, with the embargo codified by laws such as the Helms–Burton Act of 1996.

Thaw and restoration

On 17 December 2014, Presidents Barack Obama and Raúl Castro simultaneously announced the beginning of a process to normalize relations — the "Cuban thaw." The United States removed Cuba from its list of state sponsors of terrorism, and on 20 July 2015 the two countries formally restored diplomatic relations and reopened embassies in each other's capitals. President Obama visited Havana in March 2016, the first visit by a sitting U.S. president since 1928. The subsequent administration of Donald Trump reversed many of the opening measures, reimposing travel and remittance restrictions and, in January 2021, redesignating Cuba a state sponsor of terrorism.

Key issues

Major ongoing issues include the U.S. embargo (el bloqueo), the status of the U.S. naval base and detention camp at Guantánamo Bay, migration policy (including the former "wet foot, dry foot" policy ended in 2017), property claims arising from post-revolution nationalizations, and unexplained health incidents affecting U.S. diplomatic personnel in Havana beginning in 2016.
