The Mach–Zehnder interferometer is a device used to determine the relative phase shift variations between two collimated beams derived by splitting light from a single source. The interferometer has been used, among other things, to measure phase shifts between the two beams caused by a sample or a change in length of one of the paths. It is named after the physicists Ludwig Mach (the son of Ernst Mach) and Ludwig Zehnder; Zehnder's proposal in an 1891 article was refined by Mach in an 1892 article.

Design

A collimated beam is split by a half-silvered mirror (beam splitter) into two beams, which travel along separate paths. Each beam is reflected by a mirror, and the two beams are recombined by a second beam splitter so that they interfere. The output intensities at the two exit ports depend on the optical path difference between the arms: a change of one wavelength in the path difference switches the output entirely from one port to the other. Unlike the Michelson interferometer, the light traverses each arm only once, and the two output beams do not return to the source.

Applications

By placing a sample — for example a gas cell, a wind-tunnel test section, or a flowing fluid — in one arm, the resulting fringe shifts measure changes in refractive index, density, or temperature. The instrument was historically important in aerodynamic research for visualising shock waves and density gradients around models. In modern technology, integrated-optic Mach–Zehnder modulators, in which an applied voltage changes the refractive index of one arm of a waveguide interferometer, are key components of fibre-optic telecommunications, switching light intensity at gigahertz rates.

Quantum mechanics

The Mach–Zehnder interferometer is widely used in discussions and experimental tests of quantum mechanics, because single photons passing through it exhibit interference that depends on both paths — illustrating wave–particle duality, complementarity, and, with which-path detectors, the quantum eraser. Elitzur–Vaidman bomb testing and interaction-free measurement are commonly described in terms of this arrangement.
