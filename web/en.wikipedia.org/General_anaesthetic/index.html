General anaesthetic

A general anaesthetic (or anesthetic) is a drug that brings about a reversible loss of consciousness, including absence of awareness, pain, and memory, so that surgical and other medical procedures can be performed. Unlike local anaesthetics, which block sensation in one region, general anaesthetics act on the central nervous system — principally the brain and spinal cord — to produce the state of general anaesthesia. The depth of anaesthesia is monitored and controlled throughout the procedure by an anaesthetist.

Routes and agents

General anaesthetics are administered either by inhalation or by intravenous injection. Inhalational agents include the volatile liquids sevoflurane, desflurane and isoflurane, delivered through calibrated vaporisers, and the gas nitrous oxide. Common intravenous induction agents include propofol, thiopental, etomidate and ketamine. In practice, a balanced anaesthetic technique combines several drug classes: a hypnotic to maintain unconsciousness, an opioid analgesic, and a neuromuscular blocking drug to relax muscles.

Mechanism of action

Despite more than 170 years of clinical use, the precise mechanisms of general anaesthetics remain incompletely understood. Most agents are thought to potentiate inhibitory GABAA receptors and/or inhibit excitatory NMDA receptors in the central nervous system, disrupting the neural activity that underlies consciousness. The Meyer–Overton correlation — the observation that anaesthetic potency tracks lipid solubility — historically pointed to membranes as the site of action, though modern research focuses on specific ion-channel proteins.

History

The first successful public demonstration of ether anaesthesia took place in 1846 at Massachusetts General Hospital, when William T. G. Morton administered diethyl ether for a tumour removal by surgeon John Collins Warren. Chloroform was introduced by James Young Simpson in 1847. Modern agents were developed through the twentieth century, with propofol (introduced in the 1980s) becoming the most widely used intravenous induction agent.
