Pig iron

Pig iron, also known as crude iron, is an intermediate product of the iron industry in the production of steel which is obtained by smelting iron ore in a blast furnace. Pig iron has a very high carbon content, typically 3.8–4.7%, along with silica and other constituents of dross, which makes it very brittle and not useful directly as a material except for limited applications.

Etymology

The traditional shape of the moulds used for pig iron ingots is a branching structure formed in sand, with many individual ingots at right angles to a central channel or "runner". Because the arrangement resembled a litter of piglets suckling on a sow, the ingots became known as "pigs" and the metal as pig iron.

Production and use

In a blast furnace, iron ore is reduced with coke and limestone flux at temperatures around 2,000 °C; the molten iron collects at the hearth and is tapped into ladles or cast into pigs. Most pig iron is transferred hot to steelmaking vessels — basic oxygen or electric arc furnaces — where excess carbon and impurities are removed to produce steel. Solid pig iron is also remelted in foundries for cast iron production. China is by far the world's largest producer.
