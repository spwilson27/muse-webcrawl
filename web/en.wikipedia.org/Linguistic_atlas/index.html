Linguistic atlas

A linguistic atlas (or dialect atlas) is a collection of maps showing the geographical distribution of linguistic features — pronunciations, words, or grammatical forms — across a speech area. Each map typically plots the responses of informants at many localities to questionnaire items, and boundary lines called isoglosses are drawn between regions using different variants.

History

The discipline began with Georg Wenker's surveys of German dialects in the 1870s–80s, which produced the Sprachatlas des Deutschen Reichs. Jules Gilliéron's Atlas linguistique de la France (1902–1910), based on fieldwork by Edmond Edmont, became the model for the genre, promoting the motto that "every word has its own history". Later projects included the Linguistic Atlas of New England and the Atlas Linguarum Europae.

Method

Traditional atlases relied on trained fieldworkers interviewing older, rural, non-mobile informants to capture conservative local dialect. Modern dialectometry uses computational methods to aggregate many features into measures of overall dialect distance, and contemporary atlases are often published as interactive digital resources.
