Full Faith and Credit Clause

Article IV, Section 1 of the United States Constitution

The Full Faith and Credit Clause is Article IV, Section 1 of the United States Constitution, which provides: "Full Faith and Credit shall be given in each State to the public Acts, Records, and judicial Proceedings of every other State." It requires each U.S. state to recognise and give effect to the laws, official records and court judgments of the other states, binding the states into a single legal union.

Application

The clause is at its strongest for court judgments: a final judgment rendered in one state must generally be enforced in another, even if the underlying claim would not be allowed under the second state's law, subject to narrow defences such as lack of jurisdiction. For statutes and "public acts", the Supreme Court gives states more latitude to apply their own law where they have a legitimate interest, balancing the forum state's policy against the interests of sister states. Congress, under the clause's second sentence, has enacted implementing statutes, notably 28 U.S.C. § 1738.

Modern controversies

The clause figured prominently in debates over interstate recognition of marriages, including same-sex marriages before Obergefell v. Hodges (2015), and the federal Defense of Marriage Act of 1996 attempted to relieve states of any obligation to recognise such marriages from other states; the Respect for Marriage Act of 2022 instead requires recognition of valid out-of-state marriages. The clause also governs recognition of divorces, custody decrees (reinforced by federal statute), professional licences in limited contexts, and driver's licence compacts.
