Hipparcos was a scientific satellite of the European Space Agency (ESA), launched in 1989 and operated until 1993. It was the first space mission devoted to precision astrometry — the accurate measurement of the positions, parallaxes and proper motions of stars — and its name, an acronym for High Precision Parallax Collecting Satellite, also honours the ancient Greek astronomer Hipparchus.

Mission

Hipparcos was launched by an Ariane 4 rocket from Kourou on 8 August 1989. Its apogee boost motor failed to fire, leaving it in an elliptical transfer orbit instead of the planned geostationary orbit. Revised operations, with extra ground stations and frequent perigee firings of its attitude thrusters, nevertheless allowed the satellite to complete its observing programme over 3.5 years, albeit with accelerated degradation from radiation-belt passages. The satellite spun slowly, scanning the sky along great circles with two fields of view combined onto a single focal plane, allowing absolute parallax measurement over the whole sky.

Results

The Hipparcos Catalogue (1997) gave positions, parallaxes and proper motions for 118,218 stars with a median precision of about one milliarcsecond — orders of magnitude better than ground-based astrometry. The companion Tycho Catalogue, from the satellite's star mapper, covered over a million stars at lower precision; its successor, Tycho-2 (2000), listed 2.5 million. Hipparcos recalibrated the cosmic distance ladder, refined stellar luminosities, ages and the structure of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, and provided the optical reference frame later tied to the International Celestial Reference Frame. Its successor mission, Gaia, launched in 2013, extended the work to over a billion stars with still greater precision.
