Myrmidons


Labelled image: the Myrmidons, warriors of Achilles

The Myrmidons (Greek: Μυρμιδόνες, Myrmidónes) were an ancient Greek tribe and legendary people of Phthia in Thessaly, famed in mythology as the loyal and fierce followers of Achilles in the Trojan War. Their ancestral king was Peleus, father of Achilles. In Homer's Iliad they fight under Achilles and, after his withdrawal from battle, under his companion Patroclus.

Myth of origin

According to the best-known origin myth, retold by Ovid in the Metamorphoses, the island of Aegina was emptied by a plague sent by Hera. Aeacus, the island's just king and grandfather of Achilles, prayed to Zeus for new subjects; Zeus transformed the ants (myrmēkes) swarming on a sacred oak into human beings — hence the name Myrmidons, "ant-people". The resulting people were said to retain ant-like qualities: industriousness, thrift, endurance, and unquestioning loyalty to their leader.

In literature and later use

In the Iliad, Achilles compares his men to bees and wolves as much as ants, and the Myrmidons' blind devotion became proverbial. In later usage, "myrmidon" came to denote a loyal follower or subordinate who executes orders without question or scruple — a sense that survives in modern English, often with a slightly negative connotation of henchmanship. Shakespeare uses the word in this spirit, and the Myrmidons appear in numerous retellings of the Achilles story, including the film Troy (2004), where they are depicted as an elite black-clad assault force.
