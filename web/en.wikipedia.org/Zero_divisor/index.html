Zero divisor

In abstract algebra, an element a of a ring R is called a left zero divisor if there exists a nonzero element x in R such that ax = 0, or equivalently if the map from R to R that sends x to ax is not injective. Similarly, an element a of a ring R is called a right zero divisor if there exists a nonzero element y in R such that ya = 0. An element that is both a left and a right zero divisor is simply called a zero divisor. In a commutative ring the two notions coincide.

An element that is not a zero divisor is called regular. The zero element 0 itself is a zero divisor in any ring containing a nonzero element, by convention counted separately in some definitions. A ring with no nonzero zero divisors is called an integral domain (in the commutative case).

Examples

The ring of integers ℤ has no nonzero zero divisors, but in the ring ℤ/6ℤ of integers modulo 6, the classes 2, 3 and 4 are zero divisors, since 2 · 3 = 6 ≡ 0 and 4 · 3 = 12 ≡ 0. More generally, in ℤ/nℤ the zero divisors are exactly the classes not coprime to n. In the ring of 2 × 2 matrices over a field, nonzero singular matrices are zero divisors. In the ring of sedenions, zero divisors exist despite the absence of zero itself as a factor, a phenomenon that prevents the sedenions from being a division algebra.
