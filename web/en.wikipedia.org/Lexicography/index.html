Lexicography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lexicography is the practice of compiling dictionaries.

Lexicography is the practice of creating books of words and their meanings—dictionaries and related reference works. It is divided into two related fields: practical lexicography, the art or craft of writing, compiling and editing dictionaries, and theoretical lexicography, the scholarly study of the semantic, orthographic, syntagmatic and paradigmatic relationships within the lexicon of a language. A person devoted to lexicography is called a lexicographer.

History

Early word lists go back to Sumerian and Akkadian glossaries of the ancient Near East. In China, the Erya (c. 3rd century BC) organized words by subject; in India, Sanskrit nighaṇṭu listed rare Vedic words. European lexicography advanced with bilingual glossaries in the Middle Ages and monolingual dictionaries in the 17th and 18th centuries, culminating in Samuel Johnson's A Dictionary of the English Language (1755), defined on literary quotations. The Oxford English Dictionary, begun in the 19th century, applied historical principles, documenting each word's development with dated citations gathered by thousands of readers.

Practice

Modern lexicographers work from large electronic corpora, which provide evidence of how words are actually used. An entry typically gives the headword (lemma), pronunciation, part of speech, inflected forms, etymology, definitions ordered by sense, usage labels (e.g., informal, obsolete, regional), and example sentences. Lexicographers must decide which words and senses to include, how to define them without circularity, and how to treat new words, multiword expressions, and proper names.

Types of dictionaries

Lexicographic products include general and specialized dictionaries, bilingual dictionaries, learners' dictionaries, thesauri, historical dictionaries, and electronic resources such as online dictionaries and lexical databases.
