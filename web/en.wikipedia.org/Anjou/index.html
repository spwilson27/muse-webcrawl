Anjou

Anjou (French pronunciation approximately "ahn-zhoo") is a historical province of France straddling the lower Loire River, centred on the city of Angers. Its territory corresponds roughly to the present-day department of Maine-et-Loire. Anjou is known for its chateaux, its wines, and the medieval Angevin dynasty, whose counts became kings of England and ruled an extensive collection of territories often called the Angevin Empire.

History

The county of Anjou emerged in the 9th and 10th centuries under the Ingelgerian dynasty. Fulk III ("the Black") consolidated Angevin power around the year 1000 through castle-building and warfare with neighbouring Brittany, Blois and Poitou. In the 12th century, Geoffrey V of Anjou (nicknamed Plantagenet) married Matilda, daughter of Henry I of England; their son became Henry II of England in 1154, joining Anjou to the English crown. The province was recovered by the French crown under Philip II in 1204.

Later history

Anjou was later held as an apanage by cadet branches of the French royal house, including the houses that produced kings of Naples and Hungary. The duchy was permanently united to the French crown in the 16th century. The region played a part in the Wars of Religion and in the counter-revolutionary risings of the 1790s in the neighbouring Vendee.

Wine

Anjou wines, produced under appellations such as Anjou AOC, Saumur, Savennieres and Coteaux du Layon, include sweet and dry whites from Chenin blanc, roses, and reds based on Cabernet Franc.
