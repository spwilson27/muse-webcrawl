Power amplifier

A power amplifier is an electronic amplifier designed to increase the power of a signal so that it can drive a load such as a loudspeaker, an antenna, or a motor. Unlike a voltage or pre-amplifier, which prepares a small signal for further processing, the power amplifier is the final stage that delivers substantial current and voltage to the load.

Amplifier classes

Power amplifiers are classified by the conduction angle of their output devices. In class A, the output device conducts for the entire input cycle (360°), giving low distortion but poor efficiency (at most 50% theoretical, typically less in practice). Class B devices conduct for half the cycle (180°) in push–pull pairs, reaching up to about 78.5% theoretical efficiency but risking crossover distortion. Class AB biases devices slightly on, balancing fidelity and efficiency, and dominates audio design. Class C conducts for less than half a cycle and is used only in tuned radio-frequency transmitters. Class D amplifiers switch the output devices rapidly on and off (pulse-width modulation) and filter the result, achieving efficiencies above 90% and dominating modern compact and battery-powered audio; other classes (G, H) vary the supply rails to improve efficiency.

Applications

Power amplifiers are used in home and professional audio systems, guitar amplifiers, public-address systems, radio transmitters (RF power amplifiers), radar, servo and motor drives, and instrumentation. Key specifications include output power (watts into a given load impedance), total harmonic distortion, frequency response, efficiency, and damping factor.
