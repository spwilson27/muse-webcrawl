Caspar David Friedrich (5 September 1774 – 7 May 1840) was a German Romantic landscape painter, generally considered the most important German artist of his generation. His allegorical landscapes — vast, silent scenes of nature, often with small contemplative figures seen from behind (the Rückenfigur) — express a spiritual experience of the sublime and meditations on life and death.

Life

Born in Greifswald on the Baltic coast, Friedrich lost his mother, a brother (who drowned saving him from icy water), and two sisters in childhood — losses that shadowed his art. He studied in Copenhagen and settled in Dresden in 1798. His sepia works and early oils attracted the attention of Goethe and, in 1810, purchase by the Prussian crown prince. Friedrich's reputation declined in the 1830s as tastes changed; he suffered a stroke in 1835 and died in relative obscurity.

Work and reputation

Landmark paintings include The Cross in the Mountains (1808), The Abbey in the Oakwood (1810), Wanderer above the Sea of Fog (c. 1818), Chalk Cliffs on Rügen (1818), and The Sea of Ice (1823–24). Forgotten for decades, Friedrich was rediscovered in the early 20th century and is now celebrated as a defining figure of Romanticism, an influence on Symbolism, Expressionism, and modern art.
