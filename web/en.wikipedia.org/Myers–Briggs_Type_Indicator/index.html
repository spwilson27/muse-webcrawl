Myers–Briggs Type Indicator

The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is a self-report questionnaire that makes the theory of psychological types described by C. G. Jung accessible and useful in people's lives. The essence of the theory is that seemingly random variation in behaviour is actually quite orderly and consistent, being due to basic differences in the ways individuals prefer to use their perception and judgment.

History

Katharine Cook Briggs and her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers developed the instrument during the Second World War, believing that knowledge of personality preferences would help women entering the industrial workforce identify the sort of war-time jobs where they would be "most comfortable and effective". The questionnaire, first published in 1944 and as the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator in 1956, classifies people into sixteen types.

The four dichotomies

  • Extraversion (E) – Introversion (I): where one prefers to focus one's energy;
  • Sensing (S) – Intuition (N): how one prefers to take in information;
  • Thinking (T) – Feeling (F): how one prefers to make decisions;
  • Judging (J) – Perceiving (P): how one prefers to orient oneself to the external world.

Criticism

The MBTI is widely used in business, education, and career counselling, but its scientific standing is disputed. Critics note poor test–retest reliability, the lack of bimodal distributions supporting the dichotomies, and weak evidence that type predicts job performance. Supporters respond that it is intended as a tool for self-understanding rather than clinical diagnosis.
