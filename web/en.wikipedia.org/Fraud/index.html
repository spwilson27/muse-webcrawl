Fraud

Fraud is the intentional deception of another person or entity made for personal gain or to damage the victim. In law, fraud is a broad category of intentional wrongdoing that may give rise to civil liability (in a private lawsuit) or criminal prosecution, depending on the jurisdiction and the nature of the conduct.

Elements

In common-law jurisdictions, a claim of fraud typically requires proof of several elements: a false representation of a material fact; knowledge that the representation was false (scienter); an intention to induce the victim to act or refrain from acting; justifiable reliance by the victim on the representation; and damage to the victim as a result. Silence can amount to fraud where there is a duty to disclose, and statements of opinion can be actionable where the speaker holds special knowledge or a position of trust.

Types

Fraud takes many forms. Securities fraud involves deception in connection with the purchase or sale of securities, including insider trading and false financial statements. Mail fraud and wire fraud are federal offences in the United States criminalising the use of postal or electronic communications in furtherance of a scheme to defraud. Identity theft involves the misuse of another person's identifying information. Other common forms include insurance fraud, tax fraud, credit card fraud, bankruptcy fraud, electoral fraud, and academic dishonesty such as research fraud. Advance-fee fraud and romance scams are widespread forms of consumer fraud.

Prevention and detection

Organisations counter fraud through internal controls, audits, whistleblowing channels, and forensic accounting. Statistical techniques such as Benford's law have been used to detect anomalous patterns in financial data. In the United States, major corporate accounting scandals of the early 2000s, such as the collapse of Enron, led to reforms including the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002.
