In microeconomics, a consumer's Hicksian demand function (or compensated demand function) gives the quantity demanded of each good that minimises the consumer's total expenditure while delivering a fixed level of utility. It is named after the economist John Hicks, who developed the concept in his work Value and Capital (1939) and in the theory of compensating variation. The Hicksian demand for a good depends on the prices of all goods and on the target utility level: h(p, u).

Definition and derivation

Hicksian demand is the solution to the expenditure minimisation problem: minimise p·x subject to u(x) ≥ u*. It stands in duality with the ordinary (Marshallian) demand function, which maximises utility subject to a budget. By Shephard's lemma, the Hicksian demand for a good equals the partial derivative of the expenditure function with respect to that good's price: hi(p, u) = ∂e(p, u)/∂pi.

Key identities link the two demand concepts: e(p, v(p, m)) = m; h(p, u) = x(p, e(p, u)); and v(p, e(p, u)) = u, where v is the indirect utility function and m is income. Hicksian demand at a given utility equals Marshallian demand when income is set at the minimum expenditure needed to reach that utility.

Properties

Because the consumer is compensated for price changes—holding utility constant rather than income—Hicksian demand isolates the pure substitution effect of a price change. Consequently the own-price substitution effect is always non-positive: Hicksian demand curves slope downward (or are flat) even for Giffen goods, whose Marshallian demand may slope upward. Hicksian demand is homogeneous of degree zero in prices and is not directly observable, because utility is not observable; it is nonetheless fundamental to welfare analysis, since the exact measures of welfare change—compensating variation and equivalent variation—are defined as areas under Hicksian demand curves, and the Slutsky equation decomposes a Marshallian price response into the Hicksian substitution effect plus an income effect.
