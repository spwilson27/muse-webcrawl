Projectile motion

Projectile motion is a form of motion experienced by an object or particle (a projectile) that is projected in a gravitational field, such as from the surface of the Earth, and moves along a curved path under the action of gravity only. In the ideal case where air resistance is negligible, the trajectory is a parabola.

Kinematics

The motion can be analyzed into independent horizontal and vertical components. Horizontally, the projectile moves at constant velocity, since there is no horizontal acceleration (neglecting drag). Vertically, it undergoes constant acceleration g (approximately 9.81 m/s² near Earth's surface). If a projectile is launched with initial speed v₀ at angle θ, its range on level ground is R = v₀² sin(2θ)/g, which is maximal at θ = 45°. The maximum height is H = v₀² sin²θ/(2g), and the total time of flight is T = 2v₀ sin θ/g.

History

Aristotelian physics held that projectiles moved in straight lines until their impetus ran out. In the 16th and 17th centuries, Niccolò Tartaglia and then Galileo Galilei showed that the trajectory is parabolic, decomposing the motion into uniform horizontal and uniformly accelerated vertical components — a result published in Galileo's Two New Sciences (1638).

Air resistance

Real projectiles experience drag, which shortens the range, lowers the apex, and makes the descending branch of the trajectory steeper than the ascending branch. For high-velocity projectiles such as artillery shells and bullets, drag is a major factor handled by ballistics; the Magnus effect and Earth's rotation (Coriolis effect) also matter at long ranges.

Applications

The analysis of projectile motion underlies artillery, sports such as shot put and basketball, fountain and irrigation design, and introductory physics education.
