The Burr–Hamilton duel took place in Weehawken, New Jersey, on July 11, 1804, between Aaron Burr, the sitting Vice President of the United States, and Alexander Hamilton, the former Secretary of the Treasury. Hamilton was mortally wounded and died the following day. The duel ended Burr's political career and remains the most famous duel in American history.

Background

Burr and Hamilton had been political rivals since the 1790s. Hamilton had worked to defeat Burr in the deadlocked presidential election of 1800 and again in the New York gubernatorial election of 1804. During that campaign a letter was published quoting Charles D. Cooper's report that Hamilton had expressed a "despicable opinion" of Burr. Burr demanded an explanation; Hamilton's evasive replies escalated the exchange into a formal challenge under the code duello.

The duel

The parties rowed across the Hudson in the early morning to the Heights of Weehawken, a customary duelling ground below the Palisades. Both men fired; Hamilton's shot struck a tree above and behind Burr, while Burr's shot hit Hamilton in the abdomen, fracturing a rib and lodging in his spine. Whether Hamilton deliberately threw away his shot, as he had written beforehand that he intended, remains disputed.

Aftermath

Hamilton died on July 12, 1804, in New York, mourned nationally as a martyred statesman. Burr was indicted for murder in both New York and New Jersey but never tried; he completed his vice-presidential term, and his later western schemes led to a treason trial (and acquittal) in 1807. The duel intensified the anti-duelling movement in the United States.
