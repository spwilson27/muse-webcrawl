Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals

The Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785; German: Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten), also known as the Foundations of the Metaphysics of Morals, is the first of Immanuel Kant's mature works on moral philosophy and remains one of the most influential works in the field. In it Kant sets out to identify and establish the supreme principle of morality — the categorical imperative — arguing that moral requirements arise from reason itself and from the autonomy of the rational will.

Summary

The book proceeds in three sections, moving by analysis from common moral knowledge to its supreme principle, and then back to ordinary moral experience. Section I argues that nothing is good without qualification except a good will, and that an action has moral worth when done from duty rather than inclination. Section II develops the categorical imperative, in contrast to merely hypothetical imperatives, through its main formulations: the universal law formula ("act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it become a universal law"); the humanity formula (treat humanity, in one's own person or another's, always at the same time as an end and never merely as a means); and the formulas of autonomy and the kingdom of ends. Section III attempts to show that the moral law binds us because we are free, members of an intelligible world — its argument about the deduction of freedom has been debated ever since.

Influence

The Groundwork founded deontological ethics as a modern research programme and remains the standard entry point to Kant's ethics, usually read before the Critique of Practical Reason (1788) and the Metaphysics of Morals (1797). Commentators from H. J. Paton to Christine Korsgaard and Onora O'Neill have elaborated its arguments about dignity, autonomy and respect for persons.
