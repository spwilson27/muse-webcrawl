Chapter 13

Chapter 13 bankruptcy
Codification11 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1330
Chapter titleAdjustment of Debts of an Individual with Regular Income
JurisdictionUnited States federal courts

Chapter 13 of the United States Bankruptcy Code, codified at 11 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1330, allows an individual with regular income to develop a plan to repay all or part of their debts. Under Chapter 13, debtors propose a repayment plan to make installments to creditors over three to five years. If the debtor's current monthly income is less than the applicable state median, the plan will be for three years unless the court approves a longer period for cause. If the debtor's monthly income is greater than the median, the plan generally must be for five years.

Chapter 13 is sometimes called a "wage earner's plan." Unlike Chapter 7 liquidation, Chapter 13 enables debtors to keep property — including a home with a mortgage in arrears — while paying creditors in full or in part over time, according to a court-confirmed plan.

Eligibility

Only individuals, not corporations or partnerships, may file under Chapter 13, and the debtor must have regular income sufficient to fund a plan. Secured and unsecured debts must fall below statutory limits set by 11 U.S.C. § 109(e), which are adjusted periodically for inflation. A debtor who had a prior bankruptcy case dismissed within the preceding 180 days for certain reasons, or who has not received credit counseling from an approved agency within 180 days before filing, may be ineligible.

The repayment plan

The debtor files a plan that must pay priority claims in full, provide that secured creditors retain their liens, and give unsecured creditors at least as much as they would receive in a Chapter 7 liquidation (the "best interests of creditors" test). A Chapter 13 trustee, appointed by the United States Trustee, evaluates the case and distributes payments to creditors. Debtors whose income exceeds the state median must pass a means test similar to the one used in Chapter 7 cases to determine the amount that must be paid to unsecured creditors.

Automatic stay and the co-debtor stay

Filing a Chapter 13 petition triggers the automatic stay, halting most collection actions, foreclosures, and lawsuits. Chapter 13 also contains a special co-debtor stay under § 1301 that protects individuals who are jointly liable with the debtor on consumer debts — a protection not available in Chapter 7.

Discharge

Upon completion of payments under the plan, the debtor receives a discharge that is somewhat broader than the Chapter 7 discharge, historically releasing certain debts such as some tax penalties and debts arising from property settlements in divorce (a distinction narrowed by the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005). Some debts, including most student loans, recent taxes, child support, and alimony, are not dischargeable.
