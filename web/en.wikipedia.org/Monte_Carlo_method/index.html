Monte Carlo method

Monte Carlo methods, or Monte Carlo experiments, are a broad class of computational algorithms that rely on repeated random sampling to obtain numerical results. The underlying concept is to use randomness to solve problems that might be deterministic in principle. They are often used in physical and mathematical problems and are most useful when it is difficult or impossible to use other approaches. Monte Carlo methods are mainly used in three problem classes: optimization, numerical integration, and generating draws from a probability distribution.

The name refers to the Monte Carlo Casino in Monaco, and was coined in the 1940s by scientists working on the Manhattan Project, including Stanislaw Ulam, John von Neumann, and Nicholas Metropolis.

History

Early examples include Buffon's needle experiment in the 18th century for estimating π. The modern method originated with Ulam, who, while recovering from brain surgery in 1946, wondered about the probability that a game of solitaire would come out successfully and realized that sampling many games on a computer could estimate it. Von Neumann developed the idea for neutron diffusion problems at Los Alamos, and the first published account appeared in 1949 by Metropolis and Ulam.

Method

A Monte Carlo simulation generally involves: defining a domain of possible inputs; generating inputs randomly from a probability distribution over the domain; performing a deterministic computation on the inputs; and aggregating the results. By the law of large numbers, the average of many samples converges to the expected value; the error typically decreases in proportion to 1/√N, where N is the number of samples, regardless of the dimension of the problem — a property that makes Monte Carlo invaluable for high-dimensional integrals.

Applications

  • Physics — particle transport, statistical mechanics (Metropolis algorithm, Ising model), quantum Monte Carlo.
  • Finance — pricing derivatives, portfolio risk analysis, and Value at Risk calculations.
  • Engineering — reliability analysis and tolerance analysis.
  • Computer graphics — path tracing renders images by Monte Carlo integration of light transport.
  • Bayesian statistics — Markov chain Monte Carlo methods such as the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm and Gibbs sampling.
  • Artificial intelligence — Monte Carlo tree search, used in game-playing programs such as AlphaGo.

Limitations

Monte Carlo methods converge slowly compared with specialized deterministic methods for low-dimensional problems, and their accuracy depends on the quality of the pseudorandom number generator. Variance reduction techniques — including importance sampling, stratified sampling, control variates, and antithetic variates — can greatly improve efficiency.
