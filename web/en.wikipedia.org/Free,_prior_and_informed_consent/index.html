Free, prior and informed consent

Free, prior and informed consent (FPIC) is a principle in international law and human rights practice holding that indigenous peoples and local communities have the right to give or withhold consent to projects, developments, or policies that may affect them or their territories. Each element matters: consent should be given freely without coercion or manipulation, prior to the start of activities and with enough time for deliberation, informed by full and understandable disclosure of impacts, and as genuine consent through the community's own representative institutions and decision-making processes.

Legal basis

FPIC is most prominently articulated in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (2007), which calls on states to consult and obtain consent before adopting measures affecting indigenous peoples, before approving projects affecting their lands and resources, and before relocating them from their territories. It also appears in ILO Indigenous and Tribal Peoples Convention No. 169, in guidance under the Convention on Biological Diversity, and in the safeguard policies of international finance institutions and voluntary certification schemes such as the Forest Stewardship Council and the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil.

Debates

The precise legal force of FPIC is debated: states and companies often implement it as a duty of good-faith consultation, while indigenous advocates argue it includes a right to say no. Practical questions — who speaks for a community, how consent is documented, and whether it can be revisited — remain active areas of policy development.
