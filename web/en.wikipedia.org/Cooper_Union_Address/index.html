Cooper Union speech

The Cooper Union speech or Cooper Union Address was delivered by Abraham Lincoln on February 27, 1860, at Cooper Union in New York City. One of his most important speeches, it secured his nomination for the presidency in the election later that year.

Background

Lincoln, a former one-term congressman from Illinois who had gained national attention through his 1858 debates with Stephen A. Douglas, was invited to speak to prominent New York Republicans, including figures such as William Cullen Bryant and Horace Greeley. The address was held in the Great Hall of the Cooper Union, a center of intellectual life in the city.

Argument

In a carefully researched, lawyerly argument, Lincoln examined the record of the thirty-nine signers of the United States Constitution and their later votes on federal regulation of slavery in the territories. He argued that a majority of the Founders had opposed the extension of slavery and had expected federal power to restrict it. He addressed three audiences: the Democrats, the Southerners, and the Republicans, urging Republicans to stand firm against the spread of slavery while reassuring the South that a Republican government would not interfere with slavery where it already existed.

His closing line became famous: "Let us have faith that right makes might, and in that faith, let us, to the end, dare to do our duty as we understand it."

Impact

The speech was widely published in newspapers and pamphlets and established Lincoln as a formidable candidate acceptable to Eastern Republicans. Many historians regard it as a turning point that made his presidency possible.
