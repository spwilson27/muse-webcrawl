Hemolysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hemolysis (from Greek αἷμα, haima, "blood" + λύσις, lysis, "loosening") is the rupture or destruction of red blood cells (erythrocytes), releasing their contents — principally hemoglobin — into the surrounding fluid. Hemolysis occurring inside the body is called in vivo; it may be a normal part of red cell turnover or a pathological process causing hemolytic anaemia. Hemolysis in a collected blood sample (in vitro) is a common laboratory artefact that can distort test results.

In vivo hemolysis

Red blood cells normally live about 120 days before being removed by macrophages in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow — a process called extravascular hemolysis. When destruction is accelerated, the marrow may compensate; if it cannot, anaemia results, accompanied by jaundice from excess bilirubin (a breakdown product of heme), raised lactate dehydrogenase, reduced haptoglobin, and reticulocytosis. Intravascular hemolysis, in which cells burst within the circulation, releases free hemoglobin that binds haptoglobin and can spill into the urine.

Causes

  • Intrinsic red cell defects — membrane disorders such as hereditary spherocytosis, enzyme defects such as G6PD deficiency, and hemoglobin disorders such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia.
  • Immune destruction — autoimmune hemolytic anaemia, haemolytic disease of the newborn, and incompatible blood transfusion reactions.
  • Extrinsic factors — infections (malaria parasites rupture red cells from within), toxins and venoms, mechanical trauma from prosthetic heart valves or marching, hypersplenism, and certain drugs.

In microbiology

On blood agar plates, bacteria are classically classified by their hemolytic behaviour: alpha-hemolysis (partial, greenish discolouration, as with Streptococcus pneumoniae), beta-hemolysis (complete clearing, as with Streptococcus pyogenes), and gamma-hemolysis (no hemolysis). The clearing is produced by bacterial exotoxins called hemolysins.

In vitro hemolysis

Blood samples can hemolyse during collection, transport, or storage — through rough handling, extreme temperatures, or prolonged tourniquet use. Because red cells are rich in potassium and certain enzymes, hemolysed samples falsely elevate measured potassium and other analytes, and specimens are frequently rejected and redrawn.
