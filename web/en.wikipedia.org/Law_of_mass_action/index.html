Law of mass action

In chemistry, the law of mass action is the proposition that the rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the product of the activities or concentrations of the reactants. It explains and predicts the behaviour of solutions in dynamic equilibrium. The law was first formulated by the Norwegian chemists Cato Maximilian Guldberg and Peter Waage in papers published in 1864 and 1867.

Formulation

For a general chemical reaction

αA + βB ⇌ σS + τT

the rate of the forward reaction is proportional to [A]α[B]β, and the rate of the reverse reaction is proportional to [S]σ[T]τ. At equilibrium the two rates are equal, which gives the equilibrium constant

K = ([S]σ[T]τ) / ([A]α[B]β)

where the bracketed quantities are the equilibrium concentrations (or, more precisely, activities) of the chemical species.

History

Guldberg and Waage built on earlier ideas of Claude Louis Berthollet, who had recognised that chemical reactions are not always complete. Their work introduced the concept of "active mass", roughly the concentration of a substance, and showed that equilibrium is a dynamic state rather than a static one. The law was later placed on a firm thermodynamic footing through the work of Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff, who connected the equilibrium constant to the standard Gibbs free energy change of the reaction.

Applications

The law of mass action underlies the quantitative treatment of acid–base equilibria, solubility products, complex-ion formation, and the analysis of biochemical binding, such as the binding of oxygen to haemoglobin and the kinetics described by the Hill equation. In semiconductor physics, an analogous mass action law relates the concentrations of electrons and holes to the intrinsic carrier concentration.
