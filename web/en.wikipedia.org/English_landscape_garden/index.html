The English landscape garden, also called the English landscape park or simply the English garden (jardin à l'anglaise), is a style of landscape design that emerged in England in the early 18th century and spread across Europe, replacing the formal, symmetrical French baroque garden of parterres and avenues. It presented an idealised, naturalistic view of nature — sweeping lawns, gently rolling ground, serpentine lakes, artfully grouped trees, and classical temples or picturesque follies — inspired by landscape painting, especially the works of Claude Lorrain and Nicolas Poussin.

Development

Early pioneers included Charles Bridgeman and William Kent, whose gardens at Stowe, Chiswick House and Rousham introduced temples, grottoes and the sunken fence or ha-ha that made the garden seem to merge with the countryside. Kent's younger contemporary Lancelot "Capability" Brown simplified and enlarged the style across more than 170 estates — including Blenheim Palace, Chatsworth and Petworth — creating vast smooth parkland with belts and clumps of trees and large artificial lakes. Humphry Repton, Brown's successor, reintroduced terraces and flower gardens near the house. In the later 18th century, theorists such as William Gilpin developed the related idea of the Picturesque, favouring rougher, wilder scenery.

Influence

The English garden reshaped parks across Europe and North America, influenced public parks of the 19th century (such as New York's Central Park), and was itself influenced by an idealised image of Chinese garden design, reflected in the term Sharawadgi and in features such as pagodas.
