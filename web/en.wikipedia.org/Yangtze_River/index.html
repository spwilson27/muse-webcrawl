Yangtze River

The Yangtze River (長江, Cháng Jiāng) is the longest river in Asia and the third-longest in the world, flowing 6,300 km (3,915 mi) from the glaciers of the Tibetan Plateau eastward through China to the East China Sea near Shanghai. In Chinese it is generally known simply as the "Long River"; the name "Yangtze" originally applied only to its lower reaches.

Course

The river's headwaters — the Tuotuo and Dangqu — rise on the Tibetan Plateau. As the Jinsha ("Golden Sands") it plunges through the gorges of Yunnan and Sichuan, including Tiger Leaping Gorge, then becomes the Yangtze proper at Yibin. It passes Chongqing, cuts the famous Three Gorges (Qutang, Wu, and Xiling), and is dammed at the Three Gorges Dam, the world's largest hydroelectric station. Below Yichang it meanders across the lakes and plains of Hubei, Anhui, and Jiangsu — the historic flood-prone "land of fish and rice" — past Wuhan and Nanjing to its vast delta at Shanghai.

Significance

The Yangtze basin is home to more than 400 million people and produces a large share of China's grain, cotton, and industrial output; the river carries more freight than any other inland waterway in the world. Its floods have been among history's deadliest natural disasters (notably 1931 and 1954), driving millennia of dike building and modern megaprojects. Ecologically, the river supported the baiji river dolphin (declared functionally extinct in 2006), the Chinese paddlefish (declared extinct in 2022), and the finless porpoise; a ten-year fishing ban was imposed in 2021 to aid recovery.
