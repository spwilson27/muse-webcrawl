Neural tube defects (NTDs) are a group of congenital conditions in which the embryonic neural tube — the structure that becomes the brain and spinal cord — fails to close completely during the third and fourth weeks of development. They are among the most common serious birth defects, affecting roughly 1 in every 1,000–3,000 births worldwide, with rates varying greatly by region.

Types

In spina bifida, the spinal cord and vertebrae do not close fully; the most severe form, myelomeningocele, leaves the cord exposed and causes paralysis and bladder and bowel problems below the lesion, often with the Chiari II malformation and hydrocephalus. Anencephaly is the absence of a major part of the brain and skull and is invariably fatal at or shortly after birth. Encephalocele is a protrusion of brain tissue through an opening in the skull, and the rare iniencephaly combines spinal defects with extreme backward bending of the head.

Causes and prevention

The strongest established risk factor is insufficient folate (vitamin B9) around conception; certain anti-seizure medicines (especially valproate), maternal diabetes, obesity, and high body temperature in early pregnancy also raise risk, and most cases have a genetic contribution. Randomised trials in the early 1990s proved that folic acid supplements taken before conception and in the first weeks of pregnancy prevent a large majority of cases. Many countries now fortify staple foods such as flour with folic acid, and women of childbearing age are advised to take 400 micrograms daily — more for those at high risk.

Diagnosis and treatment

NTDs are usually detected by prenatal ultrasound and by raised alpha-fetoprotein in maternal blood. Treatment depends on severity: spina bifida is managed with surgery shortly after birth (and, in selected cases, in-utero surgery, shown in the 2011 MOMS trial to improve outcomes), shunting for hydrocephalus, and lifelong multidisciplinary care.
