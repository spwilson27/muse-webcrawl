Acetate

An acetate is a salt formed by the combination of acetic acid with a base (e.g. alkaline, earthy, metallic, nonmetallic or radical base). "Acetate" also describes the conjugate base or ion (specifically, the negatively charged ion called an anion) typically found in aqueous solution and written with the chemical formula C2H3O2−. The neutral molecules formed by the combination of the acetate ion and a positive ion (cation) are also commonly called "acetates". The simplest of these is hydrogen acetate (called acetic acid) with the formula CH3CO2H.

Structure

The acetate anion, [CH3COO]−, consists of a methyl group bonded to a carboxylate group; the negative charge is delocalised over the two oxygen atoms by resonance. Acetate is the conjugate base of acetic acid, a weak acid with pKa ≈ 4.76. Acetate salts are generally soluble in water and many are used as buffers, mordants, and food additives.

Occurrence and uses

Acetate is a common anion in biology: acetyl-CoA is the principal form in which acetate is used by organisms, and acetate is a product of fermentation in many microorganisms. Industrially, large quantities of acetate esters and salts are produced. Cellulose acetate is used in photographic film and textiles; sodium acetate is used in heating pads and as a food flavouring (E262); calcium acetate and potassium acetate have medical uses; and metal acetates are used in printing (as mordants), as catalysts, and in pigments such as verdigris (copper acetate).
