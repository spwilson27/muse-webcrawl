Ad hominem

Latinargumentum ad hominem — "argument to the person"
TypeRhetorical strategy; often classified as a logical fallacy

Ad hominem (Latin for "to the person") describes a rhetorical strategy in which an argument is countered by attacking the character, motive or other attribute of the person making it, rather than addressing the substance of the argument itself. In logic, the term is often classified as a fallacy of relevance, though philosophers note that not every appeal to a person's circumstances is fallacious.

Types

Common variants include the abusive ad hominem (direct insult or attack on character), the circumstantial ad hominem (alleging that the speaker's circumstances or interests bias the argument), tu quoque ("you too" — dismissing a claim because the speaker is inconsistent or hypocritical), guilt by association, and poisoning the well (pre-emptively discrediting a speaker before they argue).

Analysis

John Locke introduced the term argumentum ad hominem in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689), though in his usage it meant pressing an opponent with their own concessions — a legitimate dialectical move. Modern argumentation theory distinguishes between ad hominem attacks that are fallacious and cases where a person's credibility is genuinely at issue, such as assessing the testimony of a witness or a conflict of interest.
