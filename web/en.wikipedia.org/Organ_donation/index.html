Organ donation

Organ donation is the process by which a person allows an organ of their own body to be removed and transplanted to another person, legally, either by consent while the donor is alive or after death with the assent of the next of kin. Donated organs include the heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, pancreas and intestines; tissues such as corneas, skin, bone, heart valves and blood vessels can also be donated.

Types of donation

Living donation most commonly involves one kidney or a portion of the liver, which regenerates. Deceased donation takes place after brain death or circulatory death has been declared, and a single deceased donor can save up to eight lives. Transplantation depends on matching blood type and tissue characteristics and on cold ischaemia time — the hours an organ remains viable outside the body.

Consent systems

Countries operate either opt-in systems, where individuals register as donors, or opt-out (presumed consent) systems, where donation is authorised unless the person recorded an objection. Spain, with an opt-out law combined with a strong coordinator network, has long had the world's highest deceased-donation rates.

Ethics and law

Organ donation raises ethical questions about the definition of death, allocation fairness and the prohibition of organ trade, which is banned in most countries under laws modelled on the Declaration of Istanbul. Waiting lists in many countries greatly exceed the supply of organs, and thousands of patients die each year while waiting.
