Digital photography uses cameras containing arrays of electronic photodetectors — image sensors — to produce images focused by a lens, as opposed to an exposure on photographic film. The captured images are digitised and stored as computer files, ready for further digital processing, viewing, electronic publishing, or digital printing.

History

The charge-coupled device (CCD) was invented in 1969 at Bell Labs by Willard Boyle and George E. Smith. In 1975, Kodak engineer Steven Sasson built the first self-contained digital camera, which weighed 3.6 kg and recorded 0.01-megapixel black-and-white images to cassette tape. Commercial digital cameras appeared in the early 1990s; by the mid-2000s digital cameras had largely replaced film for most users, and camera phones — beginning with models around 2000 — made digital photography ubiquitous.

Technology

Modern sensors are usually CMOS devices with a Bayer colour filter array. Key parameters include resolution (megapixels), sensor size, sensitivity (ISO), and dynamic range. Digital files may be lossy-compressed (JPEG) or raw sensor data preserved in manufacturer RAW formats. Computational photography in smartphones combines multiple exposures for HDR, night modes, and portrait effects.

Impact

Instant review, negligible marginal cost per shot, and easy online sharing transformed photography's practice and economics, contributing to the decline of film manufacturers and photo-finishing businesses and to the rise of photo-sharing platforms.
