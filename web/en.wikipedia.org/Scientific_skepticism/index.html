Scientific skepticism

Scientific skeptics test extraordinary claims using controlled investigation.

Scientific skepticism is a philosophical and practical position that questions the veracity of claims lacking empirical evidence and subjects them to systematic investigation using the scientific method. Scientific skeptics evaluate claims about the paranormal, pseudoscience, alternative medicine, and conspiracy theories by the standards of evidence used in science, emphasising that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence.

History

The modern skeptical movement coalesced in the 1970s. In 1976, philosopher Paul Kurtz and others founded the Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal (CSICOP, now the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry), which publishes the magazine Skeptical Inquirer. Magician and investigator James Randi became the movement's best-known figure through his public debunking of fraudulent psychics and his long-standing million-dollar challenge for proof of paranormal abilities.

Methods and topics

Skeptics apply critical thinking, controlled experiments, and knowledge of cognitive biases and logical fallacies to evaluate claims. Common subjects include astrology, homeopathy, cryptozoology, UFO claims, and faith healing. Carl Sagan's book The Demon-Haunted World (1995) is a widely read statement of the skeptical outlook.

Criticism

Critics sometimes characterise skepticism as closed-minded or scientistic; proponents reply that scientific skepticism is not the denial of claims but the insistence that they be tested, and that conclusions remain provisional.
