Pennine Way

Length268 mi (431 km)
LocationNorthern England and southern Scotland
TrailheadsEdale, Derbyshire — Kirk Yetholm, Scottish Borders
EstablishedOpened 24 April 1965
Highest pointCross Fell, 893 m

The Pennine Way is a National Trail in England, running 268 miles (431 km) from Edale in the Peak District of Derbyshire northwards through the Yorkshire Dales and Northumberland National Park, across the Cheviot Hills, to Kirk Yetholm just inside the Scottish border. It follows the Pennines, the "backbone of England", and is widely regarded as Britain's toughest and most famous long-distance footpath.

History

The route was proposed by journalist and rambler Tom Stephenson in a 1935 article in the Daily Herald, inspired by long-distance trails in the United States such as the Appalachian Trail. The campaign for the trail was bound up with the movement for public access to moorland. The Pennine Way was finally opened on 24 April 1965 at a ceremony on Malham Moor, becoming Britain's first National Trail.

Route

Highlights along the way include Kinder Scout, Malham Cove, Pen-y-ghent, Tan Hill (with England's highest pub), High Force waterfall on the Tees, High Cup Nick, Cross Fell — the highest point of the Pennines — Hadrian's Wall, which the trail follows for a stretch, and the Cheviot Hills. Much of the terrain is remote, exposed upland bog, and the route crosses few settlements; in its early decades the bog sections became notoriously eroded and muddy, prompting extensive flagstone paving of the worst stretches.

Walking the trail

Most walkers take 15 to 20 days to complete the full route. A popular guide with hand-drawn maps was written by Alfred Wainwright, who offered a halfpenny (later £1) to every finisher out of his own pocket. The annual Spine Race, a winter ultramarathon along the trail, is billed as one of the world's toughest endurance races.
