The MMR vaccine is a live attenuated vaccine against measles, mumps, and rubella (German measles). It is a mixture of live weakened viruses of the three diseases, administered by injection. Developed under the direction of Maurice Hilleman at Merck, the combined vaccine was first licensed in 1971; an improved formulation, M-M-R II, using the RA 27/3 rubella strain and human diploid cell production, followed in 1979 and became the standard product in many countries.

The MMR vaccine is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. Two doses are about 97% effective at preventing measles. Widespread immunisation with MMR has reduced measles deaths worldwide by roughly four-fifths since 2000 and eliminated endemic rubella and congenital rubella syndrome from the Americas.

Use and schedule

Most national schedules give the first dose at 12–15 months of age and a second dose at 4–6 years (the United Kingdom gives the second at 3 years 4 months). The vaccine is also recommended for non-immune adults, healthcare workers, travellers and women of childbearing age (with pregnancy avoided for a month after vaccination, since it is a live vaccine). An MMRV version adds varicella (chickenpox) vaccine. Because it contains live viruses, MMR is contraindicated in people with significant immunosuppression and during pregnancy.

Safety

Common side effects are mild: fever, a mild rash, and transient joint pain (chiefly in adult women, from the rubella component). A febrile seizure occurs in roughly 1 in 3,000–4,000 doses, without lasting harm. Serious allergic reactions occur in about one per million doses. A small risk of immune thrombocytopenic purpura (about 1 in 30,000–40,000 doses) has been identified. Extensive studies involving millions of children have found no association with inflammatory bowel disease, encephalitis from the vaccine strains in healthy children, or autism.

The MMR vaccine controversy

In 1998 Andrew Wakefield and colleagues published a paper in The Lancet suggesting a link between MMR, bowel disease and autism. The paper was shown to be fraudulent — based on manipulated data and undisclosed financial conflicts — was retracted in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register. Large epidemiological studies in Denmark, Japan, the UK, the US and elsewhere have repeatedly found no link. Nevertheless, the scare drove UK MMR coverage below 80% in the early 2000s, endemic measles returned, and vaccine hesitancy amplified by the episode contributed to measles resurgences in Europe and North America in the 2010s. The WHO named vaccine hesitancy one of the top ten threats to global health in 2019.

History

The component vaccines were developed in the 1960s: John Enders's measles strain (Edmonston B, later Moraten), Hilleman's mumps vaccine (from his daughter Jeryl Lynn's throat swab), and rubella vaccines (HPV-77 and then Stanley Plotkin's RA 27/3). Hilleman combined them into MMR in 1971. Japan used its own Urabe-strain mumps component until 1993, when it was withdrawn over aseptic meningitis rates; Japan subsequently left the combined programme, relying on separate measles–rubella vaccine with optional mumps vaccination.
