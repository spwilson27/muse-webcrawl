Ugali

Ugali, also known as posho, nsima, sadza, pap and by many other local names, is a type of stiff porridge or dough made from maize flour (cornmeal) and water. It is a staple food across much of Africa — in East Africa (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda), Southern Africa (Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, South Africa) and parts of West Africa — eaten with vegetables, meat, fish, beans or sauces.

Preparation

Maize flour is gradually stirred into boiling water and cooked, with vigorous stirring using a wooden spoon, until it forms a firm, smooth mass that holds its shape. The consistency varies by region and household, from a soft porridge to a very stiff dough. Traditionally the flour was made from millet or sorghum; maize, introduced to Africa from the Americas in the Columbian exchange, became dominant because of its yield, though millet, sorghum and cassava versions persist.

Regional names and variants

  • Kenya and Tanzania: ugali, commonly served with sukuma wiki (collard greens), nyama choma (grilled meat), fish or bean stew.
  • Malawi and Zambia: nsima (nshima), regarded as the national dish and centre of the meal, with relishes called ndiwo.
  • Zimbabwe: sadza, eaten with muriwo (greens), meat or sour milk.
  • South Africa: pap (including the crumbly phuthu and smooth stywe pap), often served at braais with chakalaka and boerewors.
  • Uganda: posho, a staple in schools and institutions as well as homes.
  • Ghana: similar maize doughs include banku (fermented) and kenkey.

Cultural significance

Ugali and its counterparts are typically eaten by hand: a piece is pinched off, rolled into a ball, indented with the thumb to form a scoop, and used to pick up the accompanying relish. Because maize meal is inexpensive and filling, the dish is a cornerstone of food security in the region, and its price and availability are politically sensitive. In Kenya, government subsidies on maize flour ("unga") have been a recurring policy issue.

Nutrition

Ugali is primarily a source of carbohydrate and energy. Refined white maize flour has relatively little fibre and micronutrients; the nutritional quality of the meal depends heavily on the accompanying relishes. Whole-grain and fortified flours, and traditional millet or sorghum versions, offer more fibre and nutrients.
