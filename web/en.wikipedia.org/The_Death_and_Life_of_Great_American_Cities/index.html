The Death and Life of Great American Cities is a 1961 book by writer and activist Jane Jacobs. A sustained critique of the urban planning and renewal policies of the 1950s, the book argues that the modernist planning orthodoxy of the postwar era — championed by figures such as Robert Moses in New York — was destroying the vitality, safety, and economic health of American city neighborhoods. It is widely regarded as one of the most influential books ever written about cities.

Background

Jacobs wrote the book while living in Greenwich Village, New York, where she had become involved in campaigns against urban renewal schemes and a proposed expressway through Lower Manhattan. She had earlier worked as an associate editor at Architectural Forum, where she developed a skepticism toward the top-down planning doctrines then dominant. A grant from the Rockefeller Foundation allowed her to research and complete the book.

Main arguments

The book's central claim is that a city's safety and prosperity arise from the intricate, self-organizing order of its streets and neighborhoods. Jacobs famously described the "ballet of the sidewalk" — the complex daily choreography of residents, shopkeepers, and passersby that produces informal social control and a sense of community.

She argued that successful districts require four generators of diversity: mixed primary uses that bring people onto the streets at different times of day; short blocks that multiply routes and corners; a mixture of buildings of varying age and condition that can support enterprises of different rent levels; and a sufficient density of people. Her phrase "eyes on the street" captured the idea that natural proprietors — residents and shopkeepers watching the sidewalk — are the foundation of public safety.

Critique of orthodox planning

Jacobs attacked the intellectual foundations of modernist planning, from the Garden City movement of Ebenezer Howard to the Radiant City ideas of Le Corbusier, which she saw as anti-urban in spirit. She criticized urban renewal for clearing functioning neighborhoods, displacing the poor, and replacing the fine-grained street economy with sterile towers, shopping centers, and expressways.

Legacy

The book helped fuel the preservation and neighborhood movements of the 1960s and 1970s, reshaped the teaching of urban planning, and anticipated later movements such as New Urbanism. Although some of its claims have been debated — critics question its treatment of density, gentrification, and large-scale infrastructure — it remains a foundational text of urban studies.
