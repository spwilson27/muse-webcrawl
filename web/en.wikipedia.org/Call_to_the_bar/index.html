Call to the bar

The call to the bar is a legal term of art in most common law jurisdictions where persons must be qualified to be allowed to argue in court on behalf of another party. In England and Wales and in many Commonwealth countries, it refers to the formal ceremony by which a student member of one of the Inns of Court becomes a barrister, entitled to practise at the Bar.

England and Wales

Historically, barristers were called to the bar after keeping terms at one of the four Inns of Court — Gray's Inn, Lincoln's Inn, the Inner Temple, and the Middle Temple — which hold the exclusive right to call students to the bar. In modern practice, an aspiring barrister must join an Inn, pass the Bar course (formerly the Bar Professional Training Course), and complete the required qualifying sessions before being called, typically at a ceremony conducted by the Inn's benchers. Calls take place on set call days several times a year. After call, a barrister must complete pupillage — a period of practical training — before holding a full practising certificate.

Other jurisdictions

Variations of the ceremony exist throughout the Commonwealth. In Canada, lawyers in most provinces are called to the bar by their provincial law society after articling and passing bar examinations; in Ontario the ceremony is conducted by the Law Society of Ontario. In some jurisdictions the expression is used simply for admission to legal practice as a whole, whether or not the profession is divided into barristers and solicitors. The phrase derives from the physical bar of the courtroom — and, at the Inns, the barrier separating the benchers' table — which students crossed upon admission.

Historical notes

The call to the bar was traditionally recorded in the Inn's "call book," and degrees of call (such as call to the degree of the coif for serjeants) formed part of the old hierarchy of the legal profession. Women were first called to the bar in England in 1919–1920 following the Sex Disqualification (Removal) Act 1919.
