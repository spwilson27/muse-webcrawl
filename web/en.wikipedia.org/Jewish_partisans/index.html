Jewish partisans

Jewish partisans were fighters in irregular military groups participating in the Jewish resistance movement against Nazi Germany and its collaborators during World War II. An estimated 20,000 to 30,000 Jews fought in partisan units across Eastern Europe, in the forests of Belarus, Lithuania, Poland and Ukraine.


Jewish partisans in the forest

Background

Most Jewish partisans were escapees from ghettos or labour camps, or survivors of massacres. Lacking weapons, training and often the support of the surrounding population, they faced severe difficulties, and many partisan units and local peasants were themselves antisemitic. Some Jews formed independent units; others joined Soviet partisan formations.

Activities

Jewish partisans sabotaged railways and supply lines, attacked German garrisons and collaborators, and gathered intelligence. Family camps, such as the one led by Tuvia Bielski in the Naliboki forest of Belarus, sheltered more than 1,200 Jews — mostly noncombatants, including women, children and the elderly — making rescue itself an act of resistance. The Fareynikte Partizaner Organizatsye (United Partisan Organisation) of the Vilna Ghetto, co-led by Abba Kovner, smuggled fighters out to the forests after the ghetto's liquidation.

Legacy

The partisans' story counters the myth that Jews did not resist. Their experiences are commemorated in museums and memorials, and the Bielski story was dramatised in the 2008 film Defiance.
