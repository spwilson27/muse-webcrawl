Temple of Mithras, London

The Temple of Mithras, London, also known as the London Mithraeum, is a Roman mithraeum discovered in 1954 at Walbrook, in the City of London, during excavation work for a new building. The temple had been constructed in the 3rd century AD and dedicated to the god Mithras, a deity popular among Roman soldiers. The whole site was relocated in 1962 to allow construction, before being returned close to its original location in 2017 as an immersive museum beneath the European headquarters of Bloomberg.

Discovery and finds

Excavations directed by W. F. Grimes of the Museum of London drew enormous public interest; hundreds of thousands of people visited the site. Finds included a marble head of Mithras, sculptures of Minerva and Serapis, and a group depicting Mithras slaying the bull (the tauroctony), now in the Museum of London. The temple measured about 18 by 8 metres, with a sunken floor, raised benches along the sides for ritual dining, and an apse at the west end.

The Bloomberg Mithraeum

In 2010 Bloomberg acquired the site and committed to restoring the temple to its original position, seven metres below modern street level, within a purpose-built exhibition space. It opened in November 2017 alongside displays of hundreds of Roman artefacts recovered from the site, including the earliest surviving written document from Britain, a financial tablet dated to AD 57.
