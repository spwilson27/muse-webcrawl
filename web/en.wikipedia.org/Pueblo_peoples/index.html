The Pueblo peoples are Native American peoples of the Southwestern United States, descended from the Ancestral Puebloans and named by Spanish colonisers for their distinctive multi-storey adobe and stone towns (pueblos, Spanish for "villages"). Today there are 19 federally recognised Pueblos in New Mexico — including Acoma, Laguna, Taos, Santo Domingo (Kewa), and Zuni — along with the Hopi Tribe in Arizona and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo in Texas.

History

Pueblo culture developed over more than a millennium of dry-land farming of maize, beans, and squash in the Rio Grande valley and the Colorado Plateau, building on the cliff dwellings and great houses of Chaco Canyon and Mesa Verde. Spanish colonisation from 1598 brought missions, forced labour, and suppression of religion, provoking the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, led by Popé of San Juan Pueblo, which expelled the Spanish for twelve years — the most successful Indigenous uprising in North American colonial history. Pueblo communities later resisted assimilation under Mexican and American rule, retaining communal land grants, theocratic village governments, and ceremonial life centred on kivas and katsina religion.

Culture

Pueblo peoples speak languages from four unrelated families: Keres, Tanoan, Zuni (an isolate), and Hopi. They are renowned for pottery traditions — notably the black-on-black ware of Maria Martinez of San Ildefonso — for jewellery, weaving, and feast-day dances open to or closed from outsiders by community decision.
