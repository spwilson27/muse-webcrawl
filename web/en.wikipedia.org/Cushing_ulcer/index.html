Cushing ulcer

A Cushing ulcer, named after the neurosurgeon Harvey Cushing, is a type of stress ulcer associated with elevated intracranial pressure. It is a gastric or duodenal ulcer that develops following head injury, brain surgery, brain tumours, or other conditions that raise pressure inside the skull.

Mechanism

Raised intracranial pressure is thought to stimulate the vagus nerve nuclei in the brainstem. Increased vagal activity drives the parietal cells of the stomach to secrete excess gastric acid, which erodes the gastric and duodenal mucosa and produces ulceration. Unlike other stress ulcers, which involve mucosal ischaemia, the Cushing ulcer is primarily acid-driven and may occur in the oesophagus as well as the stomach and duodenum.

Clinical features

Cushing ulcers carry a higher risk of perforation than other types of peptic ulcer. Patients may present with gastrointestinal bleeding, haematemesis, or melaena after neurosurgery or traumatic brain injury. Because the patients are often critically ill, the ulcer may first be detected on endoscopy or at autopsy.

Treatment

Management centres on reducing gastric acid with proton pump inhibitors or histamine H2-receptor blockers, treating the underlying cause of raised intracranial pressure, and surgical intervention if perforation or uncontrolled bleeding occurs. Prophylactic acid suppression is commonly given to patients with severe head injuries.
