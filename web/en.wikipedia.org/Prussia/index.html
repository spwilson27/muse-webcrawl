Prussia (German: Preußen; Latin: Prussia, Borussia) was a German state that originated in the Baltic region and rose to prominence in the 18th and 19th centuries, eventually dominating northern Germany. Its name derives from the Old Prussians, a Baltic people conquered by the Teutonic Order in the 13th century. The Teutonic Order's monastic state was secularised in 1525 as the Duchy of Prussia; in 1618 it passed in personal union to the margraves of Brandenburg, and in 1701 Elector Frederick III crowned himself King in Prussia at Königsberg.

Rise of the Kingdom

Under Frederick William, the "Great Elector", Brandenburg-Prussia built a standing army and a centralised administration. His son Frederick I gained the royal title; Frederick William I, the "Soldier King", turned the army into one of Europe's finest instruments. Frederick II ("the Great") seized Silesia from Austria in the 1740s, survived the Seven Years' War, and made Prussia a great power, patronising the arts and philosophy while expanding its territory, including in the Partitions of Poland.

Reform and German unification

Crushed by Napoleon at Jena-Auerstedt in 1806, Prussia undertook sweeping reforms under Stein, Hardenberg and Scharnhorst, abolishing serfdom and modernising the army and bureaucracy. After the Congress of Vienna (1815) it gained the Rhineland, becoming Germany's western sentinel. Under Minister President Otto von Bismarck from 1862, Prussia defeated Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71), and in 1871 the Prussian king Wilhelm I was proclaimed German Emperor; Prussia became the largest and dominant state of the German Empire.

Free State and abolition

After the First World War Prussia became the Free State of Prussia within the Weimar Republic, governed for most of the interwar period by democratic coalitions. In 1932 Chancellor Franz von Papen deposed its government in the Preußenschlag; under the Nazis Prussia was subordinated to the Reich. After the Second World War its eastern territories were transferred to Poland and the USSR, and on 25 February 1947 the Allied Control Council formally abolished the Prussian state by Law No. 46, declaring it defunct.

Legacy

Prussia left a complex legacy: admired for its efficient administration, religious toleration, rule of law and educational system, and criticised for its militarism and authoritarian traditions. The adjective "Prussian" became associated with discipline, order and frugality, and Prussian institutions shaped the development of modern Germany.
