The Battle of Fulford was fought on 20 September 1066 at Fulford, near York, in the north of England. It was the first of three battles fought that autumn for the English throne. An invading Norwegian army under King Harald Hardrada, accompanied by the English king's exiled brother Tostig Godwinson, defeated the northern English earls Edwin of Mercia and Morcar of Northumbria. The victory cleared the way for the Norwegians to occupy York, but the invaders were themselves destroyed five days later at the Battle of Stamford Bridge.

Background

After the death of Edward the Confessor in January 1066, Harold Godwinson was crowned king of England. His brother Tostig, who had been deposed as Earl of Northumbria in 1065, sought allies abroad and joined Harald Hardrada of Norway, who claimed the English crown through an alleged agreement between his predecessor Magnus the Good and Harthacnut. In September 1066 the Norwegian fleet of some 300 ships sailed up the River Ouse toward York.

The battle

The northern earls, Edwin and Morcar, marched out of York and took up a position on the south bank of the Ouse near Fulford, with the river on one flank and marshy ground on the other. The English attacked first and initially pushed back the Norwegian right wing. Harald, however, fed reinforcements along the line and launched a counter-attack that outflanked the English position from the higher ground. The English army broke; many men drowned trying to cross the river or were cut down in the marshes. According to later tradition, the dead were so numerous that the Norwegians could cross the bog dry-shod on their bodies.

Aftermath

York surrendered to the Norwegians, who occupied the city and took hostages. Harald then withdrew eastward to Stamford Bridge to await supplies and hostages from the surrounding shires. Meanwhile King Harold, hearing of the defeat, force-marched his army north from London in four days and surprised the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, where both Harald and Tostig were killed and the invasion destroyed. The costly northern campaign left Harold's army exhausted shortly before it had to face William of Normandy's invasion in the south, ending at the Battle of Hastings on 14 October 1066.
