Economic growth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Economic growth is the increase in the inflation-adjusted market value of the goods and services produced by an economy over time, conventionally measured as the percent rate of increase in real gross domestic product (GDP). Growth is usually calculated in real terms to net out inflation, and often expressed per capita to account for population change.

Determinants

Economists distinguish growth from increases in capital, labour and land — driven by investment and population — from growth in total factor productivity, driven by technological progress, innovation and institutional quality. The Solow–Swan model treated productivity growth as exogenous, while endogenous growth theory, developed by Paul Romer and others, explains it through knowledge accumulation, human capital and incentives for research and development.

History

For most of human history, per-capita growth was close to zero. Sustained modern growth began with the Industrial Revolution in Britain in the late 18th century and spread unevenly; the resulting divergence in living standards is sometimes called the Great Divergence. In the 20th century, East Asian economies such as Japan, South Korea and later China achieved exceptionally rapid catch-up growth.

Measurement and criticism

GDP growth is criticised for ignoring income distribution, unpaid work, environmental damage and sustainability. Debate since the 1970s — from the Club of Rome's Limits to Growth to modern degrowth proposals — questions whether continuous material growth is possible or desirable on a finite planet, while others stress growth's role in poverty reduction and funding public services.
