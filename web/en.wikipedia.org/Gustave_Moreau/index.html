Gustave Moreau (6 April 1826 – 18 April 1898) was a French artist and an important figure in the Symbolist movement. His paintings, often depicting biblical and mythological figures in richly detailed, dreamlike settings, influenced a generation of younger artists.

Career

Moreau studied under François-Édouard Picot at the École des Beaux-Arts and spent several years in Italy copying Old Masters. His breakthrough came at the Salon of 1864 with Oedipus and the Sphinx. He became known for jewel-like, densely ornamented canvases such as Salome Dancing before Herod and The Apparition (1876), in which the vision of the severed head of John the Baptist appears to Salome.

Teaching and influence

Moreau was appointed professor at the École des Beaux-Arts in 1892 and proved an unusually liberal teacher; his pupils included Henri Matisse, Georges Rouault, and other future Fauves. The Decadent writer Joris-Karl Huysmans celebrated his work in the novel À rebours (1884), and the Symbolist poets admired his erudite, imaginative art.

Museum

Moreau bequeathed his house and studio, with some 15,000 works, to the French state. It opened in 1903 as the Musée Gustave Moreau in Paris, one of the first house-museums devoted to a single artist, preserving his vast unfinished canvases and watercolours.
