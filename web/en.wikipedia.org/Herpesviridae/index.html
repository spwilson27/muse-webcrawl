Herpesviridae is a large family of DNA viruses that cause diseases in animals, including humans. The name derives from the Greek herpein ("to creep"), referring to the spreading skin lesions characteristic of herpes simplex infections. At least five species of the family are extremely widespread among humans, and more than 90% of adults have been infected with at least one of them.

Structure and replication

Herpesviruses have a distinctive four-layered structure: a large double-stranded DNA genome enclosed in an icosahedral capsid, surrounded by an amorphous protein layer called the tegument and an outer lipid envelope studded with glycoprotein spikes. Their genomes are among the largest of animal viruses, ranging from about 120 to 250 kilobase pairs.

Latency

A hallmark of herpesviruses is latency: after primary infection the virus persists silently in specific host cells — nerve ganglia for alphaherpesviruses, lymphocytes for gammaherpesviruses — and can reactivate later, sometimes decades afterwards, as in shingles caused by varicella zoster virus.

Human herpesviruses

Nine herpesvirus types are known to infect humans primarily, designated human herpesviruses 1–9: herpes simplex viruses 1 and 2 (cold sores and genital herpes), varicella zoster virus (chickenpox and shingles), Epstein–Barr virus (infectious mononucleosis), cytomegalovirus, human herpesviruses 6 and 7 (roseola), and Kaposi's sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (HHV-8). Antiviral drugs such as aciclovir suppress replication but cannot eliminate latent infection.
