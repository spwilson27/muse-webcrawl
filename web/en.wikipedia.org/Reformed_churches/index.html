Reformed churches

Reformed churches are a group of Protestant Christian denominations that trace their origins to the Reformation led by figures such as Huldrych Zwingli and John Calvin in the 16th century. They are characterized by Calvinist theology, a presbyterian or congregational polity, and a regulated approach to worship.

Theology

Reformed theology emphasizes the sovereignty of God, covenant theology, and the doctrines summarized in confessions such as the Westminster Confession of Faith, the Belgic Confession, and the Heidelberg Catechism. The "five solas" and, in many traditions, the five points of Calvinism (TULIP) are defining features.

History

The movement began in Zurich under Zwingli in the 1520s and was systematized by Calvin in Geneva. Reformed churches spread to Scotland, the Netherlands, France (the Huguenots), Germany, and Hungary, and later worldwide through missions and migration. Major bodies today include Presbyterian churches and continental Reformed denominations, many united in the World Communion of Reformed Churches.

Worship and practice

Reformed worship traditionally centers on the preaching of the Word, congregational psalm singing, and the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper. Church government by elders (presbyters) distinguishes Presbyterian traditions within the broader Reformed family.
