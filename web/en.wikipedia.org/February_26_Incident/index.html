February 26 Incident

The February 26 Incident (二・二六事件, Ni Ni-Roku Jiken) was an attempted coup d'état in the Empire of Japan on 26 February 1936. It was organized by a group of young radical officers in the Imperial Japanese Army who sought to purge the government and military leadership of their factional rivals and ideological opponents. The rebels assassinated several leading officials, including the finance minister, and occupied central Tokyo, but the coup ultimately failed when Emperor Hirohito ordered its suppression.

Background

The Imperial Japanese Army of the 1930s was divided between the radical Kōdōha (Imperial Way Faction) and the more pragmatic Tōseiha (Control Faction). Young officers influenced by the writings of Kita Ikki envisioned a "Shōwa Restoration" that would sweep away corrupt politicians, zaibatsu business interests, and senior military figures, restoring direct imperial rule and relieving rural poverty, which had been worsened by the Great Depression.

The uprising

Before dawn on 26 February 1936, some 1,500 troops led by junior officers seized key government buildings in a heavy snowfall, including the prime minister's residence, the War Ministry, and police headquarters. Assassination squads murdered Finance Minister Takahashi Korekiyo, Lord Keeper of the Privy Seal Saitō Makoto, and Inspector General of Military Education Watanabe Jōtarō. Prime Minister Okada Keisuke narrowly escaped by hiding while rebels killed his brother-in-law, mistaking him for the premier. The rebels demanded a new cabinet and the implementation of their reforms.

Suppression and aftermath

Emperor Hirohito was angered by the murders of his advisers and refused to countenance the rebels' demands. The army high command, after initial hesitation, declared the rebels mutineers, and troops and naval forces were mobilized against them. By 29 February the rebels had surrendered. Nineteen of the leaders were executed after secret trials, and Kita Ikki and another civilian ideologue were also executed the following year.

The failure of the coup destroyed the Kōdōha faction but paradoxically increased the military's influence over the government: to secure the army's cooperation, civilian leaders conceded greater power to the armed services, including the restoration of the rule that service ministers must be active-duty officers. The incident is regarded as a milestone on Japan's path toward militarism and the Second Sino-Japanese and Pacific wars.
