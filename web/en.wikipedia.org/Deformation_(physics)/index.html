Deformation (physics)

Deformation
FieldContinuum mechanics, materials science
Measured byStrain (dimensionless)
Caused byStress, temperature, phase change
TypesElastic, plastic, viscous, fracture

In physics and continuum mechanics, deformation is the change in the shape or size of an object, whether it be complete or partial, due to an applied force (the deformation energy in this case is transferred through work) or to a change in temperature (transferred through heat). Deformation is described quantitatively by the strain, the relative displacement between points in the material body.

Types of deformation

Elastic deformation is reversible: the material returns to its original shape when the load is removed, and for small strains stress is proportional to strain (Hooke's law). Plastic deformation is permanent; it occurs once stress exceeds the yield strength, mediated in crystalline materials by the motion of dislocations. Viscous deformation is time-dependent flow characteristic of fluids and of creep in solids at high temperature. When local strain exceeds the material's capacity, fracture results.

Strain

Strain measures deformation as a ratio of lengths (normal strain) or as a change in angle (shear strain). In the general theory, deformation of a continuum is described by the deformation gradient tensor, from which finite strain tensors such as the Green–Lagrange and Euler–Almansi strains are derived.

Modes and analysis

Basic loading modes include tension, compression, shear, bending, and torsion. Engineers use stress–strain curves from standardized tests to characterize elasticity, yielding, work hardening, necking, and failure, and these data feed into structural design and finite element analysis.

Thermal deformation

Temperature changes also deform bodies: most materials expand on heating (thermal expansion), and constrained expansion produces thermal stress — the reason expansion joints are built into bridges and rails.
