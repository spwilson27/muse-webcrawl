Population bottleneck

A population bottleneck or genetic bottleneck is a sharp reduction in the size of a population due to environmental events such as famines, earthquakes, floods, fires, disease, and droughts; or human activities such as genocide, speciocide, widespread violence or intentional culling. Such events can reduce the variation in the gene pool of a population; thereafter, a smaller population, with a smaller genetic diversity, remains to pass on genes to future generations of offspring through sexual reproduction.

Key concepts
Related termsFounder effect, genetic drift, effective population size
FieldPopulation genetics, conservation biology
ConsequenceReduced genetic diversity; increased inbreeding

Overview

Population bottlenecks reduce the genetic diversity of a population. When a population's size is reduced for at least one generation, genetic drift can act more quickly to eliminate alleles, a phenomenon sometimes called the bottleneck effect. The smaller the population, the more likely it is that random sampling effects will shift allele frequencies away from those of the original population.

Causes

Bottlenecks may result from natural disasters, habitat destruction, disease epidemics, or deliberate extermination. A well-known example is the northern elephant seal, hunted to near extinction in the 19th century; the surviving population of fewer than a hundred individuals shows markedly reduced genetic variation. The cheetah is also believed to have passed through one or more severe bottlenecks, leaving it with unusually low genetic diversity.

Founder effect

A related phenomenon is the founder effect, in which a new population is established by a small number of individuals drawn from a larger population. The founding population carries only a subset of the original gene pool's diversity, and subsequent generations reflect that reduced variation.

Conservation implications

For endangered species, bottlenecks can increase susceptibility to disease and reduce the capacity to adapt to environmental change. Conservation biologists monitor minimum viable population sizes and sometimes use translocation or captive breeding to restore genetic diversity.
