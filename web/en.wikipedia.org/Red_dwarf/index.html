A red dwarf is the smallest and coolest kind of star on the main sequence. Red dwarfs are by far the most common type of star in the Milky Way, making up roughly three quarters of all stars, but because of their low luminosity no individual red dwarf is visible to the naked eye from Earth. The nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is a red dwarf.

Characteristics

Red dwarfs range in mass from about 0.075 to about 0.6 times the mass of the Sun. Their surface temperatures are below about 3,700 K, giving them a reddish-orange glow. They burn their hydrogen fuel extremely slowly through the proton–proton chain, and the lower-mass examples are fully convective, so helium does not accumulate in a core and the whole star's hydrogen supply is gradually consumed. As a result their main-sequence lifetimes are estimated at tens of billions to trillions of years — far longer than the current age of the universe — so no red dwarf has yet had time to evolve off the main sequence.

Planetary systems

Red dwarfs are prime targets in the search for exoplanets. Their habitable zones lie close to the star, which aids detection of Earth-sized planets by the transit and radial-velocity methods. Proxima Centauri hosts a roughly Earth-mass planet in its habitable zone, and the TRAPPIST-1 system contains seven Earth-sized planets. Habitability is complicated by the stars' flare activity, tidal locking of close-in planets, and strong stellar winds.

Examples

Well-studied red dwarfs include Proxima Centauri, Barnard's Star, Lalande 21185, Ross 128 and TRAPPIST-1. Because red dwarfs dominate the stellar population, they also dominate the Galaxy's inventory of rocky planets.
