Uhlan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Uhlans were originally Polish-Lithuanian light cavalry units armed with lances, sabres, and pistols. The name derives from the Lipka Tatar word oglan (young man, brave), and Tatar auxiliaries in Polish service were among the first to be called uhlans. From the 18th century, uhlan-style regiments were adopted by the armies of Prussia, Austria, Russia, France, and other European powers, and they remained a standard cavalry type through the 19th century and into the First World War.

Origins and spread

Uhlan units in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth combined Tatar and Polish traditions of light horsemanship. After the partitions of Poland, Polish lancers in exile carried the model abroad: Polish legions served with Napoleon, and the Vistula Uhlans distinguished themselves in Spain, famously charging the British guns at Albuera in 1811. Impressed, other armies raised their own lancer regiments; Britain converted several light dragoon regiments to lancers after 1816, and the lance-armed cavalry became a fixture of European warfare.

Uniform and equipment

The classic uhlan uniform included the czapka — a distinctive square-topped cap derived from Polish dress — a short jacket with a plastron front, and the lance with a small swallow-tailed pennant. In the German Empire, uhlans formed a large arm of the cavalry, wearing the czapka-style helmet and lance into 1914.

Later use

Uhlans fought on many fronts of the First World War, increasingly dismounted as trench warfare dominated. In recreated Polish cavalry of the interwar period, uhlan regiments retained their traditions and titles, and Polish cavalry — including uhlans — fought in 1939, generally as mobile infantry rather than in mounted charges. The title survives in modern ceremonial and armoured units' traditions in Poland and elsewhere.
