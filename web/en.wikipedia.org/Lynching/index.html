Lynching

The National Memorial for Peace and Justice, Montgomery

Type: Extrajudicial killing by a mob

U.S. peak era: 1880s–1930s

Documented U.S. victims: 4,700+ (1882–1968)

Lynching is an extrajudicial killing by a group, usually a mob, carried out as a form of summary punishment or social control, most often by hanging. The word is generally traced to "Lynch's law", associated with Colonel Charles Lynch of Virginia, who presided over irregular courts during the American Revolutionary War. Lynching has occurred in many societies, but the term is most strongly associated with the United States, where from the late 19th century to the mid-20th century it functioned above all as an instrument of racial terror against African Americans, especially in the South.

History in the United States

At least 4,743 lynchings were recorded in the United States between 1882 and 1968, and researchers at the Equal Justice Initiative have documented more than 4,400 racial terror lynchings of Black victims between 1877 and 1950 alone. Victims were accused — often without evidence — of crimes or breaches of racial etiquette; many lynchings were public spectacles attended by crowds of thousands, photographed, and commemorated on postcards. Mexicans, Chinese immigrants, Native Americans, and white victims were also lynched, particularly in the West.

Journalist Ida B. Wells pioneered investigative reporting on lynching in the 1890s, and the NAACP campaigned for decades for federal legislation, which Congress repeatedly failed to pass due to Southern filibusters. Lynching declined after the 1930s under the pressure of activism, media exposure, and migration. The Emmett Till Antilynching Act, signed in 2022, finally designated lynching as a federal hate crime. The National Memorial for Peace and Justice opened in Montgomery, Alabama in 2018 to commemorate the victims.
