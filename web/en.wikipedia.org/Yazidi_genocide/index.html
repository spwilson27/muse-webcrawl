Yazidi genocide
Perpetrator: Islamic State (ISIL)
Began: 3 August 2014
Location: Sinjar District, Nineveh, Iraq
Victims: Thousands killed; c. 6,400+ abducted; hundreds of thousands displaced

The Yazidi genocide was the mass murder, enslavement, and persecution of the Yazidi people of northern Iraq by the Islamic State beginning in August 2014. The United Nations and numerous governments and parliaments have recognised the campaign as genocide.

The Sinjar massacre

On 3 August 2014, Islamic State fighters attacked the Sinjar (Shingal) region, home to Iraq's Yazidi religious minority, after Kurdish Peshmerga forces withdrew. Tens of thousands of Yazidis fled to Mount Sinjar, where they were besieged without water or food in extreme heat; a corridor opened by Kurdish forces and coalition airstrikes — beginning with U.S. airdrops and strikes authorised by President Barack Obama — allowed many to escape. Men and older women who did not flee were massacred in village after village, including at Kocho; mass graves were later documented across the district.

Enslavement

IS systematically abducted women and girls — more than 6,400 people — and sold them into sexual slavery in markets across its territory, justifying the practice in its magazine Dabiq as religiously mandated. Boys were forcibly converted and trained as fighters. Survivor Nadia Murad, who escaped in 2014, became a leading campaigner and shared the 2018 Nobel Peace Prize. Thousands of the abducted remain missing.

Recognition and justice

A UN Commission of Inquiry concluded in 2016 that IS was committing genocide against the Yazidis. UNITAD investigated crimes for Iraqi and international prosecution; in 2021 a German court in Frankfurt delivered the first-ever conviction for the Yazidi genocide (the Taha al-J. case). Iraq's 2021 Yazidi Female Survivors Law provided reparations. Most displaced Yazidis still live in camps in the Kurdistan Region, with Sinjar's reconstruction hampered by political disputes.
