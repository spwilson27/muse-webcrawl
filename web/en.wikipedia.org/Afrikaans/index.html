Afrikaans
Native to: South Africa, Namibia
Speakers: ~7.2 million native; ~10–15 million total
Family: Indo-European > Germanic > West Germanic > Dutch

Afrikaans is a West Germanic language spoken in South Africa, Namibia and, to a lesser extent, Botswana, Zambia and Zimbabwe. It evolved from the Dutch vernacular of Holland spoken by Dutch settlers in South Africa, where it gradually began to develop distinguishing characteristics during the course of the 18th century.

History

After Dutch settlement at the Cape from 1652, the local Dutch dialect absorbed influences from Indigenous Khoisan languages, Malay, Portuguese and the languages of enslaved people. Afrikaans was long dismissed as "kitchen Dutch" before being standardised and recognised as an official language of South Africa in 1925. It was one of the two official languages (with English) during the apartheid era, and the 1976 Soweto uprising was partly sparked by its compulsory use in Black schools. Since 1994 it has been one of South Africa's 11 (now 12) official languages.

Features

Compared with Dutch, Afrikaans has greatly simplified grammar: verbs do not conjugate by person, nouns have no grammatical gender, and it uses a distinctive double negative construction. Most of its vocabulary derives from Dutch, with loanwords from many other languages.
