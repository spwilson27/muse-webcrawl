Tristan da Cunha is a remote group of volcanic islands in the South Atlantic Ocean and the main island of the archipelago, part of the British overseas territory of Saint Helena, Ascension and Tristan da Cunha. It lies 2,432 km from Saint Helena and 3,360 km from South America, making its settlement, Edinburgh of the Seven Seas, the most remote permanent human settlement on Earth. The island has no airport; access is by ship from Cape Town, a journey of about six days, weather permitting.

History

The islands were first recorded by the Portuguese explorer Tristão da Cunha in 1506, though rough seas prevented a landing. Britain annexed the islands in 1816, partly to prevent their use in a rescue attempt of Napoleon on Saint Helena. A small settler community developed; its population descends largely from a handful of 19th-century founders, and most residents today share just a few surnames (Glass, Green, Hagan, Lavarello, Repetto, Rogers, Swain, Patterson). In 1961 a volcanic eruption forced the entire population's evacuation to England; islanders voted to return in 1963.

Geography and nature

The main island is a steep volcanic cone; the only inhabited flat land is on the northwestern coast. The surrounding islands (Inaccessible, Nightingale, Gough) are important wildlife refuges; Gough Island and Inaccessible Island are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, home to the Tristan albatross and the flightless Inaccessible Island rail. In 2020 a marine protection zone was established around the islands.

Economy and society

The economy rests on lobster fishing (exported under the "Tristan" brand), the sale of postage stamps and coins, and limited tourism. All land is held communally; islanders farm cattle and potatoes on the settlement plain. The community is led by a Chief Islander and an Island Council, with a resident UK Administrator.
