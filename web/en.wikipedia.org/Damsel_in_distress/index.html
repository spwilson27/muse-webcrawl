Damsel in distress

The damsel in distress is a narrative device in which one or more men must rescue one or more women who have either been kidnapped or placed in general peril. The damsel is a stock character — helpless, beautiful, and in need of a male hero. Kidnapping and the resulting quest to free the damsel supplied much of the action in medieval romances, and the trope persisted through Gothic fiction, melodrama, pulp magazines, film serials, and early video games.

History

Precursor figures appear in Greek myth — Andromeda chained to a rock for a sea monster and rescued by Perseus — and in the captive princesses of folktale and epic. The device flourished in medieval chivalric romance, where rescuing ladies demonstrated knightly virtue, and in 18th-century Gothic novels such as Ann Radcliffe's works. In the 19th century it defined stage melodrama's imperiled heroine (famously tied to railway tracks in popular imagination, though rarer in practice), and in the 20th century, film serial heroines like Pearl White in The Perils of Pauline (1914) — who, notably, often escaped by her own efforts — and Fay Wray's character in King Kong (1933).

Criticism and subversion

Feminist critics have long noted that the trope reduces female characters to plot devices motivating male action, depriving them of agency. In video game criticism, Anita Sarkeesian's Tropes vs. Women in Video Games (2013–2017) examined its use in franchises built around rescuing princesses, such as Mario's Princess Peach and The Legend of Zelda. Modern works frequently subvert the device: rescues fail, heroines rescue themselves, or roles are reversed — while it persists, often knowingly, in adventure and superhero stories.
