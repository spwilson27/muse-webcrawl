Hybridoma technology

Fusion of a B cell with a myeloma cell produces a hybridoma
InventorsGeorges Köhler and César Milstein
Year1975
ProductMonoclonal antibodies

Hybridoma technology is a method for producing large numbers of identical antibodies (also called monoclonal antibodies). This process starts by injecting a mouse (or other mammal) with an antigen that provokes an immune response. A type of white blood cell, the B cell, produces antibodies that bind to the injected antigen. These antibody-producing B cells are then harvested from the mouse and, in turn, fused with immortal cancerous immune cells, called myeloma cells. The resulting hybrid cells, called hybridomas, can be grown in tissue culture and produce large amounts of monoclonal antibodies.

History

The method was invented in 1975 by immunologists Georges Köhler and César Milstein, working at the Laboratory of Molecular Biology in Cambridge, England. They shared the 1984 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Niels Kaj Jerne for the work. Their decision not to patent the method allowed it to spread rapidly through biomedical research.

Method

Laboratory animals (usually mice) are immunised with the antigen of interest and their spleens removed once antibody production is detected. Splenic B lymphocytes are fused with myeloma cells — commonly using polyethylene glycol (PEG) to promote membrane fusion — and the mixture is grown in selective HAT medium. The enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) is required for survival in HAT medium; the myeloma partner is HGPRT-deficient and unfused B cells die naturally within days, so only hybridomas survive.

Surviving cells are diluted and cloned by limiting dilution so that each culture derives from a single cell, and supernatants are screened (typically by ELISA) for the desired antibody specificity. Selected clones can be grown indefinitely in culture or as ascites tumours, yielding a continuous supply of a single antibody species recognising a single epitope.

Applications

Monoclonal antibodies produced by hybridomas underpin a wide range of diagnostic tests — including pregnancy tests, infectious-disease assays and blood typing — and a large class of therapeutic drugs used in cancer, autoimmune disease and transplantation, such as rituximab and trastuzumab. Hybridomas remain a standard route to research antibodies, although recombinant and phage-display methods are increasingly used as alternatives.
