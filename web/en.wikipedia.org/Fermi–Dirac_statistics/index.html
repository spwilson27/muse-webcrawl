Fermi–Dirac statistics

Fermi–Dirac statistics describes a distribution of particles over energy states in systems comprising many identical particles that obey the Pauli exclusion principle. Such particles are called fermions and include electrons, protons, neutrons, and quarks. The statistics were named after Enrico Fermi and Paul Dirac, who each discovered the distribution independently in 1926.

Definition

The average number of particles in a state of energy E is

f(E) = 1 / [e^((E − μ)/kT) + 1]

where μ is the chemical potential, k is Boltzmann's constant and T is the absolute temperature.

Applications

Fermi–Dirac statistics underlies the physics of white dwarfs and neutron stars (through degeneracy pressure), the semiconductor physics of diodes and transistors, and the electronic structure of metals, where conduction electrons near the Fermi level determine conductivity.

Derivation

The distribution follows from maximising the number of microstates subject to the constraints of fixed total particle number and fixed total energy, with each state holding at most one fermion, using the method of Lagrange multipliers.
