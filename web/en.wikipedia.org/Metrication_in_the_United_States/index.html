Metrication (or metrification) is the process of introducing the International System of Units (SI), commonly known as the metric system, to replace a country's traditional system of measurement. In the United States, the process has proceeded slowly and incompletely: although metric units have been legal since the Metric Act of 1866 and were declared the preferred system for trade and commerce in 1988, United States customary units — inches, pounds, and gallons — remain dominant in everyday life.

History

Thomas Jefferson considered adopting a decimal system in 1790 but retained English units, standardised in 1836. The United States defined its customary units in terms of metric standards in the Mendenhall Order of 1893, and signed the Treaty of the Metre in 1875. The Metric Conversion Act of 1975, signed by President Gerald Ford, established a voluntary conversion coordinated by a Metric Board, which was abolished in 1982 under President Ronald Reagan. The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act of 1988 designated the metric system as "the preferred system of weights and measures for United States trade and commerce".

Current usage

Metric units are standard in American science, medicine, the military, and much manufacturing; beverages are sold in litres alongside customary sizes, nutrition labels are metric, and many vehicles and tools use metric fasteners. Road signs, weather reporting, and most retail trade remain customary, and a planned conversion of highway signs in the 1970s was abandoned — the failed conversion of NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999, lost because of a pound–newton mix-up, is a famous consequence of the dual system.
