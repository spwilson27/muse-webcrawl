Dead reckoning

Dead reckoning is the process of calculating the current position of a vessel, aircraft or other moving object by using a previously determined position — or fix — and advancing it based upon known or estimated speeds over elapsed time and course. The term comes from "deduced reckoning" (often shortened to "ded. reckoning" in logbooks). It was the standard method of navigation for centuries before radio and satellite systems.

Method

The navigator starts from a fix, then plots the vessel's headings (corrected for compass error, leeway, and tidal set and drift at sea) and distances run — traditionally measured with a chip log and sandglass, later by patent log, pitot tube or Doppler. Because errors accumulate with distance, a dead-reckoning position (labelled "DR") is periodically compared with celestial, terrestrial or electronic fixes; the difference gives the "estimated position" (EP). In air navigation the same principle uses heading, true airspeed and forecast winds. Columbus navigated his 1492 Atlantic crossing largely by dead reckoning, as did Polynesian voyagers using their own star-compass traditions.

Modern uses

Dead reckoning survives wherever external signals are unavailable or denied. Inertial navigation systems are mechanised dead reckoning, integrating accelerometer and gyroscope outputs — with steadily growing error. Pedestrian dead reckoning uses smartphone inertial sensors for indoor positioning; autonomous vehicles and game networking use the same mathematics ("dead reckoning" extrapolates other players' positions between updates in multiplayer games), and animals appear to perform a biological version called path integration.
