La Soufrière is an active stratovolcano on the French island of Basse-Terre, in Guadeloupe in the Lesser Antilles. At 1,234 metres (4,049 ft), it is the highest point in Guadeloupe and in the Lesser Antilles. The volcano is monitored by the Observatoire Volcanologique et Sismologique de Guadeloupe (OVSG).

The name "Soufrière" derives from the French word for sulphur, referring to the sulphurous fumes emitted from vents near the summit. Several other volcanoes in the Caribbean share the name, including Soufrière Hills on Montserrat and La Soufrière on Saint Vincent.

Geology

La Soufrière is part of the volcanic arc of the Lesser Antilles, formed by the subduction of the North American Plate beneath the Caribbean Plate. The present edifice is a complex of lava domes built within a caldera formed by an earlier collapse. Its magmas are typically andesitic, and its eruptions have historically been phreatic or phreatomagmatic in character, driven by interactions between magma, groundwater and the volcano's hydrothermal system.

Eruptive history

The most significant historical eruption occurred in 1976–1977. Beginning in July 1976, a series of phreatic explosions, earthquakes and ash emissions led the authorities to evacuate some 73,000 people from the southern part of Basse-Terre, including the town of Saint-Claude and the capital Basse-Terre, in August 1976. The crisis became famous for the public disagreement between scientists Haroun Tazieff, who judged the danger limited, and Claude Allègre, who warned of a possible catastrophic eruption. In the event, no magmatic eruption occurred; activity waned through 1977 and residents returned. The episode remains a landmark case study in volcanic crisis management.

Earlier eruptions were recorded in 1690, 1797–1798, 1836–1837 and 1956. Since the 1990s the volcano has shown signs of restlessness, with increasing fumarolic activity, deformation and seismicity, prompting continued close monitoring.

Summit area

The summit dome is fissured by fractures that vent hot, acidic gases. Among its features are the Tarissan pit, the Napoléon crater and numerous fumaroles. The acidic summit environment supports only sparse vegetation, in striking contrast to the lush rainforest of the surrounding Guadeloupe National Park.
