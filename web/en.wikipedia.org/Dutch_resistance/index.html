Dutch resistance

Resistance members in the Netherlands, 1944

The Dutch resistance (Dutch: Nederlands verzet) to the German occupation of the Netherlands during World War II developed relatively slowly but grew into a nationwide network of illegal newspapers, forged-document workshops, escape lines, intelligence services and armed groups. The Netherlands was occupied from May 1940 until liberation in May 1945.

Early resistance

An early landmark was the February Strike of 1941 in Amsterdam, a general strike protesting the first round-ups of Jewish citizens — unique in occupied Europe, though brutally suppressed. Resistance groups such as the OD (Ordedienst), the communist-led groups, the LO (Landelijke Organisatie voor Hulp aan Onderduikers, which hid some 300,000 people from forced labour and persecution), and the LKP (Landelijke Knokploegen, armed assault groups) formed the backbone of organized activity.

Activities

Dutch resisters published underground papers including Trouw, Het Parool and Vrij Nederland; ran the "Dutch-Paris" escape line that moved Allied airmen and Jews to Switzerland and Spain; gathered intelligence for London; robbed distribution offices for ration cards; and carried out assassinations of collaborators, answered by German reprisals such as the Silbertanne murders. The national railway strike of September 1944, called to support Operation Market Garden, contributed to the Hongerwinter famine when Germany embargoed food transport.

Persecution of the Jews

Despite hiding efforts — most famously Anne Frank's family in Amsterdam — the Netherlands lost about 102,000 of its 140,000 Jews, the highest proportion in Western Europe, a record historians attribute to geography, efficient registration, and an accommodating bureaucracy.
