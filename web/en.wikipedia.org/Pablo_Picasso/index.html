Pablo Ruiz Picasso (25 October 1881 – 8 April 1973) was a Spanish painter, sculptor, printmaker, ceramicist and theatre designer who spent most of his adult life in France. One of the most influential artists of the 20th century, he is known for co-founding the Cubist movement, the invention of constructed sculpture, the co-invention of collage, and for the wide variety of styles that he helped develop and explore.

Early life and periods

Born in Málaga, the son of an art teacher, Picasso showed prodigious talent from childhood and studied in Barcelona and Madrid before settling in Paris in 1904. His early work is grouped into the melancholy Blue Period (1901–1904) and the warmer Rose Period (1904–1906). In 1907 he painted Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, a radical break with traditional composition influenced by African and Iberian sculpture.

Cubism and after

With Georges Braque, Picasso developed Cubism (c. 1909–1919), fragmenting objects into geometric forms shown from multiple viewpoints, first in the Analytical phase and then in the Synthetic phase that introduced collage. During and after the First World War he also worked in a neoclassical style and designed for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes. His monumental anti-war painting Guernica (1937), a response to the Nazi bombing of the Basque town, became one of the most famous artworks in the world and a universal symbol of the suffering of war.

Later life and legacy

A longtime member of the French Communist Party, Picasso remained immensely productive into old age, working in painting, sculpture, ceramics and prints from his homes in the south of France. He died at Mougins in 1973, leaving an oeuvre catalogued at some 50,000 works, including about 1,885 paintings. His works rank among the most expensive ever sold at auction, and museums devoted to him stand in Paris, Barcelona, Málaga and Antibes.
