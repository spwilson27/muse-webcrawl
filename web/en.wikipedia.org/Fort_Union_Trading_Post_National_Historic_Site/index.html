Fort Union Trading Post National Historic Site is a partial reconstruction of the most important fur trading post on the upper Missouri River between 1828 and 1867. The site lies on the North Dakota–Montana border, near the confluence of the Missouri and Yellowstone rivers, and is managed by the National Park Service.

History

Fort Union was built in 1828–29 by Kenneth McKenzie of the American Fur Company. For nearly four decades it was the principal trading center of the northern plains, where the Assiniboine, Crow, Cree, Blackfeet, Mandan, Hidatsa, and other nations exchanged buffalo robes and furs for trade goods such as blankets, beads, firearms, and kettles. It was a multicultural hub where trade, diplomacy, and sometimes conflict took place.

Notable visitors included artists George Catlin and Karl Bodmer, naturalist John James Audubon, and explorer Father Pierre-Jean De Smet, whose journals and paintings recorded life at the post in detail.

Decline and preservation

The fur trade declined as silk replaced beaver felt in fashion and the buffalo herds shrank. In 1867 the post was sold to a rival firm, dismantled, and its materials used to build nearby Fort Buford. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1961 and a National Historic Site in 1966; the National Park Service reconstructed the fort's palisade, trading house, and bourgeois house on the original foundations.
