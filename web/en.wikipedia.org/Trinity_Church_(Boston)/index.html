Trinity Church in the City of Boston, located in the Back Bay neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts, is a parish of the Episcopal Diocese of Massachusetts. The congregation, founded in 1733, is famous both for its history and for its landmark building, the masterpiece of architect Henry Hobson Richardson and the birthplace and archetype of the style known as Richardsonian Romanesque. Trinity Church appears on many lists of the ten most significant buildings in the United States.

History and building

The parish's first two churches stood on Summer Street downtown; the second was destroyed in the Great Boston Fire of 1872. Under the dynamic rector Phillips Brooks — later famous as the author of "O Little Town of Bethlehem" — the parish bought land in the newly filled Back Bay and held a design competition, won by the young H. H. Richardson. Built 1872–1877 on 4,500 wooden piles driven through the Back Bay fill, the church was consecrated on February 9, 1877.

The building is a compact Greek-cross plan of rough Roxbury puddingstone and red granite trim, dominated by a massive central tower (originally crowned differently; the present tower, modeled on the Old Cathedral of Salamanca, was completed in 1916 after Richardson's death). The west porch, added in 1894–97, was inspired by Saint-Trophime at Arles. Inside, the rich polychrome decoration was executed under John La Farge, who supervised a team of artists and later installed celebrated stained glass; murals and windows also involve William Morris, Edward Burne-Jones, and Augustus Saint-Gaudens's circle. The result is one of the great unified works of American 19th-century art.

Ministry and music

Trinity is one of the largest Episcopal parishes in the United States, noted for preaching, adult education (including the Trinity Boston Connects programs), and a distinguished choral tradition. The church sits on Copley Square across from the Boston Public Library, beside the John Hancock Tower, whose construction in the 1970s famously cracked its walls — a lawsuit whose settlement funded an ongoing program of inspection and structural monitoring of the pile foundation.
