Inflorescence

An inflorescence is a group or cluster of flowers arranged on a stem that is composed of a main branch or a complicated arrangement of branches. Morphologically, it is the modified part of the shoot of seed plants where flowers are formed on the axis of a plant. The stem holding the whole inflorescence is called a peduncle, and the individual flower stalks are pedicels.

Common inflorescence architectures: raceme, spike, panicle, umbel, corymb and head.

Types

Inflorescences are classified mainly by whether the main axis ends in a flower. In indeterminate (racemose) inflorescences, the main axis keeps growing and flowers open from the bottom up or from the outside in; examples include the raceme, spike, spadix (as in arum lilies), catkin, corymb, umbel, panicle and the capitulum (head) of the sunflower family, in which many small florets together mimic a single flower. In determinate (cymose) inflorescences, the main axis ends in a flower and growth continues from lateral branches, as in the cyme of forget-me-nots.

Function

Grouping flowers can make a plant more conspicuous to pollinators, allow efficient mass pollination (especially by wind), and stagger flowering and fruiting over time. Inflorescence architecture is an important character in plant taxonomy and has evolved repeatedly under selection from pollinators and seed dispersers.
