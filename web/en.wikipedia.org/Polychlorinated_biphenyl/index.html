Polychlorinated biphenyl

Polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) are a group of manmade organic chemicals consisting of carbon, hydrogen and chlorine atoms. The number of chlorine atoms and their location in a PCB molecule determine many of its physical and chemical properties. PCBs have no known taste or smell, and range from light yellow liquids to waxy solids.

PCBs were once widely used as dielectric and coolant fluids in electrical equipment, such as transformers and capacitors, and in heat transfer fluids, hydraulic equipment, plasticizers in paints and cements, and a variety of other industrial applications because of their non-flammability, chemical stability, and electrical insulating properties.

History

PCBs were first synthesized in the nineteenth century and manufactured commercially beginning in 1929, most notably by the Monsanto Company in the United States under the trade name Aroclor. By the mid-twentieth century they were in widespread industrial use around the world.

Concerns about their toxicity and persistence in the environment grew during the 1960s and 1970s. Incidents such as the 1968 Yusho poisoning in Japan, in which PCB-contaminated rice oil poisoned more than a thousand people, focused public attention on the hazards. The United States Congress banned most PCB production under the Toxic Substances Control Act of 1976, and manufacture ceased in 1979. PCBs were later listed as persistent organic pollutants under the Stockholm Convention of 2001.

Toxicity and health effects

PCBs are among the most thoroughly studied environmental contaminants. Depending on the congener, they show a range of toxic effects in animals and humans, including immunotoxicity, endocrine disruption, neurotoxicity, and carcinogenicity. The most toxic congeners are the coplanar "dioxin-like" PCBs, which bind to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor. The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified PCBs as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1).

Because PCBs are lipophilic and resist degradation, they bioaccumulate in fatty tissues and biomagnify through food webs, so that top predators such as killer whales, polar bears, and fish-eating birds can carry body burdens far higher than those of the surrounding environment.

Environmental fate

PCBs enter the environment through leaks, spills, improper disposal, and emissions from waste incineration. They bind strongly to soil and sediment and can persist for decades. Although production has ended in most countries, large quantities remain in old electrical equipment, buildings, and contaminated sites such as the Hudson River in New York, where dredging of PCB-contaminated sediment was undertaken in the 2000s and 2010s.
