Tala (music)

A tala (Sanskrit: ताल, tāla) is a rhythmic cycle in Indian classical music, consisting of a fixed number of beats (matras) that repeats throughout a performance and provides the temporal framework within which melody (raga) is elaborated. The word literally means "clap" or "palm of the hand", reflecting the practice of marking the cycle with claps and waves.

Structure

A tala is divided into sections (vibhag) of unequal or equal length, with the first beat of the cycle, the sam, carrying the greatest stress — soloist and accompanist alike resolve improvisations to the sam. Certain beats are "empty" (khali), marked by a wave rather than a clap, which in drumming corresponds to the absence of the resonant bass stroke. In Hindustani music, the drum pattern characteristic of a tala is its theka, played on the tabla.

Common talas

Widely used Hindustani talas include Tintal (16 beats, 4+4+4+4), Jhaptal (10 beats, 2+3+2+3), Keherwa (8 beats), Dadra (6 beats), Rupak (7 beats, beginning on khali), and Ektal (12 beats). Carnatic music organises talas systematically: seven basic suladi tala families generated from combinations of laghu, drutam, and anudrutam yield 35 talas, extendable to 175 by varying the laghu's length. The most common Carnatic tala is Adi tala (8 beats).

Role in performance

Keeping tala was traditionally done by hand gestures or by a dedicated time-keeper; in modern concerts the tabla (or mridangam in the south), often with a melodic instrument playing a repeating lehra, maintains the cycle while the soloist improvises, sometimes in intricate cross-rhythms that play against the beat structure before landing on the sam.
