Siege of Malta (World War II)

The siege of Malta in World War II was a military campaign in the Mediterranean theatre from 11 June 1940 to 20 November 1942. The Axis powers — Italy and Germany — attempted to bomb, blockade, and starve the British colony of Malta, a key naval and air base between Sicily and North Africa, into submission. The island's survival allowed Allied forces to attack Axis supply lines to the North African campaign, and Malta's resistance became one of the most celebrated episodes of the war.

Strategic importance

Malta lay astride the sea routes between Italy and Libya. Aircraft, submarines, and warships based there — notably the submarines of the 10th Flotilla and Force K surface ships — sank a substantial share of the supplies destined for Rommel's Afrika Korps. Axis planners therefore sought to neutralize or capture the island, but an invasion (Operation Herkules) was repeatedly postponed and ultimately cancelled.

The bombing

Malta was one of the most heavily bombed places of the war, enduring roughly 3,000 air raids; in April 1942 alone more tonnage fell on the island than on London during the Blitz. The population sheltered in tunnels cut into the limestone. Food and fuel were perpetually short, and relief depended on heavily escorted convoys from Gibraltar and Alexandria. The most famous, Operation Pedestal in August 1942, saw the crippled tanker SS Ohio reach Grand Harbour with vital fuel despite heavy losses.

Recognition and aftermath

On 15 April 1942, King George VI awarded the George Cross to the island of Malta "to bear witness to a heroism and devotion that will long be famous in history"; the George Cross appears on Malta's flag. By late 1942 the siege had effectively been lifted as Allied fortunes turned after El Alamein and Operation Torch, and Malta served as a base for the invasion of Sicily in 1943.
