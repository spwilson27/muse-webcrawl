Fair dealing

Fair dealing is a limitation and exception to the exclusive rights granted by copyright law to the author of a creative work. In contrast to the open-ended fair use doctrine of the United States, fair dealing is used in most countries of the British Commonwealth and applies only where the use falls within a specific list of permitted purposes and is "fair" in light of factors such as the amount used, the nature of the work, and the effect on the market for the work.

By jurisdiction

United Kingdom

Under the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, fair dealing is permitted for non-commercial research and private study, for criticism, review and quotation, for reporting current events, and (since 2014) for parody, caricature and pastiche, and for illustration for instruction. There is no general defence for uses outside the listed purposes.

Canada

The Canadian Copyright Act allows fair dealing for research, private study, education, parody, satire, criticism, review, and news reporting — education having been added by the Copyright Modernization Act of 2012. In CCH Canadian Ltd. v. Law Society of Upper Canada (2004), the Supreme Court of Canada set out six fairness factors and held that fair dealing is a user's right that must be given a large and liberal interpretation.

Australia and India

Australia's Copyright Act 1968 permits fair dealing for research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, reporting news, and professional legal advice. India's Copyright Act 1957 provides for fair dealing with works for private or personal use including research, criticism or review, and reporting of current events; Indian courts have protected uses such as course packs in the Delhi University photocopy case.

Comparison with fair use

Fair use (Section 107 of the U.S. Copyright Act) is a flexible standard applicable to any purpose, guided by four factors, whereas fair dealing begins with a closed list of purposes and then assesses fairness. The result is that fair dealing tends to be narrower and more predictable, though Canadian interpretation since 2004 has moved the two doctrines closer together.
