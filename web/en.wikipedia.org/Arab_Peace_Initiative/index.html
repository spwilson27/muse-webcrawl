The Arab Peace Initiative (Arabic: مبادرة السلام العربية, Mubādirat as-Salām al-ʿArabiyya), also known as the Saudi Initiative, is a 10-sentence proposal for an end to the Arab–Israeli conflict that was endorsed by the Arab League in 2002 at the Beirut Summit and re-endorsed at the 2007 and 2017 Arab League summits. The initiative offers normalisation of relations between the Arab region and Israel in exchange for a full withdrawal by Israel from the occupied territories (including the West Bank, Gaza, the Golan Heights, and Lebanon), a "just settlement" of the Palestinian refugee problem based on UN Resolution 194, and the establishment of a Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital.

History

The initiative was first proposed by Crown Prince Abdullah of Saudi Arabia and was unanimously adopted by the Arab League at its summit in Beirut on 27–28 March 2002, during the height of the Second Intifada. It was notable as the first time the Arab states collectively offered Israel full normalisation rather than merely an end to hostilities. The offer built on earlier frameworks, including the 1981 Fahd Plan and the Madrid Conference of 1991, but went further by promising "full normal relations" in return for a comprehensive settlement.

Terms

The initiative calls upon Israel to affirm:

  • Full Israeli withdrawal from all the territories occupied since 1967, including the Syrian Golan Heights to the lines of 4 June 1967, as well as the remaining occupied territories in southern Lebanon.
  • Achievement of a just solution to the Palestinian refugee problem to be agreed upon in accordance with U.N. General Assembly Resolution 194.
  • Acceptance of the establishment of a sovereign independent Palestinian state on the Palestinian territories occupied since 4 June 1967 in the West Bank and Gaza Strip, with East Jerusalem as its capital.

In return, the Arab states affirm that they consider the Arab–Israeli conflict ended, will enter into peace agreements with Israel, and will consider establishing normal relations with Israel within the framework of a comprehensive peace.

Reactions

The Israeli government of Ariel Sharon initially dismissed the initiative, with officials noting it was announced alongside the Passover massacre in Netanya. Later Israeli leaders, including Ehud Olmert and Shimon Peres, expressed interest in elements of the plan, and in 2015 and 2016 Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu spoke of "generalities" in the initiative that could be positive while rejecting specific provisions such as withdrawal to the 1967 lines and the refugee clause. Palestinian officials, including Mahmoud Abbas, consistently endorsed the initiative. The plan was also welcomed by the United Nations, the European Union, and the United States, though successive US administrations treated it as one element of a broader negotiating framework rather than a take-it-or-leave-it text.

Re-endorsements

The Arab League re-endorsed the initiative at the Riyadh summit of 2007 and again at the Amman summit of 2017, each time reaffirming that the offer remained on the table. Following the Abraham Accords of 2020, in which the United Arab Emirates and Bahrain normalised relations with Israel without a prior Palestinian settlement, commentators debated whether the initiative's linkage between normalisation and a comprehensive peace had been superseded, though the Arab League maintained it as its stated position.
