Groyne

A groyne (in the United States, groin) is a rigid hydraulic structure built perpendicularly from an ocean shore (in coastal engineering) or a river bank, interrupting water flow and limiting the movement of sediment. It is usually made out of wood, concrete, or rock. In the ocean, groynes create beaches, prevent beach erosion caused by longshore drift, or facilitate beach nourishment. In a river, groynes slow erosion and navigation channels can be maintained.

Coastal groynes

Groynes work by trapping sand and shingle transported along the shore by longshore drift. Material accumulates on the updrift side of the groyne, building up the beach, which then absorbs wave energy and protects the coast behind it. However, by intercepting the sediment supply, groynes can starve beaches further downdrift, worsening erosion there — a classic problem in coastal management that may require a whole groyne field to be planned as a system, sometimes combined with beach nourishment.

Design and materials

Groyne length, height, spacing, and permeability are designed for local wave and sediment conditions. Traditional groynes are timber-pile structures; others are built of rock rubble, concrete units, steel sheet piling, or gabions. Modern designs may be submerged or shaped (for example fishtail groynes) to reduce adverse effects. Groynes also create a hazard to swimmers, as they can generate rip currents along their sides.

River groynes

In rivers, groynes (also called spur dikes or wing dams) project from the bank into the channel to direct flow, deepen navigation channels by concentrating the current, and protect banks from erosion. They are widely used on rivers such as the Rhine and the Mississippi.
