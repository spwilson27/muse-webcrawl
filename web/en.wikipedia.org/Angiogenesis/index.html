Angiogenesis

Angiogenesis is the physiological process through which new blood vessels form from pre-existing vessels, extending the network of capillaries into tissues. It is distinct from vasculogenesis, the de novo formation of vessels during embryonic development. Angiogenesis is a normal and vital process in growth, development and wound healing, but it is also a fundamental step in the transition of tumours from a benign state to a malignant one, since a growing tumour must recruit its own blood supply. The process is regulated by a balance of pro-angiogenic "on" signals, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and endogenous inhibitors such as angiostatin and endostatin.

Mechanism

In sprouting angiogenesis, growth factors released by hypoxic or injured tissue activate endothelial cells in an existing vessel. The basement membrane is degraded by proteases, endothelial "tip cells" lead migration into the surrounding matrix, stalk cells proliferate behind them, and a hollow lumen forms; pericytes are recruited to stabilise the new vessel. A second mechanism, intussusceptive angiogenesis, splits an existing vessel into two by inserting tissue pillars. Hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) is a master regulator, switching on VEGF transcription when oxygen is scarce.

Role in disease

Excessive angiogenesis contributes to cancer, age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis, while insufficient angiogenesis impairs wound healing and recovery from ischaemia. Judah Folkman's 1971 hypothesis that tumours are angiogenesis-dependent launched a field of cancer research leading to anti-angiogenic drugs.

Angiogenesis inhibitors in medicine

Bevacizumab (Avastin), a monoclonal antibody against VEGF, was approved in 2004 for colorectal cancer and is used with ranibizumab and aflibercept in eye disease; numerous tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib) also block angiogenic signalling. Conversely, pro-angiogenic therapy with growth factors has been explored for ischemic heart and limb disease, with limited success to date.
