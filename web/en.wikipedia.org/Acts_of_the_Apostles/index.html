Acts of the Apostles

The Acts of the Apostles (Koine Greek: Πράξεις Ἀποστόλων, Práxeis Apostólōn) is the fifth book of the New Testament. It tells of the founding of the Christian Church and the spread of its message to the Roman Empire, beginning with the ascension of Jesus and the day of Pentecost in Jerusalem and ending with the apostle Paul preaching in Rome. It forms the second part of the two-volume work Luke–Acts, traditionally attributed to Luke the Evangelist, companion of Paul.

Contents

The first half of Acts focuses on the Jerusalem church under Peter: the coming of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost, communal life, healings, the martyrdom of Stephen, and the conversion of Paul on the road to Damascus. The Council of Jerusalem (chapter 15) decides that Gentile converts need not follow the full Mosaic law. The second half follows Paul's missionary journeys through Cyprus, Asia Minor, Greece and finally, after arrest and trials, his voyage as a prisoner to Rome.

Composition and authorship

Acts shares its preface and addressee, Theophilus, with the Gospel of Luke, and the two works together make up about a quarter of the New Testament. Passages written in the first person plural ("we" passages) have been taken as evidence of an eyewitness source. Most scholars date the book to the late first or early second century and treat it as a work of theological history rather than a bare chronicle.

Themes

Key themes include the guidance of the Holy Spirit, the universal offer of salvation extending from Jews to Gentiles, the harmony between the apostles, and the innocence of Christianity before Roman law. Acts is the principal historical source for the earliest decades of the church.
