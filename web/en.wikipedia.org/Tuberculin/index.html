Tuberculin


Reading a Mantoux tuberculin skin test

Tuberculin is a preparation of proteins derived from cultures of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the bacterium that causes tuberculosis. It is used in skin testing to detect whether a person has been infected with the bacterium.

History

Robert Koch announced tuberculin in 1890, initially promoting it—incorrectly—as a cure for tuberculosis; the failure damaged his reputation but the substance proved valuable as a diagnostic. Clemens von Pirquet and Charles Mantoux developed intradermal testing in the early 1900s, and Florence Seibert later produced purified protein derivative (PPD), which became the standard preparation.

The Mantoux test

In the Mantoux test, a small dose of PPD is injected into the skin of the forearm; the site is examined 48–72 hours later, and the diameter of the resulting induration (hardened swelling) is measured. A positive result indicates prior infection or BCG vaccination, though it cannot by itself distinguish latent infection from active disease, and false negatives can occur in immunocompromised patients. Blood-based interferon-gamma release assays are a modern alternative.
