Cordon sanitaire

Cordon sanitaire (French pronunciation: [kɔʁdɔ̃ sanitɛʁ], French for "sanitary cordon") is a public health measure involving the restriction of movement of people into and out of a defined geographic area, such as a community, region, or country, in order to prevent the spread of an infectious disease. The term is also used, by extension, in politics and diplomacy.

Public health

The practice dates to measures against plague in early modern Europe, when guards were posted along the borders of infected districts. Notable modern uses include the cordon around affected districts of West Point, Monrovia, during the 2014 Ebola epidemic in Liberia, and restrictions around outbreak areas during various plague and cholera epidemics. Public-health scholars debate their effectiveness: cordons may slow spread but can also hinder delivery of aid and drive cases underground, and they raise ethical and human-rights concerns.

Political usage

In politics, a cordon sanitaire is the refusal of mainstream parties to cooperate with a party regarded as extremist or unacceptable, excluding it from government formation and legislative alliances. The term is used notably in Belgium, where mainstream parties long maintained a cordon around the far-right Vlaams Blok/Vlaams Belang, and in France for the strategy of uniting against the National Front (now National Rally).

Diplomatic usage

After the First World War, French statesmen used the term for the belt of states in Eastern Europe — including Poland, Czechoslovakia, Romania and the Baltic states — intended to isolate Soviet Russia and contain the spread of Bolshevism, a concept associated with French foreign minister Louis Barthou and earlier with Georges Clemenceau.
