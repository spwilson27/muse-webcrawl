Da Vinci Surgical System

The da Vinci Surgical System is a robotic surgical system made by the American company Intuitive Surgical. Approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2000, it is designed to facilitate minimally invasive surgery using a robotic platform controlled by a surgeon from a console. It is used most commonly for prostatectomies and increasingly for cardiac, colorectal, and gynaecological procedures.

How it works

The surgeon sits at a console viewing a magnified high-definition three-dimensional image of the surgical field from an endoscope. Hand controls and foot pedals manipulate robotic arms fitted with wristed instruments that scale down and filter the surgeon's movements, eliminating tremor and allowing precise dissection through small incisions. The system does not act autonomously — every movement is directed by the surgeon.

Evidence and debate

Robotic surgery typically offers smaller incisions, less blood loss, and shorter hospital stays than open surgery, but studies comparing outcomes and costs with conventional laparoscopy are mixed, and systems are expensive to buy and maintain. Training requirements and the lack of tactile (haptic) feedback are recurring points of discussion.

History

The technology grew out of research at SRI International and NASA on telepresence surgery. Intuitive Surgical, founded in 1995, merged with its rival Computer Motion in 2003. Successive generations (S, Si, Xi, and the single-port SP) added imaging, dual consoles, and fluorescence imaging.
