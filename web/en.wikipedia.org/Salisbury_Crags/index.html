Salisbury Crags

LocationHolyrood Park, Edinburgh, Scotland
ElevationCliff face c. 46 m (151 ft)
Rock typeDolerite (teschenite) sill
AgeCarboniferous, c. 335 million years

Salisbury Crags is a series of 46-metre (151 ft) cliffs atop a subsidiary spur of Arthur's Seat in Holyrood Park, Edinburgh, Scotland. The cliffs are formed from dolerite of a Carboniferous sill intruded into surrounding sedimentary rocks and later exposed by glacial erosion — a classic example of a crag-and-tail landscape.

Geology and Hutton's work

The crags played a famous part in the history of geology. In the 1780s James Hutton studied the junction here where molten rock had intruded into sediments, finding evidence that the dolerite had been injected in a liquid state and baking the rocks around it. "Hutton's Section", where he demonstrated this intrusive relationship, undermined the Neptunist theory that all rocks precipitated from water and supported Hutton's Plutonist view of a dynamic Earth with immense geological time. The site remains a pilgrimage for geology students.

Quarrying and the Radical Road

Stone from the crags was quarried for centuries and used to pave streets — famously including streets in London. Quarrying was restricted in the nineteenth century as concern grew for the park's scenery. Below the cliffs runs the Radical Road, a path built in the 1820s by unemployed weavers given work after the Radical War of 1820, under the direction of Sir Walter Scott and others. Today the crags are popular with walkers and are protected within Holyrood Park; climbing is regulated to protect nesting peregrine falcons. See also Arthur's Seat and James Hutton.
