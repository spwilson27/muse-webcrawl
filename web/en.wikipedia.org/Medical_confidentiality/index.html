Medical confidentiality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medical confidentiality is a set of rules and ethical principles that govern the privacy of communications between a patient and a physician or other health care provider. Information disclosed during the course of care is generally held to be confidential and may not be revealed to third parties without the patient's consent, subject to limited exceptions.

Principles

The duty of confidentiality can be traced to the Hippocratic Oath and is enshrined in professional codes of ethics and in legislation such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States and data-protection law in Europe. It is closely connected to the concept of patient autonomy and trust in the doctor–patient relationship.

Exceptions

Confidentiality is not absolute. Disclosure may be required or permitted where there is a serious risk of harm to the patient or to others, for certain notifiable diseases, under court order, or where the patient consents. The landmark Tarasoff case in California established a duty to warn identifiable victims of a patient's threats.

Breach and consequences

Unauthorised disclosure can lead to professional discipline, civil liability and criminal penalties, and can undermine patients' willingness to seek care or to speak candidly.
