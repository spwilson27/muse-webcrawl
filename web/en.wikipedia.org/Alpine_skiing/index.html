Alpine skiing

A downhill racer on a World Cup course.

Alpine skiing, or downhill skiing, is the pastime of sliding down snow-covered slopes on skis with fixed-heel bindings, unlike cross-country skiing or ski jumping. It is practised at ski resorts with lifts, groomed pistes and other facilities, and as a competitive sport governed by the International Ski and Snowboard Federation (FIS).

History

Skiing for transport is thousands of years old in Scandinavia, but alpine technique developed in the European Alps in the late nineteenth century. Mathias Zdarsky and Hannes Schneider (with his Arlberg technique) systematised downhill skiing, and the first slalom races were held in the 1920s. Alpine skiing entered the Winter Olympics at Garmisch-Partenkirchen in 1936, and the FIS World Cup circuit began in 1967.

Competitive disciplines

The speed events are downhill — the fastest, with speeds exceeding 130 km/h — and super-G. The technical events are slalom, run between closely spaced gates, and giant slalom. The alpine combined merges a downhill or super-G run with a slalom run. Equipment differs by discipline, with longer, stiffer skis for speed events.

Recreational skiing

Millions ski recreationally at resorts in the Alps, North America, Scandinavia, Japan and elsewhere. Runs are graded by difficulty, typically green, blue, red and black in Europe. Safety equipment, especially helmets, is now standard, and avalanche risk is a concern for off-piste skiers.
