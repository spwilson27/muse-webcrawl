Ashurbanipal (Akkadian: Aššur-bāni-apli, meaning "Ashur is the creator of the heir"; 685 – c. 631 BC) was the king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 669 BC until his death. He is generally remembered as the last great king of Assyria. Ashurbanipal inherited the empire at its territorial peak; it stretched from the Persian Gulf and Elam in the east to Egypt in the west, and included Babylonia, Media, the Levant and much of Anatolia.

Although he was a popular king among his contemporaries and in later legend, Ashurbanipal is better known to modern readers for his vast library, the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, a collection of more than 30,000 clay tablets and fragments containing texts of all kinds, including the famous Epic of Gilgamesh. Ashurbanipal was one of the few ancient kings who could read and write cuneiform, and he prided himself on his scholarship, boasting in his inscriptions of his mastery of scribal arts, mathematics and divination.

Early life and accession

Ashurbanipal was born in 685 BC, the younger son of King Esarhaddon and his queen Ešar-ḫammat. He was not originally destined for the Assyrian throne: his elder brother Shamash-shum-ukin was designated crown prince of Babylon, while Ashurbanipal, somewhat unusually, was named crown prince of Assyria in 672 BC, passing over another elder brother. As crown prince he was educated in the scribal tradition and in the arts of war, hunting and horsemanship. When Esarhaddon died suddenly in 669 BC while campaigning in Egypt, Ashurbanipal ascended the throne with the support of his powerful grandmother, Zakutu, who imposed loyalty treaties on the court.

Reign

Ashurbanipal's reign was dominated by wars on several fronts. In Egypt, the Kushite pharaohs Taharqa and Tantamani repeatedly attempted to recover the country; Assyrian forces defeated them, sacking Memphis and, in 663 BC, Thebes. In the east, Ashurbanipal fought a long series of campaigns against Elam, culminating in the destruction of Susa in 646 BC. The most serious conflict was the revolt of his brother Shamash-shum-ukin of Babylon (652–648 BC), who allied with Elamites, Chaldeans and Arab tribes. The civil war ended with Babylon's capture and Shamash-shum-ukin's death. Ashurbanipal also campaigned against Arab tribes of the Syrian desert, capturing their kings and bringing them in chains to Nineveh.

The Library of Ashurbanipal

Ashurbanipal ordered scribes throughout the empire, particularly in Babylonia, to collect and copy ancient texts for his royal library at Nineveh. The library held works on religion, divination (including the omen series Enūma Anu Enlil), medicine, astronomy, incantations and literature. Its rediscovery in the ruins of Kouyunjik in the 19th century, under the direction of excavators such as Austen Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam, provided scholars with much of what is known of Mesopotamian literature, including the flood narrative of the Epic of Gilgamesh, translated by George Smith in 1872.

Death and legacy

The last years of Ashurbanipal's reign are obscure; he died in 631 BC and was succeeded by his son Ashur-etil-ilani. Within two decades of his death the Neo-Assyrian Empire had collapsed under pressure from the Babylonians and Medes, and Nineveh itself was destroyed in 612 BC. In later Mesopotamian, Greek and Armenian tradition Ashurbanipal was conflated with the legendary figure of Sardanapalus, depicted (unfairly, historians argue) as a decadent and effeminate ruler.
