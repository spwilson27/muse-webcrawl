Single transferable vote


A ranked-choice STV ballot

The single transferable vote (STV), sometimes known as proportional ranked-choice voting, is a multi-winner electoral system in which voters cast a single vote in the form of a ranked ballot. Votes are totalled, and a quota is established; candidates who reach the quota are elected, and their surplus votes are transferred to the next preferences of those voters. Candidates with the fewest votes are eliminated, and their votes likewise transfer, until all seats are filled. The system is designed to achieve proportional representation while allowing voters to choose among candidates, including within parties.

Counting

The most common quota is the Droop quota: the integer part of votes/(seats+1), plus one. Surplus transfers may use the Gregory method (fractional transfer of all ballot papers), the Wright system, or random sampling of ballot papers (the method long used in Ireland). Counting is iterative: after each election or elimination, ballots are re-examined at their next usable preference.

Use

STV is used for national elections in Ireland (since 1922) and Malta, for the Australian Senate (since 1949), for Northern Ireland Assembly and local elections, for Scottish local elections, and in various municipal and organizational elections worldwide, including in several US cities.

Properties

STV minimizes "wasted votes", gives voters power over individual candidates rather than only parties, and usually produces broadly proportional results, though proportionality depends on district magnitude. Its disadvantages include complex counting, by-elections that cannot easily preserve proportionality, and occasional non-monotonic results where ranking a candidate higher can (rarely) hurt them.
