On the Sublime

On the Sublime (Greek: Περὶ ὕψους, Perì hýpsous) is an ancient Greek treatise on literary criticism and rhetoric, traditionally attributed to "Longinus" and probably written in the 1st century AD. About a third of the work is lost. It defines the sublime — writing that lifts the audience into ecstasy and transports rather than merely persuades — and is one of the most influential works of criticism from antiquity.

Authorship

The surviving manuscript ascribes the work to "Dionysius or Longinus". Scholars have variously identified the author with Cassius Longinus, the 3rd-century rhetorician of Palmyra, or with an unknown writer of the 1st century; the latter view now generally prevails. The author is conventionally called Pseudo-Longinus.

Content

The treatise identifies five sources of sublimity: great thoughts, strong and inspired passion, the proper formation of figures, noble diction, and dignified composition. The first two are held to be largely innate; the rest can be taught. Sublimity, the author writes, is "a certain distinction and excellence in expression", the echo of a great soul. The work admires Homer, Sappho — whose Ode to Aphrodite is quoted in full, preserving the poem for posterity — Demosthenes, Plato and even the Book of Genesis, which it praises for the sublimity of "God said, 'Let there be light', and there was light."

Influence

After Nicolas Boileau's French translation of 1674, On the Sublime became a central text of European aesthetics. It shaped the 18th-century theory of the sublime developed by Edmund Burke and Immanuel Kant, and fed into Romanticism's cult of grandeur, terror and vastness in art and nature.
