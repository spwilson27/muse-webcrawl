Soft budget constraint

The soft budget constraint is an economic concept introduced by the Hungarian economist János Kornai in his 1980 book Economics of Shortage to explain the behavior of state-owned enterprises in socialist economies. A budget constraint is "soft" when an organization — typically a firm, bank, or government unit — expects that its financial deficits will repeatedly be covered by an external supporting organization, usually the state, and therefore need not fear bankruptcy or liquidation.

Under a hard budget constraint, by contrast, an organization's survival depends strictly on its own revenues covering its expenditures. Kornai argued that the soft budget constraint syndrome explained chronic shortages, weak price responsiveness, and low incentives for efficiency in classical socialist economies, where paternalistic state support was routine.

Mechanisms

Softening instruments include soft subsidies (negotiated fiscal grants), soft taxation (tailored exemptions), soft credit (loans extended regardless of repayment prospects), and soft administrative prices. Kornai later extended the concept to market economies, pointing to bank bailouts, "too big to fail" institutions, and rescued industries as instances of the same syndrome. Theoretical work by Dewatripont and Maskin modeled the phenomenon as a dynamic commitment problem: because sunk costs make refinancing ex post attractive, funders cannot credibly commit not to bail out failing projects.
