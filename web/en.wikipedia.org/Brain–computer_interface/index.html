Brain–computer interface

A brain–computer interface (BCI), sometimes called a brain–machine interface, is a direct communication pathway between the brain and an external device. BCIs acquire signals from the central nervous system, decode the user's intent with signal-processing and machine-learning algorithms, and translate it into commands — for example moving a cursor, spelling words, or controlling a robotic arm or wheelchair. Many systems also provide feedback, and some stimulate the brain in return.

Approaches

Non-invasive BCIs usually record the electroencephalogram (EEG) from scalp electrodes, exploiting phenomena such as sensorimotor rhythms, P300 event-related potentials and steady-state visually evoked potentials. Partially invasive systems use electrocorticography (ECoG) electrodes on the brain surface, while invasive systems implant microelectrode arrays — such as the Utah array — directly into cortex, achieving the highest signal quality at the cost of surgery and long-term stability challenges.

History and applications

The term was coined in the 1970s by Jacques Vidal, whose laboratory demonstrated EEG-based control of a computer. Research accelerated from the 1990s: Matthew Nagle, a man with quadriplegia, used an implanted BrainGate system in 2004 to control a cursor and prosthetic devices. Companies and labs including Neuralink, Synchron and Blackrock Neurotech have since pursued clinical implants aimed at restoring communication and movement for people with paralysis and ALS. Other applications include neurorehabilitation after stroke, neuroprosthetics, hands-free control, and experimental consumer devices for gaming and attention monitoring.

Ethics and challenges

BCIs raise questions of mental privacy, agency, security of neural data and equitable access, alongside technical hurdles of bandwidth, decoder robustness and biocompatibility.
