Photoelasticity

Photoelasticity describes changes in the optical properties of a material under mechanical deformation and is specifically used to describe a method of experimental stress analysis. It is a property of all dielectric media and is often used to experimentally determine the stress distribution in a material, where it gives a picture of stress distributions around discontinuities in materials.

Photoelastic experiments (also informally referred to as photoelasticity, and even sometimes photo-elasticity) are an important tool for determining critical stress points in a material, and are used for determining stress concentration in irregular geometries.

Principle

The method is based on the property of birefringence, as observed in certain transparent materials. Birefringence is a phenomenon in which a ray of light passing through a given material experiences two refractive indices. When a photoelastic specimen is subjected to stress, the induced birefringence is proportional to the difference of the principal stresses at each point. Viewed between crossed polarizers in a polariscope, the specimen shows coloured isochromatic fringes marking lines of equal principal stress difference, together with dark isoclinic bands indicating principal stress directions.

History

The photoelastic phenomenon was first discovered by the Scottish physicist David Brewster in 1815, who observed stress-induced birefringence in glass. In the 1930s and 1940s the method was developed into a major engineering technique using transparent plastics such as Bakelite and later epoxy resins, and it remains in use for validating finite-element stress analyses.

Applications

Photoelasticity has been widely used in civil, mechanical and aeronautical engineering to find stress concentrations around holes, notches and fillets, and in geology and medicine, for example in the study of stresses in bones and prostheses.
