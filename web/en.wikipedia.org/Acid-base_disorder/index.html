Acid–base disorder

Acid–base imbalance is an abnormality of the human body's normal balance of acids and bases that causes the plasma pH to deviate out of the normal range (7.35–7.45). In the fetus, the normal range differs based on which umbilical vessel is sampled. It can exist in varying levels of severity, some of which can be life-threatening.

Classification

An excess of acid is called acidosis or acidemia, while an excess in base is called alkalosis or alkalemia. The process that causes the imbalance is classified based on its cause: respiratory (changing carbon dioxide levels, controlled by the lungs) or metabolic (changing bicarbonate levels, controlled mainly by the kidneys). Thus the four simple disorders are metabolic acidosis, metabolic alkalosis, respiratory acidosis, and respiratory alkalosis. A patient may simultaneously have more than one disorder (mixed disorders), and compensatory mechanisms — renal adjustment of bicarbonate for respiratory disorders, and changes in ventilation for metabolic disorders — act to return pH toward normal.

Causes

Metabolic acidosis may result from increased acid production (e.g. lactic acidosis, diabetic ketoacidosis), loss of bicarbonate (e.g. severe diarrhoea), or reduced acid excretion in kidney failure. The anion gap helps distinguish causes. Metabolic alkalosis commonly follows loss of gastric acid through vomiting or use of diuretics. Respiratory acidosis results from hypoventilation (e.g. chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, respiratory depression), while respiratory alkalosis results from hyperventilation (e.g. anxiety, fever, early sepsis, altitude).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on arterial blood gas analysis together with serum electrolytes; interpretive frameworks include the Henderson–Hasselbalch approach, the base-excess method, and the Stewart (physicochemical) approach. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause, with supportive measures such as ventilation or, in selected cases, bicarbonate administration or dialysis.
