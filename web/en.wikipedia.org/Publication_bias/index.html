Publication bias

An asymmetrical funnel plot is a classic visual sign of possible publication bias.

Publication bias is the tendency of researchers, editors, and funders to publish or otherwise disseminate research results based on their direction and statistical significance: studies with positive, striking, or statistically significant findings are far more likely to be published than studies with null or negative results, which are often left in the "file drawer". Because systematic reviews and meta-analyses can only summarise what is published, publication bias can make treatments appear more effective, and effects larger, than they really are, with consequences for medicine and policy.

Evidence and causes

Empirical studies of publication bias show that trials with significant results are published sooner and in higher-profile journals, that authors are less likely even to submit null results, and that industry-sponsored studies with unfavourable findings have sometimes been delayed or suppressed — as in analyses of antidepressant trials, where comparing published papers with the full FDA registry record showed the published literature overstated effect sizes substantially. Related distortions include outcome-reporting bias (publishing only favourable endpoints), language bias, citation bias, and the incentives of a publish-or-perish culture.

Detection and remedies

Meta-analysts screen for bias using funnel plots and statistical tests such as Egger's regression and trim-and-fill. Structural remedies include prospective trial registration (e.g. ClinicalTrials.gov and the ICMJE's 2005 registration requirement), results-reporting mandates, registered reports in which journals accept studies before results are known, open data and preprints, and journals dedicated to null results. Publication bias is considered a contributing factor in the replication crisis affecting psychology and other fields.
