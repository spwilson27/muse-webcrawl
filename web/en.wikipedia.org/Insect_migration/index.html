Insect migration
Notable migrantsMonarch butterfly, painted lady, desert locust, globe skimmer dragonfly
ScaleTrillions of insects annually
MechanismsWind-assisted flight, celestial and magnetic compasses

Insect migration is the seasonal movement of insects, particularly those by species of dragonflies, beetles, butterflies, and moths. The distances travelled vary from a few dozen kilometres to thousands of kilometres across continents and oceans. Because individual insects are small and short-lived, many migrations are multi-generational: no single individual completes the round trip.

Notable examples

The monarch butterfly migrates between North America and overwintering sites in Mexico and California, a journey completed over several generations. The painted lady undertakes one of the longest butterfly migrations, between Europe and Africa. The globe skimmer dragonfly crosses the Indian Ocean between India and East Africa, exploiting seasonal winds. Desert locusts migrate in enormous swarms that can devastate crops.

Navigation

Migrating insects use a combination of cues: the position of the sun and polarised light patterns, the Earth's magnetic field, landmarks, and prevailing winds. High-altitude migrants select favourable airstreams, ascending into fast-moving winds and descending when winds turn unfavourable. Radar entomology has revealed vast seasonal flows — an estimated 3.5 trillion insects migrate over southern England each year.

Ecological significance

Migrants transfer energy and nutrients between ecosystems, pollinate plants, and provide food for birds and other predators. Migratory pests such as locusts and armyworm moths have major agricultural impacts, making migration forecasting important for food security.
