Albert Bruce Sabin (August 26, 1906 – March 3, 1993) was a Polish-American medical researcher who developed the oral polio vaccine, a live attenuated vaccine administered on a sugar cube or in drops, which became the principal weapon in the near-eradication of poliomyelitis worldwide.

Early life and research

Sabin emigrated with his family from Białystok to the United States in 1921. He studied medicine at New York University and, after work at the Rockefeller Institute, joined the University of Cincinnati in 1939. His early research established that poliovirus enters the body through the digestive tract rather than the nose, and during the Second World War he developed vaccines against dengue fever and Japanese encephalitis for the US Army.

Oral polio vaccine

Against the rival approach of Jonas Salk's inactivated (injected) vaccine, Sabin argued that a live, weakened virus vaccine would produce stronger, longer-lasting intestinal immunity and could interrupt transmission. His attenuated strains were tested in large trials in the Soviet Union, organised with Mikhail Chumakov, involving millions of children in 1959–1960. The Sabin vaccine was licensed in the United States in 1961–1962 and, because it was cheap and easy to administer orally, became the vaccine of choice for mass campaigns and for the World Health Organization's eradication programme.

Later life

Sabin declined to patent his vaccine, forgoing enormous royalties. He later served as president of the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel (1970–1972) and continued researching possible viral causes of cancer. He died in Washington, D.C., in 1993.
