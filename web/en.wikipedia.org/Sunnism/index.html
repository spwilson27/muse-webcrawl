Sunnism

Sunnism, also known as Sunni Islam (Ahl as-Sunnah wa'l-Jamā'ah, "the people of the Sunnah and the community"), is the largest branch of Islam, followed by an estimated 87–90 percent of the world's Muslims. Its name comes from sunnah, the traditions and practices of the Prophet Muhammad, which serve alongside the Quran as a principal source of guidance.

Beliefs

Sunnis affirm the six articles of faith, including belief in God, angels, revealed books, prophets, the Day of Judgement and divine decree. They recognise the first four caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — as the rightly guided caliphs (Rashidun) who succeeded Muhammad, in order. Religious authority rests with the community of scholars (ulama) rather than a clerical hierarchy.

Schools of law

Sunni jurisprudence (fiqh) developed into four surviving schools (madhhabs): the Hanafi, the largest and most widespread; the Maliki, dominant in North and West Africa; the Shafi'i, common in East Africa, Yemen and Southeast Asia; and the Hanbali, influential in Arabia. The schools differ in legal methodology and detail while recognising one another as legitimate.

History

The Sunni tradition crystallised in the centuries after Muhammad's death in 632 CE, in the course of disputes over succession to leadership of the community — the same disputes that gave rise to Shia Islam. Classical Sunni theology and law were systematised under the Abbasid caliphate, and Sunnism became the majority tradition across most of the Muslim world, from Morocco to Indonesia.

Practice

Sunni practice centres on the Five Pillars of Islam: the profession of faith (shahada), prayer (salah), almsgiving (zakat), fasting during Ramadan (sawm), and the pilgrimage to Mecca (hajj). Sufism developed largely within the Sunni world as its mystical tradition.
