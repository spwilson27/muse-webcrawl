Maya script, also known as Maya glyphs or Maya hieroglyphs, is the writing system of the Maya civilization of Mesoamerica. It is the only Mesoamerican writing system that has been substantially deciphered. The earliest inscriptions date to the 3rd century BCE, and writing continued until shortly after the arrival of the Spanish conquistadors in the 16th century. Maya writing used logograms complemented by a set of syllabic glyphs, combining to represent the full range of Mayan languages.

At its height, the script was used throughout the Maya area — modern-day southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize, and western Honduras and El Salvador — carved on stone monuments and lintels, painted on pottery and murals, and written in folding-screen bark-paper books (codices). Of the thousands of pre-Columbian books, only four codices survive, including the famous Dresden Codex, many having been destroyed by Spanish missionaries such as Diego de Landa, who nonetheless left a key account of the script.

Decipherment

The decisive breakthrough in decipherment came in the 1950s, when Yuri Knorozov demonstrated the script's partly phonetic, syllabic nature. Subsequent work by Tatiana Proskouriakoff, who showed that inscriptions recorded dynastic history, and by a generation of epigraphers including Linda Schele and David Stuart, led to the reading of most of the surviving texts by the 1990s.

Structure

Maya glyphs are arranged in blocks, each typically containing a main sign with affixes. Readings proceed in paired columns, left to right and top to bottom. The script combines logographic signs (standing for whole words) with syllabograms (consonant–vowel syllables) that could spell words phonetically and confirm logogram readings.
