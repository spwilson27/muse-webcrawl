The Malay Peninsula is a long, narrow appendage of Southeast Asia extending approximately 1,480 kilometres south-east from the mainland, separating the Indian Ocean (via the Strait of Malacca and the Andaman Sea) from the South China Sea. Politically it is divided among Myanmar, Thailand, Malaysia, and—at its southernmost point—Singapore, which lies just off the tip.

The peninsula separates the Indian Ocean from the Pacific's marginal seas.

Geography

A mountainous spine, the Tenasserim Hills and the Titiwangsa Mountains, runs down the peninsula, reaching 2,187 metres at Mount Tahan. The narrowest point is the Kra Isthmus in Thailand. The peninsula has a tropical rainforest climate and is covered in large part by equatorial rainforest, with extensive mangrove swamps on the western coast. Tin deposits and, later, rubber and oil palm plantations shaped its colonial and modern economies.

History

The peninsula stood astride the maritime trade route between India and China from antiquity. Indianised kingdoms such as Langkasuka and, later, Srivijaya dominated its coasts; in the 15th century the Malacca Sultanate controlled the strait. Portuguese, Dutch and British colonial rule followed, and the peninsula's Malaysian states formed the Federation of Malaya in 1948, independent from 1957.
