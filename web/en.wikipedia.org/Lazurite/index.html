Lazurite is a tectosilicate mineral of the sodalite group with sulfate, sulfur, and chloride, having the chemical formula (Na,Ca)8[(S,SO4,Cl)1–2|(AlSiO4)6]. It is a feldspathoid and a member of the sodalite group. Lazurite crystallizes in the isometric system, although well-formed crystals are rare; it is usually massive and forms the bulk of the gemstone lapis lazuli.

Properties

Lazurite's intense blue color is caused by the presence of trisulfur radical anions (S3−) trapped in the mineral's cage-like aluminosilicate framework. It has a Mohs hardness of 5 to 5.5, a specific gravity of about 2.4, imperfect cleavage, and a dull luster; it may show white calcite veins and brassy pyrite inclusions when part of lapis lazuli. Strong heating can intensify its color, and it is slowly attacked by hydrochloric acid, releasing hydrogen sulfide.

Occurrence

Lazurite occurs in contact-metamorphosed limestones, where silica-undersaturated magmas have reacted with carbonate rocks. The classic and historically most important locality is Sar-e Sang in Badakhshan Province, Afghanistan, mined for more than 6,000 years and the source of nearly all lapis lazuli of the ancient world. Other localities include the Lake Baikal region of Siberia, Vesuvius and the Alban Hills in Italy, Chile (Ovalle), Myanmar, Canada, and the United States.

Use

Ground lazurite (in the form of high-grade lapis lazuli) was the source of ultramarine, the most prized and expensive blue pigment of the medieval and Renaissance periods — literally "beyond the sea," referring to its Afghan origin. Ultramarine colored the robes of the Virgin Mary in countless paintings and was more costly than gold until a synthetic version was developed in the 1820s. The name lazurite derives from the Persian lazhward, meaning "blue," by way of the same root that gives "azure."
