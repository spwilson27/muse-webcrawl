Glycosylation

Glycosylation is the reaction in which a carbohydrate (a glycosyl donor) is attached to a hydroxyl or other functional group of another molecule, typically a protein or lipid. In biology, glycosylation is a form of co- and post-translational modification that occurs mainly in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and it affects the majority of proteins synthesised in the secretory pathway. The carbohydrate chains (glycans) it adds influence protein folding, stability, trafficking, and cell–cell recognition.

Types

In N-linked glycosylation, a preassembled oligosaccharide is transferred en bloc to the nitrogen of asparagine residues in the sequence Asn–X–Ser/Thr, then trimmed and elaborated in the Golgi into high-mannose, complex, or hybrid structures. In O-linked glycosylation, sugars are added one at a time to the oxygen of serine or threonine, with no single consensus sequence. Other forms include C-mannosylation, glypiation (GPI anchors), and the intracellular O-GlcNAc modification, which acts as a nutrient sensor.

Significance

Glycosylation determines the blood-group antigens on red cells, modulates the activity of antibodies (affecting the performance of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies), and is altered in cancer and in congenital disorders of glycosylation. Because most cell-surface and secreted proteins are glycosylated, glycans are often called the cell's "sugar code" for molecular recognition.
