Neutron star merger

Two neutron stars inspiral, radiating gravitational waves before they collide.

A neutron star merger is the collision of two neutron stars in a binary system. As the stars orbit each other, they lose energy by emitting gravitational waves and spiral inward over hundreds of millions of years; in the final seconds the inspiral accelerates until the stars touch and merge, forming either a more massive neutron star or a black hole surrounded by a debris torus. Binary neutron star systems such as the Hulse–Taylor binary provided the first indirect evidence for gravitational waves.

Kilonovae and element synthesis

Mergers eject neutron-rich matter in which rapid neutron capture (the r-process) forges roughly half of the elements heavier than iron, including gold, platinum, and uranium. The radioactive decay of this ejecta powers a thermal transient called a kilonova, redder and faster-evolving than a supernova. Short gamma-ray bursts are thought to be produced by relativistic jets launched during some mergers.

GW170817

On 17 August 2017 the LIGO and Virgo interferometers detected the gravitational-wave signal GW170817 from a merger about 130 million light-years away in the galaxy NGC 4993. A gamma-ray burst (GRB 170817A) followed 1.7 seconds later, and telescopes worldwide observed the kilonova AT 2017gfo across the electromagnetic spectrum — the first multimessenger observation of a neutron star merger. The event confirmed mergers as r-process sites, constrained the neutron star equation of state, gave an independent measurement of the Hubble constant, and bounded the difference between the speeds of gravity and light.
