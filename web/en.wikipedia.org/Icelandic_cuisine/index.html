Icelandic cuisine

Icelandic cuisine, the cuisine of Iceland, has a long history shaped by the island's harsh climate, sparse arable land, and reliance on fishing and sheep farming. Traditional foods are dominated by lamb, dairy, and fish, preserved by smoking, salting, drying, fermenting and pickling in whey — techniques developed when almost nothing could be wasted and fresh food was unavailable for much of the year. Modern Icelandic cooking has embraced New Nordic influences and the island's greenhouse-grown vegetables, but traditional dishes remain central to festivals and national identity.

Traditional foods

Famous (and to outsiders notorious) specialities include hákarl, Greenland shark cured by fermentation and drying, whose ammonia-rich flesh was historically detoxified by this process; harðfiskur (wind-dried haddock or cod, eaten with butter); hangikjöt (smoked lamb); svið (singed sheep's head); blóðmör and lifrarpylsa (blood and liver puddings); and skyr, a cultured dairy product similar to strained yogurt eaten for over a thousand years. Dark, sweet rye bread (rúgbrauð) is traditionally baked in pots buried in geothermal sand. Drinks include brennivín, a caraway-flavoured schnapps nicknamed "Black Death", and strong Icelandic coffee.

Þorrablót and everyday food

At the midwinter festival Þorrablót, buffets of þorramatur present the preserved foods of the old farming society, usually washed down with brennivín. Everyday modern eating includes fish and lamb dishes, the ubiquitous Icelandic hot dog (pylsa) with crispy onions and remoulade, lamb soup (kjötsúpa), and pastries such as kleinur (twisted doughnuts) and pönnukökur (thin pancakes).

History

Settlement-era Icelanders (from 874 CE) farmed sheep and cattle and exploited seabirds, eggs, whales and fish. The Little Ice Age, trade monopolies and eruptions such as Laki (1783) caused famines that suppressed culinary development, while isolation preserved medieval methods long after they vanished elsewhere in Scandinavia. Whale meat and puffin remain eaten, controversially, in small quantities. Since the early 2000s, tourism and chefs championing local ingredients have revived interest in Icelandic food traditions.
