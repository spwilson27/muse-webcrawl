Wiener deconvolution

In mathematics and signal processing, Wiener deconvolution is a technique for reversing the effect of a convolution — for example, undoing blur in an image or distortion in a recorded signal — while taking noise into account. It derives from the Wiener filter developed by Norbert Wiener in the 1940s and seeks the estimate of the original signal that minimises the mean-square error.

Principle

Suppose a signal f has been convolved with a known response function g and corrupted by additive noise. Naive division by the Fourier transform of g would exactly invert the convolution but greatly amplify noise wherever g has small values. The Wiener deconvolution instead applies, at each frequency, a filter that multiplies by the conjugate of the system's transfer function divided by the sum of its squared magnitude and the noise-to-signal power ratio. Where the signal dominates, the filter behaves like an inverse filter; where noise dominates, it suppresses the frequency.

Applications

Wiener deconvolution is used in image deblurring (including restoration of photographs and astronomical images), in seismology to sharpen reflected signals, in audio restoration, and in communications equalisation. It requires knowledge (or an estimate) of the point spread function and of the noise level; iterative alternatives such as Richardson–Lucy deconvolution are preferred when these are unknown or when the blur is not linear and stationary.
