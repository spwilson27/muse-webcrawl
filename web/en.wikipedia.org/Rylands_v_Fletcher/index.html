Rylands v Fletcher

The mill reservoir whose escape flooded Fletcher's mine.

Rylands v Fletcher
CourtHouse of Lords
Decided1868 (Exchequer Chamber 1866)
Citation(1868) LR 3 HL 330

Rylands v Fletcher [1868] is a landmark English tort law case that established a doctrine of strict liability for the escape of dangerous things. The defendants, mill owners, had a reservoir built on their land; unknown to them, it sat above disused mine shafts, and when filled, water burst through and flooded the claimant's neighbouring coal mine.

The rule

Mr Justice Blackburn held that a person who, for his own purposes, brings onto his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it in at his peril, and is prima facie liable for damage caused by its escape — regardless of negligence. Lord Cairns in the House of Lords qualified the rule to "non-natural" uses of land.

Influence

The rule in Rylands v Fletcher became influential throughout the common-law world, though its scope was later narrowed in England (notably in Cambridge Water Co v Eastern Counties Leather [1994], requiring foreseeability of the type of damage) and it was absorbed into negligence or rejected in some other jurisdictions.
