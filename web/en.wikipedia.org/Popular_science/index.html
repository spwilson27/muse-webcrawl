Popular science

Popular science (also called pop-science or popsci) is an interpretation of science intended for a general audience. While science journalism focuses on recent scientific developments, popular science is broad-ranging and often written by scientists themselves or by science writers. It may take the form of books, magazine articles, documentary television programs, podcasts, and museum exhibitions.

The genre provides a bridge between the scientific literature as a professional medium and the realms of popular political and cultural discourse. Popular science writers seek to demystify scientific concepts, present research accurately but accessibly, and convey the excitement and human interest of scientific discovery without requiring specialist training of their readers.

History

Popular science writing flourished in the 19th century alongside rising literacy and public lecture culture; figures such as Michael Faraday, whose Christmas Lectures at the Royal Institution began in 1825, and Thomas Henry Huxley brought science to wide audiences. In the 20th century, magazines such as Popular Science (founded 1872), Scientific American, and later New Scientist and Discover, along with television presenters such as Carl Sagan, whose 1980 series Cosmos: A Personal Voyage reached hundreds of millions of viewers, defined the field. Sagan's book Cosmos and Stephen Hawking's A Brief History of Time (1988) became landmark bestsellers.

Role and criticism

Popular science is credited with raising public scientific literacy, inspiring careers in science, and informing democratic debate on issues such as climate change, medicine, and technology. Critics sometimes note a tension between accuracy and simplification, the tendency to sensationalize results, and the overselling of preliminary findings; scientists and writers respond that careful popularization can be faithful while remaining readable.
