Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287 – c. 212 BC) was an Ancient Greek mathematician, physicist, engineer, astronomer, and inventor from the ancient city of Syracuse in Sicily. Although few details of his life are known, he is regarded as one of the leading scientists in classical antiquity. Considered the greatest mathematician of ancient history, and one of the greatest of all time, Archimedes anticipated modern calculus and analysis by applying the concept of the infinitely small and the method of exhaustion to derive and rigorously prove a range of geometrical theorems. These include the area of a circle, the surface area and volume of a sphere, the area of an ellipse, the area under a parabola, the volume of a segment of a paraboloid of revolution, the volume of a segment of a hyperboloid of revolution, and the area of a spiral.

Life

Archimedes was born in Syracuse, a Greek colony in Sicily, the son of an astronomer named Phidias. He may have studied in Alexandria with the successors of Euclid. He corresponded with mathematicians there, including Eratosthenes, to whom he addressed the Method of Mechanical Theorems and the "Cattle Problem". During the Roman siege of Syracuse in the Second Punic War, his war machines — cranes, catapults, and (in legendary accounts) burning mirrors — delayed the city's capture. He was killed in 212 BC by a Roman soldier, reportedly while absorbed in a geometry problem, despite orders that he be spared; Cicero later described finding his tomb, marked by a sphere inscribed in a cylinder, the figure of his proudest theorem.

Mathematics

In Measurement of a Circle Archimedes bounded π between 310/71 and 31/7 by inscribing and circumscribing polygons of up to 96 sides. In On the Sphere and Cylinder he proved that a sphere has two-thirds the volume and surface area of its circumscribing cylinder. On Spirals, Quadrature of the Parabola, and On Conoids and Spheroids extended the method of exhaustion. The Sand Reckoner devised a notation for very large numbers to show that the grains of sand filling the universe were finite (he estimated 1063). In the 20th century the Archimedes Palimpsest — a 10th-century copy of his works overwritten as a prayer book — revealed the lost Method, in which he explained how mechanical reasoning with indivisibles guided his discoveries, and the Stomachion puzzle.

Physics and inventions

Archimedes founded hydrostatics: his principle states that a body immersed in fluid experiences an upthrust equal to the weight of the fluid displaced — the insight behind the famous "Eureka!" story of detecting the adulterated golden crown. In On the Equilibrium of Planes he derived the law of the lever mathematically, prompting his boast that with a place to stand he could move the Earth. Inventions attributed to him include the screw pump for raising water, compound pulleys, an improved odometer, and a geared planetarium. The "iron hand" or claw of Archimedes supposedly lifted Roman ships from the water during the siege.

Legacy

Gauss and other mathematicians have ranked Archimedes with Newton and Euler. His works were preserved through Byzantine and Arabic scholarship and, translated into Latin in the Renaissance, deeply influenced Galileo, Kepler, and the development of calculus. A crater and mountains on the Moon, an asteroid, and the Royal Society's motto-adjacent legend "Eureka" commemorate him.
