Champagne (wine region)

CountryFrance
LocationGrand Est, northeastern France
Principal grapesPinot noir, Chardonnay, Pinot Meunier
Area under vineAbout 34,000 hectares
AppellationChampagne AOC (established 1936)

The Champagne wine region is a historic province in northeastern France, centered on the cities of Reims and Épernay, that produces the sparkling wine known as Champagne. Only sparkling wine made within the legally delimited region, from specified grapes and by the traditional method, may bear the name; the appellation is protected in most of the world.

Geography and climate

The region lies near the northern limit of viable viticulture, at about 49 degrees north latitude. Its cool continental climate and chalk subsoil — remnants of ancient seabeds — provide excellent drainage and a distinctive mineral character. Vineyards are organized into districts including the Montagne de Reims, the Vallée de la Marne, the Côte des Blancs, the Côte de Sézanne, and the Aube (Côte des Bar).

Grapes and production

The three principal grape varieties are Pinot noir, Chardonnay, and Pinot Meunier; four others, including Arbane and Petit Meslier, are permitted in small quantities. Most Champagne is a blend of grapes, villages, and vintages, designed to maintain a consistent house style. The wine undergoes a second fermentation in the bottle — the méthode champenoise — followed by aging on its lees, riddling, disgorgement, and dosage before release. Villages are graded on the historic échelle des crus scale, with seventeen grand cru and forty-four premier cru villages.

History

Wines from Champagne were known in Roman times, but the sparkling style emerged in the 17th and 18th centuries, aided by stronger glass and cork closures; the cellar master Dom Pérignon is popularly, if inaccurately, credited with its invention. The great houses of Reims and Épernay industrialized production in the 19th century. Grower riots in 1911 led to the delimitation of the region, codified in the 1927 law and the 1936 appellation. In 2015 the Champagne hillsides, houses, and cellars were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Protection of the name

The Comité Champagne defends the appellation worldwide against generic use of the name for other sparkling wines, an effort rooted in the 1891 Treaty of Madrid and reinforced by modern trade agreements.
