Diesel multiple unit

A diesel multiple unit (DMU) is a multiple-unit train consisting of self-propelled carriages powered by one or more diesel engines. Unlike a locomotive-hauled train, a DMU requires no separate locomotive, as the engines are incorporated into one or more of the carriages; multiple units can be coupled together and driven from a single cab.

A two-car diesel multiple unit in regional service.

Transmission types

  • Diesel-mechanical: the engine drives the wheels through a gearbox, common in light first-generation units.
  • Diesel-hydraulic: power is transmitted through a fluid torque converter.
  • Diesel-electric: the engine drives a generator or alternator supplying electric traction motors; the most common arrangement in modern and heavy units.

Use

DMUs are widely used on non-electrified regional and commuter routes, where their ability to accelerate quickly, reverse without running a locomotive around, and operate economically on lightly trafficked lines is valuable. Famous early examples include the German "Flying Hamburger" of the 1930s; modern examples include British Rail Classes 150–185, the German Talent and Lint families, and numerous Japanese local trains.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include operational flexibility, redundancy (a failed engine rarely disables the whole train), and low infrastructure cost on unelectrified lines. Disadvantages include higher maintenance of multiple engines, underfloor noise and vibration, and fuel costs compared with electric traction where electrification exists.
