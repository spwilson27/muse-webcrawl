The Regency era in the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland is named for the regency of George, Prince of Wales, who ruled as Prince Regent from 1811 to 1820 during the final illness of his father, George III. Strictly, the Regency covers 1811–1820, ending when the Prince Regent succeeded as George IV. In a broader cultural sense, the term often spans from about 1795 to 1837, covering the late Georgian period through the reign of William IV.

Politics and society

The era coincided with the final years of the Napoleonic Wars, culminating in the British victory at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815. Postwar economic dislocation, the Corn Laws, and agitation for parliamentary reform produced unrest, including the Spa Fields riots, the march of the Blanketeers, and the Peterloo Massacre of 1819, in which cavalry charged a peaceful reform meeting in Manchester. Parliament responded with the repressive Six Acts. The period also saw the beginnings of industrial transformation and the growth of a distinct aristocratic culture of leisure centered on London's social Season, country house parties, and the seaside resort of Brighton, where the Prince Regent built the Royal Pavilion.

Culture

Literature

The Regency is associated with Jane Austen, whose major novels — Sense and Sensibility (1811), Pride and Prejudice (1813), Mansfield Park (1814), Emma (1815), and the posthumous Persuasion (1817) — satirized the manners of the landed gentry. Romantic poets including Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley, and John Keats flourished, and Sir Walter Scott popularized the historical novel with the Waverley novels.

Architecture and fashion

Regency architecture, promoted by John Nash's work in London (Regent Street, Regent's Park, Carlton House Terrace) and by stuccoed terraces in Brighton and Bath, favored graceful neoclassical lines. Fashion moved to the high-waisted Empire silhouette for women and the tailored dandyism of Beau Brummell for men.

The Regency in popular culture

The period remains a perennial setting for historical romance, from Georgette Heyer's Regency novels to modern screen adaptations such as Bridgerton.
