Sextant

A marine sextant

A sextant is a doubly reflecting navigation instrument that measures the angular distance between two visible objects. Its primary use is to measure the angle between an astronomical object and the horizon for use in celestial navigation — a measurement known as "shooting" or "taking a sight," which yields the observer's latitude or, combined with accurate time, a line of position.

Design

The sextant's frame forms a sector of a circle — about one-sixth of a full circle, hence the name (Latin sextans, "a sixth"). An index arm pivots at the centre of the arc, carrying an index mirror; a second, half-silvered horizon glass is fixed to the frame. By reflection, the navigator brings the image of the Sun or a star down to the horizon viewed through a small telescope and reads the angle off the graduated arc. Because the reflection doubles the arm's motion, the arc is graduated to read twice the actual angle, allowing measurements up to about 120°. The instrument was developed around 1731 independently by John Hadley in England and Thomas Godfrey in America, succeeding the earlier octant.

Use in navigation

The most common sight is the Sun's altitude at local apparent noon, which gives latitude directly. Morning and evening star sights, reduced with almanac data and sight-reduction tables or calculation, give lines of position whose intersection fixes the ship. Sextants remained standard equipment on ships well into the satellite era and are still carried as a backup, immune to electronic failure, as well as being used in surveying and in early aviation (as bubble sextants) and spacecraft.

Because the sextant measures angles relative to the visible horizon, users must correct for dip of the horizon, refraction, and instrumental errors. Skilled navigators can achieve accuracy of about 0.1–0.2 nautical miles under good conditions.
