Spectrum

A spectrum (plural spectra) is a condition that is not a single set of specific values but can vary, without gaps, across a continuum. The word was first used scientifically in optics to describe the rainbow of colours in visible light after passing through a prism. Since then, the concept has been applied to many domains in which a quantity is distributed over a range — from electromagnetic radiation and sound to political opinion, biological variation and mathematical operators.

Origins in optics

Isaac Newton used the Latin word spectrum ("appearance" or "apparition") in 1671 to describe the band of colours produced when sunlight passed through a prism, showing that white light is composed of light of all colours. In the early 19th century, Joseph von Fraunhofer catalogued dark absorption lines in the solar spectrum, and spectroscopy — developed by Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen — showed that each chemical element produces a characteristic pattern of spectral lines, allowing the composition of stars and gases to be determined from their light.

Electromagnetic spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum spans radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, ordered by frequency or wavelength. Only a narrow band is visible to the human eye. Measurements across the full spectrum underpin astronomy, telecommunications, medicine and remote sensing.

Other uses

  • In physics, emission and absorption spectra reveal atomic and molecular structure; mass spectra display the distribution of particle masses.
  • In mathematics, the spectrum of an operator is the set of its eigenvalues and related values, central to functional analysis.
  • In politics, the political spectrum arranges ideologies from left to right.
  • In psychology and medicine, "spectrum" describes conditions such as autism whose presentation varies continuously in severity and form.
