Occupational license

An occupational license, also called an occupational licence, is a form of government regulation requiring a license to pursue a particular profession or vocation for compensation. It is usually claimed that occupational licensing protects the public, for example by requiring practitioners in fields such as medicine, law, and engineering to demonstrate a minimum level of competence.

A professional license displayed in a practitioner's office.

Scope

In the United States, the share of workers required to hold an occupational license grew from less than 5 percent in the early 1950s to roughly a quarter of the workforce by the early twenty-first century. Licensed occupations range from doctors and lawyers to barbers, florists, and interior designers in some jurisdictions.

Debate

Economists have debated the effects of licensing on wages, employment, consumer prices, and service quality. Critics, following arguments made by Milton Friedman, contend that licensing often restricts entry and raises earnings of incumbents without commensurate quality gains. Supporters argue that licensing assures minimum standards where consumers cannot easily judge quality. Enforcement of licensing requirements interacts with workplace regulation such as occupational hygiene standards.

Alternatives

Less restrictive alternatives include certification, in which a title is protected but practice is not restricted, and registration, in which practitioners are merely listed with a regulatory body.
