Reform movement

A reform movement is a type of social movement that aims to bring a social or political system closer to the community's ideal. A reform movement is distinguished from more radical movements, such as revolutionary movements, which seek to change a system completely, and from conservative movements, which seek to preserve the status quo. Reform movements work through existing institutions — legislatures, courts, elections and public opinion — rather than overthrowing them.

Characteristics

Reform movements typically promote gradual, legal change in specific areas: extending the franchise, abolishing slavery, regulating industry, improving public health, education or working conditions. Reformers appeal to shared values and evidence of abuses, build coalitions, publish exposés, petition and lobby. Because they accept the system's basic framework, reform movements frequently face opposition both from defenders of the status quo and from radicals who consider their aims insufficient.

Historical examples

Canonical examples include the British Reform Acts of the 19th century expanding the electorate; the abolitionist movement; the temperance and women's suffrage movements; the Progressive Era in the United States (anti-trust law, food safety, municipal reform); the labour movement's campaigns for the eight-hour day and factory legislation; and late-20th-century environmental and consumer-protection movements. Historians debate whether particular reforms demobilise deeper change by relieving pressure, or serve as stepping-stones to more extensive transformation.
