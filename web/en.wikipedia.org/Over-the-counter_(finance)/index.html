Over-the-counter (finance)

Over-the-counter (OTC) or off-exchange trading is done directly between two parties, without the supervision of an exchange. It is contrasted with exchange trading, which occurs via facilities constructed specifically for the purpose of trading (i.e., exchanges). An OTC market is not centralised and is instead a decentralised network of dealers linked by phone and computer systems.

Overview

In OTC transactions, the price is not necessarily revealed to the public. OTC contracts are bilateral and each party faces credit risk concerns regarding its counterparty. Most derivatives contracts are traded OTC, including interest rate swaps, forward rate agreements, exotic options, and many foreign exchange contracts.

Stocks

Stocks that are not listed on a major exchange may trade over the counter. In the United States, OTC markets are operated by OTC Markets Group, which organises securities into tiers such as OTCQX, OTCQB, and the Pink Open Market based on the quality and quantity of information companies provide.

Risk and regulation

OTC markets involve counterparty risk, the danger that one party will default before the contract settles. Following the 2008 financial crisis, reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and EMIR in Europe pushed many standardised OTC derivatives toward central clearing.
