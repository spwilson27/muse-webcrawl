Cytokinesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cytokinesis is the part of the cell division process during which the cytoplasm of a single eukaryotic cell divides into two daughter cells. Cytoplasmic division begins during or after the late stages of nuclear division in mitosis and meiosis. During cytokinesis the spindle apparatus partitions and transports duplicated chromatids into the cytoplasm of the separate daughter cells.

In animal cells

In animal and many other eukaryotic cells, cytokinesis is driven by a contractile ring of actin and myosin II filaments assembled at the cell equator, positioned by signals from the mitotic spindle. The ring constricts, pulling the plasma membrane inward to form a cleavage furrow that deepens until the cell is pinched in two at the midbody; the final severing step, abscission, completes the separation. Errors in cytokinesis produce binucleate or tetraploid cells and are implicated in cancer development.

In plant cells

Because of their rigid cell walls, plant cells divide differently: vesicles derived from the Golgi apparatus are guided by the phragmoplast to the cell equator, where they fuse to build a cell plate that grows outward until it fuses with the parental wall, becoming the new middle lamella between the two daughter cells.

Variations

Some cells skip cytokinesis after nuclear division, forming syncytia — as in the early embryo of Drosophila and in skeletal muscle fibres. Fungi and algae use yet other mechanisms, coupling division to septum formation.
