Social hierarchy

Social hierarchy
FieldSociology; social psychology; ethology
RelatedSocial stratification; status; dominance hierarchy

A social hierarchy is an arrangement of individuals and groups within a society in which some have higher status, power, or prestige than others. Hierarchies may be formal, as in an organisation's chain of command, or informal, arising from differences in wealth, occupation, caste, gender, or reputation.

Forms

Sociologists describe long-lasting systems of rank as social stratification: slavery, caste, estate, and class systems differ in how open they are to mobility. Karl Marx analysed hierarchy through relations to the means of production, while Max Weber distinguished class, status, and party as separate dimensions of rank.

Origins

Anthropologists relate the emergence of institutionalised hierarchy to agricultural surpluses, population growth, and the rise of chiefdoms and states, contrasting egalitarian forager bands with ranked and stratified societies. In animal behaviour, the related concept of a dominance hierarchy describes rank orders established by contests and submission.

Psychological and social effects

Research on status shows that rank affects health, cognition, and behaviour; social dominance theory examines how group-based hierarchies in gender, race, and class are legitimised by shared ideologies. Humans readily perceive and navigate status cues, yet many movements — from religious communalism to modern egalitarianism — have sought to flatten or abolish hierarchy.
