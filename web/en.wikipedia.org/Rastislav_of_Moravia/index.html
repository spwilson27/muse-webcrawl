Rastislav or Rostislav (Latin: Rastiz; died after 870) was the second known ruler of Great Moravia, reigning from 846 until his deposition in 870. Installed on the Moravian throne by the East Frankish king Louis the German after the removal of his uncle Mojmir I, Rastislav initially cooperated with the Franks but soon pursued an independent foreign policy, seeking allies among the Bulgars, the Serbs, and the Byzantine Empire.

His reign is best remembered for the mission he requested from Constantinople: in 862 or 863 he asked Emperor Michael III to send teachers who could explain Christianity in the Slavonic language. The resulting mission of the brothers Cyril and Methodius in 863 introduced the Glagolitic alphabet and a Slavonic liturgy, a turning point in the cultural history of the Slavs.

Early reign

Louis the German placed Rastislav on the throne in 846, expecting a loyal client. By the early 850s, however, Rastislav was fortifying his realm and supporting Frankish rebels, including the sons of Louis himself. In 855 a Frankish campaign against him failed before his strongholds, and Louis was forced to withdraw.

The Byzantine mission

Frustrated by Frankish clergy who served Louis's political interests, Rastislav turned first to Rome and then to Constantinople. The mission of Cyril (Constantine) and Methodius produced the first literary Slavonic language, Old Church Slavonic, and translated scripture and liturgy. In 867–868 the brothers travelled to Rome, where Pope Hadrian II approved the Slavonic liturgy.

Downfall

In 869–870 Rastislav's nephew Svatopluk, who ruled the Principality of Nitra, conspired with the Franks. In 870 Svatopluk seized Rastislav and handed him to Carloman of Bavaria. A Frankish assembly condemned Rastislav to death; Louis commuted the sentence to blinding and imprisonment. Rastislav died in captivity in Bavaria.

Legacy

Rastislav was canonised by the Orthodox Church in the Czech Lands and Slovakia in 1994. He is honoured as the ruler whose initiative brought Slavonic letters and worship to Central Europe.
