Existential clause

Existential clauses typically begin with an unstressed "there", as in "There are two chairs in the room."

In linguistics, an existential clause is a clause that refers to the existence or presence of something, such as "There are two chairs in the room" or "Is there a God?" Existential clauses in different languages have different specific grammatical constructions. In English, the most common type begins with the dummy pronoun there followed by a form of the verb be.

English

English existential clauses use the expletive subject there, which occupies the subject position but carries no referential meaning; the notional subject follows the verb and controls agreement, as in "There is a problem" versus "There are problems". Related constructions include presentational and listing uses ("There's the bus!"), and the inventory reading found in contexts such as "What is there to eat?"

Other languages

Many languages express existence without a dummy subject: Mandarin Chinese uses the verb yǒu ("to have/exist"), Irish uses tá with a following noun phrase, and Finnish places the existential subject in the partitive case. Typological work classifies the strategies languages use to mark existence, location and possession, which often overlap.
