Fraunhofer diffraction

In optics, the Fraunhofer diffraction equation is used to model the diffraction of waves when the diffraction pattern is viewed at a long distance from the diffracting object, and also when it is viewed at the focal plane of an imaging lens. In contrast, diffraction at a short distance is described by Fresnel diffraction. The equation was named in honour of Joseph von Fraunhofer, although he was not actually the first to derive it.

Description

When a beam of light passes through an aperture, the far-field diffraction pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture function. For a single slit of width w illuminated by monochromatic light of wavelength λ, the intensity pattern consists of a bright central maximum flanked by weaker secondary maxima, with minima where sin θ = mλ/w for integer m ≠ 0. For a circular aperture, the pattern is the Airy pattern, whose first dark ring sets the angular resolution limit of telescopes and other optical instruments.

Validity

The Fraunhofer approximation requires that the Fresnel number be much less than one — effectively, that both the source and the observation screen are far from the aperture compared with the aperture size divided by the wavelength. A lens can satisfy the condition at short range by bringing the far field to its focal plane.
