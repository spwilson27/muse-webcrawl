Congenital insensitivity to pain

The Nav1.7 sodium channel, encoded by SCN9A, is essential to pain signalling.

SpecialtyNeurology
CausesGenetic mutations, e.g. SCN9A, NTRK1, PRDM12
OnsetBirth

Congenital insensitivity to pain (CIP), also known as congenital analgesia, is a rare condition in which a person cannot feel, and has never felt, physical pain. Because pain normally warns of injury and illness, affected people accumulate severe damage: repeated fractures, burns, bitten tongues and lips, eye injuries, and joint destruction, often beginning in early childhood. Intelligence and other sensations such as touch, pressure, and temperature discrimination are typically preserved or only partly affected, depending on the genetic cause.

Causes

CIP is inherited, usually in an autosomal recessive pattern. Mutations in SCN9A, which encodes the voltage-gated sodium channel Nav1.7 in pain-sensing neurons, abolish pain signalling while leaving most other sensation intact; people with this form also cannot smell (anosmia). Mutations in NTRK1 cause congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis (CIPA, also classed as HSAN type IV), in which pain-sensing and autonomic nerve fibres fail to develop, so patients also cannot sweat and may have intellectual disability. Mutations in PRDM12 disrupt the development of pain neurons themselves. The hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN) group covers several related disorders.

Management and prognosis

There is no treatment that restores pain sensation. Management is preventive: protective equipment, frequent inspection of the skin, eyes, teeth, and joints, prompt treatment of injuries and infections, and education of patients and families to substitute vigilance for pain. Historically many patients died young from trauma and infection, though with careful management life expectancy improves. Study of CIP has identified Nav1.7 and related proteins as targets for new non-opioid analgesic drugs.
