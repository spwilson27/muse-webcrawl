Parietal cell

Parietal cells, also known as oxyntic cells, are epithelial cells in the glands of the stomach that secrete hydrochloric acid (HCl) and intrinsic factor. They are found mainly in the fundus and body of the stomach. The acid they produce gives gastric juice a pH as low as 1.5 to 3.5, which activates the enzyme pepsin, kills ingested microbes, and denatures proteins.

Acid secretion is driven by the hydrogen–potassium ATPase — the "proton pump" — an enzyme that exchanges intracellular hydrogen ions for potassium in the membrane of the cell's secretory canaliculi. Secretion is stimulated by histamine (acting at H₂ receptors), acetylcholine, and the hormone gastrin, and it is these pathways that acid-suppressing drugs exploit: H₂ blockers such as famotidine reduce stimulation, while proton pump inhibitors such as omeprazole block the pump directly.

Intrinsic factor and disease

Parietal cells also secrete intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein essential for the absorption of vitamin B₁₂ in the ileum. In autoimmune atrophic gastritis, the immune system destroys parietal cells, and the resulting lack of intrinsic factor causes pernicious anaemia. Chronic infection with Helicobacter pylori can also damage the acid-producing mucosa, while excessive acid production contributes to peptic ulcers and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.
