Faxian

Faxian (法風)
Born: c. 337 CE, Xiangyuan, Shanxi
Died: c. 422 CE
Occupation: Buddhist monk, traveller, translator
Known for: Pilgrimage to India, 399–412 CE
Faxian crossed Central Asia on foot along the Silk Road to reach India.

Faxian (Chinese: 法風; c. 337 – c. 422 CE), also rendered Fa-Hien or Fa-hsien, was a Chinese Buddhist monk and translator who travelled by foot from China to India between 399 and 412 CE to acquire Buddhist scriptures. His journey, undertaken when he was already in his sixties, took him through some thirty kingdoms of Central and South Asia, and his account of the journey, the Foguo Ji ("A Record of Buddhist Kingdoms"), is a priceless source for the history of early fifth-century Central Asia and India.

Life and journey

Faxian was born in Shanxi and entered the monastic order as a child. Finding the Chinese copies of the Vinaya (monastic discipline texts) incomplete and corrupt, he resolved to travel to India, the homeland of Buddhism, to obtain authoritative texts. In 399 he set out from Chang'an with several companions, crossing the deserts of the Tarim Basin, the Pamir mountains, and the Hindu Kush — a route on which he reports that the mountains were so perilous that travellers perished in large numbers.

In India, then ruled in the north by the Gupta Empire under Chandragupta II, Faxian spent several years visiting the sacred sites of the Buddha's life — Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath, and Kushinagar — studying Sanskrit and collecting manuscripts in cities such as Pataliputra and Tamralipta. His description of Gupta India portrays a prosperous, orderly, and tolerant kingdom. He then sailed to Sri Lanka, spent two years there, and returned to China by sea in 412, surviving storms and a voyage via Java before landing on the Shandong coast.

Works and legacy

Back in China, Faxian settled in the southern capital Jiankang (Nanjing), where he translated the texts he had brought back — including the Mahaparinirvana Sutra and a complete Mahasanghika Vinaya — in collaboration with the Indian monk Buddhabhadra. His travel record, translated into English in the nineteenth century by Samuel Beal and James Legge, remains fundamental to the archaeology and history of Buddhism; Alexander Cunningham used it to identify lost Buddhist sites in India. Faxian's pilgrimage preceded and inspired the even more famous journey of Xuanzang in the seventh century.
