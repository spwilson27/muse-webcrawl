Gonochorism

Gonochorism, in biology, describes a species in which individual organisms are distinctly male or female — each individual has only one sex and one set of reproductive organs throughout its life. It is the reproductive pattern of the great majority of animal species, including most vertebrates, insects, and other arthropods, and is contrasted with hermaphroditism, in which an individual has both male and female reproductive function (simultaneously or sequentially).

Terminology and scope

The term is used mainly in zoology; the equivalent state in plants and fungi is treated under other terms such as dioecy and dioecism. In gonochoristic species, sex may be determined chromosomally (as in mammals' XX/XY and birds' ZW/ZZ systems), by environmental cues such as temperature (as in many reptiles), or by social factors. Gonochorism is often, though not necessarily, accompanied by sexual dimorphism — visible differences between the sexes in size, colour, or ornamentation, as in peafowl or lions.

Evolution

Gonochorism is generally regarded as the ancestral condition in many animal groups, with hermaphroditism evolving repeatedly in lineages (such as most gastropods and many fish) where it offers advantages — for example in sessile or sparse populations where finding a mate of the opposite sex is difficult. Some fish lineages have shifted in the reverse direction, from hermaphroditism back to separate sexes.
