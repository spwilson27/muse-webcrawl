EPICA, the European Project for Ice Coring in Antarctica, was a multinational deep ice-core drilling programme in Antarctica involving ten European countries. Its aim was to reconstruct past climate and atmospheric composition from ice drilled at two sites: Dome C on the polar plateau, operated from Concordia Station, and Kohnen Station in Dronning Maud Land (the EDML core).

The Dome C core

Drilling at Dome C began in 1996. After an early drill became stuck, a new hole reached bedrock at 3,270 metres in January 2005. The recovered core (EDC) provided a continuous climate record spanning about 800,000 years and eight glacial cycles — at the time the oldest ice ever recovered — more than doubling the previous record from the Vostok core. Analysis of trapped air bubbles and stable isotopes (notably deuterium) reconstructed Antarctic temperatures and atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide and methane through successive ice ages, showing, among other results, that present greenhouse-gas concentrations lie far outside the range of the last 800,000 years, and detailing the characteristically saw-tooth rhythm of glacial terminations.

The EDML core

The second EPICA core at Kohnen Station (drilled 2001–2006, 2,774 m) reached back about 150,000 years at higher accumulation and therefore higher resolution, permitting precise comparison with Greenland ice cores and demonstrating the near-synchronous-but-offset ("bipolar seesaw") behaviour of the two hemispheres during abrupt climate changes.

Legacy

EPICA data underpin much of the IPCC's assessment of natural climate variability. The successor project, Beyond EPICA – Oldest Ice, is drilling at Little Dome C in an attempt to recover ice up to 1.5 million years old.
