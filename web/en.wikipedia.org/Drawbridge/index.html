Drawbridge

A drawbridge or draw-bridge is a type of moveable bridge typically at the entrance to a castle or tower surrounded by a moat. In some forms of English, including American English, the word drawbridge commonly refers to all types of moveable bridges, such as bascule bridges, vertical-lift bridges and swing bridges, but this article concerns the narrower, more correct use of the term.

Castle drawbridges

Medieval castles were usually defended by a ditch or moat, crossed by a wooden bridge. In early castles the bridge might be designed to be destroyed or removed in the event of an attack, but drawbridges became very common. A typical arrangement would have the drawbridge immediately in front of a gatehouse, folded flat across the moat but capable of being raised quickly if needed.

The bridge was raised or lowered using ropes or chains attached to a windlass in a chamber in the gatehouse above the gate passage. Only a very light bridge could be raised in this way without any form of counterweight, so normally the bridge was provided with a bascule arrangement: the bridge deck extended into a pit below the gate passage, and pivoting about a horizontal axis, the inner part of the bridge would sink into the pit when the outer part was raised. By attaching chains to the inner part of the bridge as well, counterweights could be attached to it to help raise the outer part.

Bascule and lift bridges

In modern usage, movable bridges over navigable waterways include the bascule bridge, in which a single or double leaf is raised on a trunnion; the vertical-lift bridge, whose span rises between towers; and the swing bridge, which rotates horizontally. The Tower Bridge in London (completed 1894) is a famous combined bascule and suspension bridge.
