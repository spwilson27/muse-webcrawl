Grey junglefowl

Scientific nameGallus sonneratii
FamilyPhasianidae
RangePeninsular India
Also calledSonnerat's junglefowl
Conservation statusLeast Concern (IUCN)

The grey junglefowl (Gallus sonneratii), also known as Sonnerat's junglefowl, is one of the wild ancestors of the domestic chicken. Endemic to peninsular India, it inhabits dry deciduous and evergreen forest, bamboo thickets and scrub from the Aravalli hills and the Western and Eastern Ghats to central India.

Description

The cock is strikingly patterned: grey plumage finely vermiculated with black and ochre, glossy black hackle feathers tipped with yellowish, wax-like spangles, and a red comb and wattles. The hen is mottled brown with bold white streaking below. The male's call is a distinctive, broken crow, given mainly in the breeding season. Unlike the red junglefowl, the male undergoes an "eclipse" moult of its neck feathers in summer.

Role in chicken domestication

Genetic studies show that while the red junglefowl is the principal ancestor of the domestic chicken, the grey junglefowl contributed heritable traits — most famously the gene for yellow skin, which comes from this species. Hybridisation between the two species occurs where their ranges meet.

Behaviour

Grey junglefowl live in small parties and feed on the ground on seeds, shoots, termites and other invertebrates, scratching in leaf litter. They roost in trees at night. The female lays four to seven eggs in a ground nest and raises the chicks alone.

Conservation

The species is widespread and listed as Least Concern, though it is hunted for food and for its hackle feathers, prized in fly-tying for trout and salmon flies, and habitat loss and hybridisation with village chickens are local concerns. It is protected under Indian wildlife law.
