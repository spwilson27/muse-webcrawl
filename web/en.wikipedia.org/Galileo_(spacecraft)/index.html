Galileo was an American robotic space probe that studied the planet Jupiter and its moons, as well as the asteroids Gaspra and Ida. Named after the Italian astronomer Galileo Galilei, it consisted of an orbiter and an atmospheric entry probe. It was delivered into Earth orbit on 18 October 1989 by Space Shuttle Atlantis on the STS-34 mission, and arrived at Jupiter on 7 December 1995 after gravitational assist flybys of Venus and Earth, becoming the first spacecraft to orbit Jupiter.

Mission

Despite suffering major antenna problems — its high-gain antenna failed to open fully — Galileo achieved its scientific goals through its low-gain antenna and data compression. It was the first spacecraft to visit an asteroid (Gaspra in 1991), first to discover an asteroid moon (Dactyl, orbiting Ida), the only one to directly observe comet Shoemaker–Levy 9's impacts with Jupiter, and the first to drop a probe into a gas giant's atmosphere.

Discoveries

The atmospheric probe measured Jupiter's winds, composition and lightning before being crushed by pressure about an hour after entry. The orbiter found evidence of subsurface oceans on Europa, Ganymede and Callisto; intense volcanic activity on Io; and that Ganymede generates its own magnetic field. Galileo measured the magnetic field and particle environment around Jupiter extensively over its 35 orbits.

End of mission

On 21 September 2003, after 14 years in space and 8 years in the Jovian system, Galileo was deliberately deorbited into Jupiter's atmosphere to avoid any possibility of it colliding with and contaminating Europa, which might harbor a subsurface ocean suitable for life.
