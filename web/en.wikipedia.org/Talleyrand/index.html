Charles Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord

Charles Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord, 1st Prince of Benevento (2 February 1754 – 17 May 1838), was a French statesman and diplomat who served under nearly every French regime from the Revolution to the July Monarchy. Renowned for his skill, wit, and political survival, he worked successively for Louis XVI, the Revolutionary governments, Napoleon, the Bourbon Restoration, and Louis-Philippe.

Early life and the Church

Born into an old aristocratic family in Paris, Talleyrand was directed into the Church because a childhood injury left him with a club foot, ruling out a military career. He became a priest, then Agent-General of the Clergy, and in 1788 Bishop of Autun. At the Estates-General of 1789 he sided with the Third Estate, proposed the nationalisation of Church property, and helped draft the Declaration of the Rights of Man. He was excommunicated for his role in the Civil Constitution of the Clergy.

Revolution and Napoleon

After diplomatic missions to London, Talleyrand fled to the United States during the Terror, returning in 1796 to become Foreign Minister under the Directory. He retained the post under Napoleon, negotiating major treaties, but grew disillusioned with Napoleonic expansion and secretly advised Austria and Russia. He resigned in 1807 and later worked for Napoleon's downfall.

Restoration and later life

In 1814 Talleyrand headed the provisional government and represented France at the Congress of Vienna, where his diplomacy preserved France's position despite its defeat. Briefly prime minister under Louis XVIII, he spent years in opposition before supporting the July Revolution of 1830, after which he served as ambassador to London and helped secure Belgian independence.

Legacy

Talleyrand is remembered as the archetypal diplomat: pragmatic, cynical, and brilliant. Critics called him corrupt and treacherous; admirers credit him with steering France through its most turbulent decades toward stability and a European balance of power.
