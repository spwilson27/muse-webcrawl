Goat cheese

Goat cheese, or chèvre (from the French word for goat), is cheese made from goat's milk. Goats were among the earliest domesticated animals used for milk, and goat cheese has been made for thousands of years around the Mediterranean and in the Middle East. It ranges from soft, fresh, spreadable logs to hard, aged cheeses, and is known for a distinctive tangy, sometimes "goaty" flavour due to its content of medium-chain fatty acids such as caproic, caprylic and capric acid.

Properties

Goat's milk forms a softer, more fragile curd than cow's milk because of differences in its casein proteins, and its fat globules are smaller. Goat cheese is typically white, as goats convert the beta-carotene in their feed fully into vitamin A. Compared with cow's-milk cheese, goat cheese is often slightly lower in lactose and contains mostly A2 casein, which some people find easier to digest, though it is not safe for those with a true milk allergy.

Varieties

France produces many celebrated goat cheeses, including Crottin de Chavignol, Sainte-Maure de Touraine (traditionally pierced by a straw), Valençay (a truncated pyramid said to date from Napoleon's era) and soft logs of chèvre often rolled in ash, herbs or pepper. Elsewhere, notable examples include Norwegian brunost-style and geitost products, Spanish Murcia al vino washed in red wine, Greek and Balkan brined cheeses, and Middle Eastern labneh-like goat cheeses. Fresh chèvre is widely used in salads — famously in the warm goat cheese salad of French bistros — and in tarts, pizzas and spreads.
