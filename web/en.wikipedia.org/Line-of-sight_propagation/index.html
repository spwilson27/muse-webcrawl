Line-of-sight propagation

Line-of-sight propagation is a characteristic of electromagnetic radiation, or of acoustic wave propagation, in which waves travel in a direct path from source to receiver, blocked or deflected by obstacles in between. It is the dominant propagation mode at VHF and higher frequencies — including FM radio, television, microwave links, radar, and satellite communications — because these waves travel essentially in straight lines and are not reflected by the ionosphere.

Radio horizon

Because the Earth is curved, direct line-of-sight range is limited to the "radio horizon". Atmospheric refraction bends radio waves slightly downward, so the radio horizon is typically about 15% farther than the geometric optical horizon. A common approximation gives the horizon distance in kilometres as about 3.57 times the square root of the antenna height in metres (optical), or roughly 4.12√h for radio propagation.

Impairments

Even with a clear geometric path, objects within the first Fresnel zone can cause diffraction losses. Rain attenuation, multipath reflection from buildings and terrain, and obstructions such as hills and structures all degrade line-of-sight links. Microwave relay networks solve the horizon limit by chaining repeater stations on towers and hilltops.

Related concepts

Line-of-sight propagation contrasts with ground-wave propagation, skywave (ionospheric) propagation used by shortwave radio, and non-line-of-sight modes that exploit reflection, diffraction, and scattering — increasingly important in mobile and Wi-Fi networks.
