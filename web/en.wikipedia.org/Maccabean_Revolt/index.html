Maccabean Revolt

The Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BCE) was a Jewish uprising against the Seleucid Empire and the Hellenistic influence it imposed on Jewish religious practice. Led first by the priest Mattathias and then by his son Judah Maccabee, the revolt culminated in the capture of Jerusalem and the rededication of the Second Temple — the event commemorated by the festival of Hanukkah — and ultimately in the establishment of the independent Hasmonean dynasty.

Causes

In 175 BCE Antiochus IV Epiphanes became Seleucid king and promoted Hellenisation in Judea. Internal Jewish factions competed for the high priesthood, and in 167 BCE Antiochus issued decrees banning central Jewish practices including Sabbath observance, circumcision, and dietary laws, and had an altar to Zeus erected in the Temple — the "abomination of desolation".

Course of the revolt

The revolt began at Modi'in, where Mattathias killed a Seleucid official and a Jew about to offer a pagan sacrifice. After Mattathias's death, his son Judah, called Maccabee ("the Hammer"), led a guerrilla campaign, winning victories at Nahal el-Haramiah, Beth Horon, Emmaus, and Beth Zur. In 164 BCE the Maccabees liberated Jerusalem and purified and rededicated the Temple. Judah fell in battle at Elasa in 160 BCE; leadership passed to his brothers Jonathan and Simon, who secured full independence by 142 BCE.

Sources and legacy

The main sources are the books of 1 and 2 Maccabees and the works of Josephus. The revolt preserved Jewish religious identity under foreign rule and became an enduring symbol of resistance and religious freedom.
