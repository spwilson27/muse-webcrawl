Skimming (reading)

Skimming is a reading technique intended to quickly identify the main ideas of a text. When skimming, the reader deliberately skips over sections that contain details, such as stories, examples or long descriptions, while reading headings, topic sentences and conclusions with greater care. Skimming is done at a speed roughly three to four times faster than normal reading and is used when a reader wishes to decide whether a longer text is worth reading in full, or to refresh their knowledge of a text already read.

Process

Skilled skimmers train themselves to respond to verbal cues, reading the first and last paragraphs of an article, the opening sentences of each body paragraph, headings, subheadings, captions and emphasized words. Because the eye can only fixate on a limited number of positions, effective skimming means accepting partial comprehension — studies commonly place comprehension during skimming at roughly half of what careful reading yields — in exchange for a large saving in time. Skimming differs from scanning, which searches for one specific item of information, and from speed reading, which aims to raise sustained reading rates while retaining more detail.

Uses

Skimming is a preliminary stage of study-reading strategies such as SQ3R, in which the reader surveys a chapter before reading it thoroughly. It is also commonly used by researchers triaging academic literature, by editors and journalists reviewing submissions, and in everyday situations such as browsing news or checking email. Digital reading has reinforced skimming habits: eye-tracking research on web pages describes an "F-shaped" pattern in which users read the top lines fully and then scan progressively less of each subsequent line.
