Hei-tiki

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hei-tiki is a small ornamental pendant worn around the neck by Māori people of New Zealand. Hei-tiki are most commonly carved from pounamu (New Zealand greenstone or jade), though earlier examples were made from human or whale bone, and they are considered treasured heirlooms (taonga tuku iho) passed down through generations.

Description

The tiki figure is a stylised human form, typically with a large head tilted to one side, a protruding tongue (whetero), and hands placed on the thighs or abdomen. The eyes are often inlaid with rings or discs of pāua (abalone) shell. The pendant is suspended from a plaited flax cord fastened with a bone toggle.

Meaning and origins

The significance of the tiki form is debated. Interpretations include a representation of the first human, Tiki, in Māori tradition; an ancestral figure embodying the spirits of forebears; or a fertility symbol, sometimes suggested by the posture and proportions of the figure. Whatever its original meaning, the hei-tiki became one of the most recognisable forms of Māori personal ornament, prized for the mana of both the material and its previous owners.

Later history

Hei-tiki were widely traded and collected during the colonial period; large numbers are held in museums around the world. They remain a popular motif in New Zealand jewellery and art, commonly reproduced for visitors, while antique pounamu examples command great cultural and monetary value.
