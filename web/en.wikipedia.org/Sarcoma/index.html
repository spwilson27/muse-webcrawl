Sarcoma

A sarcoma is a malignant tumor arising from connective tissue — bone, cartilage, fat, muscle, blood vessels, or fibrous tissue. The word derives from the Greek sárx ("flesh") plus the suffix -oma. Sarcomas are distinguished from carcinomas, which arise from epithelial cells, and are relatively rare, accounting for about 1% of adult cancers but a larger share (around 15%) of childhood cancers.

Classification

More than 100 subtypes are recognized, classified by the tissue they resemble and by molecular genetics. Bone sarcomas include osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and chondrosarcoma. Soft tissue sarcomas include liposarcoma (fat), leiomyosarcoma (smooth muscle), rhabdomyosarcoma (skeletal muscle), angiosarcoma (blood vessels), synovial sarcoma, and gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GIST). Kaposi sarcoma is associated with HHV-8 infection and immunosuppression.

Diagnosis and treatment

Sarcomas often present as a painless enlarging mass; diagnosis rests on imaging (MRI, CT) and core-needle biopsy reviewed by specialist pathologists. Treatment usually centers on wide surgical excision, often combined with radiotherapy and, for some subtypes, chemotherapy. GIST is notably treated with the targeted drug imatinib. Because of their rarity and diversity, guidelines recommend management at specialist sarcoma centers.
