Post-painterly abstraction

Post-painterly abstraction is a term coined by the critic Clement Greenberg for the title of an exhibition he curated at the Los Angeles County Museum of Art in 1964. It describes a tendency in American abstract painting of the 1960s that reacted against the gestural, heavily worked surfaces of Abstract Expressionism in favour of open compositions, clarity of colour, and smooth or stained surfaces.

Characteristics

Artists associated with the movement include Helen Frankenthaler, Morris Louis, Kenneth Noland, Jules Olitski, Sam Francis and Frank Stella. Louis and Noland, following Frankenthaler's example, soaked thinned acrylic paint (Magna) into unprimed canvas so that colour became one with the support — the "stain painting" associated with Color Field painting. Hard-edge painting, with its crisp shapes, is often grouped nearby.

Critical reception

Greenberg championed post-painterly abstraction as the progressive continuation of modernism, though later critics questioned his formalism and the movement was soon eclipsed by Minimalism and Pop art. Nevertheless, its emphasis on opticality and the flatness of the picture plane remains a key chapter in postwar American art.
