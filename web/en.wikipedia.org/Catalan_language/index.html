Catalan (endonym: català) is a Western Romance language and the official language of Andorra, and a co-official language of three Spanish autonomous communities: Catalonia, the Balearic Islands, and the Valencian Community, where it is called Valencian. It also has semi-official status in the Italian city of Alghero and is spoken in France's former Roussillon region. Some 9–10 million people can speak or understand it.

History

Catalan evolved from the Vulgar Latin of Roman Hispania Tarraconensis between the 8th and 10th centuries. Medieval Catalan flourished as the language of the Crown of Aragon: the philosopher Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1315) wrote the first major literary works, and the language produced classics such as the chivalric novel Tirant lo Blanc (1490). Use declined in administration after the War of the Spanish Succession (1714), and Catalan was suppressed under Franco's dictatorship (1939–1975), but it was revived after Spain's democratic transition, with immersion schooling and regional media such as TV3.

Features

Catalan shares features with both Ibero-Romance and Gallo-Romance languages: eight vowel phonemes in Eastern dialects, periphrastic past tense with anar ("to go"), weak pronoun clusters, and a distinctive geminate l·l. Main dialect groups are Eastern (Central Catalan, Balearic) and Western (Northwestern, Valencian).

Status today

Catalan is used in education, government, and broadcasting in its territories; virtually all speakers are bilingual with Spanish or French. Debates continue over language policy in schools and over the unity of the language versus Valencian distinctiveness.
