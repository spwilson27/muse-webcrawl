Yoshida Shōin (吉田 松陰, 20 September 1830 – 21 November 1859) was a Japanese scholar, teacher and military strategist from Chōshū Domain whose ideas and students played a decisive role in the Meiji Restoration. Although executed by the Tokugawa shogunate at the age of 29, his school, the Shōka Sonjuku, produced many of the leaders who modernised Japan.

Early life

Born in Hagi in Nagato Province to a low-ranking samurai family, Yoshida was adopted into the Yoshida family, hereditary instructors in Yamaga school military strategy to the daimyō of Chōshū. A prodigious student, he lectured before the daimyō at a young age and travelled widely in Japan to study, meeting the scholar Sakuma Shōzan in Edo and learning of Western military science.

Encounter with Perry

When Commodore Matthew C. Perry's Black Ships arrived in 1853–54, Yoshida concluded that Japan must learn from the West to resist it. In 1854 he attempted to stow away on Perry's ship to travel abroad, an illegal act under the seclusion laws; refused and arrested, he was imprisoned in Hagi.

Shōka Sonjuku

Under house arrest he ran the family school, the Shōka Sonjuku, teaching regardless of students' rank. His pupils included Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo, Takasugi Shinsaku and Kido Takayoshi — future prime ministers, generals and founders of the Meiji state. His doctrine combined reverence for the emperor (sonnō) with expulsion of the foreigners (jōi) and a belief that knowledge and decisive action mattered more than birth.

Death and legacy

In 1858 Yoshida plotted against the shogunate's signing of the Harris Treaty and was arrested in the Ansei Purge ordered by the tairō Ii Naosuke. He was executed at Tenmachō prison in Edo in 1859. He is enshrined at Shōin Shrine in Tokyo and in Hagi, and remains one of the most celebrated intellectual martyrs of the Bakumatsu period.
