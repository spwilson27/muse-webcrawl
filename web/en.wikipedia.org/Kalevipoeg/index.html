Kalevipoeg

Kalevipoeg (Estonian pronunciation: [ˈkɑ.le.ʋiˈpoe̯ɡ], "Kalev's son") is the national epic of Estonia, compiled and largely written by Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald in the 19th century. Published between 1857 and 1861 in a bilingual Estonian–German edition, the poem consists of some 19,000 verses arranged in twenty cantos (lood), recounting the adventures of the mythic giant king Kalevipoeg, son of the ancient king Kalev and his wife Linda.

Contents

The hero performs superhuman feats: he ploughs the land, founds towns and fortresses, wrestles with demons, journeys to Finland and to the gates of the underworld (Põrgu), and sails to the ends of the earth. In death, Kalevipoeg is condemned by the gods to stand guard at the gate of the underworld, his hand caught in a cleft of rock, awaiting the day he may return to build a new age of happiness for Estonia.

Background and significance

Kreutzwald drew on Estonian folk songs collected by the Estophile Learned Society and on the encouragement of Friedrich Robert Faehlmann, who first proposed an Estonian epic. The work was consciously modelled in part on the Finnish Kalevala, compiled by Elias Lönnrot, and formed a cornerstone of the Estonian national awakening, giving the small nation a heroic literary past. Kalevipoeg's figure appears throughout Estonian culture, in place names, sculpture, music and literature; a hill and grave associated with his mother Linda are landmarks in Tallinn.
