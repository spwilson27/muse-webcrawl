Etomidate

Etomidate (USAN, marketed as Amidate) is a short-acting intravenous anaesthetic agent used for induction of general anaesthesia and sedation for brief procedures such as rapid sequence intubation and cardioversion. Developed by Janssen Pharmaceutica in 1964 and introduced in the 1970s, it is prized for its cardiovascular stability.

Pharmacology

Etomidate enhances the effect of GABA at GABAA receptors, producing hypnosis with little analgesia. Onset is within about a minute and duration 5–10 minutes after a typical induction dose of 0.3 mg/kg. It causes minimal changes in heart rate, blood pressure and intracranial pressure, making it attractive in haemodynamically unstable patients.

Adverse effects

Common effects include pain on injection, myoclonus and postoperative nausea. Its major concern is suppression of adrenal steroid synthesis by inhibiting 11β-hydroxylase: even a single dose transiently lowers cortisol, and continuous infusions in critically ill patients were associated with increased mortality, leading to abandonment of infusion use and continuing debate about single-dose safety in sepsis.

Other uses

Etomidate has been used in a modified Cushing test for cortisol disorders, and analogues such as carboetomidate have been designed to avoid adrenal suppression. See also General anaesthesia, Propofol and Rapid sequence induction.
