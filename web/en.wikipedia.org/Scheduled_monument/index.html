Scheduled monument

In the United Kingdom, a scheduled monument is a nationally important archaeological site or historic building given legal protection against unauthorised change. It is placed on a register, or "schedule," maintained under the Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Areas Act 1979. Scheduled monuments are distinct from listed buildings, which are protected under planning law, though a structure can be both scheduled and listed; in such cases the scheduling regime generally takes precedence. Works to a scheduled monument require Scheduled Monument Consent from the relevant national heritage body, in addition to any planning permission.

History

Protection began with the Ancient Monuments Protection Act 1882, promoted by Sir John Lubbock, which scheduled 68 prehistoric sites in Britain and Ireland—including Stonehenge, which famously was not effectively protected until later legislation and a court case. Successive Acts in 1900, 1913, and 1931 extended the system, and the 1979 Act consolidated the modern regime. The scheduling process was expedited after the Second World War as part of a national survey prompted by wartime damage and agricultural change.

Criteria and extent

Scheduling is based on the criteria of period, rarity, documentation, group value, survival, fragility, diversity, and potential. Sites range from prehistoric standing stones, burial mounds, and Roman forts to medieval castles and moated sites, industrial remains, shipwrecks (through designation of wreck sites), and even 20th-century remains such as Cold War structures. There are roughly 20,000 scheduled monuments in England alone, with separate registers for Scotland (about 8,000) and Wales (about 4,200), administered by Historic England, Historic Environment Scotland, and Cadw respectively.

Protection

It is a criminal offence to damage a scheduled monument, to use a metal detector on one without consent, or to carry out works—including ploughing deeper than previously, drainage, or building—without Scheduled Monument Consent. Ecclesiastical buildings in use and occupied dwellings are generally excluded from scheduling. Unlike listing, scheduling protects the whole site including below-ground remains and does not grade monuments by relative importance.
