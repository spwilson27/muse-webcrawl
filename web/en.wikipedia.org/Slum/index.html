Slum

Definition (UN-Habitat)Urban household lacking one or more of: durable housing, sufficient living area, access to improved water, access to improved sanitation, secure tenure
Global populationMore than 1 billion people
Notable examplesDharavi (Mumbai), Kibera (Nairobi), Rocinha (Rio de Janeiro)

A slum is a heavily populated urban residential area consisting mostly of closely packed, decrepit housing units in a situation of deteriorated or incomplete infrastructure, inhabited primarily by impoverished people. UN-Habitat defines a slum household as one lacking durable housing, sufficient living space, easy access to safe water, adequate sanitation, or security of tenure.

History

Slums formed in industrialising 19th-century cities — the rookeries of London and the tenements of New York's Lower East Side are classic examples — as rapid urbanisation outpaced housing and sanitation. In the 20th and 21st centuries the largest slums have grown in the developing world, driven by rural-to-urban migration and high urban land prices.

Characteristics and conditions

Slum housing is often self-built from salvaged materials and incrementally improved over time. Residents typically work in the informal economy and may lack legal title to their land, exposing them to eviction. Poor drainage, contaminated water and overcrowding foster disease, yet slums can also be places of dense social networks, enterprise and culture; Dharavi in Mumbai, for example, hosts thousands of small businesses.

Responses

Policy approaches have shifted from clearance and resettlement — which often destroyed communities — toward slum upgrading: providing water, sanitation, paving, tenure regularisation and services in place, an approach promoted by UN-Habitat and the Cities Alliance. Housing rights advocates stress security of tenure as the foundation of improvement.
