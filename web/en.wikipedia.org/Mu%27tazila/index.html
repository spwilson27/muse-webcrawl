Founded8th century, Basra

The Mu'tazila were a school of Islamic theology (kalam) that emerged in 8th-century Basra and Baghdad, emphasising reason, free will and the unity and justice of God. Their name is traditionally derived from i'tizal, "withdrawal", after Wasil ibn Ata withdrew from the circle of Hasan al-Basri over the status of the grave sinner.

Five principles

Mu'tazili doctrine is summarised in five principles: (1) divine unity (tawhid) — denying eternal attributes distinct from God's essence and hence holding the Qur'an created, not eternal; (2) divine justice ('adl) — humans have free will and create their own acts, so God does not will evil; (3) the promise and the threat — God must reward obedience and punish unrepented sin; (4) the intermediate position — a grave sinner is neither believer nor unbeliever; (5) commanding right and forbidding wrong, potentially by activism.

History

Mu'tazilism flourished under Abbasid patronage after the fall of the Umayyads. Caliph al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833) made it state doctrine and instituted the Mihna (inquisition) to enforce the createdness of the Qur'an, persecuting traditionalists such as Ahmad ibn Hanbal. The policy was reversed under al-Mutawakkil. Thinkers such as Abu al-Hudhayl, al-Nazzam, al-Jahiz and Qadi Abd al-Jabbar systematised the school. It declined in Sunni lands by the 11th century, though Mu'tazili ideas influenced Twelver and Zaydi theology, which adopted much of its rationalism and doctrine of justice.

Legacy

The Mu'tazila are remembered as Islam's foremost rationalists, sparking debates about reason and revelation that shaped Ash'ari and Maturidi orthodoxy, which developed partly in response to them. Modern Muslim reformers have sometimes revived Mu'tazili themes.
