Jury trial

A jury trial, or trial by jury, is a legal proceeding in which a jury makes findings of fact and renders a verdict, as distinct from a bench trial in which a judge decides alone. Jury trials are a hallmark of the common-law tradition and are used principally in English-speaking and Commonwealth countries, most extensively in the United States.

Structure

A jury is typically composed of twelve citizens chosen through jury selection (voir dire). The judge presides over questions of law, instructs the jury on the applicable law, and rules on evidence; the jury weighs the evidence and decides the facts. In criminal cases the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt; in civil cases the standard is usually a preponderance of the evidence. Historically verdicts had to be unanimous; following Ramos v. Louisiana (2020), unanimity is required for criminal convictions in U.S. state courts.

Constitutional basis

In the United States, the right to a criminal jury trial is guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment and the right in civil cases by the Seventh Amendment. The right traces through English law to Magna Carta (1215) and developed substantially in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Other countries

England and Wales retain juries for serious criminal cases but almost never for civil trials. Many civil-law countries use mixed panels of professional and lay judges rather than juries, though some — including Spain, Russia and Japan (with its saiban-in system) — have introduced or retained juries for serious criminal offences.
