Electorate of Saxony

The Electorate of Saxony (German: Kurfürstentum Sachsen) was a state of the Holy Roman Empire established in 1356, when Emperor Charles IV elevated the Duchy of Saxe-Wittenberg to an electorate under the Golden Bull. Its ruler, the Elector of Saxony, was one of the prince-electors entitled to elect the Holy Roman Emperor.

The electorate's capital was Dresden from the 16th century. In 1806 it was elevated to the Kingdom of Saxony.

History

The electorate passed to the Wettin dynasty in 1423. After the Leipzig Partition of 1485, the lands were divided between the Ernestine and Albertine lines; the electoral dignity went to the Ernestines. In 1547, after the Schmalkaldic War, Elector John Frederick I lost the electorate to his Albertine cousin Maurice, whose line retained it thereafter.

Under Augustus the Strong (elector 1694–1733), who also became King of Poland, Saxony flourished culturally; Dresden became one of Europe's great baroque capitals. Saxony was occupied by Prussia during the Seven Years' War but was restored in 1763.

Dissolution

With the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806, the electorate became the Kingdom of Saxony, allied with Napoleon, though it lost territory to Prussia at the Congress of Vienna in 1815.
