Samuel Clarke (11 October 1675 – 17 May 1729) was an English philosopher and Anglican clergyman, one of the leading British intellectuals of the early Enlightenment and the chief defender of Isaac Newton's philosophy after Newton himself.

Life and work

Educated at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, Clarke first made his name with a Latin translation of Rohault's physics textbook, which he annotated with Newtonian corrections. His Boyle Lectures of 1704–1705, published as A Discourse Concerning the Being and Attributes of God, set out a rational demonstration of God's existence and of the obligations of natural religion, and became a standard work of English theology.

Clarke defended absolute space and time and the Newtonian world-picture in the celebrated Leibniz–Clarke correspondence of 1715–1716, an exchange with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz conducted through Caroline, Princess of Wales. The letters debate the nature of space, time, miracles and God's relation to the world, and remain a classic of early modern philosophy.

Controversy

His Scripture Doctrine of the Trinity (1712), which questioned the orthodox doctrine of the Trinity in a subordinationist direction, brought him into conflict with the Church authorities and led him to promise to write no more on the subject. He served as rector of St James's, Westminster, from 1709 until his death in 1729.
