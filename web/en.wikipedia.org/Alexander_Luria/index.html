Alexander Romanovich Luria (16 July 1902 – 14 August 1977) was a Soviet neuropsychologist and developmental psychologist. He was a founding figure of neuropsychology as a discipline and, together with Lev Vygotsky and Alexei Leontiev, developed the cultural-historical approach to psychology.

Career

Luria studied medicine and psychology at Kazan University and then in Moscow, where in the 1920s he joined Vygotsky's circle. Their early work on cultural-historical psychology included expeditions to Central Asia studying how schooling and literacy reshape perception and reasoning. After Vygotsky's death and the political suppression of pedology in the 1930s, Luria retrained in medicine and turned to the study of brain lesions.

Neuropsychology

During the Second World War, Luria treated soldiers with head wounds and developed systematic methods for diagnosing the functional effects of localised brain damage and for rehabilitating higher mental functions. His theory, set out in Higher Cortical Functions in Man (1962), described the brain as organised into three functional units — regulating tone and arousal, receiving and analysing information, and programming and verifying activity. His qualitative clinical method remains influential in neuropsychological assessment, including the Luria-Nebraska battery.

Case studies

Luria's two "romantic science" case books reached a wide audience: The Mind of a Mnemonist (1968), about Solomon Shereshevsky, a man with apparently limitless memory; and The Man with a Shattered World (1971), following a soldier recovering from a severe brain wound. The neurologist Oliver Sacks cited Luria as a major inspiration.
