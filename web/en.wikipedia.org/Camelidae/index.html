Camelidae

Camelidae is a family of even-toed ungulates (order Artiodactyla) comprising the camels, llamas, alpacas, guanacos, and vicuñas. Camelids are distinguished by their long necks, slender legs, padded two-toed feet, and their remarkable adaptations to arid and high-altitude environments. Unlike other ruminant-like herbivores, they have a three-chambered stomach and oval red blood cells.

Evolution

Camelids originated in North America about 45 million years ago during the Eocene. Ancestors such as Protylopus were small, rabbit-sized forest dwellers. Camelids migrated across the Bering land bridge to Asia (giving rise to true camels) and across the Isthmus of Panama to South America (giving rise to the lamoids), before going extinct in their North American homeland around 10,000 years ago.

Extant species

The seven living species are the dromedary camel (Camelus dromedarius), the domestic Bactrian camel (C. bactrianus), the wild Bactrian camel (C. ferus), the llama (Lama glama), the guanaco (L. guanicoe), the alpaca (Vicugna pacos), and the vicuña (V. vicugna). Camels are adapted to deserts; South American camelids to the Andes.

Characteristics

Camelids lack horns and true hooves, walking instead on broad, padded feet. They are pseudoruminants with a three-chambered stomach. Camels can lose over 25% of their body water and drink enormous quantities when water is available; their humps store fat, not water. Camelid antibodies include unusual single-chain "heavy-chain" antibodies of interest in biotechnology.

Domestication

The dromedary and Bactrian camel were domesticated in Arabia and Central Asia; llamas and alpacas in the Andes around 5,000–6,000 years ago. Domestic camels underpin transport, milk, and meat production across arid regions, while alpacas are prized for fiber.
