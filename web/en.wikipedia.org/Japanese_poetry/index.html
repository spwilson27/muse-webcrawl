Japanese poetry

Japanese poetry is poetry typical of Japan or written in the Japanese language, encompassing both works composed within Japan and the tradition that developed there over more than a millennium. From its earliest recorded era, Japanese literature has generally had a strong poetic component, and poetry has long been used to mark courtly, religious and everyday occasions.

Forms

The dominant classical form is waka ("Japanese song"), most famously the tanka, a short poem of 31 sound units arranged 5-7-5-7-7. From the first three lines of the tanka developed the haiku, a 5-7-5 form that became independent in the era of Matsuo Bashō. Linked verse (renga) was composed collaboratively in sequences, while kanshi were poems written in classical Chinese by Japanese poets.

Anthologies and history

The Man'yōshū (c. 759), the oldest extant anthology, preserves some 4,500 poems from a broad social range. The Kokin Wakashū (c. 905) established the model for twenty-one imperially commissioned anthologies (chokusenshū). In the modern era, poets such as Yosano Akiko and Ishikawa Takuboku broke with convention, and free verse (shintaishi) became dominant.

Characteristics

Classical Japanese poetry relies on suggestion, seasonal imagery (kigo), pivot words and allusion rather than rhyme, which is trivially easy in the Japanese language. Aesthetic ideals such as mono no aware — a gentle sadness at the impermanence of things — pervade the tradition.
