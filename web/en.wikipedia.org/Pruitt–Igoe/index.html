Pruitt–Igoe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pruitt–Igoe
Location: St. Louis, Missouri, U.S.
Built: 1951–1955 (opened 1954)
Demolished: 1972–1976
Architect: Minoru Yamasaki
Buildings: 33 eleven-story towers; 2,870 apartments

The Pruitt–Igoe housing project was a large urban housing development in St. Louis, Missouri, United States, first occupied in 1954. Designed by architect Minoru Yamasaki — later famous as the architect of the World Trade Center in New York — it consisted of 33 eleven-story high-rise apartment buildings on a 57-acre (23 ha) site. The complex became infamous as a symbol of the failure of modernist public housing in the United States; its demolition, begun in 1972 in a televised implosion, is often cited by architectural historian Charles Jencks as marking "the day modern architecture died."

History

Pruitt–Igoe was built under the federal Housing Act of 1949 amid postwar slum clearance. It was named for Wendell O. Pruitt, an African-American fighter pilot from St. Louis, and William L. Igoe, a former U.S. congressman; the Pruitt section was initially reserved for Black residents and the Igoe section for whites, until court-ordered desegregation in 1954. Early occupancy was high and residents initially welcomed modern amenities.

Conditions deteriorated rapidly. Federal policy funded construction but required operations to be paid from rents; as St. Louis's population and employment declined, occupancy fell, maintenance collapsed, and vandalism, crime, and vacancy spiraled. Cost-saving design decisions — skip-stop elevators, minimal insulation, inadequate ventilation, and communal spaces that proved hard to supervise — compounded management and funding failures. By the late 1960s much of the complex was abandoned.

Demolition and legacy

The first towers were demolished on March 16, 1972, and the last by 1976. The failure of Pruitt–Igoe has been attributed variously to its modernist design, to racial segregation and disinvestment, to underfunding of maintenance, and to broader economic decline in St. Louis; later scholarship, including the 2011 documentary The Pruitt–Igoe Myth, argues the project's story is more complex than the design-blame narrative. The site remained largely vacant for decades. The episode influenced U.S. policy away from high-rise public housing toward vouchers and mixed-income, lower-density development.
