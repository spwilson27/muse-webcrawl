New Woman

The New Woman was a feminist ideal that emerged in the late 19th century and flourished into the early 20th century. The term described women who pushed against the limits of a male-dominated society: they were typically educated, independent, career-minded and unwilling to confine themselves to the domestic roles of wife and mother.

Origins of the term

The phrase is generally credited to the Irish writer Sarah Grand, who used it in an 1894 article in the North American Review during a debate with the novelist Ouida. Henry James popularised it further, and the figure of the New Woman became a staple of fiction by writers such as George Gissing, Thomas Hardy and H. G. Wells.

Characteristics and reception

New Women sought higher education, professional work, financial independence and greater freedom in dress and movement; the safety bicycle became an emblem of their emancipation. Conservative commentators and satirists mocked the figure as mannish or immoral, while suffrage campaigners shared many of her aims. The ideal was largely a middle- and upper-class phenomenon and is closely linked to first-wave feminism and the suffragette movement.

Legacy

The New Woman anticipated the freer "flapper" culture of the 1920s and remains a key concept in studies of Victorian and Edwardian literature and gender history.
