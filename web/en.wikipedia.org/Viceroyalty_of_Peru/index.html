The Viceroyalty of Peru (Spanish: Virreinato del Perú) was a Spanish colonial administrative district, created in 1542, that originally contained most of Spanish-ruled South America, governed from the capital of Lima. For two centuries it was one of only two viceroyalties in Spanish America, together with the Viceroyalty of New Spain, and it was the principal source of Spanish wealth in the region thanks to the silver of Potosí and Huancavelica.

History

The viceroyalty was created by Charles V in the New Laws of 1542, following the Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire and the civil wars among the conquistadors. Its first viceroy, Blasco Núñez Vela, arrived in 1544. The fifth viceroy, Francisco de Toledo (in office 1569–1581), organized the colony's administration, establishing the mita labour system that supplied workers to the silver mines and resettling Indigenous people into reducciones.

During the 16th and 17th centuries the viceroyalty encompassed nearly all of South America except Portuguese Brazil and parts of the Caribbean coast. Its riches, shipped from Callao to Panama and across the Atlantic, made it the jewel of the Spanish Empire, though its trade was restricted by the Spanish monopoly system.

Bourbon reforms and decline

In the 18th century the Bourbon Reforms carved new jurisdictions out of Peru: the Viceroyalty of New Granada (1717, definitively 1739) took the northwest, and the Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata (1776) took Upper Peru (modern Bolivia), Paraguay, and the Río de la Plata region, with its treasure of Potosí. Peru was left as a smaller, though still important, colony.

The viceroyalty remained a royalist stronghold during the Spanish American wars of independence. It was effectively ended after the campaigns of José de San Martín, who proclaimed Peruvian independence in 1821, and Simón Bolívar, whose forces won the decisive Battle of Ayacucho in December 1824. The last viceroy, José de la Serna, surrendered after Ayacucho; the royalist garrison at Callao held out until 1826.

Administration

The viceroy in Lima was the king's personal representative, heading the government, treasury, military and patronage of the Church. Beneath him stood the audiencias — high courts in cities such as Lima, Charcas, Quito, Chile, Panama and Buenos Aires — along with corregidores and later, under the Bourbons, intendants. The viceroyalty's economy rested on silver mining, agriculture, obraje textile production, and coerced Indigenous labour.
