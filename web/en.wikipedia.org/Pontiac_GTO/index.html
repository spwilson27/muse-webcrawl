Pontiac GTO

The Pontiac GTO is an American automobile manufactured by the Pontiac division of General Motors from 1964 to 1974 in the United States, and, in a revival built by GM's Holden subsidiary in Australia, from 2004 to 2006. The name stands for Gran Turismo Omologato, borrowed from the Ferrari 250 GTO. The GTO is widely credited with starting the muscle car trend.

First generation (1964–1967)

For 1964, the GTO began as an options package on the Pontiac LeMans: a 389 cu in (6.4 L) V8, dual exhausts, a Hurst-shifted manual transmission, and heavy-duty suspension, engineered under John DeLorean, Bill Collins and Russ Gee in defiance of GM's ban on racing and its displacement limits for intermediate cars. It sold far beyond expectations, and in 1966 became a separate model. The 1965 models gained stacked headlights and optional Ram Air induction; the famous "GTO" badge and the nickname "The Goat" followed.

Second generation (1968–1972)

The 1968 GTO adopted the new GM A-body with semi-fastback styling, hidden headlights, and the innovative body-color Endura front bumper. In 1969 the performance "Judge" package was added, named after a comedy routine on Rowan & Martin's Laugh-In. Engines grew to the 455 cu in V8 by 1970. Tightening emissions rules and rising insurance rates cut power from 1971 onward.

Later years and revival

The GTO nameplate was demoted to an option on the compact Ventura for 1974 and discontinued. In 2004 Pontiac revived it as a rebadged Holden Monaro with a 5.7 L (later 6.0 L) LS V8; production ended in 2006 after about 41,000 cars.
