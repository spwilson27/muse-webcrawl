In electromagnetism, permeability is the measure of a material's magnetisation that a material obtains in response to an applied magnetic field. It is typically represented by the Greek letter μ. The term was coined by Oliver Heaviside in 1885. In SI units, permeability is measured in henries per metre (H/m), or equivalently newtons per ampere squared (N/A²).

Definition

Permeability relates the magnetic flux density B to the magnetic field strength H: B = μH. The permeability of free space, μ0 (the magnetic constant), has a value of approximately 4π × 10−7 H/m. Relative permeability μr is the ratio of a material's permeability to μ0. Diamagnetic materials have μr slightly less than 1, paramagnetic materials slightly more than 1, and ferromagnetic materials (iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys) values from hundreds to hundreds of thousands — but strongly dependent on field strength, temperature and history, as shown by the hysteresis loop.

Applications

High-permeability materials such as soft iron, silicon steel and mu-metal are used for transformer and inductor cores, magnetic shields, and magnetic recording heads, concentrating magnetic flux and improving device efficiency.
