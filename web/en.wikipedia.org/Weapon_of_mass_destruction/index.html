Weapon of mass destruction

A weapon of mass destruction (WMD) is a nuclear, radiological, chemical, or biological weapon that can kill and bring significant harm to a large number of humans or cause great damage to human-made structures, natural structures, or the biosphere. The term arose in the late 1930s and came into wide use after 2001.

Definitions

Different organizations define the term differently. The United States Department of Defense defines a WMD as a weapon capable of a high order of destruction or mass casualties, excluding the delivery system. Arms control treaties group the categories as NBC (nuclear, biological, chemical), later CBRN with radiological weapons added. Civil defense usage sometimes includes large conventional explosives, a breadth for which the term has been criticized as imprecise.

History

The phrase appeared in print in 1937 in connection with aerial bombing and poison gas. After the atomic bombings of 1945, it became closely associated with nuclear weapons. During the Cold War both superpowers stockpiled tens of thousands of nuclear warheads along with chemical and biological arsenals. The term gained renewed prominence in the justification offered for the 2003 invasion of Iraq, whose alleged WMD programs were not found after the invasion.

Control regimes

Major treaties include the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968), the Biological Weapons Convention (1972), and the Chemical Weapons Convention (1993), verified in part by the OPCW and the IAEA. The UN Security Council's Resolution 1540 obliges states to prevent non-state actors from acquiring such weapons.
