Richard Karp


Richard M. Karp, theoretical computer scientist

Richard Manning Karp (born January 3, 1935) is an American computer scientist and computational theorist, widely recognised as one of the founders of the theory of computational complexity. He received the Turing Award in 1985 for his contributions to the theory of algorithms, including the development of the theory of NP-completeness.

Education and career

Karp was born in Boston, Massachusetts, and studied at Harvard University, receiving his bachelor's degree in 1955 and his PhD in applied mathematics in 1959. He worked at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center from 1959 to 1968, then held professorships at the University of California, Berkeley, the University of Washington, and again Berkeley, where he has spent most of his career and led research at the International Computer Science Institute.

Work

In his landmark 1972 paper "Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems", Karp showed that 21 classic combinatorial problems — including satisfiability variants, clique, Hamiltonian path, knapsack and graph colouring — are NP-complete, extending Stephen Cook's breakthrough and establishing NP-completeness as a central concept in computer science; the list is known as Karp's 21 NP-complete problems. With Jack Edmonds he devised the Edmonds–Karp algorithm for maximum flow. Later, with Michael Rabin, he created the Rabin–Karp string-searching algorithm, and he made important contributions to probabilistic algorithms, parallel computation and computational biology, including work on DNA sequence assembly.

Honours

Besides the Turing Award, Karp received the U.S. National Medal of Science (1996), the EATCS Award, the Kyoto Prize (2008) and numerous other distinctions, and is a member of the National Academy of Sciences and the National Academy of Engineering.
