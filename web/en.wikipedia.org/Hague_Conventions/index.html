Hague Conventions

The Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 are a series of treaties on the laws and customs of war.

The Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 are a series of international treaties and declarations negotiated at two international peace conferences at The Hague in the Netherlands. Along with the Geneva Conventions, the Hague Conventions were among the first formal statements of the laws of war and war crimes in the body of secular international law. A third conference was scheduled for 1914 and later rescheduled for 1915, but it never took place because of the start of World War I.

The 1899 Conference

The First Hague Conference came from a proposal on 24 August 1898 by Russian Tsar Nicholas II, prompted by concerns over the cost of the arms race and by the influence of peace advocates such as Ivan Bloch. The conference was held from 18 May to 29 July 1899 and produced three conventions and three declarations. Its achievements included the establishment of the Permanent Court of Arbitration, conventions on the pacific settlement of international disputes, the laws and customs of war on land, and the adaptation of the principles of the Geneva Convention of 1864 to maritime warfare. Declarations prohibited the launching of projectiles from balloons (for five years), the use of asphyxiating gases, and expanding ("dum-dum") bullets.

The 1907 Conference

The Second Hague Conference, proposed by U.S. President Theodore Roosevelt and formally convened by Tsar Nicholas II, met from 15 June to 18 October 1907 with representatives from 44 states. It produced thirteen conventions, of which twelve were ratified and entered into force. The 1907 conventions revised the 1899 land war convention and addressed topics including the opening of hostilities, the rights and duties of neutral powers, naval bombardment, the laying of automatic submarine contact mines, and restrictions on the exercise of the right of capture in naval war. The Martens Clause, first introduced in 1899, affirmed that in cases not covered by the conventions, populations and belligerents remain under the protection of the principles of international law derived from custom, humanity, and the dictates of public conscience.

Legacy

Many of the rules laid down at The Hague were violated in World War I. The conventions nevertheless formed the basis for charges at the Nuremberg trials, where the tribunal held that by 1939 the rules in the 1907 convention were recognised by all civilised nations and were regarded as declaratory of the laws and customs of war. The Hague Conventions continue to underpin modern international humanitarian law, alongside the Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols.
