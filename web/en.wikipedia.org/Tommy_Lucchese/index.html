Gaetano "Tommy" Lucchese (December 1, 1899 – July 13, 1967) was an Italian-American mobster and a founding member of the Mafia in the United States. He was the boss of the Lucchese crime family, one of the Five Families that dominate organized crime activities in New York City, from 1951 until his death in 1967. Nicknamed "Three-Finger Brown" after losing a finger in an industrial accident as a young man — a nickname shared with the baseball pitcher Mordecai Brown — Lucchese was regarded as one of the most powerful and least ostentatious Mafia bosses of his era.

Early life

Lucchese was born in Palermo, Sicily, and immigrated to the United States with his family in 1911, settling in East Harlem, Manhattan. As a teenager he worked in a machine shop, where he lost the index finger of his right hand in an accident. He fell in with the 107th Street gang and became associated with Gaetano Reina and Joseph Pinzolo, building a career in extortion, burglary and the garment industry rackets.

Rise to power

After the Castellammarese War of 1930–1931, Lucchese aligned himself with Lucky Luciano's new order and served as underboss to Thomas Gagliano, the head of the Reina family. For two decades he quietly consolidated control over the garment district, trucking, and the construction industry, preferring anonymity to the flamboyance of bosses such as Albert Anastasia. When Gagliano retired in 1951, Lucchese succeeded him, and the family was later renamed for him by the United States Senate's organized crime hearings.

Later years and death

Lucchese maintained close alliances with Carlo Gambino and Vito Genovese and was a central figure on the Commission, the Mafia's governing body. Despite his influence, he was rarely convicted; federal investigators long considered him nearly untouchable. He died of a brain tumor on July 13, 1967, at his home in Lido Beach, Long Island. His funeral drew hundreds of mourners, including law enforcement observers. He was succeeded as boss by Carmine Tramunti, with Anthony Corallo widely regarded as the family's real power.
