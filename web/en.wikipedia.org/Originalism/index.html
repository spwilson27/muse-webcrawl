Originalism is a theory of constitutional interpretation that holds that the United States Constitution should be interpreted according to the meaning it had at the time it was adopted. Originalists argue that the Constitution's meaning is fixed at ratification and can be changed only by amendment, and that judges who depart from the original meaning improperly act as legislators.

Varieties

Early originalism emphasised the original intent of the framers; modern originalism, associated with Justice Antonin Scalia, instead seeks the original public meaning — how the text would have been understood by a reasonable reader at the time of adoption — sometimes called the "new originalism". A related approach, textualism, applies similar principles to statutes, focusing on the ordinary meaning of the enacted words rather than legislative history.

Influence

Originalism became a dominant force in American legal conservatism from the 1980s, promoted by the Federalist Society and invoked in major Supreme Court decisions, including District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) on the Second Amendment. Justices such as Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito, Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, and Amy Coney Barrett identify with originalist methods to varying degrees.

Criticism

Critics argue that the original meaning is often indeterminate, that the framers themselves expected interpretation to evolve, and that originalism can entrench the exclusionary assumptions of the ratifying generation — for example, an electorate limited to white male property holders. Defenders respond that constraint by fixed meaning is precisely what makes judicial review democratic and legitimate.
