House of Wisdom

The House of Wisdom (Arabic: بيت الحكمة, romanized: Bayt al-Ḥikmah), also known as the Grand Library of Baghdad, was believed to be a major Abbasid-era public academy and intellectual center in Baghdad. It was founded either as a library for the collections of the fifth Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809) in the late 8th century or as a private collection of the second caliph al-Mansur (r. 754–775), and later developed under al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833) into a major centre of learning.

Activities

The House of Wisdom was the centre of the Graeco-Arabic translation movement, in which scholars — many of them Christians such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq — translated Greek, Persian and Indian works of philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy into Arabic. Scholars associated with Baghdad's scholarly culture included the mathematician al-Khwarizmi, from whose name the word "algorithm" derives, the Banu Musa brothers, and the philosopher al-Kindi.

Decline and destruction

The institution declined with the weakening of the Abbasid caliphs and narrower patronage from the 10th century. It was destroyed during the Mongol siege of Baghdad in 1258, when the city's libraries were looted and, according to legend, the Tigris ran black with ink from books thrown into the river.

Historiography

Modern historians debate the exact institutional character of the Bayt al-Ḥikmah — whether a formal academy comparable to a university, or more a royal library and translation bureau — while agreeing on Baghdad's central role in preserving and extending ancient learning.
