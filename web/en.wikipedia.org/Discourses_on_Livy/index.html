The Discourses on the First Ten Books of Titus Livius (Italian: Discorsi sopra la prima deca di Tito Livio), usually shortened to the Discourses on Livy or simply the Discourses, is a work of political history and philosophy by the Italian Renaissance writer Niccolò Machiavelli, written between about 1513 and 1519 and published posthumously in 1531. Structured as a commentary on the first ten books of Livy's history of Rome, it is generally regarded as Machiavelli's most important work and a founding text of modern republican thought.

Content

The work is dedicated not to a prince but to two of Machiavelli's friends, Zanobi Buondelmonti and Cosimo Rucellai, members of the Orti Oricellari circle. Across three books and 142 chapters, Machiavelli argues that a republic is superior to a principality when it can be maintained, because the people as a whole are wiser and more constant than a single ruler. Book I treats the internal orders of Rome, Book II its expansion by war and diplomacy, and Book III the actions of great individuals.

Key ideas

Among the Discourses' famous claims are that the tumults between the Roman plebs and the Senate preserved liberty by giving the people a share in government; that a corrupt people cannot sustain free institutions; that founders must act alone but ordering must be entrusted to many; and that republics must periodically return to their beginnings. Machiavelli praises citizen armies over mercenaries and treats religion as an indispensable instrument of civic order.

Influence

The Discourses profoundly influenced republican writers including James Harrington, Algernon Sidney, Montesquieu, Rousseau, and the American Founders, and it is central to the "civic humanist" interpretation of Renaissance political thought associated with historians such as J. G. A. Pocock.
