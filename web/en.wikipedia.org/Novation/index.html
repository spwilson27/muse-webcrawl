Novation

Novation
FieldContract law
RequiresConsent of all parties, including the incoming party
EffectOriginal contract extinguished; new contract created

In contract law, a novation is the replacement of one party to a contract with a new party, or the substitution of a new contract for an existing one, with the consent of all parties concerned. Unlike an assignment, which transfers only the benefit of a contract and leaves liabilities with the assignor, a novation transfers both rights and obligations and extinguishes the original contract.

Requirements

A valid novation requires a valid existing contract, agreement of all parties (including the incoming party, or novatee), the extinguishment of the old contract, and a valid new contract. Because the original contract is destroyed, terms that are not carried forward — such as accrued rights — may be lost, and novation cannot occur without the counterparty's consent.

Use in finance and construction

Novation is central to clearing in derivatives markets: when a trade is cleared, the original contract between buyer and seller is novated so that a central counterparty becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. In the construction industry, "design and build" contracts commonly involve the novation of the client's design consultants to the main contractor once the contractor is appointed. Novation agreements are also used when businesses are sold, so that ongoing contracts continue with the purchaser.

Novation in civil law

The concept descends from Roman law novatio. Civil law systems recognise novation as the substitution of a new obligation for an old one, which is extinguished; it must be distinguished from delegation, in which a third party assumes performance but the original debtor is not necessarily discharged.
