Ecological systems theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bronfenbrenner's nested systems: microsystem, mesosystem, exosystem, macrosystem and chronosystem.

Ecological systems theory is a broad approach to studying human development formulated by the Russian-American psychologist Urie Bronfenbrenner (1917–2006). Also called the bioecological model, it holds that development is shaped by interactions between the individual and a series of nested environmental systems, from the immediately surrounding settings to broad cultural forces.

The five systems

The microsystem is the layer closest to the child, containing the structures with which the child has direct contact, such as family, school and neighbourhood. The mesosystem consists of connections between microsystems, for example between a child's home and school experiences. The exosystem includes larger social structures that do not contain the child directly but affect the child's immediate settings, such as a parent's workplace. The macrosystem is the outermost layer, comprising cultural values, customs and laws. The chronosystem, added later, captures the dimension of time — changes over the life course and historical events.

The PPCT model

In its mature form, the theory is expressed through the Process–Person–Context–Time (PPCT) model. Development is driven by proximal processes — progressively more complex reciprocal interactions between the developing person and the persons, objects and symbols in their immediate environment, occurring regularly over extended periods of time.

Applications and criticism

The model has been influential in developmental psychology, education, social work and public policy, including in the design of early-childhood interventions such as Head Start, which Bronfenbrenner helped to found in the United States. Critics have noted that the theory can be difficult to test empirically and that its very breadth may dilute its explanatory power.
