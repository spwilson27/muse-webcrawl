Mathematical proof

A mathematical proof is a deductive argument for a mathematical statement, showing that the stated assumptions logically guarantee the conclusion. The argument may use other previously established statements, such as theorems, and generally accepted statements, such as axioms. Proofs are examples of exhaustive deductive reasoning that establish logical certainty, to be distinguished from empirical arguments or non-exhaustive inductive reasoning that establish "reasonable expectation".

Nature and purpose

A proof demonstrates that a theorem follows necessarily from axioms and previously proved results. Unlike in the empirical sciences, a correct proof settles a statement permanently within its axiomatic system. Euclid's Elements set the classical standard for axiomatic presentation.

Methods

Common methods include direct proof, proof by contraposition, proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum), mathematical induction, and constructive proof. Some celebrated results, such as the four color theorem, were first proved with extensive computer assistance, prompting debate about verification and surveyability. Formal proof assistants such as Lean and Coq can check proofs mechanically.

History

Deductive proof developed in ancient Greek mathematics, with Thales and Pythagoras credited with early proofs and Euclid systematizing the method around 300 BC. Islamic and Indian mathematicians made further advances, and the nineteenth century brought increased rigor following the work of Cauchy, Weierstrass, and Dedekind.
