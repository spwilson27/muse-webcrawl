Marine biology

A coral reef, among the most diverse ecosystems studied by marine biologists.

Marine biology is the scientific study of the biology of marine life — organisms in the ocean and other saltwater environments. It is a broad field that includes the study of everything from microscopic plankton and microbes to the largest animal that has ever lived, the blue whale, and of the physical and chemical conditions that shape ocean ecosystems. Marine biology differs from oceanography in its focus on life itself, though the disciplines overlap closely: much marine life is studied in the context of the ocean zones and processes oceanographers describe.

Habitats and zones

Marine biologists classify ocean life by habitat: the sunlit surface (epipelagic) zone where photosynthesis supports most production; the twilight and dark midwater and deep-sea zones; the seabed (benthos) from intertidal shores to abyssal plains and hydrothermal vents; and special environments such as coral reefs, kelp forests, estuaries, mangroves and polar seas. Organisms are grouped functionally as plankton (drifters), nekton (swimmers) and benthos (bottom-dwellers).

History

The study of marine life extends from Aristotle's descriptions of Mediterranean species to the great nineteenth-century voyages. The British Challenger expedition (1872–1876), led scientifically by Charles Wyville Thomson, is regarded as the founding voyage of modern ocean science, cataloguing thousands of new species. Marine stations such as Naples (founded 1872), Woods Hole (1888) and Plymouth enabled experimental work, while twentieth-century figures such as Rachel Carson brought marine biology to public attention. Later advances — scuba diving, submersibles such as Alvin, satellite remote sensing and environmental DNA — transformed understanding of the deep sea and microbial ocean.

Importance and challenges

Marine ecosystems supply a large share of the world's animal protein through fisheries and aquaculture, regulate climate by absorbing carbon dioxide, and yield pharmaceuticals and genetic resources. Marine biology informs conservation in the face of overfishing, habitat destruction, ocean warming and acidification, plastic pollution and coral bleaching. Marine protected areas and international agreements such as the 2023 High Seas Treaty draw heavily on marine biological research.
