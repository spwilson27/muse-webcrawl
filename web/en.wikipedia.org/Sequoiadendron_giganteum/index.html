Sequoiadendron giganteum, commonly known as the giant sequoia or Sierra redwood, is a conifer in the family Cupressaceae and the only living species of the genus Sequoiadendron. Giant sequoias are the most massive trees on Earth by volume: the largest known individual, the General Sherman Tree in Sequoia National Park, has a trunk volume of about 1,487 cubic metres (52,500 cu ft) and stands 83.8 metres (275 ft) tall.

Distribution

The species occurs naturally only in about 68 groves on the western slopes of California's Sierra Nevada, between roughly 1,400 and 2,150 m (4,600–7,050 ft) elevation, from Placer County in the north to Tulare County in the south. Its close relative, the coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens), grows in foggy coastal forests and is taller, while the giant sequoia is more massive.

Description and ecology

Giant sequoias have cinnamon-red, fibrous bark up to 90 cm (3 ft) thick, awl-shaped leaves, and small egg-shaped cones that may remain closed on the tree for 20 years. Fire plays a central role in their reproduction: heat opens the serotinous cones, clears the forest floor and creates the ash-rich seedbeds the tiny seeds need. The oldest known specimens are over 3,200 years old, determined by ring counts of fallen trees.

Conservation

Although the wood is brittle and was used mainly for fence posts, shakes and grape stakes rather than lumber, logging destroyed several groves before protection began with the creation of Sequoia and Yosemite National Parks in 1890. The species is listed as Endangered. In 2020–2021, high-severity wildfires (the Castle and KNP Complex fires), intensified by climate change and a century of fire suppression, killed an estimated 13–19% of the world's large giant sequoias, prompting new management strategies including prescribed burning and thinning.
