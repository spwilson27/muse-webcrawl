Geothermal power

Geothermal power is electrical power generated from geothermal energy — the heat stored within the Earth. Wells are drilled into underground reservoirs of hot water and steam, which are brought to the surface to drive turbines connected to generators. Because the heat flow is continuous, geothermal plants can operate around the clock as a source of baseload power with low carbon emissions.

History

Geothermal energy was used for heating and bathing in antiquity, and industrial use began at Larderello, Italy, where Prince Piero Ginori Conti lit electric bulbs from geothermal steam in 1904; the first commercial plant followed there in 1913. Large developments came later at Wairakei in New Zealand (1958) and The Geysers in California (1960), still among the world's largest geothermal fields.

Plant types

In dry steam plants, steam from the reservoir drives the turbine directly. Flash steam plants, the most common type, pump high-pressure hot water into lower-pressure tanks where part of it flashes to steam. Binary cycle plants pass moderately hot water through a heat exchanger to vaporise a secondary fluid with a lower boiling point, allowing generation from cooler resources and releasing no geothermal fluids to the atmosphere. Spent fluids are typically reinjected to sustain the reservoir.

Global use

Geothermal electricity is generated in more than two dozen countries, led by the United States, Indonesia, the Philippines, Turkey, New Zealand, Mexico, Kenya and Iceland, where geothermal supplies roughly a quarter of the electricity. Worldwide installed capacity is on the order of 16 gigawatts. Direct uses — district heating, greenhouses, spas — are also extensive, and ground-source heat pumps exploit shallow ground temperatures almost anywhere.

Advantages and challenges

Geothermal power has a small land footprint, high capacity factors and minimal emissions, but it is largely restricted to tectonically active regions with accessible high-temperature resources. Drilling is expensive and exploratory wells may fail; poorly managed reservoirs can cool or decline in pressure, and induced seismicity has been a concern at some enhanced geothermal system (EGS) projects, which fracture hot dry rock to create artificial reservoirs. Research into EGS and superhot rock aims to make geothermal energy available far beyond natural fields.
