History of mental disorders

The history of mental disorders encompasses changing explanations and treatments of mental illness, from supernatural and humoral theories to asylums, psychoanalysis, and modern biological psychiatry and diagnostic classification.

Ancient to early modern

Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts attributed madness to gods or spirits, though Egyptian medicine also noted the brain's role. Hippocratic writers explained melancholia and mania through humoral imbalance. In the Islamic world, bimaristans (hospitals) in Baghdad and Damascus cared for the mentally ill from the 9th century. Medieval and early modern Europe mixed religious interpretations — including witchcraft accusations — with medical ones; London's Bethlem Royal Hospital ("Bedlam"), founded in 1247, became notorious for its conditions.

Moral treatment and the asylum era

In the late 18th century, Philippe Pinel in Paris and William Tuke at the York Retreat pioneered "moral treatment", removing chains and emphasizing humane care. The 19th century saw vast asylums built across Europe and America, and figures like Dorothea Dix campaigning for reform. Emil Kraepelin's classification distinguishing dementia praecox (schizophrenia) from manic-depressive illness laid foundations for modern diagnostics.

20th century to present

Freud's psychoanalysis dominated early 20th-century psychiatry. Somatic treatments — insulin coma, electroconvulsive therapy, and lobotomy — were widely used mid-century, often controversially. The discovery of chlorpromazine (1952) launched the psychopharmacology era, enabling deinstitutionalization. The DSM (first published 1952; DSM-III in 1980) standardized diagnoses. Critiques from the anti-psychiatry movement and figures such as Thomas Szasz reshaped debate, while modern psychiatry integrates genetics, neuroscience, and psychotherapy.
