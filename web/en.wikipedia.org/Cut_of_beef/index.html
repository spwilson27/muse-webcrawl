Cut of beef

During butchering, beef is first divided into large sections called primal cuts, which are then separated into individual cuts of beef suitable for retail sale. Different countries and cultures divide the carcass in different ways, so the same muscle may be given quite different names or be cut across several muscles elsewhere.

American primal cuts

In the United States, hindquarter and forequarter are divided into the chuck, rib, plate, and brisket in front, and the loin, round, and flank behind. The chuck yields pot roasts and ground beef; the rib gives rib roasts and rib-eye steaks; the short loin produces T-bone and porterhouse steaks; the sirloin gives sirloin steaks; the tenderloin, the most tender muscle, gives filet mignon. Tougher cuts such as brisket and shank are suited to slow cooking.

British and other systems

British butchery uses names such as silverside, topside, thick flank, and clod, and often cuts along different seams than American practice. French boucherie recognises cuts like the faux-filet and bavette. In Argentina, cuts such as asado (short ribs) and vacío (flank) are central to barbecue tradition.

Cooking considerations

The tenderness of a cut depends on how much work the muscle did during the animal's life and how much connective tissue it contains. Tender cuts are cooked quickly by grilling or frying, while collagen-rich cuts become tender through braising, stewing, or smoking at low temperatures.
