Animal coloration

Animal coloration is the general appearance of an animal resulting from the reflection or emission of light from its surfaces. Coloration is produced by pigments, by microscopic structures that interfere with light (structural coloration), or by bioluminescence, and it serves functions including camouflage, signalling, and physical protection.

Mechanisms

Pigments such as melanins (blacks and browns), carotenoids (reds and yellows, obtained from food), and pteridines are contained in specialised cells called chromatophores in many fishes, amphibians, and cephalopods, permitting rapid colour change. Iridescent blues and greens in feathers, butterfly wings, and beetle elytra arise from nanoscale structures rather than pigment. Some deep-sea animals produce light chemically through photophores.

Camouflage

Crypsis — background matching, disruptive patterning, countershading, and transparency — reduces detection by predators or prey. Snowshoe hares and ptarmigan change plumage seasonally, while cuttlefish and chameleons adjust colour dynamically. Masquerade, resembling an inedible object such as a twig or leaf, is widespread among insects.

Signalling

Conspicuous colours advertise unprofitability: aposematic patterns warn predators that an animal is toxic or venomous, and harmless mimics exploit the signal in Batesian mimicry. Bright colours also function in sexual selection, as in peacocks and birds of paradise, and in social signalling such as the facial patterns of primates and wasps.

Other functions

Melanin protects against ultraviolet radiation; dark coloration aids thermoregulation in ectotherms; and countershading and disruptive patterns can serve both concealment and communication depending on context.
