Middle English literature

Periodc. 1150 – c. 1500
LanguageMiddle English (regional dialects)
Major figuresGeoffrey Chaucer, the Pearl Poet, William Langland, John Gower, Julian of Norwich, Margery Kempe, Thomas Malory

Middle English literature is the body of writing in the Middle English language from roughly the mid-12th century to the later 15th century, spanning the period from after the Norman Conquest's transformation of English until the spread of printing and the onset of Early Modern English. For two centuries after 1066, French and Latin dominated writing in England, and English literature re-emerged gradually in regionally varied dialects.

Early period

Early Middle English works include the Ormulum, Layamon's Brut (a history of Britain in alliterative verse), the devotional prose of the Ancrene Wisse for anchoresses, and early romances such as King Horn and Havelok the Dane. The lyric tradition survives in collections like the Harley Lyrics, while religious drama developed into the great civic mystery cycles of York, Chester, Wakefield, and N-Town.

The fourteenth century

The reign of Richard II saw a flowering sometimes called the Ricardian age. Geoffrey Chaucer — author of The Canterbury Tales, Troilus and Criseyde, and foundational dream visions — established London English as a literary standard and is often called the father of English literature. His contemporaries included John Gower (Confessio Amantis), William Langland (Piers Plowman), and the anonymous Pearl Poet, author of Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, Pearl, Patience, and Cleanness in the alliterative tradition. Visionary religious writing flourished in Julian of Norwich's Revelations of Divine Love — the earliest surviving book in English known to be by a woman — and The Book of Margery Kempe.

Fifteenth century and print

The 15th century produced the Scottish Chaucerians (Robert Henryson, William Dunbar, Gavin Douglas), John Lydgate's vast output, and Thomas Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, printed by William Caxton in 1485. Caxton's press (established 1476) began the transition from manuscript culture to print, fixing spelling and helping standardize the language.
