Espionage

Espionage, spying, or intelligence gathering is the act of obtaining secret or confidential information (intelligence) from non-disclosed sources or divulging it without the permission of the holder of the information, for a political, military, strategic or financial advantage. A person who commits espionage is called a spy or intelligence agent. Spies help agencies uncover secret information, and the illegal practice is a crime in most jurisdictions.

History

Espionage is ancient: Sun Tzu's Art of War devotes its final chapter to five types of secret agents, and rulers from Pharaohs to Elizabeth I — whose spymaster Francis Walsingham ran extensive networks — relied on clandestine reporting. The profession was institutionalised in the twentieth century with permanent intelligence services: Britain's Secret Intelligence Service (MI6, 1909), Germany's Abwehr, the Soviet Cheka and its successors, and the American OSS and CIA (1947). The world wars and the Cold War produced celebrated cases, from Mata Hari and the Cambridge Five to the Rosenbergs and Oleg Penkovsky, while the Twenty-first century has added mass electronic surveillance revelations such as those of Edward Snowden (2013).

Methods

Espionage draws on many collection disciplines: HUMINT (human intelligence) from agents and defectors; SIGINT (signals intelligence), including code-breaking such as the Allied Ultra effort at Bletchley Park; IMINT from spy satellites and reconnaissance aircraft; and OSINT from open sources. Tradecraft includes cover identities, dead drops, cut-outs, cipher and one-time pads, and "Moscow Rules" for operating under surveillance. Counter-intelligence services seek to detect and neutralise spies, turning them as double agents when possible.

Legal status

Under international humanitarian law, spies captured behind enemy lines in war may be punished but are not protected as prisoners of war if caught while engaged in espionage — a provision carried from the Hague Regulations into the Geneva Conventions' Additional Protocol I, which protects a spy who rejoins his forces before capture. In peacetime, espionage is prosecuted under national laws such as the UK Official Secrets Acts and the United States Espionage Act of 1917. Industrial or economic espionage against companies is a related offence in many states.
