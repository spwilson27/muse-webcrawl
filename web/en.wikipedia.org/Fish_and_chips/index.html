Fish and chips

Fish and chips is a hot dish consisting of battered and deep-fried fish, usually Atlantic cod or haddock, served with chips (thick-cut fried potatoes). It originated in England in the 19th century and is a staple of British takeaway food, traditionally sold by fish and chip shops, colloquially called "chippies."

History

Fried fish coated in batter was introduced to Britain by Sephardic Jewish immigrants from Portugal and Spain, while chips arrived from France or Belgium. The two combined in the 1860s — rival claims for the first shop credit Joseph Malin in London and John Lees in Mossley, Lancashire. The dish became the fuel of the industrial working class, cheap and filling, and by the 1930s there were some 35,000 fish and chip shops in the United Kingdom.

Preparation

Fish fillets are coated in a batter of flour, water (or beer) and sometimes bicarbonate of soda or ale for lightness, then deep-fried until crisp and golden. Chips are cut thicker than French fries, giving a soft interior. The meal is seasoned with salt and malt vinegar and is often accompanied by mushy peas, curry sauce, tartar sauce, or pickled onions. In northern England, gravy is also common.

Cultural significance

During both world wars, fish and chips was one of the few foods not rationed, boosting morale. The dish remains an icon of British culture and has spread throughout the Commonwealth, including Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, Canada and South Africa, as well as the United States.
