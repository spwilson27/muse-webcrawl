Phagocyte

Phagocytes are cells that protect the body by ingesting harmful foreign particles, bacteria, and dead or dying cells, in a process called phagocytosis. They are essential to the innate immune system of animals, and the concept was established by Élie Metchnikoff, who shared the 1908 Nobel Prize for the work.

Types

The principal phagocytes in humans are neutrophils, monocytes and macrophages, and dendritic cells. Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells and the first responders to infection; macrophages reside in tissues, engulfing pathogens and debris and presenting antigens to lymphocytes. Amoebae and many other single-celled organisms use phagocytosis to feed.

Phagocytosis

During phagocytosis the phagocyte's membrane surrounds the target particle, internalising it into a phagosome, which fuses with lysosomes whose enzymes and reactive oxygen species kill and digest the contents.
