Human genetic variation

Human genetic variation is the genetic differences both within and among human populations. Humans are genetically very similar: any two individuals differ at roughly one in a thousand DNA base pairs, yet this amounts to millions of differences across the genome. Variation arises by mutation and is shaped by genetic drift, migration, natural selection, and non-random mating.

Measures and distribution

The most common type of variation is the single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP); other forms include insertions and deletions, copy-number variants, and chromosomal rearrangements. Studies consistently find that the great majority of human genetic variation — roughly 85–95% — occurs within populations rather than between continental groups, and that genetic diversity is greatest within African populations, consistent with the recent African origin of modern humans and a serial founder effect as populations spread out of Africa.

Genetic structure

Allele frequencies vary gradually with geography (clines), and statistical clustering of genotypes correlates with broad continental ancestry while also revealing admixture and fine-scale structure. Small amounts of archaic admixture persist: most non-African genomes carry around 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry, and some Oceanian populations carry additional Denisovan ancestry.

Medical significance

Genetic variation underlies differences in disease susceptibility, drug response, and inherited disorders. Large projects such as the Human Genome Project, the International HapMap Project, and the 1000 Genomes Project have catalogued common variation and underpin modern genome-wide association studies.
