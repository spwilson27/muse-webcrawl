Putsch

A putsch (from Swiss German Putsch, originally meaning a sudden push, jolt, or uprising) is an attempted violent overthrow of a government by a small group, typically by military or paramilitary conspirators rather than by mass popular action. The term is closely related to coup d'état but is often used for failed or amateurish attempts, and carries connotations of illegitimacy. It entered international usage through a series of attempted coups in German-speaking Europe in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Etymology

The word derives from Swiss dialect usage for a sudden blow or riot. It was applied in Zurich in 1839 to the Züriputsch, in which conservative rural forces marched on the city and overthrew the liberal cantonal government. From there the term passed into standard German political vocabulary and into English in the early twentieth century.

Notable examples

Famous putsch attempts include the Kapp Putsch of 1920, in which right-wing troops occupied Berlin and briefly displaced the Weimar government before a general strike defeated them, and the Beer Hall Putsch of November 1923, Adolf Hitler's failed attempt to seize power in Munich, after which he was imprisoned and wrote Mein Kampf. The term is also applied to events such as the July 1934 Nazi putsch in Austria, in which Chancellor Engelbert Dollfuss was assassinated, and retroactively to uprisings such as the 1848 Züriputsch-style revolts in Swiss cantons.

Usage

Because "putsch" often implies failure and a lack of popular support, governments and historians apply it selectively: successful seizures of power are more often called coups d'état, while failed ones are labelled putsches. Some writers use the terms interchangeably, and German usage distinguishes the Putsch (an uprising by a faction, often civilian or paramilitary) from the Staatsstreich carried out from within the state apparatus itself.
