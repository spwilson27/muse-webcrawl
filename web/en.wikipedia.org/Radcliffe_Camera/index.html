Radcliffe Camera

The Radcliffe Camera, Oxford — one of the most recognisable landmarks of the university.

The Radcliffe Camera (colloquially known as the "Rad Cam") is a building in Oxford, England, designed by James Gibbs in a Baroque style and built in 1737–1749 to house the Radcliffe Science Library. It is widely considered the earliest example of a circular library in England.

The building was funded from the estate of John Radcliffe, physician to Queen Anne, who left £40,000 for its construction. The word "camera" is Latin for "room" and has nothing to do with photography. The building stands in Radcliffe Square, surrounded by the Bodleian Library, Brasenose College and All Souls College.

History

John Radcliffe died in 1714, leaving funds for a new library. After prolonged debate over the site, construction began in 1737 to designs by James Gibbs, who modelled the domed rotunda in part on his earlier designs and on classical temples. The library opened in 1749. In 1860 the natural science collection moved to the new University Museum, and the Camera became a reading room of the Bodleian Library, a function it retains today. An underground tunnel, the Gladstone Link, connects it to the main Bodleian buildings.

Architecture

The building consists of a square rusticated basement, a circular drum ringed by paired Corinthian columns, and a lead-covered dome topped by a lantern. Its unusual round plan and prominent position make it one of the most photographed buildings in Oxford and a symbol of the university.
