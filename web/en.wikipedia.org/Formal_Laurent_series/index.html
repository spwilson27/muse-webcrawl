Formal Laurent series

In mathematics, the formal Laurent series are a generalization of formal power series, obtained by allowing finitely many terms of negative degree. They were introduced by Pierre Alphonse Laurent in 1843. The set of formal Laurent series over a field forms itself a field, and they are widely used in algebraic geometry and commutative algebra as a convenient algebraic substitute for the analytic Laurent series of complex analysis.

Definition

A formal Laurent series in one variable X over a field K is an expression of the form

f(X) = ∑n ≥ N an Xn

where the coefficients an lie in K and N is an integer that may be negative. Thus only finitely many negative powers of X occur. The smallest index with a nonzero coefficient is called the order of the series.

Operations

Addition and multiplication are defined term-by-term, extending the rules for formal power series. The nonzero formal Laurent series form a field, usually written K((X)): every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse, constructed by factoring out the lowest power of X and inverting the resulting power series. The field K((X)) is the field of fractions of the ring K[[X]] of formal power series.

Topology and completeness

Equipped with the valuation given by the order, K((X)) is a complete discretely valued field. This makes formal Laurent series a standard local model in the study of algebraic curves, where the completed local ring of a smooth point is isomorphic to a power series ring and its fraction field to a Laurent series field.

Formal residues

The coefficient of X−1 in a formal Laurent series is called its formal residue. Formal residues satisfy analogues of the theorems of complex analysis, such as invariance under change of variables, and play a role in algebraic proofs of the residue theorem.
