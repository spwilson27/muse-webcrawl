Pyrethrum

Pyrethrum was a genus of flowering plants in the family Asteraceae, now largely reclassified into Chrysanthemum and Tanacetum. The name is most widely used for the natural insecticide extracted from the dried flower heads of Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium (Dalmatian pyrethrum) and Chrysanthemum coccineum (Persian pyrethrum). The active compounds, called pyrethrins, attack the nervous systems of insects.

Pyrethrum
Family: Asteraceae
Source species: C. cinerariifolium, C. coccineum
Active compounds: Pyrethrins I and II
Use: Botanical insecticide

History and production

Pyrethrum has been used as an insecticide for centuries, in Persia and later in Dalmatia on the Adriatic coast. Kenya became the dominant world producer in the twentieth century, growing the flowers at high altitude around Mount Kenya; Tasmania, Australia, is also a major producer. The dried flowers are ground and the pyrethrins extracted with solvents.

Mode of action

Pyrethrins prolong the opening of voltage-gated sodium channels in insect nerves, causing repetitive firing, paralysis ("knockdown"), and death. They break down rapidly in sunlight, have low toxicity to mammals, but are highly toxic to fish and aquatic invertebrates and harmful to bees. Synthetic analogues, the pyrethroids, were developed to be more stable in light.
