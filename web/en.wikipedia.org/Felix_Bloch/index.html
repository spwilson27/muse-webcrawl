Felix Bloch

Felix Bloch (23 October 1905 – 10 September 1983) was a Swiss-American physicist. He shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics with Edward Mills Purcell for the development of new methods for nuclear magnetic precision measurements—specifically, the discovery of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), the basis of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). He also gave his name to Bloch's theorem in solid-state physics and to the Bloch equations and Bloch sphere in quantum mechanics and NMR.

Life and career

Bloch studied engineering and physics in Zürich and Leipzig, working with Werner Heisenberg and completing his doctorate in 1928 with a thesis on the quantum mechanics of electrons in crystal lattices—introducing the wavefunctions now called Bloch waves. After the Nazi rise to power, Bloch emigrated to the United States in 1934, joining Stanford University. During the Second World War he worked on the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos and on radar countermeasures at Harvard.

Later work

In 1946, Bloch and his Stanford colleagues independently demonstrated nuclear magnetic resonance in water, paralleling Purcell's work at Harvard. He served as the first Director-General of CERN (1954–1955).
