The Doors of Perception

The Doors of Perception is a book by Aldous Huxley, published in 1954, detailing his experiences taking the psychedelic drug mescaline in May 1953. It is one of the most influential accounts of psychedelic experience in modern literature.

Contents

Under the supervision of psychiatrist Humphry Osmond, Huxley took 400 milligrams of mescaline at his home in Los Angeles. The essay recounts how ordinary objects — flowers, draperies, the spines of books — appeared transfigured, "shining with their own inner light", while his sense of self and of time dissolved. Huxley argued, following Henri Bergson and C. D. Broad, that the brain acts as a "reducing valve" filtering reality to what is biologically useful; psychedelics, he proposed, loosen that filter, opening what William Blake called the "doors of perception".

Influence

The title, from Blake's The Marriage of Heaven and Hell ("If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, Infinite"), later gave the rock band The Doors their name. The book, often printed with its 1956 companion essay Heaven and Hell, helped launch the 1960s psychedelic movement and remains a touchstone in debates about consciousness, mysticism and drug policy. Huxley returned to the subject in his final novel, Island (1962).
