Recklessness (law)

In criminal law and tort law, recklessness is a state of mind (mens rea) in which a person consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk that harm will result from their conduct. It occupies a middle position in the hierarchy of culpable mental states: more blameworthy than negligence, but less than knowledge or purpose.

Recklessness is one of the four classic mental states in the Model Penal Code.

Model Penal Code

The American Law Institute's Model Penal Code § 2.02(2)(c) defines recklessness: a person acts recklessly when he "consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk" that a material element of an offence exists or will result from his conduct, and the disregard involves a "gross deviation from the standard of conduct that a law-abiding person would observe". Most U.S. states have adopted this or a similar formulation.

English law

In English criminal law, the leading modern test comes from R v G [2003] UKHL 50, which restored a subjective test for recklessness in criminal damage: a person acts recklessly when he is aware of a risk that a circumstance exists or a result will occur, and it is, in the circumstances known to him, unreasonable to take the risk. Earlier, R v Caldwell [1982] had applied an objective test under which a person could be reckless for failing to consider an obvious risk; R v G overruled Caldwell recklessness for the Criminal Damage Act 1971.

Distinction from negligence

The key distinction is awareness. A negligent person fails to perceive a risk that a reasonable person would have perceived (an objective standard), whereas a reckless person actually perceives the risk and unjustifiably proceeds anyway. This subjective awareness is what makes recklessness the more culpable state, though it is also harder to prove.

In tort

In tort law, reckless conduct — sometimes called willful and wanton misconduct — may support liability in contexts where ordinary negligence would not, and may expose a defendant to punitive damages. It also defeats some immunities, such as the recreational use statutes in many U.S. states that protect landowners from negligence suits but not from suits based on willful or reckless conduct.
