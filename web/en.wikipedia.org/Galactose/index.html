Galactose

Galactose (from the Ancient Greek γάλα, gála, "milk"), sometimes abbreviated Gal, is a monosaccharide sugar that is about as sweet as glucose, and about 65% as sweet as sucrose. It is a C-4 epimer of glucose. A galactose molecule linked with a glucose molecule forms a lactose molecule.

Structure and isomerism

Galactose exists both in open-chain and cyclic form. The open-chain form has a carbonyl at the end of the chain. Four isomers are cyclic, two of them with a pyranose (six-membered) ring and two with a furanose (five-membered) ring. Galactofuranose occurs in bacteria, fungi and protozoa.

Sources and metabolism

Galactose is found in dairy products, avocados, sugar beets, other gums and mucilages, and is also synthesized by the body. The main dietary source is lactose, which is hydrolyzed by the enzyme lactase into glucose and galactose. In the liver, galactose is converted through the Leloir pathway into glucose 1-phosphate and eventually into energy or glycogen.

Clinical significance

Chronic systemic exposure of mice, rats, and Drosophila to D-galactose causes accelerated senescence and has been used as a model of aging. Inherited inability to metabolize galactose properly, known as galactosemia, causes toxic buildup of galactose 1-phosphate and can lead to serious illness in infants unless lactose is removed from the diet.
