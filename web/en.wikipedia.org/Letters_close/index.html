Letters close (Latin: litterae clausae) are a type of legal document once issued by the monarch of England, and later of the United Kingdom, addressed to a specific individual or group and folded and sealed so that only the addressee could read the contents by breaking the seal. They stand in contrast to letters patent, which were open for all to read and sealed with the seal pendant on a tag or tongue of parchment.

Form and use

Letters close were typically used for private or administrative royal commands: orders to sheriffs and officials, instructions touching individuals, summonses to Parliament in early times, and grants or directions not intended for general publication. Because the Great Seal was impressed across the fold, the document could not be opened without breaking it — a guarantee both of authenticity and of confidentiality.

The Close Rolls

From 1204, in the reign of King John, the royal Chancery enrolled copies of outgoing letters close on parchment membranes stitched together as the Close Rolls, now held by The National Archives. Together with the Patent Rolls and Fine Rolls they form one of the most complete administrative records of any medieval state, and are a primary source for the governance, landholding, and daily business of medieval England.

Modern survivals

The form survives vestigially: writs summoning peers to Parliament and certain royal instruments are still issued as letters close under the Great Seal, though their practical importance has long since passed to statutory instruments and other modern instruments of government.
