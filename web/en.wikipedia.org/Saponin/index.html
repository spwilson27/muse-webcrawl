Saponin

Saponins are a class of naturally occurring chemical compounds found in various plant species. They are amphiphilic glycosides grouped by the fact that they produce a soapy foam when shaken in aqueous solutions. Saponins consist of a lipophilic triterpene or steroid aglycone (the sapogenin) attached to one or more hydrophilic sugar chains.

Sources

Saponins are widely distributed in the plant kingdom. They occur in soapwort (Saponaria officinalis), from which the name derives, as well as in quinoa, soybeans, alfalfa, yucca, ginseng, licorice, and the bark of the soap bark tree (Quillaja saponaria). Quillaja saponins are used commercially as food additives and vaccine adjuvants, notably in the QS-21 adjuvant.

Properties and uses

Because of their surfactant properties, saponins have been used in detergents, foaming agents, and traditional medicines. Many saponins are bitter-tasting and can be toxic to cold-blooded animals; they have historically been used as fish poisons. In nutrition, saponins may interfere with the absorption of lipids and certain minerals, though they are also studied for cholesterol-lowering and immunomodulatory effects.

Biological role

In plants, saponins are thought to serve as a chemical defense against pathogens and herbivores. Their antimicrobial and antifungal activity helps protect the plant, and their concentration often increases in response to environmental stress.
