Phonetics is the branch of linguistics that studies the sounds of human speech — how they are produced by the vocal tract, their physical properties as sound waves, and how they are perceived by the ear and brain. Whereas phonology concerns the abstract organisation of sounds within a particular language (see Phoneme), phonetics deals with speech sounds in general, regardless of language.

Branches

Articulatory phonetics studies how the lungs, vocal folds, tongue, lips, and other articulators shape the airstream to produce sounds. Consonants are classified by voicing, place of articulation (bilabial, alveolar, velar, and so on), and manner of articulation (plosive, fricative, nasal, approximant). Vowels are described by tongue height, frontness, and lip rounding.

Acoustic phonetics analyses the sound wave itself — its frequency, amplitude, duration, and resonant frequencies (formants) — using instruments such as the spectrograph.

Auditory phonetics investigates how speech is perceived, including the physiology of the ear and the perceptual boundaries between sound categories.

The International Phonetic Alphabet

The standard transcription system is the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), first published in 1888 by the International Phonetic Association. It assigns a unique symbol to each distinguishable speech sound, allowing any language's pronunciation to be written consistently. Phonetic transcription appears in square brackets, [ ], and ranges from broad (capturing only essential detail) to narrow (recording fine shades such as aspiration and dentalisation).

Applications

Phonetics underpins speech therapy, language teaching, forensic speaker identification, speech synthesis and recognition in computing, acting and singing training, and the documentation of endangered languages.
