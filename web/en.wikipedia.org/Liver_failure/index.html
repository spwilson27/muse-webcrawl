Liver failure

Liver failure is the inability of the liver to perform its normal synthetic and metabolic functions as part of normal physiology. Two forms are recognised: acute liver failure, which develops rapidly over days to weeks, and chronic liver failure, which occurs gradually over months to years, usually in the setting of cirrhosis. A third category, acute-on-chronic liver failure, describes sudden deterioration in a patient with established chronic liver disease.

Signs and symptoms

Features include jaundice, coagulopathy (impaired blood clotting), ascites (fluid accumulation in the abdomen), and hepatic encephalopathy — confusion and altered consciousness caused by the build-up of toxins such as ammonia that the failing liver can no longer clear. Kidney failure (hepatorenal syndrome), infection and low blood sugar are common complications.

Causes

Acute liver failure is most often caused by paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose in Western countries, and by viral hepatitis (especially hepatitis B and E) in much of Asia and Africa. Chronic liver failure results from long-standing liver disease: alcohol-related liver disease, chronic viral hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis and inherited disorders such as haemochromatosis and Wilson's disease.

Treatment

Management focuses on treating the underlying cause, supporting failing organs in intensive care, and preventing complications. N-acetylcysteine is the antidote for paracetamol toxicity. For many patients, liver transplantation is the only definitive treatment; prognostic systems such as the King's College Criteria and the MELD score help identify those most in need.
