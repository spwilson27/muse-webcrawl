Family

FieldSociology, anthropology, law
Core unitGroup related by kinship, marriage, or co-residence
Common formsNuclear family, extended family, single-parent family, blended family
FunctionsSocialization, care, emotional and economic support

In human society, a family is a group of people related either by consanguinity (by recognized birth) or affinity (by marriage or other relationship). It forms the basic unit of social organization in most societies and is the primary context in which children are raised and socialized. The purpose of families is to maintain the well-being of their members and of society as a whole, providing emotional, social, and material support across the lifespan.

Types

The nuclear family consists of two parents and their children living in one household. The extended family adds grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins, and other relatives, whether co-resident or closely involved. Other common forms include single-parent families, blended (step) families formed by remarriage, adoptive and foster families, childless couples, and chosen families bound by commitment rather than law or blood. Anthropologists distinguish the family of orientation (into which one is born) from the family of procreation (formed through partnership and children).

History

Household structures have varied widely: pre-industrial households in many regions were large and multigenerational; industrialization and urbanization are associated with smaller, more mobile nuclear households, though historians such as Peter Laslett showed small households were common in north-western Europe well before industrialization. In the 20th and 21st centuries, family life has been reshaped by later marriage, lower fertility, rising divorce and remarriage, dual-earner households, assisted reproduction, and legal recognition of same-sex marriage in many countries.

Functions and law

Sociologists from Talcott Parsons onward have described the family's functions as the primary socialization of children and the stabilization of adult personalities; critics note the diversity of real family forms and the role of families in reproducing inequality. In law, family status governs marriage, inheritance, custody, taxation, immigration, and next-of-kin rights. Article 16 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights calls the family "the natural and fundamental group unit of society".
