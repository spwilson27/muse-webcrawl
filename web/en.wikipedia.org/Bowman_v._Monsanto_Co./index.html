Bowman v. Monsanto Co.

CourtSupreme Court of the United States
DecidedMay 13, 2013
Citation569 U.S. 278
HoldingPatent exhaustion does not permit replanting patented seeds

Bowman v. Monsanto Co., 569 U.S. 278 (2013), is a United States Supreme Court decision concerning patent exhaustion and genetically modified seeds. The Court held unanimously that a farmer who buys patented soybean seeds may not reproduce them through planting and harvesting without the patent holder's permission.

Background

Indiana farmer Vernon Hugh Bowman bought commodity soybeans from a grain elevator — most of which were Monsanto's herbicide-resistant Roundup Ready seeds — planted them, and saved seed from the resulting crop. Monsanto sued for patent infringement. Bowman argued that the doctrine of patent exhaustion barred the claim once the seeds had been sold.

Decision

Justice Elena Kagan, writing for a unanimous Court, rejected the argument: exhaustion applies to the particular article sold, but planting seeds creates new copies of the patented invention, which the patent holder never authorized. The Court emphasized its holding was limited to the facts and did not address other self-replicating technologies in general.
