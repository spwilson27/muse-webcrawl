Dessert wine

Dessert wines, sometimes called pudding wines in the United Kingdom, are sweet wines typically served with dessert. There is no simple definition of a dessert wine: in the United States the term legally covers fortified wines, while in common usage it refers to any sweet wine made to be drunk in small quantities at the end of a meal.

Methods of production

Several techniques concentrate grape sugars or halt fermentation before the sugar is exhausted. Noble rot (Botrytis cinerea) shrivels grapes and concentrates their juice, producing Sauternes, Tokaji Aszú and German Beerenauslese. Late-harvest wines use grapes left on the vine to ripen further, while ice wine (Eiswein) is pressed from grapes frozen on the vine. Straw wines such as Vin Santo dry harvested grapes on mats. Fortified wines — Port, Madeira, and vin doux naturel — are made sweet by adding grape spirit during fermentation.

Characteristics

Great dessert wines balance sweetness with high acidity so they do not taste cloying; residual sugar may exceed 100 grams per litre. Because the sugar and (in fortified examples) alcohol inhibit spoilage, opened bottles keep longer than dry table wines, and well-made examples can age for decades.

Serving

Dessert wines are usually served chilled in small glasses, in pours of around 60–90 ml. They pair with fruit desserts, blue cheese, foie gras and chocolate, or may be served alone as a conclusion to the meal.
