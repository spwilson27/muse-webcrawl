Peer-to-peer lending

Peer-to-peer lending, also known as P2P lending, is the practice of lending money to individuals or businesses through online services that match lenders with borrowers, without using a traditional financial institution as an intermediary. Because the platform operator takes a fee and lenders typically spread funds across many loans, P2P lending has been marketed both as an investment product and as an alternative source of credit.

History

The first modern P2P platforms appeared in the mid-2000s: Zopa launched in the United Kingdom in 2005, followed by Prosper and LendingClub in the United States in 2006. In China the industry grew explosively after 2007, reaching thousands of platforms before a regulatory crackdown and widespread failures—most notoriously the Ezubao Ponzi scheme—collapsed the sector.

How it works

Borrowers apply online and are assigned a risk grade based on credit history and income; lenders choose loans and amounts, often investing small sums across many borrowers to diversify default risk. Interest rates are set by the platform or by auction. The platform performs identity checks, services the loan, and collects repayments for a fee.

Regulation and risks

P2P loans are generally unsecured, and lenders bear the risk of borrower default; deposits are not covered by deposit insurance. Regulators have progressively brought platforms within financial-services law: the US Securities and Exchange Commission required platforms to register loan notes as securities after 2008, and the UK Financial Conduct Authority began regulating the sector in 2014, tightening rules after several platform failures. Many early consumer P2P firms later pivoted to institutional funding or became banks, so that most volume on major platforms is no longer funded by individual "peers".
