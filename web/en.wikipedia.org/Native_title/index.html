Native title

Native title is the legal recognition in Australian law that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have rights and interests in land and waters according to their traditional laws and customs, which predate British sovereignty and continue to be observed. Native title is a bundle of rights that may include possession, occupation, use, and enjoyment of land, depending on the claim group's traditional connection.

Mabo decision

The concept entered Australian law through the High Court's decision in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), which rejected the doctrine of terra nullius and recognized that native title could survive the acquisition of sovereignty where it had not been extinguished. In response, the federal Parliament enacted the Native Title Act 1993, establishing a claims and determination process administered with the National Native Title Tribunal.

Subsequent law

The Wik decision (1996) held that native title could coexist with pastoral leases, prompting the Native Title Amendment Act 1998. Native title has since been determined over large areas of Australia, and many groups negotiate Indigenous land use agreements with governments and industry, particularly in relation to mining.
