The Hussite Wars, also called the Bohemian Wars or Hussite Revolution, were a series of conflicts fought between 1419 and about 1434 between the followers of the Bohemian religious reformer Jan Hus and the forces of the Holy Roman Empire, the papacy, and various Catholic powers. The wars were remarkable for the Hussites' innovative tactics—above all the wagenburg, a fortress of chained war wagons armed with hand cannons and light artillery—which repeatedly defeated much larger crusading armies.

Background

Jan Hus, a Prague preacher influenced by John Wycliffe, was burned at the stake at the Council of Constance in 1415 despite a safe-conduct. His execution provoked outrage in Bohemia. Tensions erupted on 30 July 1419 with the First Defenestration of Prague, when a Hussite crowd threw Catholic councillors from the windows of the New Town Hall. The death of King Wenceslaus IV shortly afterwards left Bohemia without an accepted ruler, as the Hussites rejected his brother Sigismund.

Course of the wars

Five crusades were proclaimed against the Hussites between 1420 and 1431, and all failed. Under the one-eyed general Jan Žižka, and after his death in 1424 under Prokop the Great, the Hussite armies developed the wagon-fort tactic to a high art, combining it with early firearms such as the píšťala and the howitzer (whose name derives from the Czech houfnice). Major victories included Vítkov Hill (1420), Kutná Hora (1422), Aussig (1426), and Tachov (1427); at Domažlice (1431) the crusaders fled on hearing the Hussite battle hymn Ktož jsú boží bojovníci. In the late 1420s the Hussites also launched raids—the so-called "beautiful rides"—deep into Germany, Silesia, and Hungary.

End and consequences

The movement split between radical Taborites and moderate Utraquists. Negotiations at the Council of Basel produced the Compacts of Basel (1433/1436), which conceded communion in both kinds to the Bohemians. The radicals who rejected the settlement were crushed by a combined Utraquist–Catholic army at the Battle of Lipany on 30 May 1434, where Prokop the Great was killed. Sigismund was then accepted as king of Bohemia. The wars left Bohemia with a distinctive religious settlement that endured until the Battle of White Mountain in 1620.
