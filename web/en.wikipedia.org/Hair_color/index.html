Hair color is the pigmentation of hair follicles due to two types of melanin: eumelanin and pheomelanin. Generally, if more melanin is present, the color of the hair is darker; if less melanin is present, the hair is lighter. The tone of the hair depends on the ratio of black and brown eumelanin to yellow and red pheomelanin. Melanin levels can vary over time, causing a person's hair color to change, and it is possible to have hair follicles of more than one color on the same person. Some hair colors are associated with certain ethnic groups because of the observed higher or lower frequency of occurrence in those populations: for example, straight dark hair amongst East Asians, a large variety of dark and light color among Europeans, curly dark hair among Africans, and red hair among people of Northern and Western European ancestry.

Genetics and biochemistry

The melanocytes within hair follicles produce melanin granules that are transferred into the cortex of the growing hair shaft. Eumelanin produces black and brown shades; pheomelanin produces red and yellow shades. Hair color is a polygenic trait: genes including MC1R, ASIP, TYR, and others influence melanin type and quantity. Loss-of-function variants in MC1R shift production toward pheomelanin and are the main cause of red hair and fair, freckled skin.

Natural hair colors

  • Black — the most common hair color worldwide, produced by abundant eumelanin.
  • Brown — the second most common, varying from nearly black to light chestnut.
  • Blond — low eumelanin; naturally most frequent in Northern and Eastern Europe, and often darkens with age.
  • Red — the rarest natural color (roughly 1–2% of the world's population), with the highest frequencies in Scotland and Ireland.
  • Gray and white — result from declining melanocyte activity with age; fully white hair lacks melanin entirely, while "gray" is usually a mixture of pigmented and unpigmented hairs.

Hair coloring

Hair coloring is the practice of changing hair color, for fashion or to restore color after it has been changed by hairdressing processes or lost through graying. Dyes may be temporary, semi-permanent, or permanent; permanent dyes use ammonia and peroxide to open the cuticle and deposit or remove pigment. Henna, a plant dye producing red tones, has been used since antiquity. Concerns about allergic reactions to ingredients such as paraphenylenediamine (PPD) have led to regulation and patch-testing recommendations.
