Chicago school (sociology)

The Chicago school refers to a body of sociological work that emerged from the University of Chicago's Department of Sociology, the first such department in the United States, in the first half of the 20th century. It pioneered urban sociology and the use of fieldwork and case studies in social research.

Key figures and ideas

Associated scholars include Albion Small, W. I. Thomas, Robert Park, Ernest Burgess, and Louis Wirth. Park and Burgess developed human ecology and the concentric zone model of urban growth; Thomas contributed the "definition of the situation" and the Thomas theorem. Later generations, sometimes called the second Chicago school, included Everett Hughes, Erving Goffman (briefly), and Howard S. Becker, who advanced symbolic interactionism and labeling theory.

Legacy

The Chicago school's emphasis on ethnography and the city as a social laboratory profoundly shaped modern sociology, criminology, and urban studies.
