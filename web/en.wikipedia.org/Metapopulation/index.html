Metapopulation

A metapopulation consists of a group of spatially separated populations of the same species which interact at some level. The term was coined by Richard Levins in 1969 to describe a model of population dynamics of insect pests in agricultural fields, and the idea has been widely applied to species in naturally or artificially fragmented habitats. A metapopulation is generally considered to consist of several distinct populations living in habitat patches, together with areas of unsuitable habitat (the matrix) between them.

Dynamics

In the classic Levins model, each patch is either occupied or empty; local populations frequently go extinct, and empty patches are recolonised by dispersers from occupied patches. Persistence at the regional scale depends on the balance between extinction and colonisation rates — the "rescue effect" of immigration can also reduce extinction risk. Later theory, notably by Ilkka Hanski and co-workers on the Glanville fritillary butterfly in the Åland Islands, added patch area and isolation effects: large, well-connected patches are more likely occupied.

Other structures

Not all systems are Levins-type: mainland-island and source-sink metapopulations have a large permanent population supplying migrants to smaller satellites, and in non-equilibrium systems colonisation may be too weak to balance extinctions, so the whole network declines.

Conservation

Metapopulation theory underpins conservation planning for fragmented landscapes, informing reserve design, habitat corridors and reintroductions, and is also used in epidemiology and landscape ecology.
