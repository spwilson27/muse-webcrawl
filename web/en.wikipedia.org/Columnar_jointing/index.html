Columnar jointing

Columnar jointing is a geological structure in which a set of intersecting fractures, or joints, divides rock into regular prismatic columns, most commonly hexagonal in cross-section. It forms most famously in thick lava flows, welded ignimbrites and shallow igneous intrusions as they cool and contract.

Formation

As a body of lava cools from its surfaces inward, it contracts. Tensile stresses build until the rock fractures; cracks propagate inward from the cooling surface and organise themselves into a network of roughly polygonal — ideally hexagonal — columns perpendicular to that surface, much as mud cracks form in drying mud. Column diameter reflects cooling rate: slow cooling produces thick columns, rapid cooling thin ones. Flood-basalt flows often show a regular lower tier (colonnade) and a more irregular upper tier (entablature).

Famous examples

The Giant's Causeway in Northern Ireland, Fingal's Cave on Staffa in Scotland, and Devils Tower in Wyoming are celebrated examples, as are the basalt columns of the Columbia River Basalt Group, Svartifoss in Iceland, and the Organ Pipes formations in Namibia and Tasmania. Columnar jointing also occurs in non-basaltic rocks, including rhyolite and phonolite (Devils Tower is phonolite porphyry), and has even been identified in images of Mars.
