The Kalevala (Finnish: [ˈkɑleʋɑlɑ]) is a nineteenth-century work of epic poetry compiled by Elias Lönnrot from Karelian and Finnish oral folklore and mythology. Regarded as the national epic of Finland, it played a central role in the development of Finnish national identity and the Finnish language, and is among the most significant works of Finnish literature.

Compilation

Lönnrot, a physician and philologist, made eleven field trips between 1828 and 1844 to collect runos — traditional songs in trochaic tetrameter — from singers in Finnish and Russian Karelia, including Arhippa Perttunen and Ontrei Malinen. He wove thousands of collected verses into a continuous narrative, supplying connective material of his own. The first edition, the "Old Kalevala", appeared in 1835; the expanded and definitive edition of fifty cantos (runos) and 22,795 verses followed in 1849.

Content

The epic opens with a creation myth: the world is formed from the eggs of a duck laid on the knee of Ilmatar, daughter of the air. Its principal hero is Väinämöinen, the ancient singer-shaman, whose exploits include the forging by the smith Ilmarinen of the Sampo, a magical mill that produces flour, salt, and gold. The theft and recovery of the Sampo from Louhi, mistress of the northern land of Pohjola, drives the central conflict. Other major figures include the reckless Lemminkäinen, the tragic Kullervo, and the maiden Aino. The final canto, telling of the virgin Marjatta and the child who displaces Väinämöinen, is read as an allegory of the coming of Christianity.

Influence

The Kalevala inspired the Finnish national awakening, the art of Akseli Gallen-Kallela, and the music of Jean Sibelius, including the Lemminkäinen Suite and Kullervo. Abroad, it influenced Longfellow's The Song of Hiawatha, which borrowed its metre, and J. R. R. Tolkien, whose tales of Túrin Turambar draw heavily on Kullervo. Kalevala Day is celebrated in Finland on 28 February.
