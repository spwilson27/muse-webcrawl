Pyramid of Cestius

The Pyramid of Cestius (Italian: Piramide Cestia or Piramide di Caio Cestio) is a Roman-era pyramid in Rome, Italy, near the Porta San Paolo and the Protestant Cemetery. It was built about 18–12 BC as a tomb for Gaius Cestius, a magistrate and member of the Septemviri Epulonum, one of the four great religious corporations of ancient Rome. It is one of the best-preserved ancient buildings in Rome, having been incorporated into the city's Aurelian Walls in the third century AD.

Description

The pyramid stands 36.4 metres (119 feet) tall with a square base of about 30 metres (98 feet) a side. It is built of concrete faced with slabs of white Carrara marble. Its proportions are strikingly steeper than those of the Egyptian pyramids of Giza — closer to the Nubian pyramids of Meroë — reflecting the fashion for Egyptian styles that followed Rome's conquest of Egypt in 30 BC. Inside is a single barrel-vaulted burial chamber, about 5.9 by 4.1 metres, originally decorated with frescoes of female figures, of which faint traces survive.

History

Inscriptions on the pyramid record that it was completed in 330 days under the terms of Cestius's will, and name his heirs, including Marcus Valerius Messalla Corvinus and the emperor Augustus's son-in-law Agrippa's family connections. In the Middle Ages the pyramid was popularly believed to be the tomb of Remus, brother of Romulus — a matching (and in fact unrelated) tradition attached to a now-lost pyramid near the Vatican said to be Romulus's tomb. The chamber was first entered in modern times in 1663, when excavations under Pope Alexander VII found fragments of columns and bronze statues. A comprehensive restoration carried out in 2013–2015, funded by Japanese businessman Yuzo Yagi, cleaned the marble and opened the monument to scheduled public visits.
