Mercury, the swift messenger of the Roman gods.

Mercury (Latin: Mercurius [mɛrˈkʊ.ri.ʊs]) is a major god in Roman religion and mythology, being one of the 12 Dii Consentes within the ancient Roman pantheon. He is the god of financial gain, commerce, eloquence, messages, communication (including divination), travellers, boundaries, luck, trickery, and thieves; he also serves as the guide of souls to the underworld and the "messenger of the gods".

Mythology

Mercury is the son of Maia, one of the Pleiades, and Jupiter. In his earliest forms he was associated with the Etruscan god Turms. Through the process of interpretatio romana, Mercury was identified with the Greek god Hermes, whose myths he largely inherited: the infant Mercury stole the cattle of Apollo and invented the lyre from a tortoise shell, trading it to Apollo in reconciliation.

Mercury carries the caduceus, a staff entwined by two serpents, and wears winged sandals (talaria) and often a winged cap (petasos). He escorts the dead to the underworld in his role as psychopomp, and in the Aeneid he is sent by Jupiter to remind Aeneas of his destiny in Italy, prompting Aeneas to leave Dido and Carthage.

Cult and worship

His temple on the Aventine Hill in Rome was dedicated in 495 BC, and his festival, the Mercuralia, was celebrated on 15 May, when merchants sprinkled water from his sacred well over their goods for luck. His name is often connected with the Latin word merx ("merchandise"). Wednesday is named for him in Romance languages (mercredi, miércoles, mercoledì), while Germanic peoples identified him with Odin/Woden, giving English "Wednesday".

Legacy

The planet Mercury, the fastest in the sky, was named for the swift-footed god, as was the element mercury (quicksilver). Mercury remains a common figure in art, coinage, and commercial iconography.
