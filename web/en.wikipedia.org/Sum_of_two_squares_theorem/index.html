Sum of two squares theorem

In number theory, the sum of two squares theorem relates the prime decomposition of an integer greater than 1 to whether it can be written as a sum of two squares. Specifically, the theorem states that an integer greater than 1 can be written as a sum of two squares (allowing one of them to be zero) if and only if in its prime factorisation, every prime p congruent to 3 modulo 4 occurs with an even exponent.

Examples

For example, 65 = 12 + 82 = 42 + 72, and 65 = 5 × 13, where both 5 and 13 are congruent to 1 modulo 4. By contrast, 21 = 3 × 7 cannot be written as a sum of two squares because both 3 and 7 are congruent to 3 modulo 4 with odd exponents.

History

The characterisation of prime numbers that are sums of two squares was first stated by Albert Girard in 1632 and is associated with Pierre de Fermat, who claimed a proof by infinite descent; the first published proof was given by Leonhard Euler in the mid-18th century. The general theorem for composite numbers followed from the identity of Brahmagupta–Fibonacci, which shows that a product of two sums of two squares is itself a sum of two squares.

Related results

The theorem is a special case of broader results on representations by quadratic forms. Lagrange's four-square theorem states that every natural number is a sum of four squares, and Legendre's three-square theorem characterises numbers that are sums of three squares. The sum of two squares function counts the number of representations of an integer as a sum of two squares, and is related to the theory of Gaussian integers, in which the theorem corresponds to the splitting of rational primes in the ring ℤ[i].
