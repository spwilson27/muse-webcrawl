Combined oral contraceptive pill

Combined oral contraceptive pill
Nicknames: "the Pill"
Ingredients: Estrogen + progestin
First approved: 1960 (United States)
Effectiveness: >99% with perfect use; about 93% typical use

The combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP), often called simply the Pill, is a type of birth control taken by mouth that contains both an estrogen and a progestin. It works mainly by suppressing ovulation, and also by thickening cervical mucus and altering the uterine lining.

History

The pill was developed in the 1950s through the work of Gregory Pincus, John Rock, and others, with funding from Margaret Sanger and Katharine McCormick. The first formulation, Enovid, was approved for contraceptive use in the United States in 1960. Modern pills contain far lower estrogen doses than early versions, reducing side effects.

Use

Pills are typically taken daily for 21 days followed by a 7-day break or placebo week during which withdrawal bleeding occurs; continuous and extended-cycle regimens also exist. With perfect use the failure rate is under 1% per year; with typical use it is around 7%.

Benefits and risks

Beyond contraception, COCPs are used to treat acne, menstrual disorders, and endometriosis, and they reduce the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancers. Risks include a small increased risk of venous blood clots, and cardiovascular complications in smokers and older users. The pill is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and has had wide-ranging social effects on women's education, work, and family planning.
