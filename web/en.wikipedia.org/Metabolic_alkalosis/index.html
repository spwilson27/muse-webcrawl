Metabolic alkalosis

SpecialtyNephrology, internal medicine
SymptomsOften none; confusion, tremor, tingling, muscle cramps in severe cases
CausesVomiting, diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, bicarbonate administration
Diagnostic methodArterial blood gas, serum electrolytes, urine chloride
TreatmentTreat underlying cause; saline, potassium, acetazolamide in selected cases

Metabolic alkalosis is a metabolic condition in which the pH of tissue is elevated beyond the normal range (7.35–7.45). This is the result of decreased hydrogen ion concentration, leading to increased bicarbonate (HCO3−), or alternatively a direct result of increased bicarbonate concentrations. The condition typically cannot last long if the kidneys are functioning properly.

Causes

Causes are commonly divided into chloride-responsive (urine chloride < 25 mmol/L) and chloride-resistant (urine chloride > 40 mmol/L) categories. Chloride-responsive causes include loss of gastric acid through vomiting or nasogastric suction, prior use of loop or thiazide diuretics, post-hypercapnia, and cystic fibrosis. Chloride-resistant causes include primary hyperaldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, Bartter syndrome, Gitelman syndrome, Liddle syndrome, and exogenous alkali or licorice ingestion.

Pathophysiology

Metabolic alkalosis requires both a generation process that adds bicarbonate or removes acid and a maintenance process that prevents the kidneys from excreting the excess bicarbonate. Volume depletion, hypokalemia, and elevated aldosterone all impair renal bicarbonate excretion, sustaining the alkalosis. The respiratory system compensates by hypoventilation, raising PaCO2, though compensation is limited because hypoxemia eventually stimulates ventilation.

Signs and symptoms

Mild alkalosis is usually asymptomatic. More severe elevations in pH decrease ionized calcium, producing perioral tingling, paresthesias, muscle cramps, and tetany. Alkalosis also shifts the oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, reduces cerebral blood flow, and predisposes to arrhythmias, especially in patients taking digoxin. Confusion, lethargy, and seizures may occur in extreme cases.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause. Chloride-responsive alkalosis is managed with isotonic saline and potassium repletion, along with stopping offending diuretics or gastric losses. Proton pump inhibitors can reduce gastric acid losses. Acetazolamide is useful in volume-overloaded patients, and in severe refractory cases dilute hydrochloric acid or hemodialysis may be required.
