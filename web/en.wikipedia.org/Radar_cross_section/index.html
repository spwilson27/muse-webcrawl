Radar cross section

Radar cross section measures how detectable an object is by radar.

Radar cross-section (RCS), denoted σ, is a measure of how detectable an object is by radar. A larger RCS indicates that an object is more easily detected. It is defined as the ratio of the power scattered back toward the radar receiver to the power density incident on the target, expressed in square metres; it is often quoted in decibels relative to a square metre (dBsm).

Factors

RCS depends on the size, shape and material of the target, the frequency and polarisation of the illuminating radar, and the aspect angle. It is not simply the geometric cross-section: a small object with resonant features or corner reflectors can have a very large RCS, while a large smooth object may direct most scattered energy away from the receiver and have a small one. A perfectly conducting sphere of cross-sectional area A has an RCS of A in the optical regime.

Reduction

Reducing RCS is the central goal of stealth technology. Techniques include shaping surfaces to deflect radar energy away from the source (faceting, as on the F-117 Nighthawk), radar-absorbent materials and coatings, and edge alignment. Countermeasures and low-frequency radars can partially defeat these measures.
