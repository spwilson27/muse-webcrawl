States and territories of Australia

The states and territories of Australia are the first-level administrative divisions of the Commonwealth of Australia. There are six federated states — New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia, South Australia, and Tasmania — and ten federal territories, of which two, the Australian Capital Territory and the Northern Territory, have substantial self-government.

States

  • New South Wales (NSW) — capital Sydney; the most populous state, founded as a penal colony in 1788.
  • Victoria (VIC) — capital Melbourne; the smallest mainland state and second most populous.
  • Queensland (QLD) — capital Brisbane; the second-largest state by area.
  • Western Australia (WA) — capital Perth; the largest state, covering roughly a third of the continent.
  • South Australia (SA) — capital Adelaide; founded as a planned free colony in 1836.
  • Tasmania (TAS) — capital Hobart; the island state south of the mainland, formerly Van Diemen's Land.

Territories

The two mainland self-governing territories are the Australian Capital Territory (ACT), which contains Canberra, the national capital, and the Northern Territory (NT), whose capital is Darwin. The Jervis Bay Territory, together with the external territories (including Christmas Island, the Cocos (Keeling) Islands, Norfolk Island, the Ashmore and Cartier Islands, the Coral Sea Islands, and the Australian Antarctic Territory), are administered directly by the federal government.

History

Before Federation in 1901, the states were separate British colonies. On 1 January 1901 the six colonies federated to form the Commonwealth of Australia under a written constitution, retaining considerable powers over matters such as health, education, and policing, while the Commonwealth took responsibility for defence, trade, immigration, and other national matters.

Governance

Each state has its own parliament, premier, and a governor representing the Crown. The self-governing territories have chief ministers and legislative assemblies, though the federal parliament retains the power to override territory legislation — a power it has rarely exercised since reforms in 2011.
