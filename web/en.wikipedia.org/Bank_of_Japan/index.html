The Bank of Japan (Japanese: 日本銀行, Nippon Ginkō, BOJ) is the central bank of Japan. Founded in 1882 on the model of European central banks, it holds the monopoly on issuing yen banknotes and is charged with price stability and the stability of the financial system. Its head office in Tokyo's Nihonbashi district stands on the site of the former gold mint.

History

The Bank was reorganised under the Bank of Japan Act of 1942 and again by the 1997 revision (effective 1998), which strengthened its independence from government and established the nine-member Policy Board. After the asset-price bubble burst in the early 1990s, the Bank pioneered unconventional policy: it cut rates to zero in 1999, launched quantitative easing in 2001, and from 2013 under Haruhiko Kuroda undertook massive asset purchases and, in 2016, negative interest rates and yield-curve control in pursuit of a 2 per cent inflation target.

Recent policy

Under Governor Kazuo Ueda, the Bank in March 2024 ended negative interest rates and yield-curve control — its first rate increase in seventeen years — as inflation durably exceeded the target. The BOJ also issues currency, acts as the government's bank, operates payment systems, and publishes the widely watched Tankan survey of business sentiment.
