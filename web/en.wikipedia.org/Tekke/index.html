Tekke

A tekke (Ottoman Turkish: تكيه; also spelled tekye, tekiye, or takiya, and related to the Arabic zawiya and Persian khanqah) is a lodge or retreat used by a Sufi order (tariqa). Tekkes served as places of worship, communal ritual — including the dhikr ceremonies of remembrance of God — teaching, housing for dervishes, and hospitality for travellers, and they were central institutions of Sufi life across the Ottoman Empire, the Balkans, Anatolia, and beyond.

History and layout

A typical tekke complex combined a prayer and ceremony hall (in Mevlevi lodges, the semahane where the whirling ceremony was performed), cells for resident dervishes, a kitchen, the tomb of the founding sheikh, and often a cemetery. Large imperial tekkes were endowed by sultans and notables through charitable foundations (waqf). Orders such as the Mevlevi, Bektashi, Naqshbandi, Qadiri, and Rifa'i each maintained networks of tekkes.

Closure in Turkey

In 1925, the Turkish Republic under Mustafa Kemal Atatürk closed all tekkes and Sufi lodges by law (Law No. 677), abolishing the orders' public institutions as part of the secularising reforms. Many buildings were converted to museums — most famously the Mevlana lodge in Konya — though Sufi practice continued privately and tekkes remained active in other countries, including Albania and the wider Balkans, where Bektashi tekkes are still numerous.

See also

Related institutions include the zawiya of North and West Africa, the khanqah of Persia and Central Asia, and the ribat.
