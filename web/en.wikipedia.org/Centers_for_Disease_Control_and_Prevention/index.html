Centers for Disease Control and Prevention

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) is the national public health agency of the United States. It is a United States federal agency under the Department of Health and Human Services, and is headquartered in Atlanta, Georgia. The agency's main goal is the protection of public health and safety through the control and prevention of disease, injury, and disability in the US and worldwide. The CDC focuses national attention on developing and applying disease control and prevention, especially infectious diseases, foodborne pathogens, environmental health, occupational safety and health, health promotion, injury prevention, and educational activities designed to improve the health of United States citizens.

History

The CDC grew out of the wartime Malaria Control in War Areas programme, which combated malaria around military bases in the southern United States. The Communicable Disease Center opened on July 1, 1946, occupying former MCWA offices in Atlanta; entomologist Joseph Walter Mountin was its leading founder. Early work focused on malaria, typhus and other communicable diseases, and the agency famously undertook the near-eradication of malaria from the United States. In 1951 Alexander Langmuir founded the Epidemic Intelligence Service (EIS), a two-year training programme in applied epidemiology whose officers — "disease detectives" — investigate outbreaks worldwide.

The agency's responsibilities expanded to include vaccine-preventable diseases (it played a central role in the eradication of smallpox and the near-eradication of polio), chronic disease, birth defects, environmental health and occupational safety (through NIOSH, added in 1970). Its name changed several times: Center for Disease Control (1970), Centers for Disease Control (1980), and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (1992), retaining the initials CDC.

Organisation and work

The CDC is organised into centres, institutes and offices covering infectious diseases, chronic disease prevention, environmental health, occupational safety, injury prevention, immunisation and global health. It operates high-containment laboratories, including Biosafety Level 4 facilities where pathogens such as Ebola virus are studied. Key functions include disease surveillance through notifiable-disease reporting, publication of the Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR), immunisation recommendations through the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), and emergency response coordinated by its Emergency Operations Center, activated for events including the 2014–2016 Ebola epidemic, the Zika virus outbreak, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

COVID-19 pandemic and criticism

The CDC's response to COVID-19 drew substantial criticism, including early failures of its diagnostic test kits, shifting mask guidance, and allegations of political interference in its publications. In 2022 the agency announced a major reorganisation intended to make it more responsive, emphasising faster data sharing and plain-language communication.
