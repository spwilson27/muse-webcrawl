Social model of disability

Social model of disability
OriginUnion of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS), 1976
Key theoristsVic Finkelstein, Paul Hunt, Michael Oliver
Core distinctionImpairment (individual) vs. disability (socially imposed barriers)
Contrasts withMedical model of disability

The social model of disability identifies systemic barriers, derogatory attitudes, and social exclusion (whether purposely or inadvertently) as the main contributors to the experience of disability. While physical, sensory, intellectual, or psychological variations may cause individual functional limitation or impairments, these do not themselves have to lead to disability unless society fails to take account of people's needs and include them.

Origins

The model emerged from the British disabled people's movement. In 1976 the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS) declared: "it is society which disables physically impaired people. Disability is something imposed on top of our impairments by the way we are unnecessarily isolated and excluded from full participation in society." Sociologist Michael Oliver coined the term "social model of disability" in 1983, contrasting it with the individual, medical model that locates the problem in the person's body.

Implications

The social model redirects policy from "fixing" individuals to removing barriers: step-free access, accessible information formats, flexible working practices, and anti-discrimination law. It underpins the philosophy behind instruments such as the UK Disability Discrimination Act and influences the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006). It also supports a positive disability identity: many Deaf people and autistic self-advocates use social-model reasoning to present their traits as human difference rather than defect.

Criticism and development

Critics, including some disabled scholars such as Tom Shakespeare, argue that the "strong" social model neglects the real effects of impairment—pain, fatigue, illness—that persist even in a barrier-free society, and propose more interactional accounts. The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning (ICF) adopts a biopsychosocial middle path. Defenders reply that the social model is a political and practical tool, not a complete theory of impairment.
