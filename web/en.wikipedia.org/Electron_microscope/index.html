Electron microscope

An electron microscope is a microscope that uses a beam of electrons as a source of illumination. Because the wavelength of an electron can be up to 100,000 times shorter than that of visible light photons, electron microscopes have a much higher resolving power than light microscopes and can reveal the structure of smaller objects, down to individual atoms.

History

Physicist Ernst Ruska and engineer Max Knoll built the first transmission electron microscope prototype in 1931 in Berlin, and Ruska produced an instrument surpassing light-microscope resolution by 1933; he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986. Commercial instruments followed from Siemens in 1939. The scanning electron microscope was developed through the work of Manfred von Ardenne and, decisively, Charles Oatley's group at Cambridge, reaching commercial form in 1965.

Types

In a transmission electron microscope (TEM), electrons pass through an ultrathin specimen; magnetic lenses project the transmitted beam onto a detector, yielding two-dimensional images of internal structure. In a scanning electron microscope (SEM), a focused beam rasters across the surface and secondary or backscattered electrons form a highly three-dimensional-looking image of surface topography. Scanning transmission (STEM) combines both approaches and, with aberration correctors, achieves sub-angstrom resolution. Cryo-electron microscopy, recognized by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, images flash-frozen biomolecules and revolutionized structural biology.

Preparation and limitations

Samples generally must withstand high vacuum and the electron beam: biological specimens are fixed, dehydrated, embedded, thin-sectioned, and stained with heavy metals for TEM, or coated with metal for SEM. Images are inherently grayscale and often colorized artificially. Instruments are expensive and require specialized facilities, though benchtop SEMs have broadened access.
