Politics of Uzbekistan

The politics of Uzbekistan take place in the framework of a presidential republic, in which the President of Uzbekistan is both head of state and head of government. Executive power is exercised by the government, and legislative power is vested in the two chambers of the Oliy Majlis (Supreme Assembly). In practice, Uzbekistan has been governed as a highly centralized authoritarian state since independence from the Soviet Union in 1991, though reforms since 2016 have opened parts of the economy and society.

Institutions

The president is elected by popular vote; a 2023 constitutional referendum extended presidential terms from five to seven years and reset term counts, allowing the incumbent to potentially remain in office until 2037. The Oliy Majlis consists of the Legislative Chamber (150 deputies) and the Senate (100 members). The prime minister and Cabinet of Ministers are subordinate to the president. The judiciary is formally independent but in practice subject to executive influence.

History

Islam Karimov, former Communist Party chief, ruled Uzbekistan from independence in 1991 until his death in 2016, presiding over a system criticized internationally for repression, torture, forced labour in cotton harvests, and the 2005 Andijan massacre, in which government troops killed hundreds of protesters. His successor, Shavkat Mirziyoyev, released political prisoners, ended systematic forced labour, liberalized currency and trade, and improved relations with neighbours and the West, while political competition remained tightly controlled: all legal parties support the government, and genuine opposition parties such as Erk and Birlik remain banned or exiled.

Political parties and elections

Parties represented in parliament include the Liberal Democratic Party of Uzbekistan (O'zLiDeP), the Milliy Tiklanish (National Revival) Democratic Party, the People's Democratic Party, the Adolat (Justice) Social Democratic Party, and the Ecological Party. International observers have consistently found that elections do not offer genuine competition, though OSCE assessments have noted some technical improvements since 2019.
