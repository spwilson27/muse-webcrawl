Hazardous waste

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hazardous waste is waste that has substantial or potential threats to public health or the environment. In the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) defines hazardous waste as waste that is dangerous or potentially harmful, identified through four characteristics — ignitability, corrosivity, reactivity, and toxicity — or listed specifically as hazardous in regulations.

Types and sources

Hazardous wastes arise from industry (chemical manufacturing, petroleum refining, metal processing), healthcare (sharps, pharmaceuticals, infectious waste), agriculture (pesticides), and households (paints, solvents, batteries, e-waste). They include spent solvents, electroplating sludges, mercury- and lead-contaminated materials, and radioactive waste, which is regulated separately in most jurisdictions.

Management and disposal

Management follows a hierarchy of source reduction, recycling, treatment (incineration, chemical neutralisation, stabilisation), and secure land disposal in engineered landfills with liners and leachate monitoring. In the US, the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) of 1976 created a "cradle-to-grave" manifest system tracking hazardous waste from generation to disposal, while CERCLA (the "Superfund" law, 1980) governs cleanup of contaminated sites such as Love Canal. International movement of hazardous waste is controlled by the Basel Convention (1989), which restricts exports to countries lacking safe disposal capacity.

Health and environmental effects

Improper disposal can contaminate groundwater and soil for decades, causing cancers, birth defects, and ecosystem damage; notorious incidents include the Valley of the Drums in Kentucky and the poisoning at Minamata, Japan (mercury). Environmental-justice research shows disposal sites are disproportionately located near low-income communities.
