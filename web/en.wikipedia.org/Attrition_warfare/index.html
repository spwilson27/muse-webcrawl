Attrition warfare

Attrition warfare

Nature: Military strategy

Objective: Victory through continuous losses of enemy personnel and materiel

Contrasts with: Manoeuvre warfare

Attrition warfare is a military strategy consisting of belligerent attempts to win a war by wearing down the enemy to the point of collapse through continuous losses in personnel and materiel. The war will usually be won by the side with greater resources or reserves available for replacing its losses.

Strategic considerations

A commander adopts attrition when manoeuvre is impossible or when his side enjoys superiority in resources and can sustain a favourable loss-exchange ratio. Attrition may be forced on armies by circumstances — strong defensive positions, as on the Western Front in the First World War, made flanking manoeuvres impractical and produced battles of attrition such as Verdun in 1916, where the German chief of staff Erich von Falkenhayn sought to "bleed France white".

Historical examples

Other frequently cited examples include the Eastern Front in the Second World War, the Korean War after 1951, phases of the Vietnam War measured by body counts, and the Iran–Iraq War. Modern industrial warfare between comparably equipped states tends naturally toward attrition, because each side can replace equipment and mobilise manpower at scale.

Criticism

Attrition strategies are criticised for their enormous human cost and their tendency to overlook morale, political will, and asymmetric responses: a weaker side may refuse decisive battle, disperse, or shift the conflict to another domain.
