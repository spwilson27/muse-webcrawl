Roman London, known to its inhabitants as Londinium, was the capital of Roman Britain during most of the period of Roman rule. Founded around AD 47–50 as a commercial port on the River Thames, it grew into the largest city in the province, a major trading hub linking Britain with the continental empire, before being abandoned in the early 5th century. Its street plan and wall — substantial stretches of which survive — shaped the later City of London.

Foundation and Boudican destruction

Archaeology indicates that Londinium was a planned new town established soon after the Claudian invasion of AD 43, on unoccupied gravel terraces north of a new bridge over the Thames, with a smaller settlement at Southwark on the south bank. Within a decade it was a thriving port, mentioned by Tacitus as "crowded with traders and a celebrated centre of commerce." In AD 60 or 61, barely ten years after its founding, the city was destroyed by the forces of Boudica, queen of the Iceni; the thick layer of red burnt debris found across the City marks the sack. Perhaps tens of thousands of inhabitants were killed.

Rebuilding and prosperity

Londinium was rebuilt in Roman style with a forum and basilica — eventually the largest north of the Alps — public baths, temples including the Temple of Mithras, an amphitheatre (rediscovered beneath the Guildhall in 1988), and a governor's palace. Around AD 200 a landward defensive wall was built, enclosing about 330 acres; the riverside wall followed later. At its 2nd-century peak the population may have reached 30,000–60,000, making it one of the larger cities of the northwestern empire, home to administrators, merchants, veterans, and enslaved people from across the Roman world.

The city served as the seat of the provincial procurator and probably of the governor, and later as capital of the province of Maxima Caesariensis after Diocletian's reforms. In AD 286–296 it was the base of the breakaway emperors Carausius and Allectus, recovered by Constantius Chlorus in a campaign celebrated on the Arras medallion.

Decline and end

From the late 3rd century London's commerce contracted, public buildings were demolished or repurposed, and the population shrank, although it remained an administrative and episcopal centre — a Bishop of London attended the Council of Arles in 314. After the Roman withdrawal around AD 410, the walled city was largely abandoned; Anglo-Saxon settlement shifted west to Lundenwic (around Covent Garden) before Alfred the Great reoccupied the walls in 886.

Archaeology and remains

Roman London is known almost entirely from archaeology, since the modern City is built directly over it. Highlights include the Temple of Mithras at Walbrook (excavated 1954 and reconstructed at Temple Court), the amphitheatre under Guildhall Yard, bath houses at Billingsgate and Huggin Hill, sections of the city wall at Tower Hill and the Barbican, and the extraordinary waterlogged finds of the Bloomberg excavations, including the earliest surviving handwritten documents in Britain — writing tablets that give Londinium its earliest recorded mention (c. AD 65–80) and even preserve the city's name and commercial life in ink. Much of this material is displayed at the Museum of London.
