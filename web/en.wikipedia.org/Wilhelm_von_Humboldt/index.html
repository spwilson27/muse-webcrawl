Wilhelm von Humboldt

Friedrich Wilhelm Christian Karl Ferdinand von Humboldt (22 June 1767 – 8 April 1835) was a German philosopher, linguist, government functionary, diplomat, and founder of the Humboldt University of Berlin. In 1949, the university was named in honour of him and his younger brother, Alexander von Humboldt, the naturalist.

Education reform

As Prussian minister responsible for education in 1809–1810, during the Prussian reforms after defeat by Napoleon, Humboldt designed a comprehensive school system and founded the University of Berlin (1810). His ideal of Bildung — self-cultivation through learning — and the unity of teaching and research (the "Humboldtian model" of higher education) influenced universities worldwide. He championed academic freedom and the humanistic Gymnasium with its emphasis on classical languages.

Linguistics and philosophy

Humboldt is regarded as a founder of the philosophy of language. He argued that language is not a finished product (ergon) but an activity (energeia), the creative expression of a people's worldview (Weltanschauung). Each language, he held, embodies a distinctive inner form shaping thought — an idea later developed into notions of linguistic relativity. His greatest work, On the Diversity of Human Language Construction (the introduction to his study of the Kawi language of Java), appeared posthumously in 1836.

Political thought and diplomacy

Humboldt's early treatise The Limits of State Action (written 1792, published 1851) is a classic defence of individual liberty that influenced John Stuart Mill's On Liberty. As a diplomat he served as Prussian envoy in Rome and Vienna and represented Prussia at the Congress of Vienna (1815).
