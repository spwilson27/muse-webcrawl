Euclidean space

Euclidean space is the fundamental space of geometry, intended to represent physical space. Originally, in Euclid's Elements, it was the three-dimensional space of Euclidean geometry, but in modern mathematics there are Euclidean spaces of any positive integer dimension, including Euclidean planes and higher-dimensional analogues.

Definition

Modern treatments define a Euclidean space as a real vector space equipped with an inner product, or equivalently as an affine space whose associated vector space has an inner product, so that distances and angles can be defined. With a Cartesian coordinate system, an n-dimensional Euclidean space is identified with ℝn, with distance given by the Euclidean distance formula.

Geometry

Euclidean geometry is characterized by the parallel postulate: through a point not on a given line there is exactly one line parallel to the given line. The discovery in the nineteenth century of consistent non-Euclidean geometries, in which this postulate fails, was a landmark in the history of mathematics.

Structure

Key structures in Euclidean space include points, lines, planes, angles, distances, rotations, and translations. The group of distance-preserving transformations is the Euclidean group. Euclidean spaces are complete metric spaces and serve as the local model for Riemannian manifolds.
