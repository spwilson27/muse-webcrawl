Weimar Classicism (German: Weimarer Klassik) was a German literary and cultural movement centred on the small court city of Weimar in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Its central figures were Johann Wolfgang von Goethe and Friedrich Schiller, together with Johann Gottfried Herder and Christoph Martin Wieland. Drawing on Winckelmann's idealised vision of classical antiquity and the humanism of the Enlightenment, the movement sought harmony, proportion and the all-round cultivation of the individual (Bildung), and produced many of the canonical works of German literature.

Origins

The movement is usually dated from Goethe's journey to Italy (1786–88), which transformed his writing: impressions of classical art and southern landscape reshaped his dramas Iphigenia in Tauris (rewritten in verse) and Torquato Tasso. Under Duke Karl August, Weimar's court theatre and the ducal patronage attracted an extraordinary concentration of talent for a town of some 6,000 inhabitants. Herder's philosophy of history and language and Wieland's cosmopolitan translations and journals prepared the ground.

The Goethe–Schiller alliance

The classic phase proper is often dated 1794–1805, from the beginning of the friendship between Goethe and Schiller to Schiller's early death. Their correspondence is a foundational document of German aesthetics. Together they edited the journal Die Horen, collaborated on the satirical Xenien epigrams (1796), and developed a shared programme contrasting naive and sentimental poetry. Major works of the period include Goethe's Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship (1795–96) and the verse epic Hermann and Dorothea (1797), and Schiller's Wallenstein trilogy (1798–99), Mary Stuart (1800), The Maid of Orleans (1801) and William Tell (1804), as well as his ballads and his treatise On the Aesthetic Education of Man (1795).

Ideas

Weimar Classicism combined Enlightenment rationalism with a classicist ideal of beauty as the union of form and content, freedom and law. Its humanist programme of aesthetic education was in part a response to the French Revolution and the Terror: political change was to follow from the moral and aesthetic cultivation of individuals rather than violent upheaval. Schiller's concept of the "beautiful soul" and Goethe's ideal of harmonious self-development became touchstones of the German Bildungsbürgertum.

Legacy

In the nineteenth century the Goethe–Schiller partnership was monumentalised — literally, in Ernst Rietschel's 1857 bronze double statue before the Weimar theatre — and Weimar Classicism was canonised as the summit of a German national literature, sometimes at the expense of the rival early Romantics of nearby Jena. The period's end is conventionally marked by Schiller's death in 1805, though Goethe's later work, including Faust, continued until his death in 1832. "Classical Weimar" is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
