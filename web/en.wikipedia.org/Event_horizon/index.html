Event horizon

In astrophysics, an event horizon is a boundary beyond which events cannot affect an observer. Wolfgang Rindler coined the term in the 1950s. In 1784, John Michell proposed that gravity can be strong enough that even light cannot escape, making a body invisible; the modern concept arises in general relativity, where the event horizon of a black hole is a surface from which nothing, including light, can escape.

Event horizon of a black hole

For a non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole, the event horizon coincides with the Schwarzschild radius:

rs = 2GM / c2

where G is the gravitational constant, M the mass, and c the speed of light. For rotating (Kerr) black holes the geometry is more complex; the horizon is surrounded by an ergosphere from which energy can in principle be extracted.

Properties

An observer falling into a black hole crosses the horizon in finite proper time, though a distant observer sees the infalling object redshift and fade, never quite appearing to cross. In 1974 Stephen Hawking showed that black holes emit radiation (Hawking radiation), leading to questions about information loss at the horizon.

Other horizons

Related concepts include the cosmological event horizon, particle horizons, and the Rindler horizon of an accelerating observer in flat spacetime.
