Street photography

Street photography, also sometimes called candid photography, is photography conducted for art or inquiry that features unmediated chance encounters and random incidents within public places. It usually, although not always, focuses on people and their behavior in public; in this respect street photographers are similar to social documentary photographers or photojournalists. The genre overlaps with documentary photography and photojournalism, but street photography need not document news: it often aims at capturing a "decisive moment", to use Henri Cartier-Bresson's term.

History

Early practitioners such as Eugène Atget documented Paris streets around the turn of the 20th century. The development of small, discreet 35 mm rangefinder cameras, notably the Leica, enabled the classic period of street photography from the 1930s onward, exemplified by Cartier-Bresson, Robert Doisneau, and André Kertész. In the United States, the New York School — including Robert Frank, whose book The Americans (1958) redefined the genre, William Klein, Diane Arbus, Garry Winogrand, and Lee Friedlander — produced influential work in the postwar decades.

Technique

Street photographers often use unobtrusive cameras, wide-angle lenses, and zone focusing to capture spontaneous scenes without disturbing them. Many work in black and white, though color practitioners such as William Eggleston, Martin Parr, and Saul Leiter are prominent. Legal treatment varies by country: in many jurisdictions photographing in public places is permitted, but privacy laws and commercial-use rules impose limits.
