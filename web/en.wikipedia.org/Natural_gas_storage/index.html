Natural gas storage

Natural gas storage refers to the holding of natural gas in underground or above-ground facilities so that supply can be balanced with demand. Because natural gas production is relatively steady while consumption — especially for heating — is strongly seasonal, storage allows gas injected during low-demand periods (typically April to October in North America) to be withdrawn during winter peaks. Storage also provides operational flexibility, supply security, and a buffer against production or pipeline disruptions.

Types of underground storage

The principal forms of underground storage are:

  • Depleted oil and gas reservoirs — the most common type; converted fields near existing pipeline infrastructure.
  • Aquifers — porous rock formations containing water, requiring more cushion gas and careful geological characterization.
  • Salt caverns — leached cavities in salt domes or bedded salt; they offer high injection and withdrawal rates, making them suited to peaking and frequent cycling, but have smaller capacities.

A portion of stored gas, called base or cushion gas, must remain in the reservoir to maintain pressure; the remainder is working gas available for withdrawal. Liquefied natural gas (LNG) and above-ground holders provide supplementary storage in some markets.

Scale and significance

The United States has several hundred active underground storage facilities with roughly 4–5 trillion cubic feet of working gas capacity; storage levels are reported weekly by the Energy Information Administration and closely watched by energy markets. In Europe, storage obligations and fill targets became a central policy issue following the 2022 energy crisis. The 2015 Aliso Canyon gas leak in California — the largest methane leak from a storage facility in U.S. history — highlighted safety and environmental risks of the sector.
