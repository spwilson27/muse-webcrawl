The Bronze Age in Europe covers the period from roughly 3200 BC to about 600 BC, when bronze — an alloy of copper and tin — became the principal material for tools and weapons. It succeeded the Neolithic and Copper Ages and was followed by the Iron Age, with timing varying by region: bronze working began earliest in the Aegean and spread gradually across the continent.

Regions and cultures

In the Aegean, the Bronze Age saw the rise of the Minoan and Mycenaean civilisations. In central Europe, the Únětice culture (c. 2300–1600 BC) was an early centre of bronze production, followed by the Tumulus culture and later the Urnfield culture (c. 1300–750 BC), characterised by cremation burials in urnfields. The Nordic Bronze Age (c. 1700–500 BC) flourished in Scandinavia, notable for its rock art and ship imagery, while the Atlantic Bronze Age linked coastal communities from Iberia to Britain and Ireland.

Trade and society

Because copper and tin rarely occur together, Bronze Age Europe depended on long-distance trade networks exchanging tin from Cornwall and Brittany, copper from the Alps and the Balkans, and prestige goods such as amber from the Baltic. Bronze working encouraged social stratification, chiefdoms, specialised crafts and warfare centred on warrior elites, evidenced by rich burials and hoards of weapons and ornaments.

Collapse and transition

Around 1200 BC the Late Bronze Age collapse disrupted the eastern Mediterranean world. In Europe, the spread of ironworking technology from about 800 BC onwards — later in Scandinavia — gradually displaced bronze for tools and weapons, though bronze remained in use for vessels and ornaments.
