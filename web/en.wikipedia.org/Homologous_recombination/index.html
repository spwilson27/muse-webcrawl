Homologous recombination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homologous recombination is a type of genetic recombination in which genetic information is exchanged between two similar or identical molecules of double-stranded or single-stranded nucleic acids (usually DNA in cellular organisms, but sometimes RNA in viruses). Homologous recombination is widely used by cells to accurately repair harmful DNA double-strand breaks, and it produces new combinations of DNA sequences during meiosis, the specialised cell division that generates gametes in sexually reproducing organisms. These new combinations of DNA represent genetic variation in offspring, which in turn enables populations to adapt evolutionarily over time.

Mechanism

When a double-strand break occurs, the MRN/MRX complex and other factors resect the 5′ ends of the broken DNA, leaving 3′ single-stranded tails. In eukaryotes the recombinase RAD51 (assisted in meiosis by DMC1) coats a tail and searches for a homologous template, typically the sister chromatid, invading it to form a displacement loop (D-loop). DNA synthesis from the invading strand, followed by capture of the second end, produces joint molecules containing Holliday junctions. These are resolved either as crossovers — reciprocal exchanges of chromosome arms — or as non-crossovers, often through synthesis-dependent strand annealing or dissolution. In bacteria, the RecBCD complex processes breaks and loads RecA, the bacterial recombinase; site-specific defence systems and phage recombinases exploit the same principles.

Roles in meiosis, repair, and disease

Crossovers formed in meiosis physically link homologous chromosomes, ensuring their correct segregation; failures cause aneuploidy. Mitotic homologous recombination is a high-fidelity repair pathway active in S and G2 phases, complementary to error-prone non-homologous end joining. Defects in its components, notably the tumour suppressors BRCA1 and BRCA2 and the Fanconi anaemia proteins, predispose to breast, ovarian, and other cancers, and render tumours sensitive to PARP inhibitors, which exploit the repair defect by synthetic lethality. Homologous recombination is also the natural mechanism harnessed in gene targeting and, combined with CRISPR nucleases, in precision genome editing.

History

Genetic crossing over was first described cytologically by Frans Janssens in 1909 and linked to gene exchange by Thomas Hunt Morgan's school. Robin Holliday proposed the junction model in 1964, and Jack Szostak and colleagues' double-strand-break repair model (1983) unified recombination with break repair. The pathway's elucidation is a landmark of molecular genetics, recognised in part by the broader body of work on DNA recombination and repair.
