Antitrust law

Antitrust (competition) law promotes and maintains market competition

Antitrust law, known as competition law outside the United States, is a body of law that regulates the conduct and organization of businesses in order to promote competition and prevent monopolies and unfair restraints of trade. It generally prohibits agreements that restrict competition (cartels), the abuse of a dominant market position, and mergers that would substantially lessen competition.

United States

The foundational US statutes are the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, which outlaws contracts and conspiracies in restraint of trade and monopolization; the Clayton Act of 1914, which addresses practices such as price discrimination and anticompetitive mergers; and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914, which created the Federal Trade Commission. Enforcement is shared between the FTC and the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice. Landmark cases include the breakup of Standard Oil (1911) and of AT&T (1982).

European Union and elsewhere

EU competition law rests chiefly on Articles 101 and 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, prohibiting anticompetitive agreements and abuse of dominance, enforced by the European Commission. More than 120 jurisdictions worldwide now have competition laws, most modelled on the US or EU approaches.

Key concepts

Core areas include cartel enforcement (price fixing, bid rigging, market allocation), merger control, monopolization and abuse of dominance, and the definition of relevant markets—often analysed with tools such as the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index.
