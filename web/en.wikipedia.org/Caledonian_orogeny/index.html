Caledonian orogeny

The Caledonian orogeny was a mountain-building episode recorded in the northern parts of Ireland and Britain, the Scandinavian Mountains, Svalbard, eastern Greenland, and parts of north-central Europe. It resulted from the closure of the Iapetus Ocean and the collision of the continental fragments Laurentia, Baltica, and Avalonia during the Ordovician, Silurian, and Devonian periods, roughly 490 to 390 million years ago. The name derives from Caledonia, the Latin name for Scotland.

Stages

Early events included the Grampian phase in the Ordovician, when island arcs and oceanic material were accreted to the Laurentian margin, producing the Grampian Highlands of Scotland. The main Scandian phase, in the late Silurian to early Devonian, involved the collision of Baltica with Laurentia, thrusting great sheets of rock (nappes) hundreds of kilometres over the Baltic margin to form the Scandinavian Caledonides. The Acadian phase, largely in the Devonian, records the collision of Avalonia and related terranes, with deformation extending into the Appalachian Mountains of North America, which formed as part of the same mountain system before the Atlantic Ocean opened.

Geology and legacy

The orogeny created a mountain chain comparable in scale to the modern Himalayas, joining what are now the Appalachians, the Scottish Highlands, and the Scandinavian mountains into a single belt. Subsequent erosion reduced it, and the Old Red Sandstone was deposited in basins within and beside the mountains during the Devonian. When the Atlantic opened in the Cenozoic, the belt was split: its fragments now lie on both sides of the ocean, a key line of evidence for continental drift and for reconstructing the supercontinent Pangaea.
