Riemann mapping theorem

In complex analysis, the Riemann mapping theorem states that if U is a non-empty simply connected open subset of the complex plane which is not all of the complex plane, then there exists a biholomorphic (conformal) mapping from U onto the open unit disk. The theorem was first stated by Bernhard Riemann in his 1851 dissertation.

Statement and uniqueness

The map is unique if one additionally requires it to send a prescribed interior point to the centre of the disk and to have positive real derivative at that point. Intuitively, any simply connected region without holes can be stretched, without tearing, into a disk in a way that preserves angles.

Proof and history

Riemann's argument used the Dirichlet principle, whose rigour was questioned by Weierstrass; fully rigorous proofs came later from work by Schwarz, Neumann, and others, with an elementary proof by Koebe and later streamlined versions using normal families (Montel's theorem). Carathéodory extended the result to boundary behaviour.

Applications and generalisations

The theorem underlies conformal mapping methods in physics and engineering, and its failure in higher dimensions (where conformal maps are only Möbius transformations) motivated the development of quasiconformal mappings. In several complex variables it fails outright, as shown by Poincaré. Its sweeping generalisation is the uniformization theorem for Riemann surfaces.
