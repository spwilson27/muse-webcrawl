Pre-eclampsia

Pre-eclampsia is a multi-system disorder of pregnancy characterised by the new onset of hypertension (high blood pressure) after 20 weeks of gestation, together with signs of damage to another organ system, most often protein in the urine (proteinuria) or dysfunction of the liver, kidneys, blood or nervous system.

Signs and symptoms

Many women with pre-eclampsia have no symptoms and are detected by routine blood pressure and urine screening. When symptoms occur they may include severe headache, visual disturbances, upper abdominal pain, sudden swelling of the face and hands, nausea and reduced urine output. Severe features include very high blood pressure, low platelet count, impaired liver or kidney function and fluid in the lungs. The condition can progress to eclampsia — seizures in pregnancy — and to HELLP syndrome (haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets).

Causes and risk factors

The underlying cause is abnormal development of the placenta, in particular defective remodelling of the uterine spiral arteries, leading to placental ischaemia and release of factors that damage the maternal endothelium. Risk factors include first pregnancy, previous pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension, diabetes, obesity, autoimmune disorders such as antiphospholipid syndrome, multiple pregnancy, and a family history of the condition. Pre-eclampsia affects roughly 2–8 per cent of pregnancies worldwide and is a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality.

Prevention and treatment

Low-dose aspirin started in early pregnancy reduces risk in women at high risk, and calcium supplementation helps where dietary intake is low. The only cure is delivery of the baby and placenta; management balances the risks of prematurity against maternal deterioration, using antihypertensive drugs to control blood pressure and magnesium sulfate to prevent seizures. Timing of delivery depends on severity and gestational age.
