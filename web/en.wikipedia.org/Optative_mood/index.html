Optative mood

The optative is a defining feature of the Ancient Greek verb system.

The optative mood is a grammatical mood that indicates a wish or hope regarding a given action. It is a mood found in a number of ancient and modern languages, most fully developed in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit, where it forms a complete set of verb inflections parallel to the indicative and subjunctive.

Ancient Greek

In Ancient Greek the optative is used for wishes ("may it happen!"), for potential statements with the particle ἄν (an), and in subordinate clauses after past-tense main verbs, for example in indirect speech and in purpose clauses following a secondary tense. Its characteristic marker is the diphthong -οι- (-oi-) in most formations. As Koine Greek developed, the optative fell out of everyday use and survives mainly in fixed expressions such as μὴ γένοιτο (mḕ génoito, "may it not happen!").

Sanskrit and Proto-Indo-European

The optative is reconstructed for Proto-Indo-European, where it marked wishes and possibilities with a suffix *-yeh₁-/*-ih₁-. Sanskrit preserves the mood richly; in classical Sanskrit it also serves as the normal mood of command and invitation (the "precative" use), much as in the Vedas.

Other languages

An optative mood survives in languages such as Albanian, and in Turkish a dedicated optative conjugation (dilek kipi) expresses wishes. In languages like English, which lack an inflectional optative, wishes are expressed periphrastically with auxiliaries such as may: "May you live long!"
