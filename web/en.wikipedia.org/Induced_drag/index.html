Induced drag

In fluid dynamics, induced drag is the drag that results from the production of lift by a finite wing. It arises because the lift-generating wing deflects air downward (downwash) and creates trailing vortices at the wingtips; the effective airflow over the wing is tilted, so the resultant aerodynamic force, perpendicular to the local flow, leans backward and has a component opposing the aircraft's motion.

Characteristics

Induced drag is greatest at low speeds and high lift coefficients — as in takeoff and landing — and decreases with the square of speed for a given weight. It is inversely proportional to the wing's aspect ratio: long, slender wings (as on gliders and airliners) produce less induced drag than short, stubby ones. Devices such as winglets reduce induced drag by weakening wingtip vortices, and ground effect reduces it when flying close to the surface.

Relation to total drag

Total aircraft drag is the sum of induced drag and parasite drag. The speed for minimum total drag occurs where the two are equal; induced drag dominates at low speeds and parasite drag at high speeds, a distinction central to aircraft performance and efficiency analysis.
