Halogen

The halogens are the chemical elements in group 17 of the periodic table: fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br), iodine (I), astatine (At), and tennessine (Ts). The name, proposed by Jöns Jakob Berzelius, comes from Greek roots meaning "salt-producing", because the elements form salts (halides) when they react with metals.

Properties

Halogens have seven valence electrons and need only one more to complete their octet, making them highly reactive nonmetals and strong oxidising agents; reactivity decreases down the group, fluorine being the most reactive and electronegative of all elements. At room temperature, fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine is a liquid, and iodine is a solid — a gradation reflecting increasing intermolecular forces. All are coloured, toxic and corrosive in elemental form, existing as diatomic molecules (F2, Cl2, Br2, I2).

Compounds and uses

The halogens form the halide salts, of which sodium chloride (table salt) is the best known. Hydrogen halides dissolve in water to give the hydrohalic acids. Chlorine is used to disinfect water and manufacture PVC; fluorine is used in fluoride toothpaste, Teflon and uranium enrichment; bromine compounds serve as flame retardants; iodine is an essential nutrient used in antiseptics. Organic halogen compounds include many pharmaceuticals and pesticides, as well as ozone-depleting chlorofluorocarbons.

Biological role

Iodine is essential for thyroid hormones; fluoride and chloride are also required in trace amounts. Astatine is extremely rare and radioactive, and tennessine has been made only a few atoms at a time.
