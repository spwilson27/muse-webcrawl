Richard Pryor


Richard Pryor (1940–2005), comedian and actor

Richard Franklin Lennox Thomas Pryor (December 1, 1940 – December 10, 2005) was an American stand-up comedian and actor. Widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential stand-up comedians of all time, Pryor pioneered an approach to comedy rooted in raw, confessional storytelling, vivid character voices and fearless treatment of racism, poverty and his own troubled life.

Early life

Pryor was born in Peoria, Illinois, and raised in his grandmother's brothel, where his mother worked; his childhood was marked by abuse and hardship that later furnished material for his act. After expulsion from school and a stint in the U.S. Army in Germany, he began performing in clubs in the early 1960s, initially imitating Bill Cosby's polished, race-neutral style and finding moderate success in television and nightclubs.

Transformation and stardom

Around 1969–1970 Pryor abandoned his mainstream persona — famously walking off stage mid-show in Las Vegas — and reinvented himself in Berkeley and Los Angeles with an uncompromising act reflecting the Black experience and his own life. His albums That Nigger's Crazy (1974), ...Is It Something I Said? (1975) and Bicentennial Nigger (1976) won Grammy Awards, and his concert films Richard Pryor: Live in Concert (1979), Live on the Sunset Strip (1982) and Here and Now (1983) are landmarks of the genre. He co-wrote Blazing Saddles and starred in films including Silver Streak, Stir Crazy (one of several hits with Gene Wilder), The Toy and Brewster's Millions.

Personal struggles and later years

Pryor's life was shadowed by drug addiction; in 1980 he suffered severe burns in a freebasing incident he later mined for comedy. He was diagnosed with multiple sclerosis in 1986, which progressively limited his mobility, though he continued to perform and act into the 1990s. He died of a heart attack in Encino, California, in 2005.

Legacy

Pryor received an Emmy Award, five Grammy Awards and the first Mark Twain Prize for American Humor (1998). Comedians from Eddie Murphy and Chris Rock to Dave Chappelle cite him as their foundational influence, and he routinely tops polls of the greatest stand-ups ever.
