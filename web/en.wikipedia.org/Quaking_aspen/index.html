Quaking aspen

Populus tremuloides is a deciduous tree native to cooler areas of North America, one of several species referred to by the common name aspen. It is commonly called trembling aspen, quaking aspen, American aspen, mountain aspen, golden aspen and white poplar. The trees have tall trunks, up to 25 metres (82 feet) tall, with smooth pale bark, scarred with black. The glossy green leaves, dull beneath, become golden to yellow, rarely red, in autumn. The species often propagates through its roots to form large clonal colonies originating from a shared root system.

Distribution

Quaking aspen has one of the widest distributions of any tree in North America, ranging from Canada and Alaska across the United States to the mountains of central Mexico. It grows on a wide range of soils and is a fast-growing, shade-intolerant pioneer species that colonises disturbed sites after fire or logging. Individual stems typically live 40 to 150 years, though the root system can persist far longer.

Clonal colonies

The most famous clonal colony is Pando, in the Fishlake National Forest of Utah: a single male quaking aspen whose root system supports some 47,000 stems over about 43 hectares, making it one of the largest and oldest known organisms in the world, with an estimated age of many thousands of years. Groves of aspen provide important habitat for wildlife and are noted for turning whole mountainsides gold in autumn; the leaves "quake" in the slightest breeze because of their flattened petioles.
