Effective altruism

Effective altruism is a philosophical and social movement that advocates using evidence and reason to determine the most effective ways to benefit others. Adherents, called effective altruists, aim to consider all causes and actions and to act in the way that brings about the greatest positive impact, based upon a scientific approach to social change. The movement developed during the 2000s and 2010s, drawing on the work of philosophers such as Peter Singer and Toby Ord.

Philosophy

Effective altruism rests on several core ideas: that some ways of doing good are vastly more effective than others; that impartiality matters, so a life saved far away counts as much as one nearby; and that resources should be allocated according to scale, neglectedness and tractability of problems. The movement is closely associated with utilitarianism and with consequentialism, though many adherents do not identify with either label.

History

The term "effective altruism" was adopted around 2011 at a meeting of the Centre for Effective Altruism in Oxford. Singer's 1972 essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" and his 2009 book The Life You Can Save were major influences. Giving What We Can, founded by Toby Ord in 2009, encourages members to pledge at least 10% of their income to effective charities. The charity evaluator GiveWell, founded in 2007, researches and recommends highly cost-effective charities, particularly in global health.

Priority causes

Common priority areas include global health and development (such as distributing antimalarial bed nets and deworming treatments), reducing factory farming and improving animal welfare, mitigating existential risks such as pandemics and misaligned artificial general intelligence, and addressing climate change. The idea of "earning to give" — taking a high-paying job in order to donate more — was promoted by organisations such as 80,000 Hours.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the movement's reliance on utilitarian calculation, its focus on measurable interventions over systemic change, and the influence of wealthy donors. The collapse of the cryptocurrency exchange FTX in 2022, whose founder Sam Bankman-Fried was a prominent supporter, brought significant public scrutiny to the movement.
