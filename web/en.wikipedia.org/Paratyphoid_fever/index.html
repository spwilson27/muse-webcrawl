Paratyphoid fever

Paratyphoid fever, also known simply as paratyphoid, is a bacterial infection caused by one of the serotypes of Salmonella enterica — Paratyphi A, B, or C. Together with typhoid fever it forms the group known as enteric fever. Symptoms are similar to those of typhoid — sustained high fever, headache, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhoea, and sometimes a rash of rose spots — but the disease is often (though not always) somewhat milder. Infection is spread by food or water contaminated with the faeces of an infected person.

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of about 6–30 days, illness begins gradually with fever that rises stepwise, accompanied by headache, malaise, loss of appetite and abdominal discomfort. Rose-coloured spots may appear on the trunk. Untreated, the fever can persist for weeks, and complications include intestinal perforation and bleeding, which can be fatal. Some patients become chronic carriers, shedding bacteria in stool or urine for more than a year — historically, carriers such as "Typhoid Mary" (a typhoid carrier) illustrated the public-health danger of asymptomatic carriage.

Cause and transmission

Paratyphi bacteria infect only humans. They are ingested with contaminated food or water, invade the intestinal wall, and spread via the bloodstream and reticuloendothelial system. The organisms are shed in faeces, and transmission is sustained where sanitation and clean water are lacking. Paratyphi B also occurs in parts of Europe and can have a reservoir pattern distinct from A, which predominates in Asia.

Diagnosis and treatment

The definitive diagnosis is culture of the organism from blood (most sensitive early) or bone marrow; the older Widal serological test is unreliable. Treatment is with antibiotics chosen by local resistance patterns — increasingly important as fluoroquinolone resistance and, for typhoid, extensively drug-resistant strains have spread. Supportive care and hydration are essential; surgical care may be needed for perforation.

Epidemiology and prevention

Enteric fever together causes an estimated 9–14 million illnesses and over 100,000 deaths annually, mainly in South and Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa; paratyphoid accounts for a substantial and in some regions growing share, particularly Paratyphi A in China and South Asia. Prevention depends on clean water, sanitation and food hygiene. Vaccines protect against typhoid but generally not paratyphoid B; a Paratyphi A vaccine remains a research goal.
