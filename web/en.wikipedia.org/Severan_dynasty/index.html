Severan dynasty

The Severan tondo, a painted portrait of the imperial family.

The Severan dynasty ruled the Roman Empire from AD 193 to 235, the last dynasty before the Crisis of the Third Century. It was founded by Septimius Severus, a senator and general from Leptis Magna in North Africa, who emerged victorious from the civil wars of the Year of the Five Emperors following the murder of Commodus and Pertinax.

Emperors

Septimius Severus (reigned 193–211) secured his position by defeating rival claimants, expanded the empire in Mesopotamia and Britain, rebuilt extensively in Rome and enriched the army, famously advising his sons to "enrich the soldiers, and scorn all other men". He was succeeded jointly by his sons Caracalla and Geta; Caracalla soon had Geta murdered and reigned alone until his own assassination in 217. After the brief interruption of Macrinus, the dynasty resumed with Elagabalus (218–222), a teenage priest of the Emesene sun god, and then Severus Alexander (222–235). The powerful women of the dynasty — Julia Domna, Julia Maesa, Julia Soaemias and Julia Mamaea — exercised unusual political influence. Severus Alexander's murder by his own troops in 235 ended the dynasty and opened half a century of instability.

Legacy

The Severan period is noted for Caracalla's Constitutio Antoniniana of 212, which granted Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire; for monumental building such as the Baths of Caracalla and the Arch of Septimius Severus; for a flowering of Roman law under jurists like Papinian and Ulpian; and for the growing military and provincial character of the imperial office.
