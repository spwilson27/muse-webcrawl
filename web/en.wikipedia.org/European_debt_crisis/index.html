European debt crisis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The European debt crisis, often referred to as the Eurozone crisis, was a multi-year sovereign-debt and banking crisis that affected several eurozone member states from late 2009. Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain, and Cyprus struggled to repay or refinance government debt and to rescue over-leveraged banks without the assistance of other eurozone countries, the European Central Bank (ECB), and the International Monetary Fund (IMF).

Origins

The crisis grew out of the 2007–2008 global financial crisis, large pre-crisis current-account imbalances within the eurozone, property bubbles in Ireland and Spain, and the structural weaknesses of a monetary union lacking a full fiscal and banking union. In October 2009 the incoming Greek government revealed that the budget deficit was far larger than previously reported, triggering a loss of market confidence and soaring bond yields.

Bailouts and policy response

Between 2010 and 2013 the eurozone and IMF provided bailout loans to Greece (three programmes, totalling more than €280 billion in commitments), Ireland (€85 billion), Portugal (€78 billion), Spain's banks (€100 billion facility, of which €41.3 billion was drawn), and Cyprus (€10 billion). Assistance was conditioned on fiscal austerity and structural reforms, which deepened recessions and unemployment in the recipient countries.

The ECB under Mario Draghi calmed markets in July 2012 with the pledge to do "whatever it takes" to preserve the euro, followed by the Outright Monetary Transactions programme. New institutions — the temporary EFSF and the permanent European Stability Mechanism — were created, and the EU launched banking union with the Single Supervisory Mechanism in 2014. Greece's 2012 private-sector involvement, which wrote down over €100 billion of bonds, remains the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history.

Aftermath

By the mid-2010s spreads had narrowed and the bailed-out countries returned to growth, but the crisis left lasting political scars: the rise of anti-austerity and populist parties, the 2015 Greek referendum and Grexit scare, and continuing debates about fiscal rules, debt mutualisation, and the completeness of the euro's architecture.
