Preferential attachment

In preferential attachment, new nodes preferentially link to already well-connected nodes.

Preferential attachment is a family of processes in which some quantity — most famously the number of links to a node in a growing network — is distributed among individuals or nodes according to how much they already have: the more a node has, the more likely it is to receive more. The idea underlies the familiar "rich get richer" or Matthew effect and explains how highly skewed, power-law distributions arise.

History and models

Versions of the process were described by Udny Yule (1925) for species counts and by Herbert Simon (1955) for word frequencies and incomes; Derek de Solla Price used a cumulative-advantage mechanism for citation networks in 1976. The best-known formulation is the Barabási–Albert model (1999), in which each new node joins a network and connects to existing nodes with probability proportional to their degree, generating a scale-free network with degree distribution P(k) ~ k−3.

Applications

Preferential attachment has been invoked to explain the structure of the World Wide Web, scientific citation networks, collaboration networks, wealth distributions and city sizes (Zipf's law). Variants add node fitness, ageing, or initial attractiveness, and non-linear attachment kernels produce different regimes, including winner-take-all condensation.
