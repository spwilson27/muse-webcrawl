Absolute (philosophy)

In philosophy, the Absolute is the term used for the ultimate reality — that which is self-existent, unconditioned, and independent of anything else, in contrast to the relative and dependent things of ordinary experience. The concept is central to absolute idealism and appears, in various guises, in many religious and metaphysical systems.

Idealism

The notion was developed most fully by the German idealists. F. W. J. Schelling identified the Absolute with the undifferentiated unity of subject and object, nature and spirit. G. W. F. Hegel criticised such an empty unity ("the night in which all cows are black") and conceived the Absolute instead as the whole process of reality coming to know itself through history: "the True is the whole". For Hegel, the Absolute is both substance and subject, realised in Absolute Spirit through art, religion, and philosophy.

British idealism and beyond

British idealists such as F. H. Bradley, in Appearance and Reality (1893), argued that reality is a single harmonious Absolute experience in which the contradictions of appearance are resolved. Similar notions appear in Hindu philosophy as Brahman, the unconditioned ground of being, and in some theological traditions as a description of God. Critics, including pragmatists and existentialists, have attacked the Absolute as an empty abstraction; Søren Kierkegaard and later thinkers stressed the irreducibly individual and temporal character of existence against Hegelian system-building.
