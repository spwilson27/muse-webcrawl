Spinocerebellar ataxia

Spinocerebellar ataxia (SCA) is a progressive, degenerative, genetic disease with multiple types, each of which could be considered a neurological condition in its own right. An estimated 150,000 people in the United States have a diagnosis of spinocerebellar ataxia at any given time. SCA is hereditary, progressive, degenerative, and often fatal.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include loss of balance and coordination (ataxia), slurred speech (dysarthria), difficulty swallowing, impaired eye movements, and sometimes cognitive or peripheral nerve involvement depending on the type. Onset is typically in adulthood and progression varies by type; many SCAs are caused by CAG trinucleotide repeat expansions, similar to Huntington's disease.

Classification

Dozens of types (SCA1, SCA2, SCA3/Machado–Joseph disease, SCA6, SCA7, and many others) are distinguished by the causative gene and locus. Most are autosomal dominant. SCA3 is the most common dominant ataxia worldwide.

Treatment

There is no cure or specific treatment for most forms. Management is supportive, including physical, occupational, and speech therapy, mobility aids, and treatment of associated symptoms. Genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis and enable family screening.
