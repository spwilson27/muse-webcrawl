Group velocity

The group velocity of a wave is the velocity with which the overall shape of the wave's amplitudes — known as the modulation or envelope of the wave — propagates through space. It is defined by vg = dω/dk, where ω is the angular frequency and k is the wavenumber (spatial angular frequency).

Relation to phase velocity

The phase velocity, vp = ω/k, is the speed at which individual wave crests (points of constant phase) travel. In a non-dispersive medium the two velocities are equal. In a dispersive medium, where phase velocity depends on frequency, the group and phase velocities differ: for light in normal dispersion the group velocity is less than the phase velocity, while the crests can be seen moving through the envelope.

Significance

The group velocity is usually interpreted as the velocity at which energy and information carried by the wave travel. In optical fibres and waveguides, signals propagate at the group velocity, so its frequency dependence (group-velocity dispersion) causes pulses to spread — a key limitation in telecommunications. In quantum mechanics, a free particle's wave packet moves at the group velocity, which equals the particle's classical velocity.

Anomalous cases

In regions of strong anomalous dispersion, the group velocity can exceed the speed of light in vacuum, or even become negative. This does not permit faster-than-light communication, because the true signal velocity — the speed at which genuinely new information (a wavefront) travels — remains at or below c. Careful experiments with pulses in absorbing and amplifying media have demonstrated superluminal group velocities while preserving causality.
