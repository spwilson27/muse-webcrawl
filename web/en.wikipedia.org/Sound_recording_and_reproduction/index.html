Sound recording and reproduction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A recording studio mixing console

Sound recording and reproduction is the electrical, mechanical, electronic or digital inscription and re-creation of sound waves, such as spoken voice, singing, instrumental music, or sound effects. The two main classes of sound recording technology are analog recording and digital recording.

History

The earliest device for recording sound was Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville's phonautograph (1857), which traced sound waves on paper but could not play them back. Thomas Edison's phonograph of 1877 recorded sound as indentations on tinfoil-wrapped cylinders and could reproduce it, launching the recording industry. Emile Berliner's gramophone, using flat discs, proved easier to mass-produce and overtook cylinders by the 1910s.

Electrical recording in the 1920s, using microphones and amplifiers, greatly improved fidelity. Magnetic tape, developed in Germany in the 1930s and adopted worldwide after the Second World War, allowed editing and multitrack recording, transforming studio practice. The long-playing vinyl record (1948), stereophonic sound (1950s) and the compact cassette (1963) successively reshaped home listening.

Digital era

The compact disc, launched in 1982, made digital audio the consumer standard. Computer-based digital audio workstations replaced tape in studios during the 1990s, and compressed formats such as MP3 enabled distribution over the internet. Music streaming services in the 2000s and 2010s shifted the industry from ownership of recordings to subscription access, even as vinyl records enjoyed a revival.

Process

Recording begins with transducers — microphones — that convert sound into electrical signals. In the studio those signals are captured, edited, mixed and mastered; reproduction reverses the process through amplifiers and loudspeakers or headphones. Key technical concepts include frequency response, dynamic range, signal-to-noise ratio and sampling theory for digital systems.
