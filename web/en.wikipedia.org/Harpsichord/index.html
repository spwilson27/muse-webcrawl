Harpsichord

Double-manual harpsichord, Flemish school

ClassificationKeyboard instrument (plucked string)
DevelopedLate Middle Ages; flourished 16th–18th centuries
RelatedVirginals, spinet, clavichord, piano

A harpsichord is a keyboard instrument in which the strings are plucked, rather than struck, when a key is pressed. Pressing a key raises a vertical wooden jack fitted with a plectrum (traditionally a quill), which plucks the string; on release a damper silences it. Because the pluck is mechanically uniform, loudness cannot be varied by touch, and players shape expression through timing, articulation and registration instead. The harpsichord was a principal instrument of Renaissance and Baroque music, central to the basso continuo and to a vast solo literature.

History

The instrument emerged in the late medieval period, probably in the 14th–15th centuries, and by 1500 flourishing schools of building existed in Italy, with light cypress cases and a bright, short-lived tone, and in Flanders, where the Ruckers family of Antwerp built the heavier, sonorous instruments that dominated European music-making. French makers such as Blanchet and Taskin later remodelled Flemish instruments (ravalement) and developed the classic French double-manual harpsichord. English and German schools produced their own variants, including the expressive instruments of Kirkman and Shudi in London.

Varieties

The family includes the small rectangular virginals, the triangular spinet, and full-size wing-shaped instruments with one or two manuals and multiple registers (two 8-foot stops, a 4-foot stop, buff and lute stops). Composers such as Byrd, Frescobaldi, Froberger, Couperin, Rameau, Handel, Domenico Scarlatti and J. S. Bach — whose Well-Tempered Clavier and Goldberg Variations crown the repertoire — wrote idiomatically for these varieties.

Decline and revival

The harpsichord was displaced by the piano around 1800 for its dynamic range, and production nearly ceased. A revival began in the late 19th century and gathered force with Wanda Landowska, whose playing and teaching, and the iron-framed Pleyel instruments built for her, restored the harpsichord to concert life. The later 20th century saw a return to historically based copies of antique instruments, championed by players such as Gustav Leonhardt, and the harpsichord became indispensable to the historically informed performance movement; modern composers from Poulenc to Ligeti have also written for it.
