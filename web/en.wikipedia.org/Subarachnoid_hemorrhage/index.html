Subarachnoid hemorrhage

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is bleeding into the subarachnoid space — the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. Symptoms may include a severe headache of rapid onset ("thunderclap headache"), vomiting, decreased level of consciousness, fever, seizures, and neck stiffness. A small amount of bleeding may also occur before the main event (a "sentinel bleed").

SAH may occur as a result of a head injury or spontaneously, usually from a ruptured cerebral aneurysm. Risk factors for spontaneous cases include high blood pressure, smoking, family history, alcoholism, and cocaine use. Overall, about a quarter of people with the condition die within a month of presentation and about a third of survivors have ongoing neurological problems.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis is typically made with a CT scan of the head without contrast, which has very high sensitivity within six hours of onset. If the CT is negative but suspicion remains, a lumbar puncture looking for blood or xanthochromia (yellow discolouration of cerebrospinal fluid from bilirubin) is performed. CT angiography then localises the aneurysm. Clinical grading scales, such as the Hunt and Hess and World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies scales, stratify severity and prognosis.

Treatment

Treatment is centred on securing the aneurysm as early as feasible, either by neurosurgical clipping or by endovascular coiling; the ISAT trial found better functional outcomes with coiling for aneurysms suitable for either technique. Care in a high-volume centre, nimodipine to improve outcomes, management of hydrocephalus with external ventricular drainage, and prevention or treatment of delayed cerebral ischaemia from vasospasm are key elements of management.
