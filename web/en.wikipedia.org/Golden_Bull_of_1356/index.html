Golden Bull of 1356

The Golden Bull of 1356 (Latin: Bulla Aurea) was a decree issued by the Imperial Diet at Nuremberg and Metz, headed by Emperor Charles IV, that fixed the constitutional structure of the Holy Roman Empire for more than four centuries, until the Empire's dissolution in 1806. It takes its name from the golden seal (bulla aurea) attached to the document.

Contents

The Bull definitively named the seven prince-electors entitled to elect the King of the Romans: the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne; the King of Bohemia; the Count Palatine of the Rhine; the Duke of Saxony; and the Margrave of Brandenburg. It prescribed election by majority vote at Frankfurt, coronation at Aachen, and established primogeniture and the indivisibility of the electoral territories. It also granted the electors extensive regalian rights, including minting, tolls, and mining, and regulated ceremonial precedence.

Significance

By settling the electoral procedure, the Golden Bull ended recurring disputes and double elections, while entrenching the position of the great territorial princes — and notably excluding the Habsburg dukes of Austria, who answered with the forged Privilegium Maius. It remained a fundamental law of the Empire for the rest of its existence.
