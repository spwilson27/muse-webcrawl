Sleight of hand

FieldMagic (illusion), close-up magic
Also calledPrestidigitation, legerdemain
Common propsCards, coins, balls, cups
Key techniquesPalming, false transfer, switch, ditching, misdirection

Sleight of hand — also called prestidigitation or legerdemain — refers to the fine motor skills and manual dexterity used by performing magicians to secretly manipulate objects, typically in close-up magic with cards, coins and small props. It is distinguished from effects achieved by mechanical apparatus: the secret lies in the performer's hands and in the management of the audience's attention.

Techniques

Fundamental sleights include palming (secretly holding an object in the hand), the false transfer (appearing to place an object in the other hand while retaining it), false shuffles and cuts that preserve a deck's order, the pass (secretly shifting a packet of cards), and ditching (disposing of an object unnoticed). These are combined with misdirection — timing, gaze, gesture and patter that steer spectators' attention away from the secret action.

Practice and performance

Proficiency demands thousands of hours of practice, often before a mirror, until moves can be executed without conscious effort and with natural-looking hands. Classic texts include The Expert at the Card Table (1902) and The Royal Road to Card Magic (1948). The cups and balls routine, depicted in ancient Egyptian art, is among the oldest documented sleight-of-hand effects.

Beyond magic

The same manual methods have been used by card cheats, pickpockets and confidence tricksters; professional magicians are sometimes consulted by casinos and police. Research in psychology and neuroscience also uses sleight of hand to study attention and perception.
