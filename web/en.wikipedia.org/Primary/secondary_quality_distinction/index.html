Primary/secondary quality distinction

The primary/secondary quality distinction is a conceptual distinction in epistemology and metaphysics, most famously drawn by John Locke in his Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689). Primary qualities are properties of objects that are independent of any observer — such as solidity, extension (size and shape), motion or rest, and number. Secondary qualities are properties that produce sensations in observers but do not exist in the object itself in the form in which they are experienced — such as colour, taste, smell, and sound.

For Locke, primary qualities resemble the ideas they cause; secondary qualities do not.

History

The distinction has ancient roots in Democritus ("by convention sweet, by convention bitter... in reality atoms and the void") and was developed in the scientific revolution by Galileo, who held in The Assayer (1623) that tastes, odours, and colours reside only in consciousness, and by Robert Boyle, from whom Locke took the terminology. Locke argued that ideas of primary qualities are resemblances of features genuinely in bodies, whereas ideas of secondary qualities are caused by the powers that the microscopic texture of primary qualities gives objects to affect our senses.

Criticism

George Berkeley attacked the distinction in his Principles of Human Knowledge (1710), arguing that all the arguments showing secondary qualities to be mind-dependent apply equally to primary qualities, and that extension and shape cannot be abstracted from colour and the other sensible qualities. The distinction nevertheless became foundational for the mechanistic worldview of modern science, which describes nature in terms of measurable, observer-independent properties.
