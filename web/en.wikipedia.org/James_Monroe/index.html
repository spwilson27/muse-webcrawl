James Monroe (April 28, 1758 – July 4, 1831) was an American statesman, lawyer and diplomat who served as the fifth president of the United States from 1817 to 1825. A member of the Democratic-Republican Party, he was the last president who was also a Founding Father, and the last of the "Virginia dynasty" of presidents.

Early life and Revolutionary War

Monroe was born in Westmoreland County, Virginia, and studied at the College of William and Mary. He left college in 1776 to join the Continental Army, serving with distinction and being wounded at the Battle of Trenton. He later studied law under Thomas Jefferson, became a protégé of Jefferson and James Madison, and served in the Continental Congress and the US Senate, and as governor of Virginia and minister to France and Britain. As Madison's secretary of state (1811–1817) and concurrently secretary of war, he was effectively the second most powerful man in the government during the War of 1812.

Presidency

Monroe's presidency marked the "Era of Good Feelings", a period of diminished partisan conflict following the collapse of the Federalist Party. He won re-election in 1820 almost unanimously, receiving every electoral vote but one. His administration negotiated the acquisition of Florida from Spain (Adams–Onís Treaty, 1819), settled boundaries with British Canada, and managed the sectional crisis over slavery with the Missouri Compromise of 1820.

In 1823 he announced the Monroe Doctrine in his annual message to Congress, declaring that the Americas were closed to further European colonization and that European interference in the hemisphere would be viewed as a hostile act — a cornerstone of American foreign policy for two centuries.

Later life and legacy

After leaving office, Monroe retired to his Oak Hill estate in Virginia, burdened by debt. He presided over the Virginia Constitutional Convention of 1829–30. He died in New York City on 4 July 1831 — the third president to die on Independence Day. The capital of Liberia, Monrovia, is named after him.
