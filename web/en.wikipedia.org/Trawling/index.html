Trawling

A stern trawler hauling its net; the net is held open by otter boards (trawl doors).

Trawling is a method of fishing in which a net is actively pulled through the water behind one or more vessels, called trawlers. The net — a trawl — is a large, funnel-shaped mesh whose mouth is held open horizontally, typically by a pair of heavy steel otter boards (trawl doors) that plane apart as the vessel moves, and vertically by floats along the headline and weights on the groundrope. Fish are funnelled into the closed end of the net, the "cod end", where they are retained.

Types

Bottom trawling drags the net along or just above the seabed to catch demersal species such as cod, haddock, plaice and prawns. Midwater (pelagic) trawling tows the net in the water column for schooling fish such as herring, mackerel and hake, usually with much larger nets monitored by sonar. Trawling may be done by a single vessel or by two vessels in pair trawling, each towing one side of the net.

History

Sailing trawlers worked the North Sea from the English port of Brixham in the 19th century; steam and then diesel power, combined with stern ramps and onboard freezing, produced the modern factory trawler capable of weeks at sea. Beam trawls, which hold the net open with a rigid beam, remain common for flatfish and shrimp.

Environmental concerns

Bottom trawling is controversial because the gear can damage seabed habitats such as cold-water coral and sponge communities, resuspend sediments, and catch non-target species (bycatch), including juvenile fish and marine mammals. Management measures include mesh-size rules, turtle excluder devices and bycatch reduction grids, closed areas, and bans on trawling in some deep-sea and inshore zones.
