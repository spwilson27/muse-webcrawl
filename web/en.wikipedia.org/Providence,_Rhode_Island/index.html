Providence is the capital and most populous city of the U.S. state of Rhode Island. Founded in 1636 by the theologian Roger Williams, who had been exiled from the Massachusetts Bay Colony for his religious views, it is one of the oldest cities in the United States. Williams named the settlement in gratitude for "God's merciful Providence" and established it as a refuge for religious dissenters.

History

Providence grew as a port and trading town, prospering in the 18th century on maritime commerce — including, notoriously, the slave trade — and in the 19th century became a leading industrial centre for textiles, machinery, jewellery, and silverware. Manufacturing declined sharply after the Second World War, and the city underwent a long period of economic difficulty before a revival from the 1990s, aided by the relocation of its rivers, new public spaces, and its universities.

Geography and culture

The city lies at the head of Narragansett Bay where the Woonasquatucket and Moshassuck rivers meet. It is home to Brown University, the Rhode Island School of Design (RISD), and Johnson & Wales University. Providence is noted for its preserved colonial and 19th-century architecture, particularly on Benefit Street, and for WaterFire, an art installation of braziers lit on its downtown rivers, created by artist Barnaby Evans in 1994.
