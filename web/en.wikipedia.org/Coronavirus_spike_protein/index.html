Coronavirus spike protein

The spike protein (S protein) of coronaviruses is a large trimeric class I fusion protein that protrudes from the viral envelope, giving the virion its crown-like (corona) appearance under electron microscopy. It mediates attachment to host-cell receptors and fusion of the viral and cell membranes, and it is the principal target of neutralising antibodies and of most COVID-19 vaccines.

Structure

Each spike is a homotrimer of S glycoproteins. Each monomer is divided into an S1 subunit, which contains the receptor-binding domain (RBD), and an S2 subunit, which contains the fusion machinery. The protein is heavily glycosylated, which helps shield it from the immune system. In SARS-CoV-2, a furin cleavage site at the S1/S2 boundary is a notable feature.

Function

The RBD binds the host receptor — ACE2 for SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2, DPP4 for MERS-CoV — and can adopt "up" (receptor-accessible) and "down" (closed) conformations. After receptor binding and priming by host proteases such as TMPRSS2 or cathepsins, S2 undergoes a large conformational change that drives membrane fusion and releases the viral genome into the cell.

Medical significance

Because it is exposed on the viral surface and essential for entry, the spike is the antigen used in mRNA, viral-vector, and protein-subunit COVID-19 vaccines. Mutations in the spike, especially in the RBD, define many variants of concern and can affect transmissibility and antibody evasion.
