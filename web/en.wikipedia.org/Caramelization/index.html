Caramelization

Caramelization is the browning of sugar that occurs when sugar is heated, producing the nutty, buttery, and slightly bitter flavours of caramel. It is a form of pyrolysis — a non-enzymatic browning reaction — and is widely used in cooking to produce confectionery, sauces, and the browned surfaces of foods.

Process

Caramelization begins when sugar is heated to roughly 160 °C (320 °F) for sucrose. The sugar first melts, then undergoes dehydration and isomerisation; volatile compounds are lost as steam, and the remaining molecules break down and recombine into hundreds of new compounds. Brown polymeric products — caramélanes, caramélènes, and caramélines — give caramel its colour, while compounds such as diacetyl and furans provide buttery and nutty aromas. If heating continues too far, the sugar chars and becomes bitter.

Comparison with the Maillard reaction

Caramelization is often confused with the Maillard reaction. Both are non-enzymatic browning, but the Maillard reaction involves amino acids (or proteins) reacting with reducing sugars and occurs at lower temperatures, whereas caramelization involves sugars alone and requires higher heat.

Culinary uses

Caramelization gives flavour and colour to caramel sauce and candy, crème brûlée's crisp top, flan, tarte Tatin, roasted onions (which brown slowly from their natural sugars), and the crusts of many baked and grilled foods. Caramel colour (E150) is produced commercially for soft drinks and other products.
