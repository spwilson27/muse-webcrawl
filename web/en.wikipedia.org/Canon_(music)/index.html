Canon (music)

In music, a canon is a contrapuntal (counterpoint-based) compositional technique that employs a melody with one or more imitations of the melody played after a given duration (for example, a quarter rest). The initial melody is called the leader (or dux), while the imitative melody, which is played in a different voice, is called the follower (or comes). The follower must imitate the leader perfectly, either exactly or with some transformations, for the canon to be strict.

Types

A round, such as "Frère Jacques" or "Row, Row, Row Your Boat", is a perpetual canon at the unison in which the parts continue cycling. Canons may imitate at other intervals (canon at the fifth, at the octave), or transform the melody: by inversion (mirror canon), by retrograde — the "crab canon" in which the follower plays the melody backwards, by augmentation or diminution of note values, or by several of these together. A puzzle or riddle canon presents only the leader with an enigmatic instruction, leaving performers to work out the solution.

History

The oldest known surviving canon is the thirteenth-century English rota "Sumer is icumen in". Canon was a central discipline of Renaissance polyphony (in the works of Ockeghem and Josquin) and reached a summit in the Baroque era: J. S. Bach's Goldberg Variations include a canon at every third variation, and his Musical Offering and The Art of Fugue contain elaborate canonic movements. The most famous instrumental canon is Pachelbel's Canon in D, originally scored for three violins and basso continuo. Canon remained in use by Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms, and twentieth-century composers including Webern and the minimalists.
