Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. (March 8, 1841 – March 6, 1935) was an American jurist who served as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1902 to 1932. One of the most influential American judges of all time, he is noted for his concise and quotable opinions, his pragmatic philosophy of law, and his defenses of free speech — earning him the nickname "The Great Dissenter."

Early life and Civil War

Born in Boston to the celebrated writer and physician Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr., he graduated from Harvard College in 1861 and enlisted in the Union Army. He was wounded three times — at Ball's Bluff, Antietam, and Fredericksburg (Chancellorsville) — experiences that shaped his skepticism toward absolute certainties. After the war he studied at Harvard Law School and entered Boston practice.

The Common Law and legal thought

His 1881 book The Common Law, distilled from his Lowell Lectures, opens with the famous line: "The life of the law has not been logic: it has been experience." In his 1897 essay "The Path of the Law," he proposed the "prediction theory" of law and the "bad man" perspective, foreshadowing legal realism. He served on the Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts from 1882, as its chief justice from 1899.

Supreme Court

Appointed by President Theodore Roosevelt in 1902, Holmes served nearly three decades, retiring at 90. In Lochner v. New York (1905) his dissent protested reading laissez-faire economics into the Constitution: "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics." In Schenck v. United States (1919) he coined the "clear and present danger" test; his dissent in Abrams v. United States (1919) became a foundation of modern free-speech doctrine, arguing that "the best test of truth is the power of the thought to get itself accepted in the competition of the market." His dissents also include Gitlow v. New York and his celebrated opinion in United States v. Schwimmer: "freedom for the thought that we hate."
