Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI)
Formed: 26 July 1908 (as Bureau of Investigation)
Headquarters: J. Edgar Hoover Building, Washington, D.C.
Parent agency: U.S. Department of Justice

The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) is the domestic intelligence and security service of the United States and its principal federal law enforcement agency. Operating under the jurisdiction of the U.S. Department of Justice, the FBI is a member of the U.S. Intelligence Community and reports to both the Attorney General and the Director of National Intelligence. Its stated priorities include protecting the United States from terrorist attack, foreign espionage, and cyber-based attacks, and combating violent crime, public corruption, and organised crime. The Bureau employs roughly 35,000 people and maintains 56 field offices in major American cities, as well as legal attaché offices in U.S. embassies abroad.

History

The FBI was founded in 1908 as the Bureau of Investigation, when Attorney General Charles Bonaparte created a force of special agents within the Department of Justice. It took its present name in 1935. Its most influential director, J. Edgar Hoover, led the Bureau from 1924 until his death in 1972, building its fingerprint files and forensic laboratory while also directing controversial surveillance programmes such as COINTELPRO against political groups. In the 1930s the Bureau's "G-Men" pursued famous gangsters including John Dillinger. After the September 11 attacks of 2001, counterterrorism became the FBI's top priority and its intelligence role expanded substantially.

Organisation and work

The FBI is led by a Director appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, serving a ten-year term. Its work is divided between an Intelligence Branch and branches for national security, criminal and cyber investigations, and science and technology. The Bureau runs the national Combined DNA Index System (CODIS), the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), and publishes the Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list, first issued in 1950. The FBI Academy at Quantico, Virginia, trains new agents alongside the Bureau's famous forensic laboratory.

Jurisdiction

Unlike a national police force, the FBI has authority over violations of more than 200 categories of federal crime; general policing in the United States remains with state and local agencies. The Bureau can also investigate matters at the request of state and local authorities where federal jurisdiction is engaged.
