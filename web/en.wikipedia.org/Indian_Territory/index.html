Indian Territory was a territory of the United States set aside for the relocation of Native American peoples, principally under the policy of Indian removal in the 1830s. Originally encompassing most of the Louisiana Purchase lands west of the Mississippi not assigned to states, it was progressively reduced as new territories and states were organised, and by the late nineteenth century it corresponded roughly to the eastern portion of present-day Oklahoma. It ceased to exist on 16 November 1907, when it was joined with Oklahoma Territory to form the state of Oklahoma.

Removal and the Five Civilized Tribes

The Indian Removal Act of 1830 authorised the federal government to negotiate the exchange of tribal lands in the southeastern United States for lands in the West. The Choctaw, Cherokee, Muscogee (Creek), Chickasaw and Seminole — later grouped as the "Five Civilized Tribes" — were removed along routes remembered as the Trail of Tears, on which thousands died. In their new lands the tribes re-established constitutional governments, schools, newspapers and farms, and held enslaved African Americans, a legacy that shaped the territory's later history. The Indian Intercourse Act of 1834 formally described the "Indian country".

Civil War and Reconstruction treaties

During the American Civil War, most of the Five Tribes allied with the Confederacy, and the territory became a battleground. The Reconstruction treaties of 1866 required the tribes to abolish slavery, extend citizenship to their freedmen, and cede their western lands, which were then used to resettle Plains tribes including the Cheyenne, Arapaho, Comanche, Kiowa and Apache. Railroads were authorised to cross the territory, bringing growing non-Indian settlement despite legal prohibitions.

Allotment and dissolution

The Dawes Act (1887) inaugurated the allotment of communal tribal lands into individual parcels; the Curtis Act (1898) extended allotment to the Five Tribes and dismantled their governments' authority. Land runs beginning in 1889 opened the "Unassigned Lands" and, from 1890, the western part was organised as Oklahoma Territory. Pressure for a separate Native state — the proposed State of Sequoyah (1905) — was rejected by Congress, and the Enabling Act of 1906 provided for a single state. Indian Territory was extinguished at Oklahoma statehood in 1907.

The territory's legal legacy endures: in McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), the U.S. Supreme Court held that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation had never been disestablished, with major consequences for criminal jurisdiction in eastern Oklahoma.
