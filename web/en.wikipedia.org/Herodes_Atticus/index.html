Herodes Atticus

Born: AD 101, Marathon, Greece
Died: AD 177
Occupation: Rhetorician, senator, consul (143)
Known for: Odeon of Herodes Atticus

Lucius Vibullius Hipparchus Tiberius Claudius Atticus Herodes (AD 101–177), commonly known as Herodes Atticus, was an Athenian rhetorician and Roman senator. A fabulously wealthy descendant of a prominent Athenian family, he became one of the leading sophists of the Second Sophistic, tutor to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, and consul in 143.

Wealth and public works

Herodes inherited vast estates in Attica and elsewhere, enlarged by his marriage to the wealthy Roman Appia Annia Regilla. He spent lavishly on public benefactions across Greece: he rebuilt the Panathenaic Stadium in marble, roofed a stadium at Delphi, built aqueducts at Canusium and Alexandria Troas, and funded nymphaea at Olympia. His most famous work is the Odeon in Athens (c. 161), a steep stone theatre on the southwest slope of the Acropolis, built in memory of Regilla; it is still used for performances today.

Career and controversies

Herodes taught rhetoric to Marcus Aurelius, who retained affection for him, and was honoured with the consulship in 143. Yet his career was marred by lawsuits: the Athenians prosecuted him before Marcus Aurelius at Sirmium over alleged tyranny and financial abuses, and he was implicated — though acquitted — in the death of Regilla, who died in childbirth after allegedly being kicked by one of his freedmen. In grief he covered his villa with memorials to her and to his deceased foster-sons.

Legacy

Philostratus devoted a long section of the Lives of the Sophists to Herodes, calling him the "king of rhetoric." His villa at Loukou in the Peloponnese and his estates at Marathon and Kephissia yielded important sculptures, many now in the Louvre and the National Archaeological Museum of Athens.
