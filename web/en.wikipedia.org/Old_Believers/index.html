The Old Believers, also called Old Ritualists (Russian: старообрядцы, staroobryadtsy), are Eastern Orthodox Christians who maintain the liturgical and ritual practices of the Russian Orthodox Church as they were before the reforms of Patriarch Nikon of Moscow between 1652 and 1666. Resisting the reforms — which corrected liturgical books and rites toward Greek practice, changed the spelling of Jesus's name, altered the direction of processions, and mandated making the sign of the cross with three fingers instead of two — Old Believers split from the official church after the councils of 1666–67 anathematised the old rites. The resulting schism, known as the Raskol, permanently divided Russian Orthodoxy.

History

The movement's martyr-hero was the Archpriest Avvakum, whose fiery autobiography defended the old practices; he was burned at the stake in 1682. Facing persecution, many Old Believers fled to the Russian north, Siberia, the Urals, and abroad (to Poland-Lithuania, the Danube delta in Romania, and later Alaska, the Americas and Australia). Some communities practised self-immolation rather than submit to what they saw as the reign of Antichrist. Because the priestly wing depended on runaway or converted priests from the official church, while the priestless concluded the priesthood had ceased to exist, the movement fragmented into numerous "concords" (soglasiya), major ones being the Belokrinitskaya hierarchy (established 1846 in Austrian Bukovina, with its centre at Rogozhskoye Cemetery in Moscow), the Pomortsy, Fedoseevtsy, and Chasovennye.

Practices

Old Believers retain pre-Nikonian features: the two-finger sign of the cross (dvoyeperstiye), the eight-pointed cross, prostrations, the lestovka prayer rope, unison Znamenny chant, beards (shaving being traditionally condemned), and the seven-prosphora liturgy. Edinoverie ("same faith") parishes, from 1800, allowed Old Ritualists to worship within the Moscow Patriarchate while keeping the old rites; in 1971 the Patriarchate formally lifted the 17th-century anathemas on the old rites, though the schism itself endures.

Modern communities

Today Old Believers number perhaps one to two million worldwide. Notable communities include Lipovans in Romania's Danube delta, villages in Siberian Buryatia (the Semeiskie, UNESCO-listed for their culture), Russian settlements in Alaska and Oregon, and repatriated communities from China and South America. In 2017 a Russian presidential visit to the Rogozhskoye centre symbolised full civic rehabilitation. The Lykov family, discovered in the Siberian taiga in 1978 after decades of isolation, famously illustrated the priestless Old Believer tradition of flight from the world.
