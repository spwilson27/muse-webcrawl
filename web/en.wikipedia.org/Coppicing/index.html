Coppicing

Coppicing is a traditional method of woodland management which exploits the capacity of many species of trees to put out new shoots from their stump or roots if cut down. In a coppiced wood, which is called a copse, young tree stems are repeatedly cut down close to ground level, resulting in a stump or stool, from which new shoots emerge. After a number of years, the coppiced tree is harvested, and the cycle begins anew.

Practice

Coppicing is normally carried out in coupes (also called cants or panels) on a rotation, so that a wood yields a continuous supply of poles of different ages and sizes. Typical rotation lengths range from a few years for willow or hazel grown for hurdles and thatching spars, to several decades for chestnut or oak grown for fencing and construction. Species that coppice well include hazel, sweet chestnut, hornbeam, ash, willow, oak, and lime; most conifers do not coppice. The related practice of pollarding cuts the tree above grazing height so that livestock cannot eat the regrowth.

History and uses

Coppice management dates back at least to the Neolithic — the Sweet Track in the Somerset Levels, built about 3800 BC, was made from coppiced poles — and coppiced woodland provided firewood, charcoal, tanbark, fencing and building material for centuries. Demand declined with the replacement of wood fuel by coal and fossil fuels in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Ecology

Because coppicing keeps trees in a permanently juvenile state, stools can live far longer than maiden (uncut) trees of the same species. Rotational cutting also creates a mosaic of light and shade that benefits woodland plants, butterflies, and birds such as nightingales and dormice habitat; neglect of coppice leads to darker, more uniform high forest and loss of these species. Coppicing has seen renewed interest for conservation and for biomass energy production.
