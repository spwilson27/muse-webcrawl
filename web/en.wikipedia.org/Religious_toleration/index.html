Religious toleration

Religious toleration or religious tolerance may refer to the tolerance of different religious beliefs and practices in a society, or to the acceptance of individuals' freedom to practice their religion. Religious toleration occurs when a society permits religious dissent and diversity even when one religion, or religious authorities, holds power in that society.

Religious toleration allows adherents of different faiths to coexist within one society.

Historically, toleration most often arose as a practical response to religious conflict. In ancient and medieval societies, rulers sometimes granted limited toleration to minority communities, such as the dhimmi system in Islamic law or the millet system of the Ottoman Empire. In Europe, the devastation of the religious wars of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries led to settlements such as the Peace of Augsburg (1555), the Edict of Nantes (1598), and the Peace of Westphalia (1648), each of which granted varying degrees of toleration.

Enlightenment thought

Philosophers of the Enlightenment provided influential justifications for toleration. John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) argued that the magistrate's authority extends only to civil goods and that genuine belief cannot be compelled by force. Pierre Bayle and Voltaire also defended toleration as a moral and social good. These arguments helped shape modern conceptions of freedom of religion, later enshrined in instruments such as the First Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

Toleration and tolerance

Political theorists distinguish between toleration as a policy of governments and tolerance as an attitude of individuals or groups. Toleration typically implies enduring something one disapproves of, whereas some modern accounts argue that genuine religious freedom requires equal respect rather than mere sufferance.
