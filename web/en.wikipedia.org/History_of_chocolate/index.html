History of chocolate

The history of chocolate extends from ancient Mesoamerica, where cacao was cultivated and consumed as a bitter drink, to the sweetened solid chocolate that spread worldwide after its introduction to Europe in the sixteenth century.

Mesoamerican origins

The cacao tree (Theobroma cacao) is native to the Amazon basin and was cultivated by peoples including the Olmec, Maya, and Aztec. The Maya drank a frothy beverage made from ground cacao seeds, water, and chilli; the Aztecs prized cacao so highly that its beans served as currency. The Aztec drink xocolatl was associated with the god Quetzalcoatl.

Introduction to Europe

Spanish conquistadors encountered cacao in the early 1500s, and chocolate was brought to Spain, where sugar and cinnamon were added to suit European tastes. It spread through European courts as a fashionable drink, remaining an expensive luxury for centuries.

Industrialisation

The nineteenth century transformed chocolate: Coenraad van Houten's cocoa press (1828) separated cocoa butter from solids; J. S. Fry produced the first chocolate bar in 1847; Daniel Peter added condensed milk to create milk chocolate in 1875; and Rodolphe Lindt's conching process (1879) gave chocolate its smooth texture. In the twentieth century chocolate became a mass-market product, while concerns about child labour and sustainability in West African cocoa production prompted ethical trading initiatives.
