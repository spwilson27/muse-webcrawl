Tonka bean

Dipteryx odorata
KingdomPlantae
FamilyFabaceae
GenusDipteryx
SpeciesD. odorata (Aubl.) Forsyth f.
Common namesTonka bean, cumaru, kumaru
Key compoundCoumarin

Tonka beans are the seeds of Dipteryx odorata, a species of flowering tree in the pea family, Fabaceae, native to northern South America and southern Central America. The seed is black and wrinkled and has a smooth, brown interior. It has a strong fragrance similar to vanilla, sweet woodruff and almond, caused by its high content of coumarin, and is used as a flavouring and in perfumery, although its use in food is restricted in some jurisdictions.

Description and distribution

The cumaru tree grows up to 25–30 metres tall in the rainforests of the Orinoco basin and the Guianas, and is also planted in countries such as Nigeria and Trinidad. Its fruit is a drupe containing a single seed, the tonka bean. Fresh beans are soaked in rum or alcohol and then dried and fermented, a curing process that causes coumarin to crystallise on the surface as a white frost.

Uses

Tonka beans have long been used as a vanilla substitute, in perfumery — tonka absolute is a classic base note in oriental and fougère fragrances — and in tobacco flavouring. In French cuisine the bean is grated into desserts, creams and ice creams; it has also been explored by chefs elsewhere despite regulatory restrictions. The timber of the tree, known as Brazilian teak or cumaru, is a dense, durable hardwood used in decking and construction.

Coumarin and regulation

Tonka beans contain from about 1 to 3 percent coumarin, and in high doses coumarin is hepatotoxic; its synthetic analogue dicoumarol is a potent anticoagulant from which warfarin was derived. The United States Food and Drug Administration prohibits the use of coumarin and tonka beans as food additives under regulations dating to 1954, and foods containing them are considered adulterated in the US, though enforcement against chefs using the beans has been rare. In the European Union coumarin is permitted within maximum limits in certain foods. Despite the restrictions, tonka remains a sought-after ingredient and fragrance material.
