Astronomical clock

An astronomical clock is a clock with special mechanisms and dials to display astronomical information, such as the relative positions of the Sun, Moon, zodiacal constellations and sometimes major planets. The term encompasses medieval European tower clocks, Renaissance table clocks, and modern precision instruments. Famous examples include the Prague astronomical clock (1410), the Strasbourg astronomical clock, and the clock of the Torre dei Lamberti in Verona.

Background

Astronomical clocks developed from the geared astrolabe and from medieval attempts to model the cosmos mechanically. Giovanni de' Dondi's Astrarium (completed 1364) was a celebrated early planetarium-clock. These machines embodied the Ptolemaic geocentric universe, with the Earth at the centre and hands or dials tracking the heavens.

Typical features

A typical astronomical clock displays a 24-hour dial, the zodiac ring, indicators for the Sun and Moon, eclipse prediction, the date and feast days of the church calendar, sidereal time, and sometimes planetary positions and automata — animated figures such as apostles, Death or cockerels that perform on the hour, as at Prague.

Notable examples

The Prague Orloj, installed in 1410, is the oldest astronomical clock still in operation; legend holds its maker Hanuš was blinded to prevent a repeat. Strasbourg Cathedral's third clock (1843) includes a perpetual calendar and an orrery. Others survive at Olomouc, Bern, Exeter, York and Messina, whose clock tower (1933) contains the largest astronomical clock in the world.

Modern astronomical clocks

The term is also applied to ultra-precise clocks such as the Shortt–Synchronome and modern atomic references used in observatories, though these display time rather than planetary positions.
