The Sixth Crusade (1228–1229), also known as the Crusade of Frederick II, was the first major crusade launched by an excommunicated ruler and is remarkable for achieving its aims — the recovery of Jerusalem — almost entirely by diplomacy rather than battle.

Background

Emperor Frederick II had vowed to crusade repeatedly since 1215 but delayed, to Pope Gregory IX's fury. Having married Isabella II of Jerusalem in 1225, he claimed the kingship of Jerusalem. When illness forced his fleet to turn back at Brindisi in 1227, Gregory excommunicated him. Frederick sailed anyway in June 1228.

The campaign and the Treaty of Jaffa

After a quarrelsome stop in Cyprus, Frederick reached Acre in September 1228 with a modest force. The Ayyubid sultan of Egypt, al-Kamil, was preoccupied with a struggle against his brother al-Mu'azzam's heirs in Damascus and preferred a settlement. Frederick — fluent in Arabic and familiar with Islamic culture — negotiated for months. By the Treaty of Jaffa (18 February 1229), al-Kamil ceded Jerusalem, Bethlehem, Nazareth and a corridor to the coast for ten years, five months and forty days. Jerusalem's walls were not to be rebuilt, and the Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif) with the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa Mosque remained in Muslim hands, though Christians could visit.

Aftermath

On 18 March 1229 Frederick crowned himself King of Jerusalem in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre — no priest would crown an excommunicate. He left the Holy Land in May 1229, pelted with offal by hostile Acre crowds according to some accounts, and was reconciled with the Pope in 1230. Many contemporaries, including the Patriarch of Jerusalem, denounced the treaty as a sham crusade, yet Christian Jerusalem endured until the Khwarezmian sack of 1244 — longer than it would likely have lasted had it been won and defended by war.
