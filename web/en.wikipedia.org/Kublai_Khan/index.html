Kublai Khan (1215–1294), also known by his temple name Emperor Shizu of Yuan, was the fifth khagan-emperor of the Mongol Empire, reigning from 1260 to 1294, and the founder of the Yuan dynasty of China. A grandson of Genghis Khan, he completed the Mongol conquest of China by destroying the Southern Song in 1279, becoming the first non-Han ruler to conquer all of China.

Rise to power

Kublai was the fourth son of Tolui and Sorghaghtani Beki. Under his brother Möngke he governed north China and led campaigns in the southwest, conquering the Dali Kingdom in Yunnan. On Möngke's death in 1259, Kublai was elected khagan at his base at Shangdu (Xanadu) in 1260, defeating his younger brother Ariq Böke in the Toluid Civil War (1260–1264) — a conflict that permanently fragmented the Mongol Empire into effectively independent khanates.

Rule of China

In 1271 Kublai proclaimed the Yuan ("origin") dynasty in the Chinese style and in 1272 made Khanbaliq (modern Beijing) his capital. The Song capital Hangzhou surrendered in 1276 and the last Song resistance ended at the Battle of Yamen in 1279. Kublai patronised Chinese learning, Buddhism, and the arts, employed the Tibetan lama Phags-pa to devise a universal script, and maintained the relay-post and canal systems; but his administrations were plagued by inflation of the paper currency, costly failed invasions of Japan (1274 and 1281), Vietnam, and Java (1293), and his later years were marked by grief and ill health.

Legacy

Kublai's court at Xanadu and Khanbaliq was described to Europe by Marco Polo, whose account fixed the image of "the Great Khan" in Western imagination; his summer palace inspired Coleridge's poem "Kubla Khan". His reign unified China under one administration for the first time since the Tang and made China the centre of a maritime and overland trading world.
