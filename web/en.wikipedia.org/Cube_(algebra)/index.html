Cube (algebra)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In arithmetic and algebra, the cube of a number n is its third power, that is, the result of multiplying three instances of n together: n³ = n × n × n. It is also the number multiplied by its square: n³ = n × n². This is also the volume formula for a geometric cube with sides of length n, giving rise to the name.

Properties

The cube function preserves the sign of its argument: unlike squares, cubes of negative numbers are negative, so every real number has exactly one real cube root. In the integers, the sequence of cubes begins 0, 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000. Every positive rational number is the sum of three positive rational cubes, and the sum of the first n cubes equals the square of the sum of the first n integers: 1³ + 2³ + ... + n³ = (1 + 2 + ... + n)².

Cube numbers and equations

Perfect cubes play a role in several classic problems. Waring's problem asks how many cubes are needed to represent every natural number (nine suffice, and nine are sometimes necessary). Fermat's Last Theorem states that no cube is the sum of two cubes in positive integers. The inverse operation, extracting a cube root, solves cubic equations together with the methods of Cardano's formula.

In other fields

Cubing appears throughout physics in inverse-cube laws and volume scaling, in engineering (cubic metres, cubic capacity of engines), and in the "cube law" relating windmill power to rotor diameter.
