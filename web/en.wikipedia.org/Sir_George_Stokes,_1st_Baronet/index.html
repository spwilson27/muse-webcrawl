Sir George Stokes, 1st Baronet

George Gabriel Stokes Born: 13 August 1819, Skreen, County Sligo, Ireland
Died: 1 February 1903 (aged 83), Cambridge, England
Known for: Stokes's law; Navier–Stokes equations; Stokes parameters; Stokes phenomenon; fluorescence
Awards: Rumford Medal (1852); Copley Medal (1893)

Sir George Gabriel Stokes, 1st Baronet (13 August 1819 – 1 February 1903), was an Irish physicist and mathematician. Born in County Sligo, Stokes spent all his career at the University of Cambridge, where he was the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics from 1849 until his death in 1903. As a physicist, Stokes made seminal contributions to fluid mechanics, including the Navier–Stokes equations, and to physical optics, with notable works on polarisation and fluorescence.

Early life and education

Stokes was born at Skreen, in County Sligo, the youngest son of a Church of Ireland rector. He was educated at schools in Skreen, Dublin and Bristol before entering Pembroke College, Cambridge, in 1837. He graduated in 1841 as Senior Wrangler and first Smith's Prizeman, distinctions that led to his immediate election to a fellowship of the college.

Career and research

In 1849 Stokes was appointed Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge, a chair he held for 54 years. His early work on the motion of incompressible fluids and on the internal friction of fluids in motion laid foundations of hydrodynamics. In 1851 he derived Stokes's law, giving the frictional force exerted on spherical objects moving through a viscous fluid.

In optics, Stokes studied the wave theory of light, the phenomenon of fluorescence — a term he coined in 1852 after observing the mineral fluorite — and polarisation, introducing the Stokes parameters for describing polarised light. He also worked on geodesy, serving on committees concerned with the figure of the Earth, and was president of the Royal Society from 1885 to 1890.

Personal life and honours

Stokes was created a baronet in 1889. He sat in Parliament as Conservative member for Cambridge University from 1887 to 1891. Deeply religious, he took a leading role in discussions of the relationship between science and faith and was president of the Victoria Institute. He died in Cambridge in 1903 and was buried in Mill Road cemetery.
