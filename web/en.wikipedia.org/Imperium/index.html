Imperium

In ancient Rome, imperium was a form of authority held by a citizen to control a military or governmental entity. It is distinct from auctoritas and potestas, different and generally lower forms of power in the Roman Republic and Empire. One's imperium could be limited to a specific task, or it could extend to broader functions.

Definition and scope

Imperium was the supreme executive power conferred by law on magistrates — consuls, praetors, dictators, and later provincial governors and the emperor. It included the command of armies (imperium militiae) and, within the city in early times, jurisdiction and the power of coercion and punishment. The visible symbol of imperium was the lictors bearing the fasces; axes were included in the fasces outside the pomerium (the sacred boundary of Rome), signifying the power of capital punishment in the field.

Types

Imperium domi was authority exercised within Rome, limited by the citizens' right of appeal (provocatio) and by the tribunes' veto. Imperium militiae was command authority outside the city. A magistrate's imperium could be extended beyond his term by prorogation (proconsular or propraetorian imperium). Imperium maius was "greater imperium" that overrode that of other commanders in the field, held notably by Pompey and later by the emperors.

Under the Empire

Augustus and his successors accumulated imperium over all armies and provinces, making the word a root of the terms "empire" and "emperor" (imperator). In later political philosophy, imperium came to denote sovereign executive power in general.
