Polish resistance movement in World War II

The Polish resistance movement in World War II, led principally by the Home Army (Armia Krajowa), was the largest underground resistance movement in German-occupied Europe and one of the largest resistance movements of the war overall. It operated throughout the occupation from 1939 to 1945 and was part of the Polish Underground State, a clandestine structure loyal to the Polish government-in-exile in London.

Structure and strength

The armed resistance grew out of the Service for Poland's Victory (SZP), founded in September 1939, which became the Union of Armed Struggle (ZWZ) and in February 1942 the Home Army. By the summer of 1944 the Home Army numbered around 350,000–380,000 sworn members, making it the largest resistance army in occupied Europe. Other formations included the peasant Bataliony Chłopskie, the nationalist Narodowe Siły Zbrojne, and the communist Armia Ludowa.

Activities

The resistance conducted sabotage, intelligence work (including reporting on the Holocaust and the V-2 rocket programme), diversionary operations, and assassination of Gestapo collaborators. Its intelligence network provided the Western Allies with much of their information about occupied Europe; Polish agents obtained and delivered a reconstruction of the Enigma machine before the war and passed reports on extermination camps from Witold Pilecki, who voluntarily entered Auschwitz.

Warsaw Uprising

The largest single operation was the Warsaw Uprising of August–October 1944, in which some 40,000–50,000 Home Army soldiers fought the German garrison for 63 days. The uprising, launched as the Red Army approached the Vistula, ended in capitulation on 2 October 1944; Warsaw was subsequently largely destroyed by German demolition units. After the war, Home Army veterans were persecuted by the communist authorities.
