Replicon (genetics)

A replicon is a DNA molecule or RNA molecule, or a region of DNA or RNA, that replicates from a single origin of replication. The concept was proposed in 1963 by François Jacob, Sydney Brenner and Jacques Cuzin in their "replicon model", which postulated that a genetic element (the replicator) acts on a specific site (the initiator) of a DNA molecule to trigger its replication as a unit.

Prokaryotic replicons

For most prokaryotes, the entire chromosome is a single replicon, replicated bidirectionally from one origin (such as oriC in Escherichia coli). Plasmids are autonomous replicons with their own origins and copy-number control systems; two plasmids sharing the same control machinery cannot stably coexist, the basis of plasmid incompatibility groups.

Eukaryotic replicons

Eukaryotic chromosomes contain many replicons — in humans, tens of thousands of origins — each typically 40–100 kilobases in yeast and somewhat larger in mammals. Not all origins fire in every cell cycle; clusters of replicons are activated in a temporal programme during S phase. The term "replicon" fell out of common use with the identification of autonomously replicating sequences (ARS) in yeast and the licensing system that restricts each origin to one firing per cell cycle.
