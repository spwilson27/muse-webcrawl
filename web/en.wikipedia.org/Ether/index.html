Ether may refer to several distinct concepts. In chemistry, ethers are a class of organic compounds containing an oxygen atom connected to two alkyl or aryl groups. In classical philosophy, ether (aether) was the fifth element filling the heavens. In nineteenth-century physics, the luminiferous ether was the hypothetical medium through which light waves propagated.

Chemistry

Ethers have the general formula R–O–R′. The best known is diethyl ether, commonly called simply "ether," a highly volatile, flammable liquid once widely used as a general anaesthetic and still used as a laboratory solvent and starting fluid. Ethers are relatively unreactive, which makes them useful solvents for reactions such as the Grignard reaction. Crown ethers are cyclic ethers that bind metal cations selectively.

Anaesthesia

Diethyl ether was publicly demonstrated as a surgical anaesthetic by William T. G. Morton at Massachusetts General Hospital on 16 October 1846, an event that transformed surgery. Ether's slow induction, flammability, and side effects led to its replacement by safer agents in the twentieth century, though it remained in use in some countries for decades.

Luminiferous ether

Physicists once supposed light required a medium, the luminiferous ether. The Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887 failed to detect Earth's motion through it, and Einstein's special relativity (1905) made the ether unnecessary, reshaping physics.
