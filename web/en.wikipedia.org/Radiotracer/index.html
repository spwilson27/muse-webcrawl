Radiotracer

A radiotracer, or radioactive tracer, is a chemical compound in which one or more atoms have been replaced by a radionuclide, so that by virtue of its radioactive decay it can be used to explore the mechanism of chemical reactions or to follow the distribution of a substance within an organism or system. The technique was pioneered by George de Hevesy in the 1910s–1920s, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1943.

Positron emission tomography (PET) imaging relies on radiotracers such as fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG).

Principle

Because a radioactive isotope behaves chemically almost identically to its stable counterpart, the tracer follows the same pathways as the substance under study, while its emissions (gamma rays, positrons, or beta particles) can be detected externally with great sensitivity. Very small quantities can therefore be tracked without disturbing the system.

Medical applications

In nuclear medicine, radiotracers are used for both diagnosis and therapy. Technetium-99m is the most widely used diagnostic isotope worldwide. PET scans use positron-emitting tracers such as fluorine-18 labelled FDG to image metabolic activity, particularly in oncology and neurology. Iodine-131 is used both to image and to treat thyroid disorders, exploiting the thyroid's natural uptake of iodine.

Industrial and environmental applications

Radiotracers measure flow rates, detect leaks in pipelines, study wear in machinery, and trace the movement of water, sediment, and pollutants in the environment. In biochemistry, tracers such as carbon-14 and tritium have been essential in elucidating metabolic pathways, including the Calvin cycle of photosynthesis.
