Is–ought problem

The problem asks how statements of obligation can be derived from statements of fact.

The is–ought problem, as articulated by the Scottish philosopher David Hume, is the idea that many writers make claims about what ought to be on the basis of statements about what is. Hume argued that there is a significant difference between descriptive statements (about what is) and prescriptive or normative statements (about what ought to be), and that it is not obvious how one can coherently move from the former to the latter.

Hume's formulation

In Book III of A Treatise of Human Nature (1739), Hume noted that moral systems he read began with ordinary statements connected by "is" and "is not", and then imperceptibly shifted to conclusions connected by "ought" and "ought not", without explaining the new relation. He demanded that a reason be given for the inference.

Related ideas and responses

The problem is closely related to G. E. Moore's "naturalistic fallacy" and the fact–value distinction in meta-ethics. Responses include denying that the gap is unbridgeable (as in some forms of ethical naturalism), treating moral claims as non-descriptive (expressivism), or locating normativity in constitutive features of agency. The is–ought gap remains a central challenge for attempts to ground morality in facts.
