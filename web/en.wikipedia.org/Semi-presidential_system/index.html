Semi-presidential system

A semi-presidential republic, or dual executive republic, is a republic in which a president exists alongside a prime minister and a cabinet, with the latter two being responsible to the legislature of the state. A semi-presidential system differs from a parliamentary system in that the head of state is popularly elected, and differs from a presidential system in that the cabinet, although named by the president, is responsible to the legislature, which may force the cabinet to resign through a motion of no confidence.

The term was coined by French political scientist Maurice Duverger in 1970 to describe the French Fifth Republic. Duverger's criteria included a president elected by universal suffrage, endowed with considerable powers, and opposed by a prime minister and ministers who hold executive power and can remain in office only if parliament does not oppose them.

Subtypes

Political scientist Robert Elgie and others distinguish two subtypes: in premier-presidential systems (e.g., France, Poland, Portugal), the prime minister and cabinet are accountable solely to the legislature; in president-parliamentary systems (e.g., Russia, Ukraine historically), the cabinet is dually accountable to both the president and the legislature.

Cohabitation

In France, when the president and the parliamentary majority come from opposing parties, the resulting arrangement is called cohabitation: the president largely retreats to foreign and defence policy while the prime minister directs domestic government. Cohabitation occurred in 1986–1988, 1993–1995, and 1997–2002.

Countries

Countries often classified as semi-presidential include France, Portugal, Romania, Lithuania, Ukraine, Russia, Egypt, and Taiwan, though classification of individual cases is sometimes contested.
