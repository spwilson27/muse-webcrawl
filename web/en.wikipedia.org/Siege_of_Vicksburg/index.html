The Siege of Vicksburg (May 18 – July 4, 1863) was the final major military action in the Vicksburg campaign of the American Civil War. In a series of maneuvers, Union Major General Ulysses S. Grant and his Army of the Tennessee crossed the Mississippi River and drove the Confederate Army of Mississippi, led by Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton, into the defensive lines surrounding the fortress city of Vicksburg. When two major assaults against the Confederate fortifications were repulsed with heavy casualties, Grant decided to besiege the city, which surrendered on the Fourth of July, 1863.

The successful siege gave the Union control of the Mississippi River — "the Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea," as President Abraham Lincoln put it — splitting the Confederacy in two and denying it the use of the river and the resources of Texas, Louisiana, and Arkansas. Together with the near-simultaneous Union victory at Gettysburg, the fall of Vicksburg is widely regarded as a turning point of the war.

Background

Vicksburg, perched on high bluffs above a bend of the Mississippi, was called the "Gibraltar of the Confederacy". After earlier attempts to approach it failed, Grant marched his army down the Louisiana side of the river, ran transports past the Vicksburg batteries with naval support from Admiral David Dixon Porter, crossed below the city, and advanced inland. In a brilliant three-week campaign he won battles at Port Gibson, Raymond, Jackson, Champion Hill, and the Big Black River, driving Pemberton into Vicksburg's defences despite orders from General Joseph E. Johnston to evacuate.

The siege

Grant assaulted the Confederate works on 19 and 22 May; both attacks were repulsed with heavy loss, and he settled into a formal siege. Union engineers dug approach trenches toward the Confederate line, and on 25 June and 1 July exploded mines beneath the 3rd Louisiana Redan, though the resulting assaults failed to break through. Inside the city, soldiers and civilians endured constant bombardment, dwindling rations — reduced to pea bread and mule meat — and hunger; residents dug caves into the loess hillsides for shelter. Pemberton, facing starvation and with no hope of relief from Johnston, opened negotiations on 3 July and surrendered his army of nearly 30,000 men on 4 July.

Aftermath

Grant paroled the captured Confederates rather than shipping them north. The fall of Vicksburg, followed days later by the surrender of Port Hudson, gave the Union unimpeded control of the Mississippi. The victory greatly enhanced Grant's reputation and led to his eventual appointment as general-in-chief of all Union armies in 1864. The city of Vicksburg, by local tradition, did not officially celebrate the Fourth of July for decades afterward.
