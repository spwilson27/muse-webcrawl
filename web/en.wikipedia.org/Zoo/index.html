Zoo

A zoo (short for zoological garden; also called an animal park or menagerie) is a facility in which animals are kept within enclosures for public exhibition. The term "zoological garden" refers to zoology, the study of animals. Zoos range from traditional barred cages to expansive naturalistic habitats and safari parks, and modern institutions typically combine display with conservation breeding, education and scientific research.

The oldest scientific zoo in operation is the Tiergarten Schönbrunn in Vienna, founded in 1752 as an imperial menagerie. The Zoological Society of London, founded in 1826, opened London Zoo in Regent's Park in 1828 for scientific study and to the public in 1847; the word "zoo" itself was popularised in the 1860s by the music-hall song "Walking in the Zoo". Animal collections are far older: menageries of exotic animals were kept by rulers in ancient Egypt, China and Mesoamerica, and the Aztec emperor Moctezuma II maintained extensive aviaries and animal houses in Tenochtitlan.

Modern zoos

By the late 20th century many zoos had shifted from entertainment towards conservation, participating in coordinated breeding programmes for endangered species (such as the Species Survival Plan and European Endangered Species Programme), funding field conservation, and reintroducing captive-bred animals to the wild — successes include the Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the golden lion tamarin. Zoos remain controversial: animal-rights critics argue that confining wild animals is inherently harmful, while supporters cite conservation, education and veterinary research. More than 700 million people visit zoos and aquariums worldwide each year.
