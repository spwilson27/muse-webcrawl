Hydrogeology

Hydrogeology studies the distribution and movement of groundwater in soil and rock.

Hydrogeology (from Greek hydro-, water, and geology) is the branch of geology concerned with the distribution and movement of groundwater in the soil and rocks of the Earth's crust, especially in aquifers. Hydrogeologists apply principles from geology, physics, chemistry, and mathematics to understand and manage groundwater resources.

Key concepts

Groundwater occupies pores and fractures below the water table. It flows from areas of high hydraulic head to low, following Darcy's law, which relates flow rate to hydraulic conductivity and gradient. Pumping tests estimate aquifer properties such as transmissivity and storativity.

Aquifers and wells

An aquifer is a permeable formation that yields usable water to wells and springs. Aquifers may be unconfined or confined between impermeable layers, and artesian wells tap confined aquifers under pressure. Large systems such as the Ogallala Aquifer in North America supply irrigation and drinking water to millions.

Applications

Hydrogeology supports water-supply development, prediction of contaminant migration from landfills and industrial sites, mine and construction dewatering, geothermal energy, and the siting of waste repositories. Over-extraction can cause falling water tables, land subsidence, and saltwater intrusion in coastal aquifers.

History

Scientific hydrogeology began in the 19th century with Henry Darcy's experiments on water flow through sand in Dijon (1856) and Jules Dupuit's theory of well hydraulics. In the 1930s C. V. Theis developed the non-equilibrium well equation, founding modern quantitative groundwater hydraulics, and numerical models became standard tools in the late 20th century.
