Addition reaction

An addition reaction, in organic chemistry, is a chemical reaction in which two or more molecules combine to form a larger molecule, the adduct, with no atoms left over. Addition reactions are limited to chemical compounds that have multiple bonds, such as molecules with carbon–carbon double bonds (alkenes) or with triple bonds (alkynes), and compounds that have rings, which are also considered points of unsaturation.

Types

There are two main types of polar addition reactions: electrophilic addition and nucleophilic addition. Two non-polar addition reactions also exist: free-radical addition and cycloadditions. Addition reactions are also encountered in polymerizations and are called addition polymerization.

Electrophilic addition

In electrophilic addition, an electrophile adds to a multiple bond. A common example is the addition of hydrogen halides to alkenes, which follows Markovnikov's rule: the hydrogen atom attaches to the carbon with more hydrogen substituents, and the halogen attaches to the more substituted carbon.

Hydrogenation

Hydrogenation involves the addition of molecular hydrogen (H2) across a multiple bond, usually in the presence of a metal catalyst such as palladium, platinum, or nickel. It is widely used in the food industry to convert liquid vegetable oils into semi-solid fats.

Addition–elimination

An addition reaction may be followed by an elimination reaction, as in the nucleophilic acyl substitution reactions of carboxylic acid derivatives, where a nucleophile first adds to the carbonyl carbon and a leaving group is then expelled.
