Salmonella enterica (formerly Salmonella choleraesuis) is a rod-headed, flagellate, facultatively anaerobic, Gram-negative bacterium and the type species of the genus Salmonella. Together with Salmonella bongori it accounts for essentially all Salmonella infections of warm-blooded animals, including nearly all human salmonellosis and typhoid fever.

Subspecies and serovars

S. enterica is divided into six subspecies, of which subspecies I (enterica) is associated with warm-blooded animals. Within it, over 2,600 serovars are distinguished by their surface (O) and flagellar (H) antigens under the Kauffmann–White scheme. Clinically important serovars include Typhi, which causes typhoid fever and infects only humans; Paratyphi, causing paratyphoid fever; and the non-typhoidal serovars Typhimurium and Enteritidis, leading causes of foodborne gastroenteritis worldwide, commonly acquired from poultry, eggs, and other animal products.

Pathogenesis

The bacterium invades the intestinal mucosa using a type III secretion system encoded on Salmonella pathogenicity islands, injecting effector proteins that force uptake by epithelial cells and manipulate host-cell signalling. Inside cells it survives within a modified vacuole. Invasive non-typhoidal salmonellosis is a major killer of children and immunocompromised adults in sub-Saharan Africa. Control rests on food hygiene, safe water, sanitation, and vaccination against typhoid (Vi conjugate and live oral Ty21a vaccines). See also Salmonellosis and Typhoid fever.
