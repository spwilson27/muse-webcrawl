Daniel Burnham

Daniel Hudson Burnham (September 4, 1846 – June 1, 1912) was an American architect and urban designer. A proponent of the Beaux-Arts movement, he was one of the most influential figures in the development of the American skyscraper and modern city planning. He is famous for the dictum attributed to him: "Make no little plans; they have no magic to stir men's blood."

Daniel Burnham, c. 1902.

Career

Born in Henderson, New York, and raised in Chicago, Burnham failed the entrance examinations for Harvard and Yale before apprenticing in architecture. In 1873 he formed a partnership with John Wellborn Root, and together they built some of the earliest skyscrapers, including the Montauk Building (1882), the Rookery (1888) and the Monadnock Building (1891), landmarks of the Chicago School. After Root's death in 1891 Burnham continued as D. H. Burnham and Company, designing the Flatiron Building in New York (1902) and Washington's Union Station (1908).

World's Columbian Exposition

Burnham served as director of works for the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893, coordinating an army of architects and designers to create the gleaming neoclassical "White City". The fair's monumental, ordered ensemble helped launch the City Beautiful movement and convinced Americans of the value of comprehensive planning.

City planning

Burnham's planning work includes the McMillan Plan for Washington, D.C. (1902), plans for Cleveland, San Francisco and Manila, and his crowning achievement, the 1909 Plan of Chicago, co-authored with Edward H. Bennett and sponsored by the Commercial Club. The Chicago Plan proposed lakefront parks, a system of diagonal avenues, consolidated rail terminals and a regional highway network; much of it, including the protected lakefront, was gradually implemented and it is often considered the first comprehensive plan for an American metropolis. Burnham died in Heidelberg, Germany, in 1912 while travelling in Europe.
