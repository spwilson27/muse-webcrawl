Superbase

In chemistry, a superbase is an extremely basic compound, one with a very high affinity for protons — a proton affinity greater than that of the hydroxide ion or comparable reference bases. Superbases are used where exceptionally strong deprotonation is required, including the generation of carbanions and other highly reactive intermediates in organic synthesis.

Classes

Superbases are commonly grouped into three families. Organic superbases include amidines and guanidines such as DBU and TBD, and phosphazene bases developed by Schwesinger. Organometallic superbases include alkyllithium reagents and mixtures such as the Schlosser base (n-butyllithium with potassium tert-butoxide). Inorganic superbases include alkali-metal hydrides, amides, and nitrides.

Applications

Because they can remove very weakly acidic protons, superbases enable eliminations, isomerizations, and carbon–carbon bond-forming reactions that ordinary bases cannot drive. Their high reactivity also generally demands handling under inert atmosphere and careful control of moisture.
