Japanese Buddhism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: The Great Buddha of Kamakura (Kamakura Daibutsu)]

Buddhism in Japan has been practiced for well over a millennium. Buddhism was officially transmitted to Japan in the 6th century CE from the Korean kingdom of Baekje, and it profoundly shaped Japanese religion, art, architecture, and culture.

History

Under the patronage of Prince Shōtoku in the Asuka period, Buddhism became intertwined with the state. In the Nara period (710–794), great temples such as Tōdai-ji were built. The Heian period saw the rise of the esoteric Tendai and Shingon schools founded by Saichō and Kūkai. The Kamakura period (1185–1333) produced reform movements emphasizing simple, accessible practice for all: Pure Land (Jōdo) teaching of Hōnen and Shinran, Nichiren Buddhism, and Zen, transmitted from China by Eisai (Rinzai) and Dōgen (Sōtō).

Major schools

The principal traditions in Japan today include Tendai, Shingon, Jōdo-shū and Jōdo Shinshū (Pure Land), Nichiren schools, and the Zen schools Sōtō, Rinzai, and Ōbaku. Buddhism in Japan has long coexisted syncretically with Shinto, and most Japanese funerals are conducted in Buddhist rites. In the Meiji era, the state briefly persecuted Buddhism during the haibutsu kishaku movement, but it recovered as a central part of Japanese religious life.
