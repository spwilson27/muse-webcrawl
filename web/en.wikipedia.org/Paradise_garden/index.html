Paradise garden

The paradise garden is a form of garden of Old Persian origin, specifically the Achaemenid Empire's enclosed, irrigated formal garden. The word "paradise" derives through Greek paradeisos from Old Persian pairidaeza, meaning a walled enclosure — originally the royal hunting parks and gardens of the Persian kings.

The classic form, known in Persian as a charbagh ("four gardens"), is a quadrilateral garden divided by walkways or flowing water into four smaller, symmetrical parts. Water — channels, pools, and fountains — is central, symbolising life and the four rivers of paradise; shade trees, fruit trees, and fragrant flowers complete the design. In Islamic tradition the garden became an earthly image of the paradise promised in the Qur'an, and the form spread from Persia through the Islamic world.

History and examples

The earliest known examples belong to the Achaemenid period, including the garden of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae (c. 546 BC). Famous later examples include the gardens of the Alhambra in Granada, the Shalimar Gardens in Lahore, and the Taj Mahal complex at Agra, where the charbagh frames the tomb. In Mughal India the style reached a peak in tomb gardens such as Humayun's Tomb in Delhi. Persian carpets frequently depict the charbagh layout, preserving the garden in textile form for use through the winter months.

Symbolism

Beyond their beauty, paradise gardens carried cosmological meaning: the four parts could represent the four elements, the four rivers of Eden, or the four Zoroastrian sacred elements of sky, earth, water, and plants. The enclosed garden also offered a literal paradise — cool, green, and ordered — in contrast to the surrounding desert.
