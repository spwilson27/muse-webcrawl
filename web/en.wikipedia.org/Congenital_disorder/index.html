Congenital disorder

A congenital disorder, also known as a birth defect, is a condition present at birth regardless of its cause. Birth defects may result in disabilities that may be physical, intellectual, or developmental. Congenital disorders can arise from genetic abnormalities, the intrauterine environment, errors of morphogenesis, infection, or chromosomal abnormalities. They range from mild anomalies (such as a small birthmark) to severe multisystem conditions. Most congenital disorders are of unknown cause; about 3% of all babies are born with a major congenital anomaly.

Classification

Congenital disorders may be structural (abnormalities of body structure — malformations, disruptions, deformations, and dysplasias) or functional (errors of metabolism, congenital infections, and developmental disorders). They are further classified as single-system or multiple-anomaly syndromes. Malformations arise from intrinsically abnormal development (e.g., neural tube defects, congenital heart defects); disruptions from destruction of previously normal tissue (e.g., amniotic band syndrome); and deformations from mechanical forces (e.g., positional plagiocephaly).

Causes

Genetic

Chromosomal abnormalities include aneuploidies such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18), Patau syndrome (trisomy 13), and Turner syndrome; see chromosome. Single-gene disorders follow Mendelian patterns — autosomal dominant (achondroplasia), autosomal recessive (cystic fibrosis), or X-linked (hemophilia) — while many common anomalies, such as congenital heart disease and cleft lip, are multifactorial.

Environmental

Teratogens are agents that disturb embryonic development. Classic examples include thalidomide (limb-reduction defects), alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorders), isotretinoin, and certain anticonvulsants. Maternal infections such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, Zika virus, and syphilis can cause severe congenital disease. Nutritional deficiencies matter too: folate deficiency causes neural tube defects, which is why folic-acid fortification and supplementation are major preventive successes. Maternal diabetes and obesity also increase risk.

Diagnosis and screening

Many anomalies are detected by prenatal ultrasound (including the mid-pregnancy anomaly scan), maternal serum screening, cell-free DNA screening, and diagnostic tests such as amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling. After birth, newborn screening programs test dried blood spots for dozens of treatable metabolic and endocrine conditions (e.g., phenylketonuria, congenital hypothyroidism), and pulse oximetry screens for critical congenital heart disease.

Prevention and treatment

Preventive measures include rubella vaccination, folic-acid supplementation, avoidance of alcohol and known teratogens, good control of maternal diabetes, and genetic counseling for at-risk families. Treatment ranges from corrective surgery (e.g., for congenital heart defects, cleft lip and palate, spina bifida) to lifelong medical management and early developmental intervention.

Epidemiology

The World Health Organization estimates that congenital disorders affect roughly 6% of babies worldwide and cause about 240,000 deaths in the first month of life each year, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries. Congenital heart defects are the most common major category, affecting roughly 1% of live births.
