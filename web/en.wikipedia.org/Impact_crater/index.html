An impact crater is a depression in the surface of a solid astronomical body formed by the hypervelocity impact of a smaller object. In contrast to volcanic craters, which result from explosion or internal collapse, impact craters typically have raised rims and floors that lie below the surrounding terrain, and are often surrounded by an ejecta blanket. Impact craters are the dominant geological features on many solid bodies of the Solar System, including the Moon, Mercury, Callisto and most small moons and asteroids; on Earth they are rarer because erosion, tectonics and sedimentation erase them.

Formation

Crater formation proceeds in three stages: contact and compression, in which the impactor transfers its kinetic energy as a shock wave that vaporises and melts rock; excavation, in which the shock opens a bowl-shaped transient crater some 10–20 times the impactor's diameter; and modification, in which gravity causes the walls of larger craters to slump, producing terraces and central peaks. Simple craters (below about 2–4 km on Earth) retain their bowl shape; complex craters develop central uplifts and ring structures, and the largest basins have multiple rings.

Identification

On Earth, impact structures are confirmed by shock-metamorphic evidence: shatter cones, planar deformation features in quartz, high-pressure minerals such as coesite and stishovite, and siderophile-element (e.g. iridium) anomalies. About 200 impact structures are confirmed in the Earth Impact Database.

Notable craters

  • Vredefort (South Africa, ~2.02 billion years ago) — the largest confirmed impact structure on Earth.
  • Chicxulub (Yucatán, Mexico, 66 million years ago, ~150–180 km) — the impact linked to the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction that ended the reign of non-avian dinosaurs.
  • Sudbury (Canada, 1.85 billion years) — source of major nickel–copper deposits.
  • Barringer (Meteor) Crater (Arizona, ~50,000 years) — the best-preserved young simple crater on Earth.
  • Tycho and Copernicus — prominent rayed craters on the Moon.

Significance

Impacts shaped the surfaces and early evolution of the planets, may have delivered water and organic material to the early Earth, and remain a natural hazard assessed by planetary-defence surveys of near-Earth objects. Their ejecta layers also serve as precise marker horizons in the geological record.
