Inferior vena cava

The inferior vena cava (IVC) is a large vein that carries deoxygenated blood from the lower and middle body into the right atrium of the heart. It is the largest vein in the human body. It is formed by the joining of the right and left common iliac veins, usually at the level of the fifth lumbar vertebra, and ascends on the right side of the vertebral column, passing through the caval opening of the diaphragm at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra to enter the heart.

Tributaries and structure

The IVC receives the lumbar veins, the right testicular or ovarian vein, the renal veins, the right suprarenal vein, the inferior phrenic veins and the hepatic veins; the left gonadal and left suprarenal veins drain first into the left renal vein. Unlike many veins it contains no valve except the vestigial valve at its cardiac opening (the valve of the inferior vena cava, or Eustachian valve).

Clinical significance

The IVC may be compressed in pregnancy (causing supine hypotensive syndrome) and may be obstructed by tumour or thrombosis. Inferior vena cava filters — small mesh devices placed percutaneously — are used to trap emboli and prevent pulmonary embolism in patients who cannot take anticoagulants. Congenital anomalies, such as a left-sided or duplicated IVC and azygos continuation, are important variations in imaging and surgery.
