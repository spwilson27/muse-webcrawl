Gobi Desert

The dunes of the Khongoryn Els in Mongolia's Gobi Desert.

The Gobi Desert is a large, cold desert and semidesert region in northern China and southern Mongolia. It is the sixth-largest desert in the world and the largest in Asia, covering roughly 1.3 million square kilometres. The Gobi is bounded by the Altai Mountains and the grasslands of Mongolia to the north, the Tibetan Plateau to the southwest, and the North China Plain to the southeast.

Geography and climate

Much of the Gobi is not sandy but bare, gravelly rock — the name comes from a Mongolian word for such rocky desert. It is a cold desert lying in the rain shadow of the Himalayas; winters are bitter, with temperatures plunging below −40 °C, and the region is famed for the discovery of dinosaur fossils in its flaming red sandstone cliffs at Bayanzag, first excavated by Roy Chapman Andrews' expeditions in the 1920s. Famous regions include the Flaming Cliffs, the singing dunes of Khongoryn Els, and the oasis country of Dunhuang along the ancient Silk Road.

Ecology

Despite its harshness, the Gobi supports distinctive wildlife, including the Bactrian camel, snow leopard, Gobi bear (mazaalai), wild ass, and jerboas. Saxaul shrubs and drought-adapted grasses stabilize parts of its surface.

Desertification

The Gobi has been expanding southward through desertification, consuming grassland on its margins in China, driven by overgrazing, deforestation, and climate change. China's "Three-North Shelter Forest" program — the so-called Great Green Wall — plants vast belts of trees intended to hold back the desert and the dust storms that carry Gobi sand as far as Beijing and across the Pacific.
