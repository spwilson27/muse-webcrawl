Lincoln–Douglas debates

The Lincoln–Douglas debates were a series of seven debates in 1858 between Abraham Lincoln, the Republican candidate for the United States Senate from Illinois, and the incumbent Democrat Stephen A. Douglas. The central issue was the expansion of slavery into the federal territories. Although state legislatures then chose senators, the debates drew enormous crowds and national press coverage, and they made Lincoln a national political figure.

Background

The debates followed Lincoln's "House Divided" speech of June 1858 and took place against the backdrop of the Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854), which Douglas authored, and the Supreme Court's Dred Scott decision (1857). Lincoln challenged Douglas to joint appearances in each of Illinois's nine congressional districts; they met in seven: Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy and Alton, between 21 August and 15 October 1858.

Key exchanges

At Freeport, Lincoln pressed Douglas on whether territorial settlers could lawfully exclude slavery despite Dred Scott; Douglas's answer — that a territory could keep slavery out by withholding supportive police legislation — became known as the Freeport Doctrine, preserving his Senate seat but alienating Southern Democrats and helping split the Democratic Party in 1860. Lincoln argued that slavery was a moral wrong that must be placed on a course of "ultimate extinction", while disavowing, in the terms of the day, full political equality claims his opponent attributed to him.

Outcome and legacy

Republicans won the popular vote for the legislature, but apportionment returned Douglas to the Senate. The published texts of the debates, carefully transcribed by newspaper stenographers, were issued as a book in 1860 and propelled Lincoln toward the Republican presidential nomination. The debates remain the archetype of sustained American political argument; the modern Lincoln–Douglas debate format in schools is named for them.
