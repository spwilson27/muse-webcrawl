Cyclone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In meteorology, a cyclone is a large air mass that rotates around a strong centre of low atmospheric pressure. Cyclones are characterised by inward-spiralling winds that rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere. The term covers several distinct kinds of weather system, from vast mid-latitude storms to compact, intense tropical cyclones.

Types

Tropical cyclones form over warm ocean waters and draw energy from the evaporation and condensation of moist air; they are called hurricanes in the Atlantic and eastern Pacific, typhoons in the northwestern Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific. Extratropical cyclones form along weather fronts in the middle latitudes and drive much of the day-to-day weather there. Mesocyclones are small-scale rotating updrafts within severe thunderstorms and can spawn tornadoes; polar lows and subtropical cyclones occupy intermediate niches.

Structure

A mature tropical cyclone consists of rainbands spiralling into an eyewall of intense convection surrounding a calm, clear eye where air subsides. Wind speeds increase toward the eyewall and pressure falls toward the centre; storm surge, torrential rain and tornadoes accompany landfall. Extratropical cyclones are broader and asymmetric, with warm and cold fronts extending from the low centre.

Impacts

Cyclones are among the most destructive natural phenomena: the 1970 Bhola cyclone in East Pakistan killed an estimated 300,000–500,000 people, mostly by storm surge, and remains the deadliest tropical cyclone on record. Modern forecasting, satellites and evacuation planning have greatly reduced death tolls, though economic damage from storms such as Katrina (2005) and Haiyan (2013) continues to rise.
