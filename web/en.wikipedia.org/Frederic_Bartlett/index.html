Frederic Bartlett

Sir Frederic Charles Bartlett
Born: 20 October 1886, Stow-on-the-Wold, England
Died: 30 September 1969, Cambridge, England
Known for: Schema theory, reconstructive memory
Fields: Experimental psychology

Sir Frederic Charles Bartlett (20 October 1886 – 30 September 1969) was a British psychologist and the first professor of experimental psychology at the University of Cambridge. He was a foundational figure in cognitive psychology, best known for his work on memory and the concept of the schema.

Life and career

Bartlett studied at St John's College, Cambridge, and became director of the Cambridge Psychological Laboratory, a post he held for decades. During the Second World War he directed applied research for the Royal Air Force, including work on the design of aircraft controls, and was knighted in 1948 for this work. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1932.

Remembering (1932)

Bartlett's most famous work is Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology (1932). Using serial reproduction experiments — in which participants retold an unfamiliar story, such as the Native American folktale "The War of the Ghosts", or redrew an image from memory — he showed that remembering is not a passive retrieval of fixed traces but an active, reconstructive process. Details were rationalised, omitted, or transformed to fit the rememberer's existing knowledge structures, which he called schemas. His findings anticipated later research on false memories and eyewitness testimony.

Legacy

Bartlett's schema theory became central to cognitive psychology, and his applied work helped establish ergonomics in Britain. The UK Ergonomics Society awards a Bartlett medal in his honour.
