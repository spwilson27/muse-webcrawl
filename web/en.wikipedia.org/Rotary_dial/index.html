Rotary dial

A rotary dial is a component of a telephone or a telephone switchboard that implements pulse dialling, a signalling technology used to initiate a telephone call. It was widely used from the early 20th century until the 1980s, when it was gradually replaced by push-button telephones using dual-tone multi-frequency (DTMF) signalling.

Operation

The dial is a disc with ten finger holes labelled 1 through 9 and 0. To dial a digit, the user inserts a finger into the corresponding hole and rotates the dial clockwise until the finger reaches a fixed finger stop, then releases it. A spring and governor return the dial to its rest position at a regulated speed of about ten pulses per second. During the return, a cam opens and closes electrical contacts, interrupting the loop current to produce a train of pulses equal in number to the digit dialled (ten pulses for 0). At the exchange, stepping switches or registers count the pulses to route the call. A mechanical interlock prevents pulsing while the user is winding the dial.

History

Automatic telephony began with Almon Strowger's 1891 patent for an automatic exchange, motivated by his belief that operators were diverting his undertaker business calls to a competitor. Early dials appeared on subscribers' instruments in the 1900s; the first large-scale installation of dial service by the Bell System took place in Norfolk, Virginia, in 1919. Classic instruments such as the Western Electric 302 (1937) and 500 (1950) made the rotary dial a fixture of homes and offices for decades.

Layout and conventions

On most dials the digits run clockwise with 1 at upper right and 0 at the bottom, near the finger stop; letters were printed alongside digits in North America to enable exchange names such as "MUrray Hill 5". A few countries, including Sweden and New Zealand, used reversed numbering arrangements — New Zealand's dial famously ran in the opposite order, requiring special exchange equipment.

Decline

Push-button dialling, introduced by the Bell System in 1963 as Touch-Tone, was faster and enabled new services. Rotary phones remained common into the 1980s and 1990s, but pulse dialling support has since been withdrawn from many networks, although the rotary dial retains a nostalgic following, and retro-styled phones remain in production.
