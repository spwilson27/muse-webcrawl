Advaita Vedanta

SchoolVedanta, Hindu philosophy
Key figureAdi Shankara (c. 8th century CE)
Core claimAtman (self) is identical with Brahman (ultimate reality)
Key textsUpanishads, Bhagavad Gita, Brahma Sutras and commentaries

Advaita Vedanta (Sanskrit: अद्वैत वेदान्त, "non-dual Vedanta") is a Hindu school of philosophy and religious practice, and one of the classical Indian paths to spiritual realisation. Its central teaching is that the true self (atman) is not different from the highest reality (Brahman): consciousness is not a part of reality but the whole of it, and the perceived multiplicity of the world is the product of ignorance (avidya) and maya (illusion or appearance).

The school's ideas appear in the oldest Upanishads — in declarations such as tat tvam asi ("that thou art") and aham brahmasmi ("I am Brahman") — and were systematised by Gaudapada in the Mandukya Karika and above all by Adi Shankara (c. 700–750 CE), whose commentaries on the Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita and Brahma Sutras became the tradition's foundation. Shankara is also credited with organising monastic orders (mathas) across India.

Doctrine and practice

Advaita distinguishes two standpoints: from the ultimate standpoint (paramarthika), only Brahman is real and the world of separate selves is sublated by knowledge; from the empirical standpoint (vyavaharika), the world, its gods and its moral order function conventionally. Liberation (moksha) is not something produced but recognised: the removal of ignorance through scriptural study, reflection and meditation under a teacher, often summarised in the path of knowledge (jnana yoga). Later Advaitins such as Vachaspati Mishra and the authors of the Vivaraṇa school refined its epistemology in debate with rival Vedanta schools such as Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita and Madhva's Dvaita, which reject strict non-dualism.

In the modern era, Swami Vivekananda popularised a form of Advaita ("neo-Vedanta") in the West and in Indian public life.
