Mariano Guadalupe Vallejo (4 July 1807 – 18 January 1890) was a Californio general, statesman, and prominent landowner. He was born a subject of Spain, performed his military duties as an officer of Mexico, and shaped the transition of Alta California from a Mexican territory to a U.S. state. He helped settle the northern California frontier, founded the town of Sonoma, and was among the most influential Californios of the nineteenth century.

Early life

Vallejo was born in Monterey, the capital of Alta California, into a distinguished military family. As a teenager he served as personal assistant to Governor Luis Antonio Argüello and received a good education unusual for the frontier, learning Latin, French, and English.

Military career

Vallejo rose through the ranks of the presidial army at Monterey and San Francisco. In the 1830s he was appointed military commander and director of colonization on the northern frontier, charged with checking Russian expansion from Fort Ross and managing relations with Native American peoples. He founded the pueblo of Sonoma in 1835 and built a large rancho at Petaluma.

Bear Flag Revolt and American rule

Though Vallejo favored eventual annexation of California by the United States, he was taken prisoner by American settlers during the Bear Flag Revolt of 1846 and held for several weeks. After his release he supported the new order, served as a delegate to the California constitutional convention of 1849, and became one of the first state senators. His vast landholdings were nevertheless steadily eroded by legal challenges and squatters under the U.S. land commission system, and he ended his life in modest circumstances in Sonoma.

Legacy

The city of Vallejo, California, is named after him, as is the Vallejo estate Lachryma Montis in Sonoma, preserved as part of Sonoma State Historic Park. His memoirs and the documents he collected remain important sources for the history of Mexican California.
