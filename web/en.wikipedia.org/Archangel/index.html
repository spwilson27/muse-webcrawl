Archangel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An archangel (from Ancient Greek ἀρχάγγελος archángelos, from ἀρχι- "chief" and ἄγγελος "messenger") is an angel of high rank in the celestial hierarchies of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Archangels appear in the scriptures and traditions of these religions as God's principal messengers and as leaders of the heavenly host. The word "archangel" itself occurs in the Bible only in the singular and only in reference to Michael (Jude 1:9; 1 Thessalonians 4:16), but tradition applies the title more widely.

In Judaism

Post-biblical Jewish literature, particularly the Second Temple texts and later works such as the Book of Enoch and the Talmud, names Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and Uriel as the four chief angels who stand at the four sides of God's throne. Michael is the guardian prince of Israel (Daniel 10–12), Gabriel a messenger of revelation, Raphael a healer, and Uriel an angel of wisdom and repentance. Kabbalah later elaborated further hierarchies and associations.

In Christianity

Catholic and Orthodox traditions venerate as archangels Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael — the three named in canonical or deuterocanonical scripture. Michael is the warrior who defeats Satan (Revelation 12), Gabriel the messenger of the Annunciation, and Raphael the guide and healer of the Book of Tobit. Eastern Orthodox tradition adds Uriel, Selaphiel, Jegudiel, and Barachiel, making seven. In Pseudo-Dionysius's influential celestial hierarchy, archangels form the eighth of nine orders of angels, ranking above ordinary angels but below principalities and the higher choirs; most Protestant traditions recognise Michael alone as an archangel in strict biblical terms. The feast of the archangels (Michaelmas) is kept on 29 September.

In Islam

Islam honours four great angels: Jibril (Gabriel), who conveyed the Qur'an to Muhammad; Mikail (Michael), who provides nourishment and rain; Israfil, who will blow the trumpet at the Day of Resurrection; and Azrael (Malak al-Mawt), the angel of death. Angels in Islam are created from light, do not possess free will to disobey, and serve as God's agents and recorders of human deeds.
