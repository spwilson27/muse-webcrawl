Electric motorsport

This article is about racing with electric vehicles. For electric aircraft racing, see Air racing.


An electric single-seater competing in a city-centre race

Electric motorsport is a category of motor racing that involves the racing of electric powered vehicles for competition, either in all-electric series, or in open-series against vehicles with different power sources. The field grew rapidly in the 2010s as battery technology improved and major manufacturers entered dedicated championships.

History

Electric vehicles competed in speed events as early as the late nineteenth century, when electric cars briefly held land speed records before internal combustion engines took over. Interest revived in the 1990s with events such as the Solar Challenge for solar-powered cars, and with drag racing associations for electric vehicles in North America.

The modern era began with the launch of the FIA Formula E Championship in 2014, a single-seater series held largely on temporary street circuits in major cities. Formula E gained FIA world championship status for the 2020–21 season. Its success encouraged incumbent manufacturers including Porsche, Nissan, Jaguar and McLaren-affiliated teams to compete.

Categories

Major categories of electric motorsport include:

  • Formula E – open-wheel single-seaters racing on street circuits.
  • Extreme E – off-road electric SUV racing in remote locations affected by climate change, launched in 2021.
  • Electric rallycross – including the Projekt E series and electric classes in the World Rallycross Championship.
  • MotoE – an electric motorcycle World Cup run alongside MotoGP from 2019 to 2025.
  • ETCR / eTouring cars – electric touring car racing.
  • Electric drag racing and hillclimb – including record attempts at the Pikes Peak International Hill Climb, where an electric prototype set the outright course record in 2018.

Technology

Electric race cars are characterised by instant torque delivery, regenerative braking and high-voltage battery packs. Series commonly restrict power output and battery capacity by regulation, and some use standardised chassis and battery units to control costs. Energy management — balancing outright pace against battery state of charge — is a central strategic element unique to the discipline.
