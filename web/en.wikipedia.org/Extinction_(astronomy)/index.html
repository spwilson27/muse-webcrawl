Extinction (astronomy)

In astronomy, extinction is the absorption and scattering of electromagnetic radiation by dust and gas between an emitting astronomical object and the observer. Interstellar extinction, first demonstrated conclusively by Robert Trumpler in 1930, plays a crucial role in observational astronomy: it dims objects and makes them appear redder, an effect that must be corrected before their true luminosities and distances can be determined.

Characteristics

Extinction is wavelength-dependent: blue light is scattered and absorbed more strongly than red light, so extinguished objects suffer interstellar reddening. The total extinction in the visual band, AV, is commonly expressed relative to the colour excess E(B−V) through the ratio RV = AV/E(B−V), which averages about 3.1 in the diffuse interstellar medium of the Milky Way but varies between sight lines. The measured run of extinction with wavelength, the extinction curve, shows a broad absorption bump near 2175 Å attributed to carbonaceous grains, and is nearly flat — "grey" — in the near-infrared.

Atmospheric extinction

The term also applies to Earth's atmosphere: starlight is attenuated as it passes through the air, increasingly toward the horizon, so astronomers measure extinction coefficients nightly and observe targets, where possible, near the meridian at small airmass. Atmospheric and interstellar extinction must both be removed from photometric measurements, and extinction severely limits optical observations toward the dusty galactic centre, which is studied mainly at infrared and radio wavelengths.
