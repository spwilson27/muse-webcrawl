The jalapeño is a medium-sized chili pepper pod type cultivar of the species Capsicum annuum. A mature jalapeño generally measures 5–10 cm (2–4 in) long and is commonly picked and consumed while still green, though it ripens to red, orange, or yellow. It is named after Xalapa (Jalapa), the capital of the Mexican state of Veracruz, where it was traditionally cultivated.

Heat and flavor

Jalapeños rate roughly 4,000 to 8,500 Scoville heat units—hotter than a bell pepper but milder than a habanero—with heat concentrated in the seeds and inner membranes by the compound capsaicin. When ripe jalapeños are smoke-dried, they are known as chipotles.

Culinary use

The pepper is a staple of Mexican and Tex-Mex cuisine: it is eaten raw in salsas and guacamole, pickled (jalapeños en escabeche), sliced over nachos, and stuffed—for example in jalapeño poppers, where it is filled with cheese, breaded, and fried. The United States is a major market, and large crops are also grown in New Mexico, Texas, and California.
