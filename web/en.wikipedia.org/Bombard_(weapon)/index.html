Bombard (weapon)

A bombard is a type of muzzle-loading cannon, characterised by a large calibre, that was used during sieges in the late medieval and early modern periods to hurl stone balls at fortifications. Bombards were among the largest early gunpowder artillery pieces; their massive stone projectiles could weigh hundreds of kilograms and were intended to smash through the curtain walls of castles and city defences that had resisted traditional siege engines.

Bombards fired large stone balls against castle and city walls.

Design

Early bombards were built from wrought-iron bars welded together and bound by iron rings, or cast in bronze. Bores could exceed 50 cm, and the largest pieces—such as the Austrian Pumhart von Steyr (about 80 cm calibre) and the great Ottoman bombards cast by Orban (Urban) for Mehmed II in 1453—were among the biggest guns ever built. Bombards were transported in pieces, mounted in fixed wooden frames without recoil mechanisms or trunnions, and typically fired only a few shots per day, being slow to load and cool.

Famous bombards

Mons Meg, built in 1449 and presented to James II of Scotland, has a calibre of 20 inches (510 mm) and fired stones of about 175 kg. The Dardanelles Gun, cast in 1464 and modelled on the bombards of the siege of Constantinople, remained in active Ottoman service for centuries and famously fired on a British fleet in 1807. The Basilica, Orban's giant cannon for the 1453 siege of Constantinople, reportedly required dozens of oxen and hundreds of men to move.

Decline

By the later 16th century, bombards were supplanted by smaller, more mobile cast-iron cannon firing iron shot, which delivered more energy more efficiently, and by mortars for high-angle siege fire. The arrival of the low, thick, angled bastion (the trace italienne) further changed siege warfare. Surviving bombards are now museum pieces, including examples at the Royal Armouries and Edinburgh Castle.
