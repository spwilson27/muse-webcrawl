Yosemite National Park is a national park in California's Sierra Nevada, renowned for its granite cliffs, waterfalls, giant sequoia groves and glacially carved Yosemite Valley. It covers over 3,000 km², of which about 95% is designated wilderness, and is administered by the National Park Service.

Geology and landmarks

Ice-age glaciers carved the U-shaped Yosemite Valley from Sierra granite, leaving landmarks including El Capitan, a 900-metre vertical wall famed among climbers; Half Dome; and waterfalls such as Yosemite Falls (739 m total drop), Bridalveil Fall and, above Hetch Hetchy, others. The Mariposa Grove shelters some 500 mature giant sequoias, including the Grizzly Giant.

History

The valley was home to the Ahwahnechee people; their displacement came with the 1851 Mariposa Battalion during the Gold Rush era. Galen Clark and others persuaded Abraham Lincoln to sign the 1864 Yosemite Grant protecting the valley and Mariposa Grove — a precedent for the national park idea. John Muir's advocacy led to the surrounding national park in 1890; the valley was incorporated into it in 1906. Muir lost the battle against damming Hetch Hetchy valley in 1913.

Ecology and recreation

The park spans elevations from 648 to 3,997 metres and five vegetation zones, supporting black bears, mule deer and peregrine falcons. Hiking, rock climbing (including free ascents of El Capitan) and winter sports at Badger Pass draw millions of visitors yearly.
