Charcuterie

Charcuterie (from French chair, "flesh", and cuit, "cooked") is a branch of cooking devoted to prepared meat products, such as bacon, ham, sausage, terrines, galantines, ballotines, pâtés, and confit, primarily from pork. Charcuterie is part of the garde manger chef's repertoire. Originally intended as a way to preserve meat before the advent of refrigeration, the products are prepared today for the flavors derived from the preservation processes.

In France, a charcutier is a tradesperson specializing in these preparations; the trade is regulated and historically was organized into guilds. The Romans are credited with early laws regulating the preparation of pork products, and charcuterie traditions developed across Europe, with regional specialties such as Italian salumi, Spanish embutidos, and German Wurstwaren.

Products and techniques

Charcuterie relies on techniques including curing with salt, smoking, fermentation, and cooking in fat. Common products include:

Forcemeats — ground meat emulsified with fat, used in terrines and pâtés. Salami — fermented and air-dried sausage. Confit — meat salted and cooked in its own fat, notably duck confit. Rillettes — meat cooked slowly in fat until tender enough to be shredded into a spreadable paste. Galantines — deboned, stuffed poultry shaped and poached, typically served cold. Ham — cured leg of pork, ranging from wet-cured cooked hams to dry-cured prosciutto and jamón.

Modern usage

In contemporary North American usage, "charcuterie board" has come to describe an arrangement of cured meats, cheeses, breads, fruits, nuts, and condiments served as an appetizer or light meal — an extension of the traditional meaning that some French practitioners regard as imprecise, since cheese is not charcuterie in the strict sense.
