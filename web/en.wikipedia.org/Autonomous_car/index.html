Autonomous car

ClassificationSelf-driving vehicle
IndustryAutomotive, artificial intelligence
ApplicationPersonal transport, ride-hailing, freight
ComponentsLidar, radar, cameras, GPS, computer vision, machine learning

An autonomous car, also known as a self-driving car or driverless car, is a vehicle that is capable of sensing its environment and moving safely with little or no human input. It combines a variety of sensors to perceive its surroundings, such as optical and thermographic cameras, radar, lidar, sonar, GPS, odometry and inertial measurement units.

Advanced control systems interpret sensory information to identify obstacles, relevant signage, and navigation paths. The development of these technologies has been driven by major automotive manufacturers, technology companies, and research institutions, with the goal of reducing traffic collisions, improving traffic flow, and increasing mobility for people unable to drive.

History

Experiments with automated driving have been conducted since at least the 1920s, when radio-controlled cars were demonstrated in New York City. Promising trials took place in the 1950s, and work advanced considerably in the 1980s with projects such as Carnegie Mellon University's Navlab and the ALV projects funded by DARPA, and Mercedes-Benz's and Bundeswehr University Munich's Eureka Prometheus Project.

In the twenty-first century, the DARPA Grand Challenges of 2004 and 2005 spurred rapid progress. Google began its self-driving car project in 2009, later spun off as Waymo, and many other companies including Tesla, Cruise, Aurora, and major automakers launched their own programs.

Classification

The Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) defines six levels of driving automation, from Level 0 (no automation) to Level 5 (full automation). Under Level 2 systems such as partial automation, the human driver must remain engaged and monitor the environment. Level 4 systems can operate without human intervention within defined conditions, known as an operational design domain.

Technology

Autonomous vehicles rely on sensors including lidar, which builds a three-dimensional map of the environment using laser pulses; radar for detecting objects in poor weather; and cameras for reading signs and detecting lane markings. Sensor fusion algorithms combine these data streams, and machine learning models classify objects and predict the behavior of other road users. High-definition maps and satellite navigation support localization.

Benefits and challenges

Potential benefits include reduced accidents caused by human error, lower emissions through optimized driving, and increased productivity. Challenges include technical reliability in edge cases, liability and regulation, cybersecurity, public acceptance, and the impact on employment in driving occupations.
