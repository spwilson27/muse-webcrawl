Unclassified language

An unclassified language is a language whose genetic affiliation with other languages has not been established. Unclassified languages may be living, recently extinct, or known only from records of antiquity. A language may remain unclassified because the surviving evidence is too scant to apply the comparative method, because it has not yet been studied adequately, or because heavy borrowing and language contact obscure its inheritance — not necessarily because it lacks relatives altogether.

Unclassified versus isolate

The term differs from language isolate. An isolate, such as Basque, Korean or Burushaski, is a language that has been studied well enough that scholars can assert it has no known living relatives. An unclassified language, by contrast, may in fact have relatives — the evidence simply does not (yet) allow the relationship to be demonstrated or ruled out. With new documentation, an unclassified language may later be assigned to a family, recognised as an isolate, or, in some cases, suspected of being a creole, a mixed language or even a hoax.

Examples

Several languages spoken in Africa have resisted classification, including the Laal language of Chad (spoken by only a few hundred people), Shabo of Ethiopia, and Bangime of Mali, which is surrounded by Dogon languages but appears unrelated to them. In South America, languages such as the extinct Pankararú and Gorgotoqui remained unclassified due to sparse wordlists. In the ancient world, languages known from fragmentary records — Kassite and Gutian of Mesopotamia, or the undeciphered Linear A script of Minoan Crete — are generally treated as unclassified rather than isolates, since the attestation is too limited for proof either way.

Causes

Factors that leave languages unclassified include: very small or extinct speaker communities; data limited to short colonial-era wordlists; intense multilingual contact, as in the Amazon and New Guinea, which blurs inherited vocabulary; and deliberate secrecy or ritual registers. Classification projects such as Glottolog mark such languages as "unclassifiable" pending better data.
