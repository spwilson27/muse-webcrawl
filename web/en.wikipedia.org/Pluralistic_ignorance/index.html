Pluralistic ignorance

Pluralistic ignorance is a phenomenon in social psychology where members of a group privately reject a norm or belief but incorrectly assume that most other members accept it, and therefore go along with it publicly. The term was coined in the 1920s by Floyd Allport and popularised by Daniel Katz and Richard Schanck's 1931 study of attitudes toward racial integration and alcohol use.

Examples

Classic examples include the bystander effect, in which each witness to an emergency assumes others will intervene; students who privately dislike heavy campus drinking but assume peers enjoy it; and subjects in studies of attitudes toward segregation who misjudged the views of others. Pluralistic ignorance is related to the Abilene paradox and to Timur Kuran's concept of preference falsification.

Consequences

Pluralistic ignorance can sustain unpopular norms, delay social change, and discourage individuals from speaking out or helping in emergencies. Making private opinions public — for example through surveys or discussion — can correct the misperception and dissolve the effect.
