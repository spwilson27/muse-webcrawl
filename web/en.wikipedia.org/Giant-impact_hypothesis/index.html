The giant-impact hypothesis, sometimes called the Theia Impact, is the prevailing scientific hypothesis for the formation of the Moon. It proposes that the Moon formed from the ejecta of a collision between the early Earth and a Mars-sized planet, named Theia, approximately 4.5 billion years ago — during the Hadean eon, some 20 to 100 million years after the Solar System coalesced.

Evidence

The hypothesis accounts for several features of the Earth–Moon system: the Moon's small iron core and low density; the Earth–Moon system's high angular momentum; the depletion of volatile elements in lunar samples; and the near-identical isotopic signatures (especially oxygen isotopes) of Earth and Moon rocks returned by the Apollo missions, suggesting the Moon formed largely from material well mixed with Earth's mantle. Lunar samples also show the Moon was once largely molten, consistent with the energy of a giant impact.

Theia

Theia is hypothesized to have been roughly the size of Mars, possibly forming at a Lagrange point near Earth before gravitational perturbations caused it to collide at a relatively low velocity and oblique angle. In the canonical model, Theia's core merged with Earth's, while silicate debris formed a circumterrestrial disk that accreted into the Moon, initially much closer to Earth than today.

Variants and open questions

Variants of the model include multiple smaller impacts, a faster-spinning early Earth struck by a smaller impactor, and the synestia model, in which the collision produced a vaporized, torus-shaped structure from which both Earth and Moon condensed. Precise isotopic similarities between Earth and Moon remain a constraint that models must satisfy.
