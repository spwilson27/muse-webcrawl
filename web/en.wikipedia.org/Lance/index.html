Lance

A lance is a long thrusting spear designed to be used by a mounted warrior or cavalryman. Longer and stouter than infantry spears and javelins, the war lance was the principal weapon of heavy cavalry from antiquity through the Middle Ages, and the term survives in ceremonial and sporting contexts.

History

Mounted spearmen existed from the earliest cavalry: Assyrian, Persian, and Macedonian horsemen used long spears such as the xyston, and Sarmatian and Roman contarii wielded the two-handed contus. The medieval war lance reached its classic form between the 11th and 14th centuries, when the adoption of the stirrup, high-cantled saddle, and couched grip allowed a knight to put the full momentum of a charging horse behind the point. Lances of this era were typically 3–4.5 m of ash or similar wood, tipped with steel, and often fitted with a vamplate to protect the hand.

Decline and later use

As plate armour, pike formations, and firearms developed, the lance faded from European battlefields by the 17th century, though lighter cavalry lances saw a revival in the Napoleonic era and remained in service — among Polish, German, Russian, and British lancers — into the First World War. Lances persist in ceremonial units such as the Indian Army's President's Bodyguard and in the sport of tent pegging. In the joust, a specialised sporting form, blunted and often hollow lances were designed to shatter safely on impact.

Terminology

The word derives from Latin lancea. A group of medieval knights under one banner formed a tactical unit called a "lance," and the rank "lance corporal" preserves the memory of lance-armed soldiers. Related weapons include the pike, halberd, and kontos.
