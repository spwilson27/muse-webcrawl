The rare-earth elements (REEs), also called the rare-earth metals or rare earths, are a set of 17 nearly indistinguishable, lustrous, silvery-white soft heavy metals in the periodic table: scandium and yttrium together with the 15 lanthanides (lanthanum through lutetium). Despite their name, most are not especially rare in the Earth's crust — cerium is about as abundant as copper — but they are seldom found in concentrated, economically workable deposits, and separating them from one another is chemically difficult because of their similar properties.

Properties and classification

The lanthanides fill the 4f electron shell, which shields poorly, causing the steady "lanthanide contraction" of atomic radius across the series; their chemistry is dominated by the +3 oxidation state. By convention the series divides into light rare earths (La–Gd) and heavy rare earths (Tb–Lu, plus yttrium, which behaves like a heavy lanthanide). Their partially filled f-orbitals give several REEs exceptional magnetic and optical properties: neodymium-iron-boron magnets are the strongest permanent magnets known, and europium and terbium phosphors produce the red and green of displays.

Occurrence and production

The principal ores are bastnäsite, monazite, and xenotime, plus ion-adsorption clays in southern China, a key source of heavy rare earths. The largest mine is Bayan Obo in Inner Mongolia; other major deposits include Mountain Pass in California and Mount Weld in Australia. China has dominated mining since the 1990s and performs roughly 90% of global separation and refining, a concentration that has made rare earths a focus of trade and national-security policy: Beijing's 2010 export restrictions spiked prices, and in 2025 China imposed export controls on several REEs and magnets amid trade tensions, prompting US, EU, Australian, and Japanese investment in alternative supply chains.

Applications

Rare earths are indispensable to modern technology: neodymium and dysprosium in magnets for electric-vehicle motors, wind turbines, and headphones; lanthanum in nickel-metal-hydride batteries and camera lenses; cerium in catalytic converters and glass polishing; europium and yttrium in phosphors and LEDs; erbium in fibre-optic amplifiers; and gadolinium in MRI contrast agents. Demand grows with the energy transition, while recycling rates remain below a few percent.

Environmental issues

REE ores often contain thorium and uranium, making mining and refining radiochemically hazardous; tailings pollution around Baotou, China, and the Lynas plant controversy in Malaysia exemplify the environmental costs that have pushed production toward jurisdictions with weaker regulation.
