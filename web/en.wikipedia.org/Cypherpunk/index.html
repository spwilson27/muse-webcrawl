Cypherpunk

"Cypherpunks write code" — the movement's motto emphasised building privacy technology rather than merely advocating it.

Cypherpunks are activists advocating widespread use of strong cryptography and privacy-enhancing technologies as a route to social and political change. The movement coalesced in the late 1980s and early 1990s around an electronic mailing list founded in 1992 by Eric Hughes, Timothy C. May and John Gilmore. The name, coined by Jude Milhon, blends "cipher" with "cyberpunk".

Ideas

The cypherpunk worldview holds that privacy in an open society requires cryptography: in Hughes's 1993 "A Cypherpunk's Manifesto", privacy is "the power to selectively reveal oneself to the world", and anonymous transaction systems are needed to protect it. May's "Crypto Anarchist Manifesto" (1988) predicted that public-key cryptography and anonymous networks would fundamentally alter the nature of corporations and government interference in economic transactions. Rather than lobbying, cypherpunks emphasised building tools: "Cypherpunks write code."

Projects and legacy

Members of the list created or promoted many foundational privacy technologies: Phil Zimmermann's PGP email encryption (1991); anonymous remailers, beginning with Johan Helsingius's anon.penet.fi; David Chaum's DigiCash and blind-signature e-cash; Adam Back's Hashcash proof-of-work (1997); Wei Dai's b-money proposal; and Nick Szabo's "bit gold". Hal Finney, an early list member and PGP contributor, became the first recipient of a Bitcoin transaction in 2009 — Bitcoin's pseudonymous creator, Satoshi Nakamoto, drew explicitly on this literature. Julian Assange identified as a cypherpunk, and WikiLeaks applied its ideas to publishing. The movement's battles against 1990s U.S. export controls on cryptography — including the Clipper chip controversy and the Bernstein v. United States case establishing code as speech — shaped modern internet privacy law and practice.
