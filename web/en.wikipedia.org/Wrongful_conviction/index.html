Wrongful conviction

A wrongful conviction is a miscarriage of justice occurring when an innocent person is convicted of a crime they did not commit. Wrongful convictions can result from eyewitness misidentification, false confessions, flawed forensic science, official misconduct, perjured testimony, or ineffective assistance of counsel. Those exonerated may have spent years or decades in prison, in some cases on death row.

Related concepts
Miscarriage of justice, exoneration
Key causes
Eyewitness error, false confessions, forensic error, misconduct
Notable organisations
Innocence Project, National Registry of Exonerations

Causes

Research on exonerations, particularly those secured through post-conviction DNA testing, has identified recurring contributing factors. Eyewitness misidentification is the single largest contributor, implicated in roughly two-thirds of DNA exonerations in the United States. False confessions, often obtained through lengthy or deceptive interrogation, feature in a significant minority of cases, as do misapplied forensic techniques such as bite-mark comparison and microscopic hair analysis.

Exoneration and compensation

Since 1989, thousands of exonerations have been recorded in the United States by the National Registry of Exonerations, representing more than 25,000 years lost in prison. Many jurisdictions provide statutory compensation for the wrongfully convicted, though amounts and eligibility vary widely, and exonerees often face difficulties with housing, employment, and health after release.

Prevention

Reforms advocateded by innocence organisations include recording interrogations, double-blind lineup administration, improved forensic standards, conviction integrity units within prosecutors' offices, and preservation of biological evidence.
