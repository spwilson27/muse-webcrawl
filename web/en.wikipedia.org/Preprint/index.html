Preprint

In academic publishing, a preprint is a version of a scholarly or scientific paper that precedes formal peer review and publication in a peer-reviewed scholarly journal. The preprint may be available, often as a non-typeset version, before, or after, a paper is published in a journal. Preprints are typically shared on dedicated servers such as arXiv (founded 1991 for physics, mathematics and computer science), bioRxiv and medRxiv for the life and health sciences, SSRN for the social sciences, and ChemRxiv for chemistry.

Role in scholarly communication

Preprints speed up the dissemination of research from months or years to days, establish priority of discovery, and allow authors to receive feedback before formal submission. They are generally assigned a digital object identifier (DOI), are citable, and are indexed by scholarly search engines. Funders and institutions increasingly recognise preprints in grant and hiring evaluations.

Advantages and criticisms

Supporters emphasise speed, openness and wider access, especially for readers without journal subscriptions. Critics note that preprints have not been peer reviewed and may contain errors; during the COVID-19 pandemic, rapid sharing through medRxiv accelerated research but also led to media coverage of preliminary findings — some later withdrawn — contributing to public confusion. Most servers perform basic screening for plagiarism, offensive content and non-scientific material but do not evaluate methodological quality.

History

Informal circulation of draft manuscripts by mail long predates the internet. arXiv, created by Paul Ginsparg in 1991, transformed the practice in physics and mathematics, where posting a preprint before journal publication became standard. Adoption spread more slowly in chemistry and biomedicine, accelerating in the 2010s with new servers and funder encouragement.
