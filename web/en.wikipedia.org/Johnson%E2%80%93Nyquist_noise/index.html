Johnson–Nyquist noise (also called thermal noise or Johnson noise) is the electronic noise generated by the thermal agitation of charge carriers — usually electrons — inside an electrical conductor. It occurs in every resistive element regardless of applied voltage and sets a fundamental limit on the sensitivity of amplifiers and detectors.

History

The phenomenon was first measured by John B. Johnson of Bell Labs in 1926 and explained theoretically by his colleague Harry Nyquist in 1928 using thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, a classic application of the fluctuation–dissipation theorem.

Characteristics

The noise has a nearly flat ("white") power spectral density: the mean-square noise voltage across a resistance R over bandwidth Δf at absolute temperature T is proportional to 4kBTRΔf, where kB is Boltzmann's constant. The available noise power, kBTΔf, is independent of resistance. At high frequencies or very low temperatures, quantum corrections described by the Planck factor become significant.

Applications

Johnson noise limits radio receivers, low-noise amplifiers and imaging sensors. It is also exploited deliberately: noise thermometry measures absolute temperature from the noise of a resistor, and Johnson noise is used in some random number generators and secure key-exchange schemes.
