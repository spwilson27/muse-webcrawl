Standard deviation

A normal distribution with bands of one standard deviation.

In statistics, the standard deviation is a measure of the amount of variation of a set of values. A low standard deviation indicates that the values tend to be close to the mean of the set, while a high standard deviation indicates that the values are spread out over a wider range. It is denoted by the Greek letter sigma (σ) for a population and s for a sample.

Definition

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, which is the average of the squared differences from the mean. For a population with mean μ, it is defined as σ = √( Σ(xi − μ)² / N ). For a sample, Bessel's correction divides by n − 1 instead of n to give an unbiased estimate of the population variance.

Properties

For a normal distribution, about 68% of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three—the empirical rule. The standard deviation has the same units as the data, unlike the variance. Chebyshev's inequality guarantees that for any distribution, at least 1 − 1/k² of values lie within k standard deviations of the mean.

Applications

Standard deviation is used throughout science, industry and finance: as a measure of experimental uncertainty, in quality control, in polling margins of error, and as a measure of investment volatility. Karl Pearson coined the term in 1894, building on earlier work by Francis Galton and others.
