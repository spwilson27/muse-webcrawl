Civil union

A civil union is a legally recognised partnership distinct from marriage.

A civil union is a legally recognised union of two people that provides rights similar to those of marriage but is distinct from marriage itself. Civil unions have been established in many jurisdictions, often initially as a compromise offering legal recognition to same-sex couples where same-sex marriage was not permitted.

History and variants

Denmark was the first country to establish registered partnerships for same-sex couples (1989), soon followed by Norway, Sweden, Iceland and the Netherlands. Vermont introduced civil unions in the United States in 2000 in response to the state supreme court's Baker v. Vermont decision; several other US states followed before nationwide same-sex marriage was established by Obergefell v. Hodges (2015). New Zealand introduced civil unions in 2005; the United Kingdom introduced civil partnerships in 2004, later extending them to opposite-sex couples. France's PACS (1999) is a closely related model.

Characteristics

Civil unions typically confer rights in inheritance, taxation, medical decision-making and shared property, though often with differences from marriage — historically including exclusion from federal-level recognition and from some pension and immigration rights. After the spread of same-sex marriage, some jurisdictions converted civil unions automatically into marriages, while others retain them as an alternative available to any couple.
