Quark model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The quark model is a classification scheme for hadrons — the subatomic particles composed of quarks. It was proposed independently by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig in 1964, building on the "Eightfold Way" pattern that ordered the proliferating particles discovered in the 1950s into multiplets of approximate SU(3) flavour symmetry.

Structure

In the original model there were three quark flavours — up, down, and strange — and every hadron is a composite: baryons such as the proton (uud) and neutron (udd) contain three quarks, while mesons such as the pion contain a quark and an antiquark. The model explained the masses and quantum numbers of the known hadrons and predicted the existence and mass of the Ω⁻ baryon, whose discovery in 1964 was a striking confirmation. The scheme later grew to six flavours with the discoveries of charm (1974), bottom (1977), and top (1995) quarks.

Colour and QCD

A difficulty — that baryons like the Δ⁺⁺ seemed to violate the Pauli exclusion principle — was resolved by introducing a new quantum number, colour charge, which became the basis of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the accepted theory of the strong interaction. The quark model remains the standard bookkeeping framework within the Standard Model, even though modern theory treats hadrons through QCD and also recognises exotic states such as tetraquarks and pentaquarks.
