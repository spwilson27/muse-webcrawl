Social Security Act

Enacted14 August 1935
Signed byPresident Franklin D. Roosevelt
Codified42 U.S.C. ch. 7
Administered bySocial Security Administration

The Social Security Act of 1935 is a landmark United States federal statute, enacted as part of President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal, that created the Social Security programme — the federal old-age pension system — as well as unemployment insurance and several welfare programmes. Signed into law on 14 August 1935, it established the framework of the American social safety net and remains, heavily amended, in force today.

Provisions

The Act created a contributory old-age pension financed by payroll taxes on employers and employees (collected from 1937; monthly benefits began in 1940). It set up a federal-state system of unemployment compensation, and provided federal aid to the states for old-age assistance, aid to dependent children, maternal and child health, aid to the blind, and public health services. The Social Security Board (later the Social Security Administration) was created to administer the programme, and Social Security numbers were introduced to track workers' earnings. Initially many categories — notably agricultural and domestic workers, exclusions that disproportionately affected African Americans — were left out of coverage.

History and amendments

The Act responded to the mass insecurity of the Great Depression and to popular movements such as Francis Townsend's old-age pension plan. Its constitutionality was upheld by the Supreme Court in Steward Machine Co. v. Davis and Helvering v. Davis (1937). Major later amendments added survivors' and dependents' benefits (1939), disability insurance (1956), and — under separate titles of the Act — Medicare and Medicaid (1965). Cost-of-living adjustments were introduced in the 1970s, and the 1983 amendments raised the retirement age and taxed some benefits in response to financing shortfalls.

Significance

Social Security is the largest single programme in the US federal budget and has dramatically reduced poverty among the elderly; its terms — the retirement age, payroll tax rates and the long-term solvency of its trust funds — remain a perennial subject of political debate. The first monthly benefit cheque, of $22.54, was issued to Ida May Fuller of Vermont in January 1940.
