Cartomancy

TypeDivination
ToolsPlaying cards; tarot cards; oracle cards
OriginsEurope, after the introduction of playing cards in the 14th century
Notable figuresEtteilla; Marie Anne Lenormand

Cartomancy is fortune-telling or divination using a deck of cards. Forms of cartomancy appeared in Europe soon after playing cards were introduced in the fourteenth century. Practitioners assign meanings to individual cards and to their positions in a spread, and interpret them in relation to a question or the querent's circumstances.

History

The first printed guide to cartomancy is generally credited to the Italian Francesco Marcolini in the sixteenth century. The practice was systematised in eighteenth-century France by Jean-Baptiste Alliette, known as Etteilla, who published influential manuals and designed a deck for divination. During the Napoleonic era, Marie Anne Lenormand became famous as a card reader to Parisian society; the Petit Lenormand deck that bears her name (published after her death) remains popular, particularly in Germany and Central Europe.

Decks and methods

Cartomancy may be practised with ordinary playing cards, with the tarot (especially the Major Arcana and full 78-card decks such as the Rider–Waite tarot), or with dedicated oracle decks such as the Lenormand (36 cards) and Kipper cards. Common methods include drawing a single card, three-card spreads for past–present–future, and larger layouts such as the Celtic Cross and the Lenormand Grand Tableau, which uses the whole deck.

Criticism

Cartomancy is regarded by scientists and sceptics as a pseudoscience; its effects are commonly explained by cold reading, confirmation bias, and the Barnum effect. It nevertheless remains a widespread practice in popular culture and the occult community.
