Full-frame digital SLR

A full-frame digital single-lens reflex camera (full-frame DSLR) is a digital SLR whose image sensor is the same size as a frame of 35 mm film — approximately 36 × 24 mm. The format offers shallower depth of field, wider effective fields of view for a given focal length, and typically better low-light performance than smaller-sensor cameras.

History

Early digital SLRs used smaller APS-C-sized sensors because large sensors were expensive to manufacture. Kodak's DCS Pro 14n of 2002 was among the first affordable full-frame models, and Canon's EOS-1Ds line established the format at the professional level. Nikon followed with the D3 in 2007. From the 2010s, mirrorless cameras increasingly displaced full-frame DSLRs, and the major manufacturers wound down DSLR development in the early 2020s.

Advantages and disadvantages

Full-frame sensors gather more light per image, improving signal-to-noise ratios, and allow the use of classic 35 mm lens designs at their intended field of view. The drawbacks are larger, heavier, and more expensive bodies and lenses.
