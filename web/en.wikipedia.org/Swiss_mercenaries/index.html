Swiss mercenaries

Swiss pikemen were the most sought-after mercenary infantry of the Renaissance.

Swiss mercenaries (in German: Reisläufer) were soldiers from the cantons of the Old Swiss Confederacy who, from the late Middle Ages into the early modern period, were renowned across Europe for their battlefield effectiveness and were hired by numerous foreign powers — above all France, Spain, the Papal States, and various Italian states.

Tactics and reputation

Swiss infantry fought in dense pike squares (Gewalthaufen), supported by halberdiers and, later, crossbowmen and arquebusiers. Victories over Burgundy in the 1470s and in the Swabian War of 1499 made their reputation; they were considered nearly unbeatable until the combined arms of Spanish tercios and artillery checked them at battles such as Bicocca (1522) and Pavia (1525).

Service abroad

Mercenary service became a major source of income for the Swiss cantons, governed by capitulations (contracts) negotiated between cantons and foreign rulers. The most famous surviving Swiss unit is the Pontifical Swiss Guard in the Vatican, founded in 1506. The Swiss Federal Constitution of 1848 banned new mercenary capitulations, and a federal law of 1859 prohibited individual enlistment in foreign armies, with the Vatican guard as the sole exception.
