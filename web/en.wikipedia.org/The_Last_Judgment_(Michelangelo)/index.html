The Last Judgment (Michelangelo)

ArtistMichelangelo
Year1536–1541
MediumFresco
Dimensions13.7 m × 12 m
LocationSistine Chapel, Vatican City

The Last Judgment (Italian: Il Giudizio Universale) is a fresco by the Italian Renaissance painter Michelangelo covering the whole altar wall of the Sistine Chapel in Vatican City. It depicts the Second Coming of Christ and the final and eternal judgment by God of all humanity. The work took over four years to complete, between 1536 and 1541, beginning some 25 years after Michelangelo had finished the Sistine Chapel ceiling.

Composition

Christ stands at the centre of the composition, beardless and massive, his right hand raised in judgment, surrounded by the Virgin Mary and a host of saints who display the instruments of their martyrdom. Below, angels blow trumpets to wake the dead, who rise from their graves on the lower left, while the damned on the lower right are dragged toward Hell, where Charon ferries souls across and Minos, depicted with donkey's ears and a serpent, judges them. The fresco contains more than 300 figures.

Controversy

The work was controversial from the start. Critics within the Church objected to the extensive nudity and to pagan figures such as Charon and Minos appearing in the holiest of shrines. Biagio da Cesena, the papal master of ceremonies, reportedly complained that the painting belonged in a bathhouse rather than a chapel; Michelangelo retaliated by painting his face onto Minos. After the Council of Trent condemned nudity in religious art, the painter Daniele da Volterra was hired in 1564–65 to paint draperies over the offending figures, earning him the nickname "Il Braghettone" ("the breeches-maker").

Self-portrait

Michelangelo is believed to have painted his own face on the flayed skin of Saint Bartholomew, held by the saint just below Christ — an image often read as an expression of the artist's anxiety about his own salvation.

Restoration

A major restoration between 1980 and 1994 removed centuries of grime and candle soot, revealing the fresco's bright colours and provoking its own debate among art historians about how much of what was removed had been Michelangelo's own final modelling.
