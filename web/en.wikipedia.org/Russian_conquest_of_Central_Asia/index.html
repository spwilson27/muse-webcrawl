Russian conquest of Central Asia

Periodc. 1847–1885 (to 1895 in the Pamirs)
BelligerentsRussian Empire vs Kazakh, Khivan, Bukharan, Kokandi and Turkmen forces
ResultAnnexation of the steppe and Turkestan; Bukhara and Khiva reduced to protectorates
Territoryc. 1.6 million square miles added to the empire

The Russian conquest of Central Asia was the 19th-century expansion of the Russian Empire into the Kazakh steppe and the khanates of Turkestan. Between the 1840s and the 1880s, Russia annexed the territories of present-day Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Turkmenistan, bringing its frontier to Persia and Afghanistan and fuelling the Anglo-Russian rivalry known as the Great Game.

Advance across the steppe

Russia first consolidated control over the Kazakh hordes, building lines of forts along the Syr Darya. In 1864–65, forces under Mikhail Chernyayev took Tashkent; the Governorate-General of Turkestan was created in 1867 under Konstantin von Kaufman. Samarkand fell in 1868, and the Emirate of Bukhara became a protectorate.

Khiva, Kokand and the Turkmen

The Khanate of Khiva was subdued in 1873 and likewise made a protectorate. Kokand, after repeated revolts, was annexed outright in 1876 as the Fergana Oblast. The fiercest resistance came from the Turkmen tribes: Mikhail Skobelev's storming of Geok Tepe in 1881, accompanied by a massacre of the defenders, broke Turkmen resistance, and Merv submitted in 1884. The Panjdeh incident of 1885 nearly brought war with Britain; a boundary commission then delimited the Afghan frontier, completed in the Pamirs by 1895.

Motives and consequences

Motives debated by historians include securing the frontier against raids, markets and cotton for Russian industry (sharpened by the American Civil War's cotton famine), prestige, and the momentum generated by ambitious officers on the spot. Conquest brought Russian settlement, railways such as the Trans-Caspian line, and a colonial administration; it ended the Central Asian slave trade but entrenched cotton monoculture, with long-lasting social and ecological consequences.
