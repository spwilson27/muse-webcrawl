Polynesian mythology

Polynesian mythology comprises the oral traditions, narratives and beliefs of the Polynesian peoples of the Pacific Ocean, including Māori, Native Hawaiians, Samoans, Tahitians, Tongans, Cook Islanders and others. Transmitted orally through genealogies, chants and stories, these traditions share many cognate gods, heroes and creation accounts across widely scattered islands, reflecting the common ancestry and voyaging culture of Polynesian societies.

Common themes and figures

Widely shared elements include the sky father and earth mother (Rangi and Papa in Māori tradition; Wākea and Papahānaumoku in Hawaiian), the culture hero and demigod Māui, who fishes up islands, snares the sun and seeks immortality, and great gods such as Tangaroa (god of the sea), Tāne (god of forests), Tū (god of war) and Rongo (god of agriculture), whose names recur across the region with local variations. Creation accounts often proceed from darkness and nothingness (Te Kore, Pō) through successive stages to the world of light.

Regional traditions

Māori mythology centres on the separation of Ranginui and Papatūānuku by their children and on heroes such as Tāwhaki. Hawaiian religion honoured four great gods—Kū, Kāne, Lono and Kanaloa—under a strict kapu system abolished in 1819, shortly before the arrival of Christian missionaries. Samoan and Tongan traditions emphasise the creator Tagaloa and elaborate royal genealogies, while the French Polynesian traditions of Tahiti and the Marquesas preserve cosmogonies centred on Ta'aroa.

Preservation

Much Polynesian mythology was recorded in the nineteenth century by indigenous scholars and European collectors, and it remains a living source of cultural identity, language revitalisation and art; narratives such as the Hawaiian Kumulipo creation chant and Māori whakapapa continue to be transmitted and reinterpreted today.
