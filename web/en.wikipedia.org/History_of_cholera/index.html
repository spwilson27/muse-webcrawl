History of cholera

The history of cholera concerns seven global pandemics of the disease caused by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae, spread from its home in the Ganges delta since 1817, together with the scientific investigations — above all John Snow's work on the 1854 London outbreak — that founded modern epidemiology and transformed public health.

Origins and first pandemics

Cholera-like illnesses were described in ancient Indian texts, and the Ganges delta of Bengal is regarded as the disease's historic homeland. The first pandemic (1817–1824) began near Calcutta and spread through Asia to the Caspian. The second pandemic (1826–1837) reached Europe and the Americas, killing hundreds of thousands and provoking riots and quarantines. Cholera became the archetypal epidemic disease of the industrial city, striking the overcrowded, poorly sanitised districts of the rapidly growing nineteenth-century world.

John Snow and the Broad Street pump

Cholera was long blamed on "miasma" (bad air). The London physician John Snow argued it was waterborne: his investigation of the Soho outbreak of 1854, mapping deaths clustered around the Broad Street pump, led the parish to remove the pump handle. Snow's analysis of London water companies in 1855 further demonstrated the waterborne route. In 1854 Filippo Pacini identified the comma-shaped bacterium, though his work was ignored; Robert Koch isolated Vibrio cholerae in 1883–84 and established its role.

Later pandemics

The third to sixth pandemics (1846–1920) repeatedly swept the world, including the Hamburg outbreak of 1892 that vindicated water filtration, while cholera drove the sanitary revolution: clean water supplies, sewers and international sanitary conferences. The seventh pandemic, caused by the El Tor biotype, began in Indonesia in 1961 and reached Africa and the Americas; it continues today. Major modern outbreaks have struck Zimbabwe (2008), Haiti (from 2010, introduced by peacekeepers) and Yemen (from 2016, the largest recorded).

Treatment and control

Cholera kills by catastrophic dehydration. Intravenous rehydration was pioneered in the early twentieth century, and the development of oral rehydration therapy in the 1960s–70s — hailed by The Lancet as potentially the century's most important medical advance — reduced case fatality from over 50% to under 1% when applied promptly. Oral cholera vaccines and water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) programmes underpin the global strategy to end cholera deaths by 2030.
