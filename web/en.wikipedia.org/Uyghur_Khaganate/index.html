The Uyghur Khaganate (also spelled Uighur; Old Turkic: Toquz Oghuz) was a Turkic steppe empire that ruled Mongolia and surrounding regions from 744 to 840. Founded by the Uyghur-led Toquz Oghuz ("Nine Oghuz") confederation after it overthrew the Second Turkic Khaganate, it was, at its height, one of the great powers of Asia and a close — if often dominant — partner of Tang China.

History

In 744 the Uyghur chief Qutlugh Bilge Köl, allied with the Karluks and Basmyls, destroyed the Turkic khaganate and took the title of khagan, founding his capital at Ordu-Baliq on the Orkhon River. His successor Moyun Chur (r. 747–759) consolidated the steppe, campaigned in the west, and left the great trilingual and runic inscriptions of the era. Uyghur cavalry twice saved the Tang dynasty during the An Lushan Rebellion (756 and 762), after which the Uyghurs extracted enormous payments in silk and privileged trade terms, including the notorious horse-for-silk trade.

Manichaeism and culture

Khagan Bögü (r. 759–779), encountering Sogdian Manichaean priests in Luoyang in 762/763, adopted Manichaeism as the state religion — a unique case for a steppe empire. Sogdian merchants and script deeply influenced Uyghur administration: the Uyghurs adapted the Sogdian alphabet into the Old Uyghur script, later used across Central Asia and ancestral to the Mongolian script. Ordu-Baliq grew into a walled city with palaces, temples and workshops unusual for a nomadic capital.

Decline and fall

Succession struggles, court factionalism between Manichaean and traditionalist parties, and over-dependence on Tang payments weakened the khaganate. A devastating winter (dzud) in 839, famine and internal revolt preceded the invasion of the Yenisei Kyrgyz, who sacked Ordu-Baliq in 840 and killed the khagan. The Uyghurs fled south and west: one group founded the Buddhist kingdom of Qocho in the Turfan depression and another the Ganzhou Uyghur Kingdom in Gansu — states that preserved Uyghur culture long after the steppe empire fell.
