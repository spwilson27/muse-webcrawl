Cyclic adenosine monophosphate

Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP, cyclic AMP, or 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate) is a second messenger derived from adenosine triphosphate (ATP). It is used for intracellular signal transduction in many different organisms, conveying the cAMP-dependent pathway.

Chemical formulaC10H12N5O6P
Molar mass329.21 g/mol
Synthesised byAdenylyl cyclase (from ATP)
Degraded byPhosphodiesterases
Discovered byEarl Sutherland and co-workers, 1950s

Synthesis and breakdown

cAMP is synthesised from ATP by the enzyme adenylyl cyclase, located in cell membranes and activated or inhibited by G proteins coupled to membrane receptors. It is broken down to AMP by phosphodiesterases; caffeine and various drugs raise cAMP levels by inhibiting these enzymes.

Function

cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates target proteins, and also acts on ion channels and exchange proteins such as Epac. In eukaryotes it mediates the action of hormones such as glucagon and adrenaline, which cannot cross the cell membrane. In bacteria, cAMP binds the catabolite activator protein to regulate sugar metabolism; in the slime mould Dictyostelium discoideum it acts extracellularly as a chemotactic signal for aggregation.

History

Earl Wilbur Sutherland Jr. discovered cAMP while studying how adrenaline stimulates glycogen breakdown in liver cells, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1971. The discovery established the concept of the second messenger.
