Retinal detachment

Retinal detachment is a disorder of the eye in which the retina separates from the layer underneath, the retinal pigment epithelium. Symptoms include the sudden appearance of floaters, flashes of light (photopsia), and a shadow or "curtain" spreading across the visual field. It is a medical emergency that can cause permanent vision loss if untreated.

SpecialtyOphthalmology
SymptomsFloaters, flashes of light, visual field shadow
Risk factorsSevere myopia, eye injury, previous cataract surgery, family history
TreatmentLaser photocoagulation, cryotherapy, scleral buckle, vitrectomy, pneumatic retinopexy

Types and causes

In rhegmatogenous detachment, the commonest type, a tear or hole in the retina lets fluid from the vitreous pass underneath and lift the retina away; it is often preceded by posterior vitreous detachment. Tractional detachment results from scar tissue pulling on the retina, as in advanced diabetic eye disease, while exudative detachment arises from fluid leaking beneath an intact retina due to inflammation or tumours.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by examination of the back of the eye with an ophthalmoscope after dilation; where the view is obscured, ultrasound can confirm the detachment. Prompt assessment is essential, since detachment involving the macula has a worse prognosis for central vision.

Treatment

Small tears may be sealed with laser or freezing treatment. Established detachments require surgery: a scleral buckle indents the wall of the eye toward the tear; vitrectomy removes the vitreous gel and replaces it with gas or oil; pneumatic retinopexy injects a gas bubble to push the retina back into place. Most detachments can be reattached anatomically, though visual recovery varies.
