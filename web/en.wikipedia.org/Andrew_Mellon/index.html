Andrew William Mellon (24 March 1855 – 26 August 1937) was an American banker, businessman, industrialist, philanthropist and art collector. One of the wealthiest men in the United States, he served as Secretary of the Treasury under three presidents — Harding, Coolidge and Hoover — from 1921 to 1932, and founded the National Gallery of Art in Washington, D.C.

Business career

Mellon joined his father Thomas Mellon's Pittsburgh banking house, T. Mellon & Sons, and built it into the centre of a vast industrial and financial empire. He helped finance and direct companies including Gulf Oil, the Aluminum Company of America (Alcoa), the Carborundum Company and Union Steel, recognising early the value of backing new technologies such as aluminium and oil refining.

Secretary of the Treasury

As Treasury Secretary, Mellon pursued the "Mellon Plan": steep reductions in income tax rates (the top rate fell from 73% to 25%), reduced government spending, and retirement of the national debt. Admirers credited him with fuelling the prosperity of the 1920s and he was celebrated as the greatest Treasury secretary since Alexander Hamilton. Critics blamed his tax cuts for worsening inequality and his orthodox response — balancing budgets and raising interest rates — for deepening the Great Depression after 1929. He resigned in 1932 to become ambassador to Britain, ending the third-longest tenure of any Treasury secretary.

Philanthropy and the National Gallery

Mellon assembled one of the world's great private art collections, including 21 paintings bought from the Soviet government's Hermitage sales in 1930–31. In 1937 he offered his collection and an endowment to the nation, funding the National Gallery of Art, which opened in 1941. He also co-founded the Mellon Institute of Industrial Research (now part of Carnegie Mellon University).
