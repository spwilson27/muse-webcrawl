John Milton (9 December 1608 – 8 November 1674) was an English poet, polemicist and civil servant. His 1667 epic poem Paradise Lost, written in blank verse and including over ten thousand lines, is generally considered the greatest epic in English literature, and it brought him wide acclaim and recognition from posterity. Also noted for the early elegy Lycidas, the masque Comus and the tragedy Samson Agonistes, Milton was a master of several languages and a formidable controversialist in prose.

Milton studied at Christ's College, Cambridge, travelled in Italy in 1638–39 (meeting Galileo, by then under house arrest), and returned to an England sliding toward civil war. He threw himself into pamphleteering for the parliamentary cause: tracts on church government, on divorce (prompted by his own unhappy first marriage), and, in Areopagitica (1644), one of the most famous defences of free speech and against pre-publication censorship ever written. After the execution of Charles I he defended the regicide in The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates and Eikonoklastes, and served the Commonwealth as Secretary for Foreign Tongues, composing Latin state letters and the Defensio pro Populo Anglicano (1651).

Blindness and later works

Milton went completely blind by 1652, probably from glaucoma, but continued his work with the help of amanuenses; his sonnet "When I consider how my light is spent" reflects on his blindness and duty. At the Restoration in 1660 he was briefly imprisoned and in danger of execution, but was released and lived in retirement. He composed Paradise Lost by dictation, publishing it in ten books in 1667 (revised to twelve books in 1674), followed by Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes in 1671.

Legacy

Milton's influence on English poetry—on Blake, Wordsworth, Shelley and Keats among many others—is immense, and his assertive republicanism and defence of liberty made him a hero to later radicals and liberals. William Blake's claim that Milton was "a true Poet and of the Devils party without knowing it" captures the enduring fascination of his Satan, one of literature's most compelling characters.
