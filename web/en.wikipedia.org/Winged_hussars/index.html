Country: Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

Winged hussars

The winged hussars (Polish: husaria) were a heavy cavalry formation of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, active from the 1570s to 1776 and renowned as one of the most effective cavalry forces in early modern Europe. Their name came from the large feathered "wings" worn on the back, made of eagle, vulture or other feathers set in wooden or metal frames.

Equipment and tactics

Recruited from the nobility, winged hussars wore steel cuirasses, helmets (often the lobster-tailed szyszak), and carried the very long hollow lance (kopia, up to 6 metres), a sabre, and often pistols and a war hammer or axe. They charged in tight formation, knee to knee, at increasing speed, delivering a devastating shock attack intended to break enemy lines in a single blow. The function of the wings is debated — they may have served for display and intimidation, protected against lassoes, or produced a distinctive noise.

Famous battles

The hussars won celebrated victories at Kircholm (1605), Klushino (1610), and Vienna (1683), where the largest cavalry charge in history — some 18,000 horsemen led by King John III Sobieski — helped relieve the Ottoman siege. Their role declined as firearms and disciplined infantry dominated battlefields, and the formation was formally disbanded in 1776.
