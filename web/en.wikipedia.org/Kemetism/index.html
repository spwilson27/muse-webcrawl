Kemetism

Kemetism (also Kemeticism or Kemetic paganism) is a modern pagan religion that seeks to revive or reconstruct the religion of ancient Egypt. Its name derives from Kemet (km.t), the ancient Egyptian name for Egypt, "the Black Land". Kemetic practitioners worship the ancient Egyptian gods — the netjeru — and draw on Egyptological scholarship, temple inscriptions and funerary texts to shape modern practice.

Beliefs and practice

Kemetism centres on the netjeru, the many gods and goddesses of ancient Egypt, understood variously as distinct beings or as aspects of a single divine force (sometimes called Netjer, "god/divinity"). Central to its ethics is ma'at — truth, justice, balance and right order — which practitioners strive to uphold in daily life. Typical practice includes daily offerings of food, drink, incense and candlelight before a shrine (senut rite in Kemetic Orthodoxy), prayers, festivals following the ancient Egyptian calendar, and observances for the akhu, the blessed dead.

History

Modern Kemetic religion emerged in the 1970s amid the wider growth of neopaganism, with groups such as the Church of the Eternal Source (founded 1970) and the Ausar Auset Society. In 1988 Tamara Siuda founded the movement that became Kemetic Orthodoxy, formally organised as the House of Netjer, which recognises a living Nisut (spiritual leader) in the pharaonic tradition and grew into one of the largest Kemetic organisations. Many practitioners are solitary "Kemetic reconstructionists" who build practice directly from academic sources.

Varieties

Kemetic groups range from reconstructionist — attempting to follow ancient practice as closely as scholarship allows — to eclectic and revivalist groups that blend Egyptian material with other traditions. Symbols in common use include the ankh, the Eye of Horus and the djed pillar, and festivals mark events such as Wep Ronpet, the opening of the Egyptian New Year at the heliacal rising of Sirius.
