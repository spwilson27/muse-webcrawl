Ectoparasite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ectoparasite is a parasite that lives on the external surface of its host, in contrast to an endoparasite, which lives inside the host's body. Ectoparasites are found across many groups of organisms, but the term is most often applied to arthropods such as ticks, fleas, lice and mites, as well as to some annelids (leeches), monogenean flatworms of fish, and parasitic crustaceans.

Feeding and effects

Most ectoparasites feed on blood (haematophagy), skin, hair, feathers or secretions of their host. Their effects range from minor irritation and itching to anaemia, allergic reactions and secondary infection. Many are important vectors of disease: ticks transmit Lyme disease and tick-borne encephalitis, fleas carried plague, body lice transmit typhus, and mosquitoes — sometimes classed as micropredators rather than parasites — transmit malaria, dengue and other pathogens.

Adaptations

Ectoparasites show adaptations including flattened bodies for clinging among hairs or feathers, hooked claws and suckers, piercing mouthparts, anticoagulants in saliva, and in some groups the complete loss of wings or eyes. Some are permanent residents of the host (lice, many mites); others, like ticks and bed bugs, visit only to feed. Hosts counter with grooming, immune responses and symbiotic cleaners — birds and fish that remove ectoparasites.

Control

Control measures include insecticides and acaricides, physical removal, environmental management, and biological control. Ectoparasites are of major economic importance in livestock production and veterinary medicine, and of clinical importance in human dermatology.
