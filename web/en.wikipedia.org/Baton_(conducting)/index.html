Baton (conducting)

A baton is a stick that is used by conductors primarily to enlarge and enhance the manual and bodily movements associated with directing an ensemble of musicians. Batons are typically made of a lightweight material — wood, fibreglass or carbon fibre — with a handle (grip) of cork or wood, balanced so that the weight rests comfortably in the hand. Their length ranges from about 25 to 45 centimetres.

Before the baton's adoption in the 19th century, ensembles were led from the keyboard, by the first violinist's bow, or by a time-beater using a heavy staff thumped on the floor — the practice that killed the composer Jean-Baptiste Lully, who struck his foot with his conducting staff in 1687 and died of the resulting gangrene. Composers such as Louis Spohr and Carl Maria von Weber were among the first to use a slim baton, and by the later 19th century it had become standard for orchestral conducting.

Some conductors prefer not to use a baton, conducting with hands alone — a choice common among choral conductors and certain orchestra directors — arguing it allows greater expressive nuance. The baton is held in one hand, traditionally the right, and its tip traces beat patterns while the free hand shapes phrasing and dynamics.
