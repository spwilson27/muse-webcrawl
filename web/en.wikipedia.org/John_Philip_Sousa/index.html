John Philip Sousa

Born: 6 November 1854, Washington, D.C., US
Died: 6 March 1932 (aged 77), Reading, Pennsylvania, US
Occupation: Composer, conductor
Known for: "The March King"; "The Stars and Stripes Forever"; "Semper Fidelis"; the sousaphone

John Philip Sousa (November 6, 1854 – March 6, 1932) was an American composer and conductor of the late Romantic era, known primarily for American military marches. He is known as "The March King" or the "American March King", to distinguish him from his British counterpart Kenneth J. Alford. Among his best-known marches are "The Stars and Stripes Forever" (National March of the United States of America), "Semper Fidelis" (official march of the United States Marine Corps), "The Liberty Bell", "The Thunderer" and "The Washington Post".

Life and career

Sousa was born in Washington, D.C., the third of ten children of Portuguese and German immigrants. He began his musical education as a pupil of John Esputa, studying voice, violin, piano and several band instruments. At 13 he was apprenticed to the United States Marine Band, serving as its youngest member; after his discharge he played in theatre orchestras and learned conducting. In 1880, aged 25, he returned to the Marine Band as its leader, transforming it over twelve years into the country's premier military band.

In 1892 Sousa formed his own civilian band, which toured North America and Europe to enormous acclaim for four decades, performing at venues from small towns to the Paris Exposition and giving more than 15,000 concerts. His band popularised the works of Wagner and other composers alongside his own marches.

Works and inventions

Sousa composed 136 marches, 15 operettas (including El Capitan), and numerous songs, suites and arrangements. Aiming for a deeper bass sound that could be heard over a marching band, he worked with instrument maker J. W. Pepper to develop the sousaphone, the large circular tuba that bears his name. He also wrote an autobiography, Marching Along (1928), and an influential essay, "The Menace of the Mechanical Mother", arguing against the displacement of live music by recordings. Sousa died of heart failure in Reading, Pennsylvania, in 1932, after conducting a rehearsal of "The Stars and Stripes Forever".
