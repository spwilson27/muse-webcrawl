M. C. Escher

Maurits Cornelis Escher
Born 17 June 1898, Leeuwarden, Netherlands
Died 27 March 1972, Laren, Netherlands
Known for: drawing, printmaking, woodcuts, lithography, mezzotint

Maurits Cornelis Escher (17 June 1898 – 27 March 1972), usually referred to as M. C. Escher, was a Dutch graphic artist who made mathematically inspired woodcuts, lithographs, and mezzotints. His work features impossible constructions, explorations of infinity, architecture, and tessellations.

Early life and training

Escher was born in Leeuwarden, the youngest son of a civil engineer. He studied at the School for Architecture and Decorative Arts in Haarlem, where he was taught by Samuel Jessurun de Mesquita, whose printmaking strongly influenced him. Travels in Italy and Spain in the 1920s and 1930s — including a visit to the Alhambra in Granada, whose Moorish tilework fascinated him — supplied the landscapes, townscapes, and tessellation ideas that shaped his art.

Mathematical art

Although Escher had no formal mathematical training, his work draws on concepts of symmetry, hyperbolic geometry, topology, and impossible objects. Works such as Relativity (1953), with its mutually incompatible gravity wells; Ascending and Descending (1960), based on the Penrose stairs; Waterfall (1961); and the Circle Limit series, developed with geometer H. S. M. Coxeter, remain icons of the intersection of art and mathematics.

The 1955 exhibition of his work in Amsterdam, held alongside a mathematics congress, brought him to the attention of scientists and mathematicians, and Roger Penrose and Coxeter both corresponded with him. His images later became staples of popular culture, appearing on book covers — most famously Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979).
