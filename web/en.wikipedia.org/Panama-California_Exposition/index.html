Panama–California Exposition

The Panama–California Exposition was an exposition held in San Diego, California, from 1915 to 1917, celebrating the opening of the Panama Canal and promoting San Diego as the first United States port of call for ships travelling north after passing through the canal. It was held in Balboa Park, where its Spanish Colonial Revival buildings still define the park's character.

Planning and design

San Diego competed with San Francisco's Panama–Pacific International Exposition of 1915; San Diego's fair was smaller and privately financed. Architect Bertram Goodhue, with planner John Charles Olmsted's earlier park plan set aside, designed a romantic Spanish colonial city with ornate Churrigueresque facades, centred on the California Building with its iconic tower and dome, reached across the Cabrillo Bridge. Landscape designer Kate Sessions contributed planting. The fair's architecture launched a wave of Spanish Revival building across Southern California.

Exhibits

Key structures included the Spreckels Organ Pavilion (with what was then the world's largest outdoor pipe organ), the Botanical Building and lily pond, and the controversial "Painted Desert" exhibit and anthropology displays arranged by the Smithsonian's Aleš Hrdlička, including skeletal remains that later became the subject of repatriation debates. The exposition drew nearly four million visitors over two years; it reopened in 1916 as the Panama–California International Exposition with an international focus.

Legacy

Unlike most expositions, many of San Diego's fair buildings were permanent. They now house the San Diego Museum of Art, the Museum of Us (formerly Museum of Man), the San Diego Natural History Museum, and other institutions. A second fair, the California Pacific International Exposition of 1935–36, reused the grounds and added further buildings.
