Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City

Citation438 U.S. 104 (1978)
DecidedJune 26, 1978
MajorityBrennan, J.
SubjectRegulatory takings; historic preservation

Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City, 438 U.S. 104 (1978), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision on the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause. The Court upheld New York City's Landmarks Preservation Law against a claim that applying it to Grand Central Terminal effected a taking requiring just compensation, and it articulated the multi-factor "Penn Central test" that remains the framework for most regulatory takings analysis.

Background

Grand Central Terminal was designated a New York City landmark in 1967. Its owner, the bankrupt Penn Central railroad, agreed to lease the air space above the terminal for a 50-storey office tower designed by Marcel Breuer. The Landmarks Preservation Commission rejected the plans, and Penn Central sued, arguing that the restrictions took its property without just compensation.

Decision

By a 6–3 majority, Justice William J. Brennan Jr. held there was no taking. Unable to find a "set formula", the Court identified several significant factors in regulatory takings inquiries: (1) the economic impact of the regulation on the claimant; (2) the extent to which the regulation interferes with distinct investment-backed expectations; and (3) the character of the government action. The terminal could still earn a reasonable return in its existing use, and transferable development rights mitigated the burden.

Significance

The decision preserved Grand Central and validated historic preservation laws across the United States. The Penn Central balancing test has been applied in countless later cases and criticised as ad hoc, but it remains the default analysis where a regulation is alleged to go "too far" without a physical appropriation or total loss of value.
