Claude Monet

Oscar-Claude Monet (14 November 1840 – 5 December 1926) was a French painter and founder of Impressionism. His painting Impression, Sunrise (1872), exhibited in Paris in 1874, gave the movement its name — initially as a critic's derisive term. Monet pursued the depiction of light and atmosphere through broken colour and open composition, painting en plein air and repeatedly returning to the same subjects in series — haystacks, poplars, Rouen Cathedral, and the water lilies of his garden at Giverny — to record changing conditions of light and season.

Early life

Monet grew up in Le Havre, where he gained local fame for caricatures and met the landscape painter Eugène Boudin, who taught him to paint outdoors. After military service in Algeria (1861–62), he studied in Paris, meeting Renoir, Sisley, and Bazille. Financial hardship marked his early career; the Franco-Prussian War drove him to London, where he painted the Thames and studied Turner and Constable.

Impressionism

Rejected by the official Salon, Monet and his colleagues mounted independent exhibitions from 1874. His method — high-key colour, visible brushwork, and composition without black — codified the Impressionist approach. From the 1880s, supported by the dealer Paul Durand-Ruel and growing American demand, Monet achieved financial security, buying the house at Giverny in 1890.

Series paintings and Giverny

At Giverny Monet constructed an elaborate garden and a Japanese-style water garden with a pond of water lilies, which became his principal subject for the last thirty years of his life. His series paintings of the 1890s — Haystacks, Poplars, Rouen Cathedral — explored one motif under dozens of light conditions. The monumental Water Lilies cycle, eight murals now in the Musée de l'Orangerie, was donated to France after the First World War. Despite failing eyesight from cataracts, Monet painted until shortly before his death in 1926.
