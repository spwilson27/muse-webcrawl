The Sea Peoples are a group of seafaring peoples recorded in Egyptian sources of the late 13th and early 12th centuries BC as invading and raiding the eastern Mediterranean during the Late Bronze Age collapse. Pharaohs Merneptah (c. 1208 BC) and Ramesses III (c. 1175 BC) claim great victories over coalitions of named groups — among them the Peleset, Sherden, Shekelesh, Denyen, Ekwesh, Lukka, Teresh, Tjeker and Weshesh — attacking Egypt by land and sea, with families and ox-carts accompanying the land forces.

Identity and origins

Who the Sea Peoples were remains debated. The Peleset are widely identified with the biblical Philistines, who settled the southern Levantine coast; the Sherden have been linked (contentiously) with Sardinia, the Shekelesh with Sicily, the Teresh with the Tyrsenoi/Etruscans, and the Denyen and Ekwesh with Greek groups (Danaoi and Achaeans) — identifications that may reflect later migrations as much as origins. The famous reliefs at Medinet Habu depict their feathered headdresses, round shields and distinctive ships.

Role in the Bronze Age collapse

Their raids coincided with the destruction of Ugarit, the fall of the Hittite Empire and widespread palace burnings around 1200 BC. Modern scholarship generally treats the Sea Peoples as both a symptom and an accelerant of a wider systems collapse involving drought, famine, earthquakes and trade breakdown, rather than its sole cause. Ramesses III settled defeated groups as garrisons in Canaan, where the Peleset became the Philistines of the Hebrew Bible.
