Chlorofluorocarbon

A chlorofluorocarbon (CFC) is an organic compound composed only of carbon, chlorine, and fluorine. CFCs were developed in the late 1920s and widely used as refrigerants (marketed under the trade name Freon), aerosol propellants, solvents, and foam-blowing agents, because they are non-toxic, non-flammable, and chemically stable. That same stability allows them to reach the stratosphere, where they destroy ozone; production has therefore been phased out under the Montreal Protocol of 1987.

CFCs were widely used as aerosol propellants before the phase-out.

History

CFCs were synthesized by the American chemist Thomas Midgley Jr. and colleagues in 1928 while seeking a safe refrigerant to replace toxic gases such as ammonia and sulfur dioxide. Dichlorodifluoromethane (CFC-12) was demonstrated publicly in 1930 and commercialized by DuPont as Freon. Production grew enormously after the Second World War.

Ozone depletion

In 1974 chemists Mario Molina and F. Sherwood Rowland proposed that CFCs, inert in the lower atmosphere, would be broken down by ultraviolet radiation in the stratosphere, releasing chlorine atoms that catalytically destroy ozone. The discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole in 1985 confirmed the danger, and Molina, Rowland, and Paul Crutzen shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

Phase-out and substitutes

Under the Montreal Protocol, developed countries ended CFC production in 1996 and developing countries by 2010. Hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs) served as transitional substitutes, followed by hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), which do not deplete ozone but are potent greenhouse gases and are themselves being phased down under the 2016 Kigali Amendment. Atmospheric monitoring suggests the ozone layer is slowly recovering.
