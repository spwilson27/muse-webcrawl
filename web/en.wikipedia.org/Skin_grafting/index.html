Skin grafting

Skin grafting is a type of graft surgery involving the transplantation of skin. The transplanted tissue is called a skin graft. It is widely used to treat extensive wounds, burns, and areas of skin loss due to infection, trauma, or surgery such as the removal of skin cancers.

Types of grafts

Skin grafts are classified by thickness. Split-thickness grafts contain the epidermis and part of the dermis; they are harvested with a dermatome from donor sites such as the thigh and can cover large areas, and are often meshed to expand them. Full-thickness grafts include the entire dermis, contract less, and give a better cosmetic result, so they are used on the face and hands. Grafts are also classified by source: autografts come from the patient, allografts from another human (often cadaver skin used as temporary cover), and xenografts from another species, commonly pig skin.

Procedure and healing

The graft survives initially by absorbing nutrients from the wound bed (plasmatic imbibition) before new blood vessels grow into it (inosculation and revascularization). Successful “take” requires a clean, vascular wound bed and immobilization of the graft; failure may result from infection, hematoma, or shear forces. Donor sites for split-thickness grafts heal spontaneously within about two weeks. Modern adjuncts include negative-pressure wound therapy and tissue-engineered skin substitutes.
