Taijitu

Chinese: 太極圖 (tàijítú)
Meaning: "Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate"
Represents: Yin and yang
Culture: Chinese philosophy, Taoism

The taijitu (Chinese: 太極圖, "diagram of the supreme ultimate") is a symbol in Chinese philosophy representing taiji, the supreme ultimate, in both its monist and dualist aspects. The most familiar form shows a circle divided into interlocking black and white teardrop shapes, each containing a small dot of the opposite colour, symbolising the interdependence of yin and yang.

Forms and history

Early taijitu diagrams differed from the familiar design: diagrams associated with the Song dynasty philosopher Zhou Dunyi (11th century) used concentric circles and linked diagrams of the five phases, while the swirling two-part "yin-yang fish" design became common during the Ming dynasty. Variants appear across East Asia — in Korea the symbol (taegeuk) appears on the national flag, and it is used in Japan as tomoe-related designs.

Interpretation

The two halves represent complementary opposites — dark and light, passive and active, feminine and masculine — in dynamic balance. The dots signify that each force contains the seed of the other, and the curved boundary shows that the balance is one of continual change rather than a fixed division.
