International poverty line

The international poverty line is a monetary threshold set by the World Bank below which a person is considered to live in extreme poverty. It is used to track global poverty in a comparable way across countries, by converting local incomes or consumption into a common standard using purchasing power parity (PPP) exchange rates.

Definition

The line was originally set at about one US dollar a day in 1985 purchasing power parity terms, derived from the national poverty lines of the world's poorest countries. It has been periodically updated as new PPP benchmarks became available: to $1.25 a day in 2005 PPP terms in 2008, to $1.90 in 2011 PPP terms in 2015, and to $2.15 in 2017 PPP terms in September 2022. The Bank also publishes higher lines typical of lower-middle-income ($3.65) and upper-middle-income ($6.85) countries.

Global poverty trends

Measured against the line, the share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from roughly 36 per cent in 1990 to under 9 per cent in the late 2010s, largely because of rapid growth in China and later India. The COVID-19 pandemic caused the first increase in the global extreme poverty rate in decades in 2020.

Criticism

Critics argue that a single monetary line understates deprivation by ignoring health, education, housing and social exclusion, and that PPP conversions and survey methods can distort comparisons. The World Bank complements the monetary measure with a multidimensional poverty measure covering education and basic infrastructure.
