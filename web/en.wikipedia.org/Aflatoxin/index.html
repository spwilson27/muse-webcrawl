Aflatoxin

ClassMycotoxins
Main typesB1, B2, G1, G2, M1
Produced byAspergillus flavus, A. parasiticus

Aflatoxins are a family of toxins produced by certain fungi, principally Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus, which grow on agricultural crops such as maize, peanuts, cottonseed, and tree nuts, especially under warm, humid conditions. They are among the most potent naturally occurring carcinogens known, and contamination of food and animal feed is a major public-health and trade problem.

Discovery

Aflatoxins were identified in 1960 after the "Turkey X disease" outbreak in England killed around 100,000 turkey poults fed contaminated peanut meal. The toxins take their name from A. flavus toxin.

Types and toxicity

The major aflatoxins are B1, B2, G1, and G2 (named for their blue or green fluorescence), plus M1, a metabolite of B1 excreted in the milk of animals fed contaminated feed. Aflatoxin B1 is the most potent and is metabolized in the liver to a reactive epoxide that binds DNA, characteristically causing a G→T transversion at codon 249 of the TP53 tumor-suppressor gene. Chronic exposure is strongly associated with hepatocellular carcinoma, particularly in combination with hepatitis B infection; acute high-dose exposure causes aflatoxicosis, with liver failure and death, as in the 2004 Kenyan outbreak that killed over 100 people.

Regulation and control

Many countries set maximum limits for aflatoxins in food (the US FDA action level is 20 parts per billion for most foods; the EU limit is stricter). Control strategies include rapid drying and proper storage of crops, sorting, biocontrol with atoxigenic Aspergillus strains, and resistant crop varieties.
