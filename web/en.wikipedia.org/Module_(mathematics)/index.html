Module (mathematics)


Image placeholder: vector spaces generalised to modules

In mathematics, a module is a generalisation of the notion of a vector space in which the field of scalars is replaced by a ring. A module over a ring R consists of an abelian group M together with an operation of scalar multiplication by elements of R, satisfying distributive and associativity laws analogous to those for vector spaces. When R is a field, a module is exactly a vector space.

Examples

Every abelian group is a module over the ring of integers, with scalar multiplication by n meaning repeated addition. A ring is a module over itself. If R = k[x] is a polynomial ring over a field, a module over R amounts to a vector space equipped with a linear operator (multiplication by x), which makes module theory the natural framework for the structure theory of linear operators, including Jordan normal form.

Submodules and structure

Submodules generalise both subspaces and ideals. Unlike vector spaces, modules need not have bases: a module possessing a basis is called free. Finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain admit a clean classification (the structure theorem), analogous to the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups, but over general rings module theory is far wilder — for instance, submodules of free modules need not be free, and invariant basis number can fail for exotic rings.

Importance

Modules are central objects of commutative algebra and homological algebra: ideals, quotient rings, localisations, tensor products, and representations of groups (modules over the group ring) are all instances, and much of modern algebraic geometry and representation theory is phrased in their language.
