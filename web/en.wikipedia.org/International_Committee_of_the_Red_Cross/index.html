The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC) is a humanitarian organisation based in Geneva, Switzerland. Founded in 1863, it is the oldest and one of the most widely recognised organisations of the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement. The ICRC is a private association under Swiss law, but it is granted a special role by states through the Geneva Conventions and their Additional Protocols, which give it a unique mandate under international law to protect the lives and dignity of victims of armed conflict and other situations of violence and to provide them with assistance.

The committee is one of three components of the International Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement, alongside the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies (IFRC) and the more than 190 national Red Cross and Red Crescent societies. Unlike the national societies, the ICRC is a neutral, impartial and independent intermediary in conflict situations: it visits prisoners of war and civilian detainees, restores contact between separated family members, delivers relief, supports hospitals and water infrastructure, and promotes respect for international humanitarian law.

The ICRC has been awarded the Nobel Peace Prize three times, in 1917, 1944 and 1963, a distinction it shares only with the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees among organisations. Its emblem, the red cross on a white background — the reverse of the Swiss flag — is one of the most recognised protective symbols in the world.

History

Founding

The ICRC traces its origins to the Battle of Solferino of June 1859, during the Second Italian War of Independence. The Swiss businessman Henry Dunant, who witnessed the aftermath of the battle in northern Italy, was appalled by the suffering of tens of thousands of wounded soldiers left largely unattended on the battlefield. In 1862 he published A Memory of Solferino, in which he proposed the creation of voluntary relief societies that would care for the wounded in wartime regardless of nationality, and an international agreement protecting medical personnel and the wounded.

Dunant's proposals were taken up by the Geneva Society for Public Welfare, which on 17 February 1863 formed a five-member committee — Dunant, the lawyer Gustave Moynier, the physicians Louis Appia and Théodore Maunoir, and General Guillaume-Henri Dufour — known as the International Committee for Relief to the Wounded. An international conference in Geneva in October 1863 laid the foundations of the Red Cross, and in August 1864 twelve states signed the first Geneva Convention, "for the Amelioration of the Condition of the Wounded in Armies in the Field". The committee adopted the name International Committee of the Red Cross in 1876.

World wars

During the First World War, the ICRC established the International Prisoners-of-War Agency in Geneva, which processed millions of index cards on prisoners and organised parcels, correspondence and inspections of prison camps. Its work earned the organisation its first Nobel Peace Prize in 1917. In the Second World War the ICRC expanded its activities to civilian internees and operated a vast parcel and tracing service, receiving a second Nobel Prize in 1944. Its wartime record has also been the subject of historical criticism, particularly concerning its limited public response to Nazi persecution and extermination of civilians, a failing the organisation has since acknowledged.

Post-war period

After 1945 the ICRC was central to the diplomatic effort that produced the four Geneva Conventions of 1949, which remain the cornerstone of international humanitarian law, and later the Additional Protocols of 1977. During the Cold War and subsequent conflicts — from Korea and Vietnam to the Middle East, Africa and the Balkans — the ICRC developed its modern operational profile: detention visits, tracing, emergency relief, surgical hospitals and the confidential dialogue with warring parties that remains its characteristic working method. It received a third Nobel Peace Prize, jointly with the League of Red Cross Societies, in 1963, its centenary year.

Mandate and legal basis

The ICRC's mandate derives chiefly from the four Geneva Conventions of 1949, which have been ratified by every state in the world, and their Additional Protocols. In international armed conflict, the Conventions give the ICRC the right to visit prisoners of war and civilian internees, to interview them without witnesses, and to carry out relief actions. Common Article 3 of the Conventions, applicable in non-international armed conflicts, recognises that "an impartial humanitarian body, such as the International Committee of the Red Cross, may offer its services to the Parties to the conflict". The Statutes of the Red Cross and Red Crescent Movement, adopted by states and the Movement's components, extend the ICRC's recognised role to internal disturbances and other situations of violence.

Because of this treaty-based mandate, the ICRC is often described as a sui generis entity: formally a private Swiss association governed by a committee of Swiss citizens, yet exercising functions conferred by international law and recognised by states and by the United Nations, which granted it observer status in 1990.

Organisation

The ICRC is governed by the Committee itself, an Assembly of between 15 and 25 members, all Swiss nationals, co-opted for renewable terms; this exclusively Swiss composition is a legacy of the organisation's origins and is intended to symbolise its neutrality. The Assembly elects the president, who represents the ICRC internationally. Day-to-day operations are run by a Directorate headed by a director-general. The headquarters in Geneva employs a few thousand staff, while delegations in more than 100 countries employ the great majority of the organisation's personnel, most of them locally recruited. The ICRC's annual budget exceeds two billion Swiss francs, funded overwhelmingly by voluntary contributions from governments, with additional support from national societies, the European Union and private donors.

Activities

Protection

ICRC delegates visit places of detention in dozens of countries, registering detainees, monitoring their treatment and conditions, and making confidential recommendations to the detaining authorities. Its Central Tracing Agency and national society networks restore family links broken by conflict, migration and disaster, deliver Red Cross messages, and work to clarify the fate of missing persons. The ICRC also supports forensic services and the dignified management of the dead.

Assistance

In war-affected areas, the ICRC distributes food and household essentials, restores drinking-water and sanitation systems, supports hospitals with surgical teams and supplies, runs physical rehabilitation centres for people with disabilities, and helps communities sustain livelihoods. Its water and habitat engineers are a distinctive part of its field presence.

Promoting humanitarian law

The ICRC acts as guardian and promoter of international humanitarian law: it advises governments on ratification and national implementation, trains armed forces and armed groups, maintains a leading study of customary international humanitarian law, and convenes expert processes on issues such as autonomous weapons and warfare in cyberspace.

Emblems

The protective emblems recognised by the Geneva Conventions are the red cross, the red crescent and, since 2005, the red crystal. Under the Conventions, the emblems protect military medical services and authorised humanitarian personnel and units; their unauthorised use, and their perfidious misuse in combat, are prohibited. The ICRC uses the red cross emblem in its own identity.

Criticism and controversies

The ICRC's policy of confidentiality — reporting findings on detention and violations privately to the responsible authorities rather than publicly denouncing them — is both the foundation of its access and a recurring source of criticism, debated most prominently in relation to the Holocaust and, in more recent decades, detention practices in various conflicts. The organisation states that it resorts to public statements when confidential representations fail and grave violations persist. Its exclusively Swiss governing committee and its large share of funding from Western governments also periodically attract scrutiny, though its operational neutrality is widely accepted by parties to conflicts.
