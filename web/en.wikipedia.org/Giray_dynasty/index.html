The Giray dynasty (Crimean Tatar: Geraylar) was the ruling house of the Crimean Khanate from its founding in the fifteenth century until the Russian annexation of Crimea in 1783 — one of the longest-reigning dynasties of the Islamic world. The Girays claimed descent from Genghis Khan, a lineage that gave them exceptional prestige among the successor states of the Golden Horde: after the Ottoman sultans, they were regarded as the senior Muslim royal house, and by treaty the Giray khan stood next in line, in ceremonial terms, to inherit the Ottoman throne should the House of Osman fail.

Origins

The dynasty's founder, Hacı I Giray, a descendant of Tuqa-Timur, son of Jochi, seized power in Crimea around 1441 (some sources give 1449) with support from the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, establishing an independent khanate centred first at Qırq Yer and later at Bakhchisarai. His son Meñli I Giray was twice deposed and restored; in 1475 the Ottomans intervened, made Crimea a vassal state, and Meñli ruled thereafter under Ottoman suzerainty, though the khanate retained wide internal autonomy and its own foreign policy.

The khanate under the Girays

Giray khans led the Crimean Tatars' famed cavalry in campaigns across the Pontic steppe, raiding Muscovy and Poland–Lithuania for slaves traded through the port of Kaffa, and supplying the Ottoman Empire with light cavalry — Tatar forces famously reached Vienna in 1683. Notable khans include Sahib I Giray, founder of Bakhchisarai; Devlet I Giray, who burned Moscow in 1571; İslâm III Giray, who played a pivotal role in the Khmelnytsky Uprising; and Selim I Giray, four times khan and a patron of music and letters. The dynasty's internal politics were marked by competition among Giray princes and the great clan families (the Shirin, Barın, Arghın and Qıpçaq beys), with the offices of kalga and nureddin — second and third in the succession — held by the khan's brothers or sons.

Decline and fall

After the Russo-Turkish War of 1768–74, the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) made the khanate nominally independent of the Ottomans, opening the way to Russian domination. The last khan, Şahin Giray, an unpopular westernizing reformer, abdicated in 1783, and Catherine the Great annexed Crimea to the Russian Empire. Members of the dynasty later lived in the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria; descendants survive to the present day.
