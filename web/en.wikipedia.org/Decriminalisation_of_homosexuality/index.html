Decriminalisation of homosexuality

The decriminalisation of homosexuality is the process by which laws prohibiting consensual same-sex sexual activity have been repealed or struck down. Such laws, often inherited from colonial penal codes or religious legal traditions, historically prescribed punishments ranging from fines and imprisonment to death.

History

France decriminalised sodomy in 1791 during the Revolution, and the Napoleonic Code exported that approach to much of Europe and Latin America. By contrast, the British Empire spread anti-sodomy statutes, such as Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, across Asia and Africa, many of which survived independence. In the twentieth century, reform accelerated: England and Wales partially decriminalised male homosexuality with the Sexual Offences Act 1967, following the Wolfenden Report, and the European Court of Human Rights ruled in Dudgeon v United Kingdom (1981) that criminalisation violated the right to privacy.

Recent developments

The United States Supreme Court invalidated remaining state sodomy laws in Lawrence v. Texas (2003). India's Supreme Court read down Section 377 in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018). Courts or legislatures in Botswana, Angola, Belize, Trinidad and Tobago, Mauritius and several Caribbean states have removed prohibitions in the last decade. As of the mid-2020s, roughly two-thirds of countries permit consensual same-sex activity, while around sixty jurisdictions still criminalise it, a handful prescribing the death penalty.

Arguments

Reformers have argued from privacy, equality and public health — noting that criminalisation impedes HIV prevention — while opponents have cited religious doctrine and public morality. International human rights bodies, including the UN Human Rights Committee in Toonen v. Australia (1994), have held that such laws breach privacy protections.
