Horace

Quintus Horatius Flaccus (8 December 65 BC – 27 November 8 BC), known in English as Horace, was the leading Roman lyric poet of the Augustan age. His works — the Odes, Epodes, Satires, Epistles, and the Ars Poetica — have influenced Western literature for two millennia.

Life

Born at Venusia in southern Italy, the son of a freedman who invested in his education, Horace studied in Rome and Athens. He fought on the losing republican side at Philippi (42 BC), returned to Rome impoverished, and began writing. Taken up by Maecenas, Augustus's cultural minister, he received a farm in the Sabine hills — the setting celebrated throughout his poetry — and declined, politely, an invitation to become Augustus's private secretary.

Works

The four books of Odes adapt Greek lyric metres to Latin and range from drinking songs to the state poetry of the Carmen Saeculare, performed at Augustus's Secular Games in 17 BC. The Satires and Epistles are conversational hexameter poems; the second book of Epistles includes the Ars Poetica, the most influential classical treatise on poetry. Famous phrases from Horace include carpe diem ("seize the day"), dulce et decorum est pro patria mori, and in medias res.
