The West Coast air raids were a small number of attacks by Imperial Japanese forces on the West Coast of the United States during World War II, together with the false-alarm air raid panic in Los Angeles. Although militarily minor, they had an outsized effect on American morale and contributed to the atmosphere that led to the internment of Japanese Americans.

Incidents

On 23 February 1942 the Japanese submarine I-17 shelled the Ellwood oil field near Santa Barbara, California, causing minor damage. The following night, the so-called Battle of Los Angeles saw anti-aircraft batteries fire over 1,400 shells at reported — but nonexistent — enemy aircraft; several deaths resulted from accidents and heart attacks. In June 1942 a Japanese submarine shelled Fort Stevens at the mouth of the Columbia River in Oregon. In September 1942, the Lookout air raids by a floatplane launched from the submarine I-25 dropped incendiary bombs on Oregon forests near Brookings in an attempt to start forest fires — the only aerial bombing of the contiguous United States by a foreign power during the war. Japan also launched thousands of Fu-Go balloon bombs in 1944–45, one of which killed six civilians in Oregon.

Effects

The raids, widely reported, fed fears of invasion and espionage on the Pacific Coast and helped spur the mass removal and incarceration of Japanese Americans under Executive Order 9066.
