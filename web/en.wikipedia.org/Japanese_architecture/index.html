Japanese architecture (日本建築, Nihon kenchiku) has been shaped over two millennia by the islands' climate, earthquake risk, abundant timber, and cultural exchange with China and Korea, evolving a distinctive tradition of wooden construction, flexible interiors, and close integration with landscape.

Early and religious architecture

Prehistoric pit dwellings and raised-floor storehouses (takayuka) established patterns later monumentalised in Shinto shrine architecture, exemplified by the Ise Grand Shrine, ritually rebuilt every twenty years in unadorned cypress (the shinmei-zukuri style). From the 6th century, Buddhism brought continental temple architecture — pagodas, halls, and stone lanterns — seen at Hōryū-ji near Nara, whose 7th–8th-century buildings are among the world's oldest surviving wooden structures. Timber post-and-beam construction with flexible joints proved resilient in earthquakes, and complex bracketing supported heavy tiled roofs with wide overhanging eaves.

Castles, houses and the tea aesthetic

In the feudal period, fortified castles with white-plastered keeps, such as Himeji Castle, combined military engineering with elegance. Domestic architecture developed the shoin-zukuri style — tatami-mat floors of standard size, sliding fusuma and shōji screens, built-in alcoves (tokonoma) — while the tea-house aesthetic of rustic simplicity (sukiya style), perfected at the Katsura Imperial Villa in Kyoto, profoundly influenced ideals of proportion and understatement.

Modern era

After the Meiji Restoration (1868), Japan imported Western styles in brick and stone, then produced its own synthesis: Frank Lloyd Wright's Imperial Hotel (1923) famously survived the Great Kantō earthquake. Post-war Metabolists such as Kenzō Tange merged modernism with Japanese spatial ideas. Contemporary Japanese architects — Tadao Ando, Toyo Ito, SANAA (Kazuyo Sejima and Ryue Nishizawa), Shigeru Ban, Kengo Kuma — have won international prominence and an exceptional share of Pritzker Prizes, while traditional carpentry survives in shrine rebuilding and a deep national culture of periodic reconstruction.
