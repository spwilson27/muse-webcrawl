John Henry Patterson (author)

Died18 June 1947, Bel-Air, Los Angeles, U.S.

Lieutenant-Colonel John Henry Patterson (10 November 1867 – 18 June 1947) was an Anglo-Irish soldier, engineer and author, best known for his book The Man-Eaters of Tsavo (1907), which recounts how in 1898, while supervising the construction of a railway bridge over the Tsavo River in Kenya, he hunted down two man-eating lions that were terrorising the workforce. The episode inspired the film The Ghost and the Darkness (1996).

Tsavo

Patterson arrived in East Africa in 1898 to build a bridge for the Uganda Railway. Beginning that spring, two large maneless male lions repeatedly dragged workers from their tents at night, bringing construction to a halt and causing hundreds of labourers to flee. After months of failed traps and ambushes, Patterson shot the first lion on 9 December 1898 and the second about three weeks later. The lions' skins and skulls were eventually sold to the Field Museum in Chicago, where they remain on display.

Later career

Patterson served in the Second Boer War and in the First World War, during which he commanded the Jewish Legion, an experience recounted in With the Zionists in Gallipoli (1916) and With the Judaeans in the Palestine Campaign (1922). A committed Zionist, he became a close friend of Ze'ev Jabotinsky and godfather to Yonatan Netanyahu, named after Patterson's fallen comrade John (Yonatan) Netanyahu's grandfather's friend and commander. Patterson died in California in 1947; his remains were reinterred in Israel in 2014.
