Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a group of gram-positive bacteria that are genetically distinct from other strains of Staphylococcus aureus. MRSA is responsible for several difficult-to-treat infections in humans because it has acquired resistance to many commonly used antibiotics, including the beta-lactam class.

Resistance mechanism

MRSA strains carry the mecA gene (or the related mecC), usually on the SCCmec mobile genetic element, which encodes an alternative penicillin-binding protein, PBP2a, with low affinity for beta-lactam antibiotics. MRSA was first identified in the United Kingdom in 1961, only a year after methicillin entered clinical use.

Epidemiology

MRSA infections are classified as healthcare-associated (HA-MRSA), historically acquired in hospitals and care facilities, and community-associated (CA-MRSA), which emerged in the 1990s among otherwise healthy people, including athletes, prisoners, and military recruits. Livestock-associated strains (LA-MRSA) are also recognised. Infections range from skin and soft-tissue infections to pneumonia, bloodstream infection, and endocarditis.

Prevention and treatment

Control relies on hand hygiene, screening and isolation in hospitals, and antimicrobial stewardship. Antibiotics that remain active include vancomycin, linezolid, daptomycin, and newer cephalosporins such as ceftaroline; many community strains remain susceptible to oral agents such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and doxycycline.
