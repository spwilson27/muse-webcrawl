A standard-gauge railway is a railway with a track gauge of 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in). The standard gauge, also called the Stephenson gauge after its early promoter George Stephenson, is the most widely used rail gauge in the world, being used on about 55% of all railway lines, including most high-speed rail.

History

The gauge derives from the coal-carrying wagonways of north-east England, which used a gauge of 4 ft 8 in; Stephenson added an extra half inch to give more clearance for wheel flanges. The Liverpool and Manchester Railway of 1830 adopted it, and it spread with British railway engineering. In 1846 the British Parliament passed the Gauge Act mandating 4 ft 8½ in for new railways in Great Britain, ending the "gauge war" with Brunel's broad gauge of 7 ft ¼ in. The United States standardized on the gauge in the 1880s, converting thousands of miles of southern 5 ft gauge track in 1886.

Use

Standard gauge is used across most of Europe, North America, China, and much of the Middle East, as well as in Japan's Shinkansen network. Countries using other gauges include Russia and former Soviet states (1,520 mm), India (1,676 mm), Spain's conventional network (1,668 mm), and Ireland (1,600 mm). Breaks of gauge between standard and other gauges require transshipment, variable-gauge axles, or dual-gauge track.
