Monroe Doctrine

The Monroe Doctrine is a United States foreign policy position that opposes European colonialism in the Western Hemisphere. It holds that any intervention in the political affairs of the Americas by foreign powers is a potentially hostile act against the United States. The doctrine was central to American foreign policy for much of the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Origins

President James Monroe first stated the doctrine during his seventh annual State of the Union address to Congress on 2 December 1823. It was developed principally by his Secretary of State, John Quincy Adams, in response to fears that the European powers of the Holy Alliance might help Spain recover its newly independent former colonies in Latin America, and to Russian expansion on the northwest coast of North America. Monroe declared:

"The American continents, by the free and independent condition which they have assumed and maintain, are henceforth not to be considered as subjects for future colonization by any European powers."

The doctrine asserted two principles: that the United States would not interfere in European affairs or in existing European colonies, and that the Americas were closed to new colonisation, with any attempt to extend the European political system to the hemisphere viewed as dangerous to the peace and safety of the United States.

Application

For its first decades the doctrine carried little weight, as the young United States lacked the navy to enforce it; in practice it was underwritten by British sea power, since Britain also wished to keep rival powers out of Latin America. The phrase "Monroe Doctrine" itself was coined only in 1850. The doctrine was invoked during the French intervention in Mexico in the 1860s, and gained new force as the United States grew stronger; in the Olney interpretation of 1895, Secretary of State Richard Olney asserted that the United States was "practically sovereign on this continent".

Corollaries

In 1904 President Theodore Roosevelt added the Roosevelt Corollary, asserting a right of the United States to intervene in Latin American states guilty of "chronic wrongdoing" to pre-empt European intervention — a justification used for interventions in the Caribbean and Central America. The Clark Memorandum of 1928 repudiated the corollary, and under Franklin D. Roosevelt's Good Neighbor policy the United States renounced armed intervention in the region. During the Cold War the doctrine was invoked against communist influence, notably during the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962.

Legacy

The Monroe Doctrine established the Western Hemisphere as a United States sphere of influence and became a defining statement of American foreign policy. Critics in Latin America have long regarded it as a cover for American hegemony, while in the United States it was periodically revived — and in 2013 Secretary of State John Kerry declared "the era of the Monroe Doctrine is over", a statement later walked back by subsequent administrations.
