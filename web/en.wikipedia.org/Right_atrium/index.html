Right atrium

The right atrium is one of the four chambers of the human heart. It receives deoxygenated blood from the systemic circulation and passes it to the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs.

Structure

The right atrium forms the right border of the heart. Deoxygenated blood enters it through three vessels: the superior vena cava, the inferior vena cava, and the coronary sinus (which drains the heart muscle itself). Its internal surface is partly smooth and partly ridged with muscular pectinate muscles, especially in the right auricle (an ear-like appendage). The interatrial septum separating it from the left atrium bears the fossa ovalis, a remnant of the fetal foramen ovale. The sinoatrial node, the heart's natural pacemaker, lies in its wall near the opening of the superior vena cava.

Function

During diastole the right atrium fills with venous blood; atrial contraction then tops up the right ventricle through the open tricuspid valve. Pressure in the right atrium — central venous pressure — is an important clinical measurement of blood volume and heart function.

Clinical significance

Enlargement of the right atrium occurs with tricuspid valve disease, pulmonary hypertension and congenital defects such as atrial septal defect. The chamber is also commonly involved in atrial fibrillation and is the usual site for pacemaker and central-line access to the heart.
