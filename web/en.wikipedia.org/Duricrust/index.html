Duricrust

A duricrust is a hard layer on or near the surface of soil, typically a few millimetres to several metres thick, formed by the accumulation and cementation of minerals such as iron oxides, aluminium oxides, silica, or calcium carbonate from soil solutions.

Formation

Duricrusts form in landscapes subject to seasonal wetting and drying, where dissolved minerals are drawn upward by capillary action during dry periods and precipitated at or near the surface. Over long periods this process produces an indurated crust that resists erosion.

Types

Common types include laterite (iron-rich), bauxite (aluminium-rich), silcrete (silica-cemented), calcrete (calcium carbonate) and gypcrete (gypsum). Ferricrete and alcrete are further varieties distinguished by their dominant cementing agents.

Geomorphology

Because duricrusts protect underlying material, they often cap plateaus and mesas, producing relief inversion in which former valley floors stand as high ground. They are especially widespread in Australia, Africa, India and South America, and are important in interpreting ancient climates and landscape history.
