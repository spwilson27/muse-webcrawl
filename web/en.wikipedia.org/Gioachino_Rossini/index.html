Gioachino Rossini

Gioachino Antonio Rossini (29 February 1792 – 13 November 1868) was an Italian composer who gained fame for his 39 operas, particularly his comic operas. Born in Pesaro to a musical family, he set new standards for both comic and serious opera and was one of the most popular composers of his era, nicknamed "the Italian Mozart".

His best-known works include the comic operas The Barber of Seville (1816) and La Cenerentola (1817), and the French-language epic William Tell (1829), whose overture — with its famous fast concluding section — became one of the most recognisable pieces in classical music. Other major works include L'italiana in Algeri (1813), Il turco in Italia (1814), Otello (1816), Semiramide (1823), and Le comte Ory (1828).

Life and career

Rossini's first opera was performed in Venice in 1810, when he was 18. By his early twenties he was famous across Italy, and between 1815 and 1822 he was based in Naples, composing for the Teatro di San Carlo. He then worked in Vienna, London, and Paris, where he became director of the Théâtre-Italien and later wrote operas for the Paris Opéra.

After William Tell in 1829, at the age of 37, Rossini largely withdrew from opera composition — a famous early "retirement" attributed to ill health, creative exhaustion, and disputes over his contract and pension with the French government. In his later years he composed sacred music, including the Stabat Mater (1841) and the Petite messe solennelle (1864), along with the witty piano pieces he called Péchés de vieillesse ("Sins of Old Age"). He died in Passy, near Paris, in 1868 and was initially buried in Père Lachaise Cemetery; his remains were moved to the Basilica of Santa Croce in Florence in 1887.

Style and legacy

Rossini is celebrated for his melodic invention, rhythmic energy, ensemble writing, and the trademark "Rossini crescendo". He was also a renowned gourmet; dishes such as Tournedos Rossini are named after him. His birthplace of Pesaro hosts the annual Rossini Opera Festival.
