Irreducible polynomial

Field: Algebra

Related concepts: Irreducible element, factorisation, finite fields

In mathematics, an irreducible polynomial is, roughly speaking, a non-constant polynomial that cannot be factored into the product of two non-constant polynomials. The property of irreducibility depends on the coefficient field over which the polynomial is considered. For example, the polynomial x2 − 2 is irreducible over the rational numbers but reducible over the real numbers.

Definition

A polynomial P in one variable over a field F (or, more generally, a unique factorisation domain R) is called irreducible if it is not a constant, is not a unit, and whenever P = Q·S with Q, S in F[x], either Q or S is a constant. Over a field, every polynomial of degree one is irreducible. Polynomial rings over fields are unique factorisation domains, so every non-constant polynomial factorises uniquely into irreducible factors.

Examples and tests

Over the complex numbers, the fundamental theorem of algebra implies that the irreducible polynomials are exactly the linear ones. Over the reals, irreducibles have degree at most two. Over a finite field, irreducible polynomials of every degree exist and are used to construct field extensions — for example, AES cryptography uses the irreducible polynomial x8 + x4 + x3 + x + 1 over GF(2). Eisenstein's criterion gives a sufficient condition for irreducibility over the rationals: if a prime p divides all coefficients except the leading one, does not divide the leading coefficient, and p2 does not divide the constant term, the polynomial is irreducible over ℚ.
