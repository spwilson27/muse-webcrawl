Jacques Charles

Born12 November 1746, Beaugency, France
Died7 April 1823 (aged 76), Paris, France

Jacques Alexandre César Charles (12 November 1746 – 7 April 1823) was a French inventor, scientist, mathematician and balloonist. He is remembered for Charles's law, which describes how gases expand when heated, and for launching the first hydrogen-filled balloon in 1783 and making the first manned hydrogen balloon flight days later.

Ballooning

When the Montgolfier brothers demonstrated their hot-air balloon in 1783, Charles — working with the instrument makers Anne-Jean and Nicolas-Louis Robert — developed a rival design filled with hydrogen, produced by pouring acid over iron filings. The unmanned balloon, launched from the Champ de Mars in Paris on 27 August 1783, travelled about 21 km before terrified villagers destroyed it. On 1 December 1783 Charles and Nicolas-Louis Robert ascended from the Tuileries Garden before a huge crowd, flew for over two hours, and landed some 36 km away; Charles then rose alone a second time to about 3,000 m, becoming the first person to fly solo in a hydrogen balloon. Gas balloons of this type were known as "Charlières", in contrast to hot-air "Montgolfières".

Charles's law

Around 1787 Charles observed that oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen and carbon dioxide all expand by the same proportion over the same temperature increase — that the volume of a gas at constant pressure is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. He never published the result; it was announced by Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac in 1802, who credited Charles, and the law now bears Charles's name.

Later career

Charles lectured on experimental physics with demonstrations that drew large audiences, improved instruments such as the hydrometer and reflecting goniometer, and was elected to the Académie des Sciences in 1785. He largely abandoned ballooning after 1783 — reportedly vowing never to fly again after ear pain during his rapid second ascent — and lived out the French Revolution quietly in Paris.
