Deterrence theory

Deterrence theory is a body of thought in international relations and criminology holding that an action can be prevented by the threat of unacceptable retaliation or punishment. In military strategy it became the organizing principle of the nuclear age: a state deters attack by maintaining a credible capability to inflict devastating damage in response, even after absorbing a first strike. Bernard Brodie wrote in 1946 that the chief purpose of military establishments must shift from winning wars to averting them.

Nuclear deterrence

Key concepts include the second-strike capability — the ability to retaliate after being attacked, protected by submarine-launched missiles and hardened silos — and mutually assured destruction (MAD), under which both sides' vulnerability paradoxically stabilizes the peace. Thomas Schelling's The Strategy of Conflict (1960) formalized deterrence as bargaining, emphasizing credibility, commitment and communication, and introduced ideas such as the threat that "leaves something to chance". Deterrence by punishment (threatening retaliation) is distinguished from deterrence by denial (defences that make attack fail). Critics question whether leaders behave as the rational calculators the theory assumes, and whether deterrence can be extended reliably to allies ("extended deterrence") or against non-state actors and cyber attacks.

Criminological deterrence

In criminal law, deterrence is one justification of punishment, descending from Cesare Beccaria and Jeremy Bentham. Research summarized by the U.S. National Research Council and criminologists such as Daniel Nardo finds that the certainty of punishment deters far more than its severity, a result with major implications for policing and sentencing policy.
