Hans Joachim Morgenthau (February 17, 1904 – July 19, 1980) was a German-American political scientist and historian, considered one of the most influential figures in the study of international relations in the twentieth century. He is best known as the founder of the classical realist school of international politics, which holds that states act primarily in pursuit of power and national interest.

Life and career

Morgenthau was born in Coburg, Germany, and studied law and philosophy at the universities of Berlin, Frankfurt, and Munich. He practiced law in Frankfurt and taught at the University of Geneva before fleeing Nazi Germany. After periods in Spain and Switzerland, he emigrated to the United States in 1937. He taught at Brooklyn College, the University of Kansas City, and, from 1949, at the University of Chicago, where he became one of the leading voices in the new discipline of international relations.

Politics Among Nations

His 1948 book Politics Among Nations set out six principles of political realism, including the claims that politics is governed by objective laws rooted in human nature, that interest is defined in terms of power, and that the moral significance of political action cannot be separated from its consequences. Morgenthau criticized what he saw as the naive legalism and moralism of interwar idealism and argued that statesmen must think in terms of interests and prudence. Later in his career he became a prominent critic of the Vietnam War, arguing that it misread American interests.

Legacy

Morgenthau's work shaped generations of scholars and policymakers. Although later challenged by structural realism and constructivism, his emphasis on power, interest, and the tragedy of politics remains a reference point in the field. Related topics include realism in international relations and the work of his contemporary Harold Lasswell.
