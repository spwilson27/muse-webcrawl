Opera

Opera
Origin: Florence, c. 1600
Components: Music, drama, staging, dance
Key forms: Aria, recitative, overture, chorus

Opera is a form of theatre in which music is a fundamental component and dramatic roles are taken by singers. Such a "work" combines a text (the libretto) with a musical score, and is typically performed in an opera house accompanied by an orchestra or smaller ensemble. Unlike spoken theatre, opera's drama is conveyed primarily through singing — in solos (arias), ensembles and choruses — interspersed with recitative.

History

Opera emerged around 1600 in Florence, where the circle known as the Florentine Camerata sought to revive Greek drama. Jacopo Peri's Dafne (1598) and Euridice (1600) were among the first; Claudio Monteverdi's L'Orfeo (1607) is the earliest opera still regularly performed. The first public opera house opened in Venice in 1637. In the Baroque era, Handel dominated London; the 18th century brought Gluck's reforms and Mozart's masterpieces (Don Giovanni, The Marriage of Figaro). The 19th century was the age of Italian bel canto (Rossini, Donizetti, Bellini), of Verdi, and of Wagner, whose "music dramas" and use of leitmotif transformed the art. Puccini carried Italian opera into the 20th century (La bohème, Tosca, Madama Butterfly).

Elements

Singers are classified by voice type — soprano, mezzo-soprano, contralto, tenor, baritone and bass — and in earlier centuries castrati took leading roles. Works are structured in acts and scenes, often beginning with an overture. National schools developed distinct styles, including French grand opera, German Singspiel and Romantic opera, Russian opera, and later forms such as verismo and operetta.

Today

Major houses such as La Scala, the Metropolitan Opera, the Royal Opera House and the Vienna State Opera anchor an international repertoire of a few hundred core works, supplemented by new commissions and cinema broadcasts. Opera also has regional cousins, including Chinese opera forms that developed independently.
