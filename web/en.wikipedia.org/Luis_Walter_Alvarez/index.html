Luis Walter Alvarez (13 June 1911 – 1 September 1988) was an American experimental physicist, inventor and professor, winner of the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1968 for his work on the hydrogen bubble chamber and the discovery of resonance states in particle physics. A restless polymath, he contributed to radar and the atomic bomb during the Second World War, helped discover cosmic-ray physics landmarks as a young man, and late in life co-authored the hypothesis that an asteroid impact caused the extinction of the dinosaurs.

Early work

Alvarez was born in San Francisco, the son of Walter C. Alvarez, a prominent Mayo Clinic physician, and grandson of a Spanish journalist. He earned his degrees at the University of Chicago (PhD 1936) and joined Ernest Lawrence's Radiation Laboratory at Berkeley. With Felix Bloch he made the first measurement of the magnetic moment of the neutron (1940); he demonstrated the east–west asymmetry of cosmic rays and observed K-electron capture. During the war he worked at MIT's Radiation Laboratory on radar — developing the Ground Controlled Approach blind-landing system — then at Los Alamos on the Manhattan Project, where he devised exploding-bridgewire detonators and flew as scientific observer on the mission that accompanied the Hiroshima bombing.

Bubble chambers and the Nobel Prize

After the war Alvarez built at Berkeley a succession of liquid-hydrogen bubble chambers — culminating in the 72-inch chamber — coupled with high-speed film scanning and computerised measurement (including the "Franckenstein" measuring machines). The programme produced a flood of new particles and resonance states in the 1950s–60s and effectively created "big science" particle physics; Alvarez received the 1968 Nobel Prize "for the discovery of a large number of resonance states, made possible through his development of the technique of using hydrogen bubble chamber and data analysis".

Other adventures

Alvarez used muon tomography in 1965–69 to search for hidden chambers in Egypt's Pyramid of Khafre (none were found). He analysed the Zapruder film of the Kennedy assassination. Most famously, with his son, geologist Walter Alvarez, and chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Michel, he reported in 1980 an anomalous iridium layer at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary and proposed that a ~10 km asteroid impact caused the end-Cretaceous mass extinction — the Alvarez hypothesis, later strongly supported by the discovery of the Chicxulub crater in Mexico. He died of complications after surgery for esophageal cancer in 1988; his memoir Alvarez: Adventures of a Physicist appeared in 1987.
