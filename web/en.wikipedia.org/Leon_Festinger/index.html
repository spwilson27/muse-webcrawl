Leon Festinger

Leon Festinger (8 May 1919 – 11 February 1989) was an American social psychologist who originated the theory of cognitive dissonance and social comparison theory. The rejection of the previously dominant behaviourist view of social psychology by demonstrating the inadequacy of the stimulus-response accounting of human behaviour is attributed to Festinger. Festinger is also credited with advancing the use of laboratory experimentation in social psychology, although he simultaneously noted its limitations. In a Review of General Psychology survey published in 2002, Festinger was ranked as the fifth most cited psychologist of the 20th century.

Life and career

Festinger was born in New York City to Russian Jewish immigrants. He studied psychology at the City College of New York and completed his doctorate at the University of Iowa in 1942 under Kurt Lewin, a founder of modern social psychology. He worked with Lewin's Research Center for Group Dynamics at MIT and the University of Michigan, and later taught at the University of Minnesota, Stanford University, and the New School for Social Research.

Cognitive dissonance

Festinger's best-known work is his theory of cognitive dissonance, set out in A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance (1957). The theory holds that inconsistency among cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, knowledge of one's behaviour) produces an aversive state of dissonance that motivates people to reduce it, for example by changing beliefs or rationalising behaviour. His earlier book When Prophecy Fails (1956, with Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter) described a flying-saucer cult whose failed prophecy led members, paradoxically, to proselytise more fervently — a founding case study of dissonance. The famous induced-compliance experiment with J. Merrill Carlsmith (1959) showed that participants paid only $1 to lie about a boring task rated it more enjoyable than those paid $20.

Social comparison and other work

Festinger's social comparison theory (1954) proposed that people evaluate their opinions and abilities by comparing themselves with others, especially similar others. He also developed theories of informal social communication and group cohesion, and studied the "proximity effect" in housing — friendships form disproportionately with physically near neighbours. Later in his career he turned to perception, archaeology and the history of science; his final book, The Human Legacy (1983), ranged widely over human prehistory.
