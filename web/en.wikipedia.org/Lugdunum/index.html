Lugdunum

The ancient theatre of Lugdunum at Fourvière

Location: Modern Lyon, France

Founded: 43 BC

Founder: Lucius Munatius Plancus

Region: Gallia Lugdunensis

Lugdunum (also spelled Lugudunum, modern Lyon) was an important Roman city in Gaul, established on the site of a Celtic settlement at the confluence of the Rhône and Saône rivers, on the hill of Fourvière. Founded in 43 BC by Lucius Munatius Plancus, it became the capital of the Roman province of Gallia Lugdunensis and one of the most significant cities in the western Roman Empire.

History

Lugdunum was founded as a colony for Roman refugees expelled from Vienne by the Allobroges. Its strategic position at the junction of two navigable rivers and at the hub of Agrippa's network of Roman roads made it the natural administrative and commercial centre of the Three Gauls. The Sanctuary of the Three Gauls, established in 12 BC, hosted an annual council of Gallic tribes and an altar dedicated to Roma and Augustus.

The city was the birthplace of the emperors Claudius (10 BC) and Caracalla (188 AD). Claudius, whose famous speech advocating the admission of Gallic notables to the Senate is preserved in part on the Lyon Tablet, had himself been born at Lugdunum on the day the altar to Augustus was dedicated. In 197 AD the city was devastated after the Battle of Lugdunum, in which Septimius Severus defeated Clodius Albinus in one of the largest battles ever fought between Roman armies.

Archaeology

Excavations on Fourvière hill have uncovered a great theatre seating some 10,000 spectators, a smaller odeon, baths, temples, and the aqueducts that supplied the city. The Lugdunum museum (formerly the Gallo-Roman Museum of Lyon) displays mosaics, inscriptions, and the bronze Lyon Tablet. Remains of the amphitheatre associated with the Sanctuary of the Three Gauls, where tradition holds that early Christian martyrs such as Saint Blandina suffered in 177 AD, survive on the slopes of Croix-Rousse.
