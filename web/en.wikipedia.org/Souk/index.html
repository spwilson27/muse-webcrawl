A souk (Arabic: سوق, romanized: sūq, also spelled suq, souq, sooq among other variants) is a marketplace or commercial quarter in Middle Eastern and North African cities. Historically, souks were at the centre of city life: beyond trade in food, spices, textiles, metalwork, perfumes and crafts, they were places of social gathering, news exchange and craft production. Many historic souks are covered with vaulted roofs and organised so that each trade has its own street or section.

Etymology

The word derives from the Arabic sūq, meaning market. Related terms include the Aramaic shūqā (street, market) and the Hebrew shuq. In French the word appears as souk, reflecting its use in the Maghreb.

History

Markets existed in the region from antiquity, but the souk took its characteristic form in Islamic cities from the early medieval period onward. Goods arrived by caravan and by sea; the souk typically stood adjacent to the main mosque and was often endowed as a waqf (religious endowment) whose rents funded charitable institutions. Famous examples include the souks of Aleppo (among the largest covered markets in the world before the Syrian civil war), the Khan el-Khalili in Cairo, the medina souks of Fez and Marrakech, and the Grand Bazaar of Istanbul (Kapalıçarşı).

Organisation

Trades were arranged hierarchically: the most prestigious crafts, such as goldsmiths and booksellers, clustered nearest the mosque, while noisier or smellier trades — tanners, dyers, blacksmiths — were placed at the periphery. Prices were traditionally negotiated by haggling. Guilds (asnaf) regulated quality and apprenticeship in many cities.
