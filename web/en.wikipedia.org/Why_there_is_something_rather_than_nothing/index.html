Why there is something rather than nothing

"Why is there something rather than nothing?" is a question about the reason for the existence of anything at all, rather than nothingness. It has been posed by philosophers including Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, Ludwig Wittgenstein, and Martin Heidegger, and is closely related to questions about the existence of God, the origin of the universe, and the principle of sufficient reason.

Leibniz and the principle of sufficient reason

Leibniz argued that nothing happens without a reason why it should be so rather than otherwise. Applying this principle to existence itself, he concluded that the sufficient reason for the universe must lie outside the series of contingent things, in a necessary being — God. His formulation in the Principles of Nature and Grace (1714) is often credited as the first clear statement of the question.

Scientific responses

Some physicists and philosophers argue that physics can address the question. Quantum mechanics permits particles to arise from vacuum fluctuations, leading some, such as Lawrence Krauss in A Universe from Nothing (2012), to propose that a universe could emerge from "nothing." Critics reply that the quantum vacuum is not truly nothing, since it is governed by physical laws and structured by fields and energy.

Criticism of the question

Other thinkers hold that the question may be illegitimate: it presupposes that "nothing" is a possible or default state requiring no explanation, and that existence requires a cause in the same way events within the universe do. David Hume suggested that asking why the whole series of things exists may be answered by explaining each part, while Wittgenstein treated the existence of the world as a mystical fact not expressible in propositions.
