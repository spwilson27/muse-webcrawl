Cinema of Italy

The cinema of Italy comprises the films made in Italy or by Italian filmmakers. Italian cinema has been among the most influential in film history, and Italy is the birthplace of several major movements, most famously Italian neorealism. The industry is centred on Rome, above all the Cinecittà studios, and its festivals include the Venice Film Festival, founded in 1932 and the oldest in the world.

Early cinema and the silent epics

Film arrived in Italy in 1896, and pioneers such as Filoteo Alberini built an industry that boomed before the First World War. Italian silent cinema became famous for spectacular historical epics, including Quo Vadis? (1913) and Giovanni Pastrone's Cabiria (1914), whose scale, sets and camera movements influenced filmmakers worldwide, among them D. W. Griffith. The diva films of the 1910s, starring actresses such as Lyda Borelli, were another trademark.

The Fascist era

Under Mussolini the regime supported cinema as propaganda while encouraging escapist entertainment. The Cinecittà studios opened in 1937, and the Venice festival was founded under government sponsorship. Light romantic comedies — the so-called telefoni bianchi ("white telephone") films — dominated production, alongside state newsreels and historical spectacles.

Neorealism and the golden age

Out of the war's ruins emerged neorealism: Roberto Rossellini's Rome, Open City (1945) and Vittorio De Sica's Bicycle Thieves (1948) redefined world cinema. In the 1950s and 1960s the movement's heirs transformed Italian film into an art-house powerhouse. Federico Fellini moved from neorealist beginnings to the oneiric worlds of La Dolce Vita (1960) and 8½ (1963); Michelangelo Antonioni explored modern alienation in L'Avventura (1960); Luchino Visconti ranged from La Terra Trema (1948) to The Leopard (1963). The popular commedia all'italiana — satirical comedies by Mario Monicelli, Dino Risi and others — flourished alongside auteur cinema.

Genre cinema

Italy also dominated popular genres: the peplum or sword-and-sandal epics of the late 1950s; the spaghetti westerns of Sergio Leone, scored by Ennio Morricone, which reinvented the western with Clint Eastwood's Man with No Name; and the giallo thrillers of Mario Bava and Dario Argento, whose style shaped the horror genre internationally.

Later decades

Economic crises and television competition shrank the industry from the 1970s, but Italian films continued to win international honours: Giuseppe Tornatore's Cinema Paradiso (1988), Roberto Benigni's Life Is Beautiful (1997) and Paolo Sorrentino's The Great Beauty (2013) all won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film. Italy has won more of these awards than any other country.
