Kwame Nkrumah (21 September 1909 – 27 April 1972) was a Ghanaian politician, political theorist, and revolutionary. He served as Prime Minister of the Gold Coast from 1952 until 1957, when it became independent as Ghana, and then as the country's first president from 1960 until he was deposed in a coup in 1966. An influential advocate of Pan-Africanism, Nkrumah was a founding member of the Organization of African Unity.

Early life and education

Nkrumah was born in Nkroful in the British Gold Coast. He trained as a teacher at Achimota School, then travelled to the United States in 1935, earning degrees from Lincoln University and the University of Pennsylvania. In London he helped organise the Fifth Pan-African Congress in Manchester in 1945, alongside figures such as W. E. B. Du Bois and Hastings Banda.

Path to independence

Invited home in 1947 as general secretary of the United Gold Coast Convention, Nkrumah soon broke away to found the Convention People's Party (CPP) in 1949, demanding "self-government now." Imprisoned by the colonial authorities after the "Positive Action" campaign of 1950, he won the 1951 elections from prison and became Leader of Government Business, then Prime Minister. On 6 March 1957 the Gold Coast became Ghana, the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence.

Presidency and overthrow

Nkrumah pursued rapid industrialisation, including the Akosombo Dam and Volta Aluminium project, and promoted African unity abroad. His rule grew increasingly authoritarian: Ghana became a republic in 1960 and a one-party state in 1964. On 24 February 1966, while Nkrumah was on a state visit to China and North Vietnam, the National Liberation Council overthrew his government. He spent the rest of his life in exile in Conakry, Guinea, and died in Bucharest in 1972.

Legacy

Nkrumah was voted Africa's "Man of the Millennium" by BBC listeners in 2000. His writings, including Neo-Colonialism, the Last Stage of Imperialism (1965), remain foundational texts of anti-colonial thought.
