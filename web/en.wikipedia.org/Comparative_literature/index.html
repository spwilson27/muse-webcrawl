Comparative literature

Comparative literature is an academic field dealing with the study of literature and cultural expression across linguistic, national, geographic, and disciplinary boundaries. Comparatists typically work in several languages and study the relationships among literatures — influences, translations, shared themes and genres — as well as literature's relations with other arts and disciplines such as philosophy, history, film, and music.

Literary traditions studied across languages and cultures

History

The discipline took shape in nineteenth-century Europe, influenced by figures such as Goethe, who promoted the idea of Weltliteratur (world literature), and by philologists tracing themes across national traditions. A distinctive "French school" emphasized documented influence and factual connections, while the postwar "American school," associated with René Wellek, favored intrinsic literary analysis and analogy across traditions. Erich Auerbach's Mimesis (1946), written in Istanbul exile, became a founding monument of the field's humanistic ambitions.

Crisis and expansion

Comparative literature has repeatedly debated its own identity — in celebrated (and sometimes anxious) state-of-the-discipline reports — as it expanded beyond a Eurocentric canon to global literatures, and absorbed literary theory, postcolonial studies, and translation studies. Edward Said's Orientalism (1978) grew out of the field, and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's Death of a Discipline (2003) urged a comparatism attentive to the languages and literatures of the Global South.

Practice today

University programs in comparative literature typically require advanced competence in two or more languages and combine close reading with questions of translation, circulation, genre, and media. Its objects of study now range from ancient epic and the transnational novel to cinema, graphic narrative, and digital culture.
