Conservation movement

The conservation movement, also known as nature conservation, is a political, environmental and social movement that seeks to manage and protect natural resources, including animal, plant and habitat conservation, for the future. It pursues the sustainable use of nature by humans together with the preservation of significant areas in a natural state.

Origins

Conservation ideas have roots in nineteenth-century reactions to industrialisation and resource depletion. In the United States the movement crystallised around figures such as John Muir, who championed preservation of wilderness and helped inspire the national parks, and Gifford Pinchot, first chief of the US Forest Service, who advocated the managed, efficient use of forests "for the greatest good of the greatest number". President Theodore Roosevelt placed conservation at the centre of national policy, setting aside vast federal lands. Aldo Leopold's A Sand County Almanac (1949) later articulated a "land ethic" extending moral concern to ecosystems as wholes.

Development

Early conservation focused on forests, soil, water and game; twentieth-century conservation broadened to pollution, endangered species and ecosystem protection, often merging with the wider environmental movement after the 1960s. International organisations such as the IUCN (founded 1948) and WWF (1961) professionalised conservation science and fundraising, and protected-area networks expanded worldwide.

Modern conservation

Contemporary conservation biology combines ecology, genetics and social science to combat biodiversity loss driven by habitat destruction, climate change, invasive species and overexploitation. Debates continue over "fortress conservation" versus community-based approaches, rewilding, and how to reconcile conservation with the livelihoods and rights of Indigenous peoples, whose stewardship is increasingly recognised as central to effective protection.
