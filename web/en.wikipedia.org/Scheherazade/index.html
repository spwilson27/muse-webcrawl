Scheherazade

Scheherazade (Persian: شهرزاد, Šahrzād; from Middle Persian Čehrāzād, "of noble lineage") is the legendary queen, storyteller, and narrator of the frame tale of the Middle Eastern collection of folktales known as One Thousand and One Nights (Alf Layla wa-Layla). A vizier's daughter, she volunteers to marry the murderous king Shahryar and saves her life—and ends his campaign of killing—by telling him a story each night and always breaking off at dawn at a moment of suspense, so that he postpones her execution to hear the ending.

The frame story

King Shahryar, betrayed by his first wife, vows to marry a new bride each day and have her executed the next morning so that no wife can dishonour him. When the supply of brides runs low, Scheherazade, the elder daughter of the royal vizier—well read in history, philosophy, poetry, and medicine—insists on offering herself, over her father's protests, with her sister Dunyazad enlisted to ask for a bedtime story. Each night Scheherazade narrates a tale—of Sindbad, Aladdin (in later versions), Ali Baba, enchanted cities, and tricksters—but stops at daybreak mid-climax. The king, desperate for the conclusion, spares her for another day. After 1,001 nights and stories within stories, during which she has borne the king three sons, Scheherazade asks pardon; the king, cured of his madness and in love with her wisdom and courage, makes her his queen in earnest.

Origins and influence

The figure is thought to descend from Persian sources behind the lost Hazār Afsān ("Thousand Stories"), and the frame-tale device itself is often credited to Indian storytelling traditions. Scheherazade has become an archetype of intelligence and narration as survival, and her name titles Rimsky-Korsakov's symphonic suite Scheherazade (1888), in which a solo violin voice represents her narration. She has been reinterpreted constantly in literature, ballet, film, and feminist scholarship—as victim, strategist, author, and the presiding genius of storytelling itself.
