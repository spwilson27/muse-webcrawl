Friedmann equations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Friedmann equations, also known as the Friedmann–Lemaître or FL equations, are a set of equations in physical cosmology that govern the expansion of space in homogeneous and isotropic models of the universe within the context of general relativity. They were first derived by Alexander Friedmann in 1922 from Einstein's field equations of gravitation for the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric and a fluid with a given mass density and pressure.

The equations

The first Friedmann equation relates the expansion rate, expressed by the Hubble parameter H = ȧ/a, to the density of the universe:

H² = (8πG/3)ρ − kc²/a² + Λc²/3

where a is the scale factor, ρ is the total mass–energy density, k is the spatial curvature, and Λ is the cosmological constant. The second Friedmann equation describes the acceleration of the expansion:

ä/a = −(4πG/3)(ρ + 3p/c²) + Λc²/3

Together with a continuity equation expressing conservation of energy, these determine how a homogeneous, isotropic universe evolves.

Significance

Friedmann's solutions showed that general relativity does not naturally admit a static universe — space itself must expand or contract. Einstein initially resisted this conclusion and introduced the cosmological constant to force a static model, but Edwin Hubble's 1929 observations of galactic redshifts confirmed the expansion the equations predicted. The Friedmann equations remain the mathematical backbone of the Big Bang model and the modern ΛCDM cosmology, in which the cosmological constant accounts for the observed acceleration of the expansion attributed to dark energy.
