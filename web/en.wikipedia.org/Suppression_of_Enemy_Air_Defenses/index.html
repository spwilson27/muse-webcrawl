Suppression of Enemy Air Defenses

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Suppression of Enemy Air Defenses (SEAD, pronounced "seed") is a military mission to neutralise, destroy, or temporarily degrade enemy surface-based air defenses, including radar, surface-to-air missile systems, and anti-aircraft artillery, so that friendly aircraft can operate more safely. The related mission dedicated to physically destroying such systems is called DEAD (Destruction of Enemy Air Defenses).

Methods

SEAD employs anti-radiation missiles such as the AGM-88 HARM, which home on radar emissions, together with electronic warfare and jamming, decoys, precision-guided munitions, and standoff weapons. Merely forcing enemy radars to shut down or restrict their emissions ("suppression") can be nearly as effective as destroying them. Specialised aircraft have included the US Air Force's Wild Weasel units — from Vietnam-era F-105s to F-16s — and the US Navy's EA-6B Prowler and EA-18G Growler electronic-warfare aircraft.

History

The mission emerged during the Vietnam War in response to Soviet-supplied SA-2 missile systems threatening US strike aircraft over North Vietnam; the first Wild Weasel missions flew in 1965. SEAD was central to coalition air operations in the 1991 Gulf War, which dismantled Iraq's integrated air defence system, and to NATO operations over the Balkans and Libya, while difficulties against mobile, emission-disciplined defences — as in Kosovo in 1999 — shaped later doctrine.
