Buddhism in India

Buddhism in India began with the life of Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha, in the 6th to 5th century BC. Born in the Indian subcontinent, Buddhism grew into one of Asia's great religions, spread by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BC, and flourished in India for over a millennium before declining sharply after the 7th century AD and nearly disappearing from its homeland by the 13th century. In the 20th century it experienced a significant revival, particularly through the Navayana movement led by B. R. Ambedkar.

Origins and early spread

After the Buddha's enlightenment at Bodh Gaya and first sermon at Sarnath, monastic communities (sangha) spread across the Gangetic plain. The Third Buddhist Council under Ashoka's patronage and the emperor's missions — including, by tradition, his son Mahinda to Sri Lanka — carried Buddhism across Asia. Great monastic universities such as Nalanda and Vikramashila became centres of learning for monks from across the Buddhist world.

Decline

From the Gupta period onward, royal patronage increasingly favoured Hindu traditions, and devotional Hinduism absorbed much Buddhist practice. Invasions by the Hunas and, later, the destruction of monasteries during the Muslim conquests of northern India in the 12th–13th centuries destroyed the institutional base in the heartland, though Buddhism survived in the Himalayas and the far east of India.

Modern revival

In 1956, B. R. Ambedkar converted to Buddhism at Nagpur along with several hundred thousand followers, mostly Dalits seeking escape from the caste system, founding the Navayana school. The arrival of the 14th Dalai Lama and the Tibetan exile community in 1959 also re-established a Buddhist presence. The 2011 census recorded about 8.4 million Buddhists in India, roughly 0.7% of the population, concentrated in Maharashtra.
