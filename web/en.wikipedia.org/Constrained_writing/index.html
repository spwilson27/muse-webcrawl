Constrained writing

Constrained writing imposes deliberate limits on the writer's choices.

Constrained writing is a literary technique in which the writer is bound by a deliberate restriction that forbids certain things or imposes a pattern on the text. Constraints are used as a spur to creativity: by closing off the obvious choices, the writer is forced into unexpected solutions.

Types of constraint

Among the most common constraints are the lipogram, which omits one or more letters of the alphabet; the palindrome, which reads the same forwards and backwards; the acrostic, in which initial letters spell out a word; and the univocalic, which uses only one vowel. Formal poetic constraints include fixed forms such as the sonnet, the haiku and the sestina, and mathematical constraints such as writing a text whose word lengths correspond to the digits of pi (a "piem").

Oulipo

The most famous practitioners of constrained writing are the members of the French group Oulipo (Ouvroir de littérature potentielle), founded in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais. Georges Perec, the group's most celebrated member, wrote the novel La Disparition (1969) entirely without the letter e, and its mirror image, Les Revenentes, using no vowel other than e. Ernest Vincent Wright's English novel Gadsby (1939) likewise avoided the letter e throughout.

Other notable examples include Walter Abish's Alphabetical Africa, in which the letters permitted expand chapter by chapter, and Christian Bök's Eunoia, whose chapters are each univocalic. Constrained writing has ancient roots, from the lipogrammatic rewritings attributed to Lasus of Hermione to the word squares and acrostics of late antiquity and the Middle Ages.
