CQD

CQD was one of the first distress signals adopted for radio use. It was announced on 7 January 1904 by a circular of the Marconi International Marine Communication Company and became effective on 1 February 1904 for Marconi-operated stations. It consisted of the general call CQ followed by D for "danger" or "distress".

History

Landline telegraph operators had long used CQ to address all stations on a line. The Marconi company added a D to indicate distress. Although sometimes popularly explained as "Come Quick, Danger" or "Come Quick, Distress", these are backronyms; the letters were chosen as a general call plus a distress suffix.

Replacement by SOS

At the International Radiotelegraphic Convention of 1906, Germany proposed the SOS signal, which was simpler and more distinctive in Morse code (··· ––– ···). SOS was adopted internationally in 1908, but CQD remained in use for some years, particularly by British and Marconi operators.

Famous uses

CQD was transmitted during several notable maritime emergencies. In 1909, Jack Binns, the wireless operator of the RMS Republic, sent CQD after the ship collided with the SS Florida, helping to save nearly all aboard. On 15 April 1912, the RMS Titanic's operators sent both CQD and SOS during the sinking.
