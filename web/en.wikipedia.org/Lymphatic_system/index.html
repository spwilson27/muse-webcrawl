Lymphatic system

The lymphatic system, or lymphoid system, is an organ system in vertebrates that is part of the circulatory system and the immune system. It is a network of lymphatic vessels that carry a clear fluid called lymph from the tissues back toward the heart, together with lymphoid organs and tissues such as the lymph nodes, spleen, thymus and tonsils.

Functions

  • Fluid homeostasis. The system returns excess interstitial fluid — about several litres a day that leak from blood capillaries — to the venous circulation, preventing tissue swelling (oedema).
  • Fat absorption. Specialised vessels called lacteals in the small intestine absorb dietary lipids and fat-soluble vitamins and carry them as chyle to the blood.
  • Immune function. Lymph transports antigens and antigen-presenting cells to lymph nodes, where lymphocytes are activated; the lymphoid organs are sites where immune responses are generated and where lymphocytes mature (the thymus for T cells, the bone marrow for B cells).

Structure

Lymph capillaries begin blindly in tissues and converge into larger collecting vessels fitted with one-way valves; flow is driven by skeletal-muscle contraction, breathing movements and vessel-wall contraction rather than by a central pump. Lymph from most of the body drains through the thoracic duct into the left subclavian vein, while the right upper quadrant drains via the right lymphatic duct. Several hundred lymph nodes are distributed along the vessels, often clustered in the neck, armpits and groin.

Clinical significance

Blockage or removal of lymph nodes can cause lymphedema, a chronic swelling of a limb. Cancers of lymphoid tissue include the lymphomas, and many solid cancers spread first through lymphatics to regional nodes, so nodal status is a key part of cancer staging. The parasite Wuchereria bancrofti can obstruct lymph vessels and cause elephantiasis.
