Desktop metaphor

The desktop metaphor is an interface metaphor in which the computer's screen is treated as a user's physical desk, upon which documents and folders can be placed. Windows opened on the screen represent papers or folders on the desk, a "wastebasket" or "recycle bin" receives discarded files, and tools such as calculators, calendars and notepads mimic their desk-top counterparts. The metaphor lets users transfer their knowledge of the physical office to the unfamiliar domain of file systems and programs.

History

The metaphor was pioneered at Xerox PARC in the 1970s, most fully in the Xerox Star (1981), which presented documents, folders, filing cabinets and an "in-basket" on a bit-mapped display operated by a mouse. Alan Kay's work at PARC on overlapping windows fed into the Alto and subsequent systems. Apple popularized the concept with the Lisa (1983) and Macintosh (1984); Microsoft followed with Windows, and the desktop became the dominant organizing scheme of personal computing through the 1980s and 1990s, appearing also in environments such as GNOME and KDE on Linux.

Elements

Conventional elements include the desktop itself, icons, files and folders (directories), drag-and-drop manipulation, a trash/recycle container, a clipboard, and desk accessories. The windows, icons, menus and pointer combination is often called the WIMP interface.

Criticism and successors

Critics note that the metaphor constrains design: physical desks cannot search their contents instantly, scale to millions of documents, or exist in multiple places at once, and skeuomorphic imitations of leather and paper have been mocked as clutter. Mobile operating systems largely abandoned the desktop in favour of grids of app icons and full-screen applications, and commentators periodically predict a "post-desktop" era. Nevertheless, the desktop metaphor remains the default interface of macOS, Windows and Linux desktops.
