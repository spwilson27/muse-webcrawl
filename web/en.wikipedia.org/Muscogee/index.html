The Muscogee, also known as the Creek, are a group of related Indigenous peoples of the Southeastern Woodlands of the United States. Their autonym is Mvskoke. Historically they formed the Creek Confederacy, a loose alliance of towns speaking Muscogee and other languages across present-day Alabama, Georgia, and northern Florida. Today the largest Muscogee government is the federally recognised Muscogee Nation, headquartered in Okmulgee, Oklahoma, with roughly 100,000 enrolled citizens.

History

Muscogee ancestors were part of the Mississippian culture, building mound towns along the rivers of the Southeast. In the colonial era the Creeks became central trading partners of the British, French, and Spanish, playing the empires against one another. The Creek War of 1813–14 — partly a civil war between the traditionalist Red Sticks and accommodationist factions — ended with Andrew Jackson's victory at Horseshoe Bend and the loss of some 23 million acres of Creek land. Despite adopting farming, a written constitution, and centralised government, the Muscogee were forcibly removed to Indian Territory (Oklahoma) in the 1830s under the Indian Removal Act, on a journey remembered as part of the Trail of Tears.

Government and culture

The Muscogee Nation operates under a constitution adopted in 1979, with an elected Principal Chief, Second Chief, and National Council. Tribal towns (tvlofvt) remain the foundation of Muscogee society, and many maintain ceremonial grounds where the annual Green Corn Ceremony (Busk) renews the community. The Muscogee language, a member of the Muskogean family, is taught in immersion and community programs, though fluent first-language speakers are few.

Related communities

Descendant communities include the Poarch Band of Creek Indians in Alabama, the Seminole peoples of Oklahoma and Florida (who trace much of their origin to Muscogee towns), and Muscogee communities in Texas and elsewhere.
