A haka performance

Haka are a variety of ceremonial dances in Māori culture. A performance art involving dance accompanied by a strong chant, haka are traditionally performed as part of specific events and are a formal part of Māori protocol. They involve vigorous movements—stamping feet, rhythmic body movements—and are accompanied by facial expressions including pūkana (widening the eyes) and whetero (protruding the tongue), intended to challenge opponents and honour guests, ancestors, or achievements.

History and uses

Haka originated as war dances performed by warriors before battle to intimidate the enemy and raise their own spirits, but the repertoire is far broader: haka are performed at welcomes (pōwhiri), weddings, funerals (tangihanga), school and community events, and formal occasions of state. The most famous haka, "Ka Mate," was composed around 1820 by the Ngāti Toa chief Te Rauparaha after escaping his enemies; legislation in 2014 formally acknowledged Ngāti Toa's authorship as part of a Treaty of Waitangi settlement.

In sport

Internationally, the haka is best known through New Zealand sport—the All Blacks rugby team has performed one before matches since the early 20th century (institutionalised on the 1905 "Originals" tour), alternating "Ka Mate" with "Kapa o Pango," composed for the team in 2005. Other New Zealand national teams, including the Black Ferns and the basketball Tall Blacks, also perform haka. The practice occasionally provokes controversy over opponents' responses and over commercial use of haka by advertisers, which Māori regard as a cultural treasure (taonga) requiring respect.
