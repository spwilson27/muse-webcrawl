Sequence (musical form)

TypeMusical form / compositional device
DomainMusic theory, Gregorian chant, Baroque music
Related termsMotif, repetition, transposition, sequence (liturgy)
Notable usersNotker Balbulus, Antonio Vivaldi, J. S. Bach

In music, a sequence is the restatement of a melodic or harmonic pattern at a higher or lower pitch level. It is one of the most common and simple methods of elaborating a musical idea, because it takes a short fragment and develops it by repetition at different pitch levels, producing a sense of direction and momentum.

The term also has an older, distinct meaning in medieval music: a sequence (Latin: sequentia) was a chant sung in the Roman Catholic Mass after the Alleluia and before the Gospel, consisting of paired verses sung to the same melody. Famous examples include the Dies Irae, Stabat Mater, Lauda Sion and Veni Sancte Spiritus. This article covers both senses of the term.

Melodic and harmonic sequence

A melodic sequence repeats a motive at a consistent interval, most often a second or a third higher or lower. When the repetition occurs at the same pitch with only rhythmic change it is sometimes called an imitation or a literal repeat; a true sequence always involves transposition. Harmonic sequences repeat a chord progression, such as the descending-fifths sequence (a chain of chords each a fifth below the previous), which underpins much Baroque and Classical harmony, including the famous progression in Pachelbel's Canon.

Sequences are classified as ascending or descending, and as tonal (staying within the key, adjusting intervals) or real (exact transposition, possibly leaving the key). Sequences were a workhorse of Baroque composers such as Antonio Vivaldi and Johann Sebastian Bach, and remain fundamental to popular music, jazz improvisation and film scoring.

The liturgical sequence

The liturgical sequence originated in the 9th century, traditionally credited to Notker Balbulus of the Abbey of Saint Gall, who added texts to the long melismas of the Alleluia chant. Hundreds of sequences were composed during the Middle Ages, sung on feast days. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) drastically reduced the repertoire, retaining only four (Victimae Paschali Laudes, Veni Sancte Spiritus, Lauda Sion and Dies Irae); the Stabat Mater was restored in 1727. Structurally, most sequences consist of a series of paired versicles (aa bb cc ...) in which each melody is sung twice to different texts, a form that influenced later hymnody.
