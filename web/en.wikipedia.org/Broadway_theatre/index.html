Broadway theatre, or a Broadway show, refers to a theatrical performance in one of the 41 professional theatres, each with 500 or more seats, in the Theater District and at Lincoln Center along Broadway in Midtown Manhattan, New York City. Broadway, and London's West End, together represent the highest commercial level of live theatre in the English-speaking world.

History

New York's theatre district migrated uptown to the area around Times Square (Longacre Square) around 1900, as theatres followed cheaper land and the new subway. The district flourished in the 1920s, survived the Depression and the competition of cinema, and was renewed from the 1980s as falling crime and investment revived Times Square. Musicals became Broadway's signature product, from Oklahoma! (1943) through the works of Rodgers and Hammerstein, Sondheim, and Andrew Lloyd Webber, to Hamilton (2015).

The industry

Broadway productions are commercial ventures mounted by producers, governed by contracts with unions including Actors' Equity. Shows are classified as musicals or plays, open-ended runs or limited engagements. The Broadway League reports grosses and attendance; in strong recent seasons Broadway has grossed around $1.5–1.8 billion annually and drawn some 12–14 million attendees, most of them tourists. The annual Tony Awards, founded in 1947, honour excellence in Broadway theatre.

Off-Broadway and beyond

Smaller professional houses (100–499 seats) are "Off-Broadway", and still smaller ones "Off-Off-Broadway" — tiers that began as alternatives to Broadway's commercialism and became vital incubators of new work; many Broadway hits, including Hair, A Chorus Line and Hamilton, began Off-Broadway.
