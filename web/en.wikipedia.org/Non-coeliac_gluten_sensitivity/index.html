Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (NCGS), also called gluten sensitivity, is a proposed condition in which ingestion of gluten-containing foods causes intestinal and extraintestinal symptoms in people in whom coeliac disease and wheat allergy have been excluded. Reported symptoms include abdominal pain, bloating, altered bowel habit, headache, fatigue, "brain fog", joint pain and skin rashes. Interest in the condition grew in the 2010s alongside the popularity of gluten-free diets.

Diagnosis

NCGS remains controversial and lacks validated biomarkers. It is a diagnosis of exclusion: coeliac serology and duodenal biopsy (while on a gluten-containing diet) and tests for wheat allergy must be negative. Expert consensus documents, such as the Salerno criteria, propose confirmation with a double-blind, placebo-controlled gluten challenge — which is impractical in routine care, so most diagnoses rest on symptom improvement on a gluten-free diet and recurrence on reintroduction.

Possible mechanisms

Whether gluten itself is the trigger is unresolved. Double-blind trials have produced mixed results, and other wheat components are candidate culprits, including amylase-trypsin inhibitors (ATIs), which activate innate immunity, and fructans — FODMAP carbohydrates that cause symptoms through fermentation and osmotic effects, especially in people with irritable bowel syndrome. Some researchers therefore prefer terms such as "non-coeliac wheat sensitivity". There is no evidence that NCGS causes the intestinal damage or autoimmune complications of coeliac disease, and the strictness of dietary avoidance required is correspondingly less absolute, though symptomatic benefit guides individual management.
