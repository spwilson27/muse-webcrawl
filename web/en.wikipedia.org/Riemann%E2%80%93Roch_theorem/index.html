Riemann–Roch theorem

The Riemann–Roch theorem is an important theorem in mathematics, specifically in complex analysis and algebraic geometry, for the computation of the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions with prescribed zeros and allowed poles. It relates the complex analysis of a connected compact Riemann surface with the surface's purely topological genus g, in a way that can be carried over into purely algebraic settings.

Initially proved as Riemann's inequality by Bernhard Riemann in the 1850s, the theorem reached its definitive form for algebraic curves after the work of Riemann's student Gustav Roch in 1865. It was later generalised to algebraic surfaces and to higher dimensions.

Statement for Riemann surfaces

For a divisor D on a compact Riemann surface X of genus g, the theorem states:

l(D) − l(K − D) = deg(D) − g + 1,

where l(D) is the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions whose divisor is greater than or equal to −D, and K is the canonical divisor. Riemann's inequality gives l(D) ≥ deg(D) − g + 1; Roch's contribution identified the correction term l(K − D).

Consequences

The theorem implies, among other things, that a Riemann surface of genus 0 is the Riemann sphere, that every compact Riemann surface can be embedded in projective space, and it yields the genus formula for plane curves. It also underlies the computation of dimensions of spaces of modular forms.

Generalisations

The theorem was generalised by Friedrich Hirzebruch (the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem, 1954), Alexander Grothendieck (the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem, 1957), and Michael Atiyah and Isadore Singer (the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, 1963), each step carrying the result into broader and more abstract settings.
