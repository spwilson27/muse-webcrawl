The Doppler effect (also Doppler shift) is the change in the frequency of a wave in relation to an observer who is moving relative to the wave source. It is named after the Austrian physicist Christian Doppler, who described the phenomenon in 1842. A common example is the change of pitch heard when a vehicle sounding a siren approaches and recedes: the pitch is higher during the approach and lower during the recession.

Explanation

When a source moves toward an observer, each successive wave crest is emitted from a position closer to the observer, so the waves are compressed and the observed frequency increases. When the source recedes, the crests are stretched apart and the frequency decreases. For waves in a medium such as sound, the effect depends on the velocities of source and observer relative to the medium; for light and other electromagnetic waves, which need no medium, only the relative motion matters, and the relativistic Doppler effect includes time dilation.

Applications

The Doppler effect underlies Doppler radar used in weather forecasting and speed enforcement, medical ultrasound imaging of blood flow, and laser Doppler velocimetry. In astronomy, the Doppler shift of spectral lines produces redshift for objects moving away and blueshift for those approaching; measurements of redshift revealed the expansion of the universe and are used to detect exoplanets by the radial-velocity method. Satellites and spacecraft navigation, including the Hubble effect in cosmology, likewise depend on the shift.

If the source moves faster than the wave speed, a shock wave forms — the sonic boom of a supersonic aircraft — and the analogous phenomenon for charged particles in a medium is Cherenkov radiation.
