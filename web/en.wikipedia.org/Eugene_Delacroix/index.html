Eugène Delacroix

Born: 26 April 1798, Charenton-Saint-Maurice, France
Died: 13 August 1863 (aged 65), Paris, France
Movement: Romanticism

Ferdinand Victor Eugène Delacroix (26 April 1798 – 13 August 1863) was a French Romantic artist regarded from the outset of his career as the leader of the French Romantic school. In contrast to the Neoclassical perfectionism of his chief rival Ingres, Delacroix took for his inspiration the art of Rubens and painters of the Venetian Renaissance, with an emphasis on colour and movement rather than clarity of outline and classical form.

Major works

Delacroix's first major painting, The Barque of Dante (1822), was accepted into the Paris Salon. His Massacres at Chios (1824) and The Death of Sardanapalus (1827) established his Romantic reputation. His most famous work, Liberty Leading the People (1830), commemorating the July Revolution, shows a bare-breasted woman personifying Liberty leading combatants over the barricades.

A trip to Morocco in 1832 profoundly affected his later work, inspiring more than a hundred paintings of North African life, including Women of Algiers in their Apartment (1834). He also received major decorative commissions, including murals in the Palais Bourbon and the Louvre.

Legacy

Delacroix's expressive brushstrokes and study of the optical effects of colour profoundly shaped the work of the Impressionists and later modern artists. His journals are a celebrated record of a painter's life and thought.
