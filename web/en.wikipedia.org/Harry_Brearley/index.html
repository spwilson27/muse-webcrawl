Harry Brearley, metallurgist

Harry Brearley (18 February 1871 – 12 July 1948) was an English metallurgist credited with the invention of stainless steel (he called it "rustless steel") in Sheffield in 1913, a discovery that transformed cutlery, engineering, and architecture.

Life and career

Born into a Sheffield steelworking family, Brearley left school at twelve to work as a cellar labourer in a steelworks, studying metallurgy at night and by correspondence. He became an assayer and then works chemist, and in 1908 was appointed to direct the new Brown-Firth research laboratory, jointly funded by two Sheffield steel firms.

Discovery of stainless steel

In 1912–13, investigating erosion and fouling in rifle barrels for an armaments project, Brearley experimented with high-chromium steel alloys. In August 1913 he produced a steel with about 12.8% chromium and 0.24% carbon that resisted corrosion—famously surviving the vinegar test applied when a local cutler, Ernest Stuart of R. F. Mosley, tried etching blades and found they would not stain. Stuart coined the name "stainless steel," and Sheffield cutlery in the new alloy was being advertised during the First World War. Priority disputes followed—similar alloys had been patented in Germany and the United States, and Leon Guillet had described chromium steels earlier—but Brearley's role in recognising and commercialising the material's corrosion resistance is widely acknowledged. He later wrote a memoir, Knotted String, about his self-education.
