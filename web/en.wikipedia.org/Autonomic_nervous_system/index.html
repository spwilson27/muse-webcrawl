Autonomic nervous system

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is a component of the peripheral nervous system that regulates involuntary physiological processes including heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, digestion, and sexual arousal. It contains three anatomically distinct divisions: the sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric nervous systems. It operates largely unconsciously, through a two-neuron chain running from the central nervous system to an autonomic ganglion and then to the target organ.

Divisions

Sympathetic nervous system

The sympathetic division arises from the thoracolumbar spinal cord and prepares the body for action — the "fight or flight" response. It increases heart rate and blood pressure, dilates the bronchi and pupils, diverts blood to skeletal muscles, and inhibits digestion. Postganglionic sympathetic neurons mostly release norepinephrine.

Parasympathetic nervous system

The parasympathetic division arises from the brainstem and sacral spinal cord and promotes "rest and digest" functions: it slows the heart, stimulates gastrointestinal motility and secretion, constricts the pupils, and promotes glandular activity. Its neurons release acetylcholine. The vagus nerve carries the great majority of parasympathetic output.

Enteric nervous system

The enteric nervous system consists of networks of neurons embedded in the wall of the gastrointestinal tract (the myenteric and submucosal plexuses). With hundreds of millions of neurons, it can coordinate gut motility and secretion semi-autonomously, though it is modulated by the other divisions.

Function and control

Central control of the ANS involves the hypothalamus, brainstem nuclei such as the nucleus tractus solitarius, and reflex arcs such as the baroreceptor reflex, which stabilizes blood pressure from moment to moment. Dysfunction of the autonomic system (dysautonomia) can cause orthostatic hypotension, abnormal sweating, gastroparesis, and other disorders.
