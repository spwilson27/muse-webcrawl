Tithe barn

A tithe barn was a type of barn used in much of northern Europe in the Middle Ages for storing rents and tithes — one tenth of a farm's produce, paid to a church, monastery, or rector. Because tithes were paid in kind, usually grain, tithe barns were often very large structures built to hold the produce of an entire parish or monastic estate.

Architecture

Medieval tithe barns were typically built of stone or timber with large threshing floors, opposing wagon doors, and buttressed walls supporting a steeply pitched roof. Outstanding surviving examples in England include the barns at Bradford-on-Avon in Wiltshire, Great Coxwell in Oxfordshire, and Harmondsworth in London, among the largest timber barns in the country. William Morris called the Great Coxwell barn "unapproachable in its dignity".

Later history

With the commutation of tithes to money payments in the 19th century, most tithe barns lost their original function. Many were converted to other agricultural uses, and some survive as museums, theatres, wedding venues, or private houses.
