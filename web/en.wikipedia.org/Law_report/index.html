Law report

A law report or reporter is a series of books that contains judicial opinions from a selection of case law decided by courts. Because common law systems operate on the doctrine of precedent (stare decisis), the publication of written judicial opinions is essential so that lawyers, judges, and the public can know what the law is. When a particular judicial opinion is referenced, the law report in which the opinion is printed is cited alongside the case name, court, and year.

History

The earliest English law reports were the Year Books, compiled from the late 13th century to 1535, which recorded oral pleadings in law French. They were succeeded by privately published "nominate reports," named after their compilers (Plowden, Coke, Burrow, and many others), whose quality varied widely. Sir Edward Coke's Reports were especially influential. Dissatisfaction with the nominate reports led to the creation in 1865 of the Incorporated Council of Law Reporting for England and Wales, which has published the semi-official series simply called The Law Reports ever since, with volumes for the Appeal Cases, Queen's/King's Bench, Chancery, and Family divisions.

In the United States, official reporters were published by federal and state governments — such as the United States Reports for Supreme Court decisions — while the West Publishing Company's National Reporter System (including the Federal Reporter, Federal Supplement, and regional reporters like the Pacific Reporter) made case law from all jurisdictions widely available; much reporting is now electronic through databases such as Westlaw and LexisNexis.

Contents and citation

A reported judgment typically includes a headnote summarizing the facts and holdings, catchwords, the names of counsel, the full text of the judgment(s), and editorial apparatus. Citations take the form of year, volume, report abbreviation, and page or paragraph — for example, a case in the Appeal Cases. Since the early 2000s, courts in many Commonwealth jurisdictions have adopted neutral citations (court-issued identifiers independent of any publisher) and numbered paragraphs so that judgments can be cited precisely in online form, with free access provided by services such as BAILII.
