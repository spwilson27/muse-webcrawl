Immigration to the United States is the international movement of non-U.S. nationals to reside permanently in the country. The United States has historically been the world's leading destination for immigrants: its foreign-born population, about 45 million or more in recent years (roughly 14% of the population), is the largest of any country, and the U.S. is often described as a "nation of immigrants", though Native Americans were displaced by colonisation and many Africans were brought by force through the slave trade.

History

Colonial era to 1820

European colonisation from the 17th century brought English, Dutch, German, Scots-Irish and other settlers, alongside enslaved Africans, who by 1790 made up about a fifth of the population. The Naturalization Act of 1790 limited citizenship to "free white persons".

The great waves, 1820–1924

Mass immigration began in the 1840s with Irish fleeing the Great Famine and Germans fleeing unrest. After the Civil War, industrialisation drew millions from southern and eastern Europe — Italians, Poles, Russian Jews and others — processed after 1892 at Ellis Island. On the West Coast, Chinese migrants came for the Gold Rush and railroad construction until the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 barred them; Japanese and other Asian immigration was later restricted by the Immigration Act of 1917 and the Immigration Act of 1924, which imposed national-origins quotas favouring northern and western Europe and nearly halted immigration from Asia.

1965 to the present

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 (Hart–Celler Act) abolished the national-origins quotas, prioritising family reunification and skilled workers. Immigration subsequently rose and diversified, with the largest flows from Latin America (especially Mexico) and Asia (China, India, the Philippines, Vietnam). The Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986 legalised nearly three million unauthorised immigrants while sanctioning employers. Debates over unauthorised immigration, border enforcement, asylum and visa policy remain central in U.S. politics.

Demographics and effects

In recent decades the largest origin countries of the foreign-born have been Mexico, India, China and the Philippines. Immigrants and their children are credited with major contributions to U.S. population growth, labour force, entrepreneurship and culture, while immigration's effects on wages, public finances and social cohesion remain subjects of scholarly and political debate.
