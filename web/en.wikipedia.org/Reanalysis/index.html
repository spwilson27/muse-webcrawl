Reanalysis

Reanalysis relocates a morpheme boundary without any immediate change in pronunciation.

Reanalysis (also called rebracketing) is a process in historical linguistics in which speakers reinterpret the internal structure of a word or phrase, assigning its sounds to different morphemes than before. The pronunciation of the item is initially unchanged, but the new analysis can later surface in novel formations. Reanalysis is one of the principal mechanisms of morphological change and is closely related to folk etymology and back-formation.

Examples in English

A classic locus of reanalysis is the boundary between a noun and the indefinite article. In Middle English, a napron was resegmented as an apron; conversely, an ewt became a newt, and an ekename ("an additional name") became a nickname. The same process moved the boundary in the opposite direction in a nadder → an adder.

Reanalysis also operates on affixes. The suffix -burger was extracted from hamburger (itself from Hamburg + -er), giving cheeseburger, fishburger and the like; similarly -holic was abstracted from alcoholic, and -gate from Watergate.

Syntactic reanalysis

In syntax, reanalysis occurs when hearers assign a new constituent structure to a string. The development of English modal auxiliaries, and the grammaticalisation of constructions such as be going to from a motion verb plus purpose clause into a future marker, are standard examples. Because the change happens first in the grammar of individual learners, reanalysis is often invisible until a later generation produces forms that only the new structure can generate.

Other fields

The term is also used in meteorology for the retrospective assimilation of historical observations into a consistent model of the atmosphere, as in the ERA5 dataset, and in psychoanalysis for the re-examination of a case. In each usage the common thread is a fresh analysis of material previously understood in a different way.
