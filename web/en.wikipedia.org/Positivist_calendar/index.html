The Positivist calendar was a calendar reform proposal by the French philosopher Auguste Comte in 1849. It formed part of Comte's positivist philosophy and his proposed secular "Religion of Humanity", replacing saints' days with commemorations of great figures from history.

Structure

The calendar divides the year into 13 months of exactly 28 days (four weeks each), giving 364 days, with one additional festival day at the end of the year (and a second in leap years) not belonging to any month or week. Each month is named after a great figure: Moses, Homer, Aristotle, Archimedes, Caesar, Saint Paul, Charlemagne, Dante, Gutenberg, Shakespeare, Descartes, Frederick the Great, and Bichat.

Days and weeks

Within each month, days were dedicated to lesser figures representing the stages of human progress, arranged so that every day of the year commemorated a person. The week retained seven days, named after the Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn and the Sun in the positivist scheme.

Legacy

The calendar was adopted in modified form by the International Fixed Calendar promoted in the 20th century by Moses B. Cotsworth and used internally by the Eastman Kodak Company from 1928 to 1989, though without Comte's commemorative names.
