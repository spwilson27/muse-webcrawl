Multiple independently targetable reentry vehicle

A multiple independently targetable reentry vehicle (MIRV) is an exoatmospheric ballistic missile payload containing several warheads, each capable of being aimed at a different target. A MIRVed missile carries its warheads on a post-boost vehicle, or "bus", which manoeuvres in space and releases each reentry vehicle on its own ballistic trajectory. The concept allowed a single missile to strike multiple targets, dramatically increasing the number of deliverable warheads in an arsenal without increasing the number of launchers.

A test of the LGM-118 Peacekeeper: eight reentry vehicles from one missile strike separate targets.

History

The United States pioneered MIRV technology, motivated by the need to overwhelm Soviet anti-ballistic missile defences around Moscow and to cover more targets as missile accuracy improved. The first MIRVed missile deployed was the Minuteman III in 1970, carrying three warheads; the submarine-launched Poseidon followed with up to fourteen. The Soviet Union deployed its own MIRVed systems, such as the R-36 (SS-18), from the mid-1970s. The most heavily MIRVed US missile, the LGM-118 Peacekeeper, carried ten warheads. China, France, the United Kingdom, India, and Pakistan have also developed or deployed MIRV-capable systems; India tested the technology on its Agni-V missile in 2024 under Mission Divyastra.

Strategic implications

MIRVs were controversial in arms control. Because one attacking MIRVed missile could destroy several enemy missiles in their silos, MIRVed land-based forces created a destabilizing first-strike incentive sometimes called a "use it or lose it" pressure. The START II treaty sought to ban MIRVed ICBMs entirely, though it never entered into force; other agreements instead limited total warhead numbers. Because MIRVs multiply warheads per launcher, they also complicate verification and missile-defence planning, since defenders must engage each reentry vehicle separately.

Design

A MIRV bus carries its own guidance system, small rocket motors for attitude and velocity adjustments, and releases warheads with slight differences in trajectory so that they land in a pattern of separate aim points along and across the missile's ground track. Reentry vehicles are typically accompanied by penetration aids such as decoys and chaff to complicate interception.
