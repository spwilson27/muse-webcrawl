Mead

Mead (/miːd/), also called honey wine or honey beer in some contexts, is an alcoholic beverage created by fermenting honey with water, sometimes with various fruits, spices, grains, or hops. The alcoholic content ranges from about 3.5% ABV to more than 20%. The defining characteristic of mead is that the majority of the drink's fermentable sugar is derived from honey. It may be still, carbonated, or naturally sparkling; dry, semi-sweet, or sweet.

A glass of mead, one of the oldest known alcoholic beverages.

History

Mead may be the oldest known alcoholic beverage. Pottery vessels discovered in Jiahu, China, dating to around 7000 BCE, contained chemical residues of a fermented beverage made of rice, honey, and fruit. Mead was produced in ancient times throughout Europe, Africa, and Asia, and has played an important role in the mythology of many peoples. In Norse mythology, the Mead of Poetry was brewed from the blood of the wise god Kvasir and turned anyone who drank it into a poet or scholar. In Greek mythology, mead was believed to be the drink of the gods — ambrosia.

In medieval Europe, mead was especially popular in northern regions where grapes did not grow well. It was associated with weddings; the word "honeymoon" is sometimes said to derive from the tradition of newlyweds drinking mead for a month after marriage, though this etymology is uncertain.

Production

Mead is produced by diluting honey with water and fermenting the mixture with yeast. Depending on the recipe, fermentation may take from a few weeks to several months, and mead is often aged for a year or more. Modern meaderies produce many styles, including traditional mead (honey only), melomel (with fruit), metheglin (with spices), braggot (with malt), and cyser (with apple juice).

Varieties

  • Melomel — mead made with fruit.
  • Cyser — mead made with apples or apple juice.
  • Pyment — mead made with grapes or grape juice.
  • Braggot — mead made with malted grain, similar to beer.
  • Metheglin — mead made with spices and herbs.
