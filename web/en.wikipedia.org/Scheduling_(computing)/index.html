Scheduling (computing)


Processes moving between ready and waiting queues

In computing, scheduling is the action of assigning resources to perform tasks. The resources may be processors, network links or memory, and the tasks may be threads, processes or data flows. The scheduler is the component — usually part of an operating system — that carries out the scheduling activity, deciding which runnable process executes next and for how long.

Goals

Schedulers balance competing goals: maximising throughput and CPU utilisation, minimising waiting and response times, ensuring fairness between processes, and meeting deadlines in real-time systems. The dispatcher performs the actual context switch to the chosen process.

Scheduling algorithms

Common algorithms include first-come, first-served (FCFS); shortest job next (SJN); priority scheduling; round-robin, which gives each process a fixed time slice; and multilevel queues, which separate processes into classes with different policies. Scheduling may be preemptive, allowing a running task to be interrupted, or non-preemptive (cooperative). Modern general-purpose operating systems use sophisticated variants, such as Linux's Completely Fair Scheduler, that model idealised fair sharing of the processor.

Types

Long-term (job) scheduling admits processes to the system, medium-term scheduling swaps processes in and out of memory, and short-term (CPU) scheduling selects among ready processes many times per second. Scheduling concepts also apply to disk I/O, network packets and job scheduling in clusters and batch systems.
