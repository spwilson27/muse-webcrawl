Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death dependent on iron and characterised by the accumulation of lipid peroxides in cell membranes. It is genetically and biochemically distinct from other forms of cell death such as apoptosis and necrosis. The term was coined in 2012 by Brent Stockwell and colleagues at Columbia University.

Mechanism

Ferroptosis is driven by the iron-catalysed peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids in membrane phospholipids. Under normal conditions, the enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) uses glutathione to detoxify these lipid peroxides. Ferroptosis ensues when this defence fails — for example when the cystine/glutamate antiporter system Xc⁻ is blocked by the compound erastin, starving the cell of cysteine needed for glutathione synthesis, or when GPX4 is directly inhibited by compounds such as RSL3.

Morphology

Cells dying by ferroptosis show distinctive mitochondrial changes under the electron microscope: the mitochondria shrink, their cristae diminish or vanish, and the outer membrane becomes denser. The nucleus, by contrast, remains normal in size without the chromatin condensation typical of apoptosis.

Medical relevance

Ferroptosis has been implicated in degenerative diseases of the brain and kidney, ischaemia–reperfusion injury, and cancer. Because some therapy-resistant cancer cells are highly susceptible to ferroptosis, inducing it is an active strategy in oncology research; conversely, ferroptosis inhibitors such as ferrostatin-1 and iron chelators are studied as protective agents in neurodegeneration.
