Neoclassicism

Neoclassicism
Years activec. 1760 – c. 1830 (and later revivals)
LocationEurope and the Americas
InfluencesClassical antiquity, the Enlightenment, archaeological discoveries at Pompeii and Herculaneum
Key figuresJacques-Louis David, Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, Antonio Canova, Robert Adam, Karl Friedrich Schinkel

Neoclassicism (also spelled Neo-classicism) was a Western cultural movement in the decorative and visual arts, literature, theatre, music and architecture that drew inspiration from the art and culture of classical antiquity. Emerging in the mid-18th century as a reaction against the ornate Rococo style and in step with Enlightenment ideals of reason, order and civic virtue, it dominated European culture from about 1760 to the 1830s, with revivals extending into the 20th century.

Origins

The excavations of Herculaneum (from 1738) and Pompeii (from 1748), the engravings of Piranesi, and the writings of Johann Joachim Winckelmann — who praised the "noble simplicity and quiet grandeur" of Greek art — made antiquity vividly present to European artists. Rome became the movement's first capital, drawing painters and sculptors from across the continent, before its centre of gravity shifted to Paris.

Painting and sculpture

Neoclassical painting favoured clear drawing, sculptural modelling, restrained colour and morally elevated subjects from ancient history and myth. Jacques-Louis David's Oath of the Horatii (1784) became the movement's manifesto, and David went on to shape the visual identity of the French Revolution and Napoleonic Empire. His pupil Ingres carried the style deep into the 19th century. In sculpture, Antonio Canova's marble figures — poised between sensuality and ideal restraint — achieved unmatched fame, alongside Bertel Thorvaldsen in Denmark.

Architecture

Neoclassical architecture revived the temple front, the orders and antique ornament in buildings meant to embody public virtue: Robert Adam's interiors in Britain, Schinkel's Berlin, the Panthéon in Paris, and the civic architecture of the early United States (Jefferson's Virginia State Capitol, modelled on a Roman temple) are characteristic. The style's later, more austere phase is sometimes called Greek Revival.

Legacy

By the 1820s and 1830s Romanticism had overtaken Neoclassicism as the avant-garde, but neoclassical forms remained the default language of official building well into the 20th century and were revived, in stripped form, by totalitarian and democratic regimes alike.
