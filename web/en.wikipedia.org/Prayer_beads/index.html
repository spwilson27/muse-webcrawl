Prayer beads

Prayer beads are a form of beadwork used to count repetitions of prayers, chants, or mantras in many religious traditions. The beads help the practitioner keep track of cycles of prayer without having to count consciously, allowing attention to rest on the prayer itself. The English word "bead" derives from the Old English bede, meaning prayer.

Origins

Counting devices for prayer are ancient. Hindu practice with the japa mala is among the oldest documented uses, dating back many centuries, and stringed beads spread widely through Asia and the Mediterranean. Some scholars trace the concept to third-century Christian desert monks in Egypt, who used knotted ropes and pebbles to count prayers.

By tradition

Hinduism: the japa mala typically has 108 beads (plus a "guru" bead), made of tulsi wood for Vaishnavas or rudraksha seeds for Shaivites, used to repeat mantras such as the Gayatri or the names of deities. Buddhism: malas of 108, 54, or 27 beads are used similarly across Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna traditions; materials include sandalwood, bodhi seeds, and crystal. Roman Catholicism: the rosary comprises five decades (sets of ten Hail Marys) interspersed with Our Fathers, used to meditate on the Mysteries of the life of Christ and Mary; chaplets use other configurations. Eastern Orthodoxy: the prayer rope (komboskini/chotki) of 33, 50, 100, or 300 wool knots is used with the Jesus Prayer. Islam: the misbaha or tasbih of 99 beads (for the names of God) or 33 beads (repeated three times) accompanies dhikr, the remembrance of God. Sikhism and the Baháʼí Faith also employ beads — Baháʼís use 95 beads for the daily repetition of the Greatest Name.

Materials and structure

Beads are made from wood, seeds, bone, coral, amber, glass, stone, and precious metals, often with a tassel, cross, pendant, or guru bead marking the start and end of a circuit. The number of beads usually has symbolic significance within each tradition.
