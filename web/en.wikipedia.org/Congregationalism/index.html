Congregationalism

Congregationalism is a tradition of Protestant Christianity that arose in England in the late 16th and early 17th centuries. Its defining feature is congregational polity: each local congregation is independent and autonomous, governing its own affairs under the headship of Christ. Congregationalists, historically called Independents, emerged from Puritanism alongside Separatists and Presbyterians during the English Reformation.

History

Early Congregational theorists such as Robert Browne, Henry Barrow, and John Greenwood argued that true churches were voluntary gatherings of visible saints covenanted together. Persecution drove many to the Netherlands, and in 1620 Separatist "Pilgrims" founded Plymouth Colony in New England, followed by Puritan Congregationalists in Massachusetts Bay. Congregational churches were established by law in much of New England until disestablishment in the early 19th century. The Savoy Declaration of 1658 adapted the Westminster Confession for Congregational use.

In the United States, Congregationalists were central to the founding of colleges such as Harvard and Yale and to missionary and abolitionist movements. Through a series of mergers, most American Congregational churches joined the United Church of Christ in 1957; others continued in the Conservative Congregational Christian Conference or the National Association of Congregational Christian Churches. In England, Congregational churches united with Presbyterians in 1972 to form the United Reformed Church.

Beliefs and practice

Congregationalists historically hold Reformed theology with an emphasis on the covenant, the authority of Scripture, and the autonomy of the local church. Worship centres on preaching, prayer, and congregational singing, and the sacraments (or ordinances) of baptism and the Lord's Supper are observed, with infant baptism commonly practised in the historic Congregational tradition.
