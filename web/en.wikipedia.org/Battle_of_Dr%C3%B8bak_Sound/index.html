Battle of Drøbak Sound

Date9 April 1940
LocationDrøbak Sound, Oslofjord, Norway
ResultNorwegian victory — German naval force repelled; sinking of Blücher
BelligerentsNorway vs. Nazi Germany
CommandersCol. Birger Eriksen (Oscarsborg Fortress) vs. Adm. Oskar Kummetz

The Battle of Drøbak Sound took place in the early hours of 9 April 1940, the first day of Operation Weserübung, the German invasion of Norway. The obsolescent Norwegian coastal fortress of Oscarsborg, guarding the narrows of the Oslofjord at Drøbak, engaged the German naval force steaming for Oslo and sank the heavy cruiser Blücher, the flagship of the invasion group. The delay imposed on the German seizure of the capital allowed the Norwegian king, government and parliament to escape Oslo, along with the national gold reserves.

Background

German Naval Group 5, led by the new heavy cruiser Blücher and including the cruiser Lützow (the former Deutschland), the light cruiser Emden and smaller vessels, was to slip up the Oslofjord at night and land troops to seize Oslo by surprise at dawn. Oscarsborg Fortress, under Colonel Birger Eriksen, was manned largely by recruits and equipped with weapons of remarkable vintage: three 28 cm Krupp guns dating from the 1890s (nicknamed Moses, Aron and Josva) and a torpedo battery whose Whitehead torpedoes were some forty years old.

Battle

At about 04:21 the leading German ships entered the fortress's range in the narrow sound. Eriksen, uncertain of the intruders' nationality but judging them hostile, gave the famous order to open fire — remarking, "Either I will be decorated or I will be court-martialled." The fortress's guns scored direct hits on Blücher at point-blank range; fires reached her magazines and aviation fuel, and two torpedoes from the concealed shore battery struck her amidships. Blücher capsized and sank with heavy loss of life, including much of the Gestapo and administrative staff intended to take over Norway. Lützow was also hit and the surviving German ships withdrew down the fjord, landing their troops at Moss and Drøbak instead.

Consequences

The German timetable collapsed. Oslo was eventually taken by airborne troops landed at Fornebu airport, but King Haakon VII, the Nygaardsvold government and the Storting escaped to Hamar and Elverum, rejected the German ultimatum, and continued Norwegian resistance for two months before going into exile in Britain. The action remains the most celebrated episode of the Norwegian campaign of 1940, commemorated in the 2016 film The King's Choice.
