The Hamidian massacres were massacres of Armenians in the Ottoman Empire in the mid-1890s, during the reign of Sultan Abdul Hamid II. Estimates of the number of Armenians killed range from 80,000 to 300,000, with tens of thousands more orphaned or forcibly displaced. The massacres are named after the sultan and are also known in Armenian as the "Great Massacres".

Background

After the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78, the Treaty of Berlin promised reforms and protection for Armenians in the eastern provinces, which were never implemented. Armenian revolutionary parties such as the Hunchaks and the Armenian Revolutionary Federation agitated for reform, while the sultan raised irregular Kurdish cavalry regiments — the Hamidiye — that were used to police and pressure Armenian communities.

Course

Violence began with the suppression of Armenian tax resistance at Sasun in 1894. In 1895–96, massacres spread through Trabzon, Erzurum, Urfa and many other towns, and in August 1896, after Armenian revolutionaries seized the Ottoman Bank in Constantinople, a pogrom in the capital killed thousands. Churches were desecrated, villages plundered, and many Armenians were forcibly converted.

Aftermath

The massacres provoked outrage abroad and large-scale emigration, but no effective intervention or reform. They are regarded as a precursor to the Armenian genocide of 1915, carried out under the Young Turk government two decades later.
