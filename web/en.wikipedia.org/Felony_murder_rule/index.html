Felony murder rule

The felony murder rule is a legal doctrine in some common-law jurisdictions that broadens the crime of murder: when someone is killed in the course of, or in furtherance of, a felony, the person committing the felony (and usually their accomplices) may be charged with murder even if they did not intend the death and did not personally kill. The intent to commit the underlying felony is treated as a substitute for the intent to kill.

Application in the United States

Most U.S. states retain some form of the rule, though with wide variation. Many states limit first-degree felony murder to enumerated dangerous felonies such as robbery, burglary, arson, rape, and kidnapping. States differ on whether a defendant is liable when the killing is committed by a co-felon, by a police officer, or by a victim resisting the crime (the "agency" versus "proximate cause" approaches). Some states have narrowed or abolished the rule: California reformed its law in 2018 (and expanded relief in 2021) so that accomplices who did not kill, intend to kill, or act as major participants with reckless indifference generally cannot be convicted of felony murder. Hawaii and Kentucky have abolished the doctrine entirely.

History and other jurisdictions

The rule's origins in English common law are debated, but England itself abolished constructive malice by the Homicide Act 1957. Canada's Supreme Court struck down felony (constructive) murder provisions as unconstitutional in R v Vaillancourt (1987) and R v Martineau (1990), holding that murder requires subjective foresight of death. Australia has retained limited forms of the doctrine in some states.

Criticism

Critics argue the rule violates the principle that punishment should track culpability, punishes accidental and unforeseen deaths as murder, and falls heavily on young and minor accomplices — for example, getaway drivers — who may face life sentences. Supporters argue it deters dangerous felonies and reflects the moral judgment that someone who chooses to commit a serious felony bears responsibility for its lethal consequences.

Capital punishment limits

In Enmund v. Florida (1982) and Tison v. Arizona (1987), the U.S. Supreme Court limited the death penalty for felony murder accomplices: execution is barred for a non-killer who did not intend death, but permitted for a major participant who acted with reckless indifference to human life.
