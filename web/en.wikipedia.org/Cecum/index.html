Cecum

The cecum (also spelled caecum) is a pouch within the peritoneum that marks the beginning of the large intestine. It receives chyme from the ileum, the final part of the small intestine, through the ileocecal valve, and connects to the ascending colon. In humans it is typically about 6 cm (2.4 in) long, and the appendix branches off it.

Function

The cecum absorbs fluids and salts remaining after digestion in the small intestine and mixes the contents with mucus. In many herbivorous mammals the cecum is greatly enlarged and houses bacteria that ferment plant material such as cellulose, which the animal cannot digest on its own; horses and rabbits are notable examples of hindgut fermenters.

Structure

The cecum lies in the right iliac fossa of the abdomen. Its blood supply comes from the ileocolic artery, a branch of the superior mesenteric artery. The vermiform appendix, a narrow tube of debated function, opens into the cecum below the ileocecal valve.

Clinical significance

The cecum can be affected by conditions such as cecal volvulus (twisting of the bowel), tumours, and inflammation associated with appendicitis or inflammatory bowel disease. Imaging and colonoscopy can visualise it directly.
