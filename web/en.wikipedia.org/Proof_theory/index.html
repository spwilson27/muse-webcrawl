Proof theory

Proof theory is a major branch of mathematical logic and theoretical computer science in which proofs are treated as formal mathematical objects, facilitating their analysis by mathematical techniques. Proofs are typically presented as inductively-defined data structures such as lists, boxed lists, or trees, which are constructed according to the axioms and rules of inference of the logical system. Consequently, proof theory is syntactic in nature, in contrast to model theory, which is semantic in nature. Some of the major areas of proof theory include structural proof theory, ordinal analysis, provability logic, reverse mathematics, proof mining, automated theorem proving, and proof complexity.

History

The field emerged from David Hilbert's program of the 1920s, which sought finitistic consistency proofs for formalized mathematics. Gerhard Gentzen founded modern structural proof theory in the 1930s with his natural deduction and sequent calculus, proving the cut-elimination theorem and giving a consistency proof for arithmetic using transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε0. Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) had already shown that Hilbert's original aims could not be fully achieved, redirecting proof theory toward the analysis of the strength of formal systems.

Key concepts

Structural proof theory studies the fine structure of proofs, including normalization and cut elimination, which yield consistency and interpolation results. Ordinal analysis measures the strength of theories by the proof-theoretic ordinals they can show to be well-founded. The Curry–Howard correspondence links proofs in intuitionistic logic to programs in typed lambda calculus, giving proof theory direct relevance to programming languages and interactive theorem provers such as Coq and Lean.
