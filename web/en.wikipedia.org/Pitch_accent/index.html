Pitch accent

Pitch accent is a term used in phonology for a language in which the pitch, or fundamental frequency, of a syllable plays a role in distinguishing words, but in a more restricted way than in a full tone language. In pitch-accent languages, typically only one syllable per word can bear the accent, and the contrast is usually limited to high versus low pitch on that syllable or the following one.

Examples

Japanese is the best-known pitch-accent language: in standard Tokyo Japanese, words are distinguished by the position at which the pitch falls after the accented mora — famously, hashi can mean "chopsticks", "bridge" or "edge" depending on the accent pattern. Other pitch-accent languages include Swedish and Norwegian (with their two word tones), Serbo-Croatian, Ancient Greek, Vedic Sanskrit, and many dialects of Korean, Slovene and Basque.

Typology

Pitch accent sits between stress accent and tone on a typological continuum. Scholars disagree about whether it is a distinct category or simply a restricted form of tonal system, and the term has been applied in several different senses. What unites the systems so labelled is the culminativity of the accent: at most one syllable in a word is specified for pitch.
