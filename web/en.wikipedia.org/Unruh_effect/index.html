Unruh effect

An accelerating observer in flat spacetime detects a thermal bath of particles where an inertial observer sees only vacuum.

The Unruh effect (also known as the Fulling–Davies–Unruh effect) is a prediction of quantum field theory that an accelerating observer will observe a thermal spectrum of particles — a warm bath of radiation — even in what an inertial observer regards as empty vacuum. It is named after the Canadian physicist William G. Unruh, who described it in 1976, building on work by Stephen Fulling (1973) and Paul Davies (1975).

Theory

The temperature of the radiation, the Unruh temperature, is proportional to the observer's proper acceleration a:

T = ħa / (2πckB)

where ħ is the reduced Planck constant, c the speed of light, and kB the Boltzmann constant. The effect is extremely small under ordinary conditions: an acceleration of about 1020 m/s² is required for a temperature of one kelvin, making direct experimental detection exceptionally difficult.

Relation to Hawking radiation

The Unruh effect is closely related to Hawking radiation: by the equivalence principle, a stationary observer near a black hole's event horizon is analogous to an accelerating observer in flat spacetime, and the black hole's temperature can be obtained by redshift of the local Unruh temperature. Both effects show that, in quantum field theory, the notion of a particle depends on the observer's motion through spacetime.

Experimental status

Direct confirmation remains lacking, though analogue experiments in fluids, Bose–Einstein condensates, and accelerated electron systems (such as the Sokolov–Ternov effect in storage rings) are cited as related phenomena. Analogue gravity experiments continue to probe the effect's predictions.
