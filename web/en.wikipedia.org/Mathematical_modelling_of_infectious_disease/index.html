Mathematical modelling of infectious disease

Mathematical models can project how infectious diseases progress to show the likely outcome of an epidemic and help inform public health interventions. Models use basic assumptions or collected statistics along with mathematics to find parameters for various infectious diseases and use those parameters to calculate the effects of different interventions, like mass vaccination programmes. The modelling can help decide which intervention(s) to avoid and which to trial, or can predict future growth patterns.

Compartmental models

Compartmental models divide the population into compartments such as susceptible (S), infectious (I), and recovered (R). In the classic SIR model, individuals move from S to I through contact with infectious individuals, and from I to R through recovery. Extensions add an exposed (E) compartment for latent infection, or allow recovered individuals to become susceptible again (SIRS). The basic reproduction number R₀ determines whether an introduction leads to an epidemic: if R₀ > 1, the disease can spread.

History

Daniel Bernoulli published an early mathematical analysis of smallpox inoculation in 1760. The modern era began with the work of Kermack and McKendrick in 1927, whose threshold theorem showed that epidemics subside because susceptibles are depleted, not because the pathogen disappears. Models played a central role in responses to foot-and-mouth disease, SARS, Ebola, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Limitations

Models depend on assumptions about mixing, behaviour, and parameters that may be poorly measured early in an outbreak, so their projections carry substantial uncertainty.
