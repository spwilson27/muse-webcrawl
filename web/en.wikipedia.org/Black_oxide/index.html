Black oxide

Tools finished with black oxide coating.

Black oxide or blackening is a conversion coating for ferrous materials, stainless steel, copper and copper-based alloys, zinc, powdered metals, and silver solder. It is called "black oxide" because it blackens the surface of the metal being treated. The process converts the surface of iron and steel into magnetite (Fe3O4), giving the part a black appearance while adding minimal dimensional change — an important advantage over paint or plating for precision parts.

Process

In the hot blackening process, parts are immersed in baths of alkaline oxidizing salts at temperatures around 140 °C (285 °F). Mid-temperature and cold blackening processes also exist; cold processes are performed at room temperature but generally produce less durable coatings that rely on deposited compounds rather than a true converted magnetite layer.

Properties and applications

Black oxide provides mild corrosion resistance, which is improved substantially when the porous coating is sealed with oil, wax, or lacquer. It reduces light reflection, improves lubricity and resistance to galling, and does not chip, peel, or flake like paint. It is widely used on firearms, hand tools, drill bits, fasteners, gears, and machine components. Black oxide on firearms is often loosely called "bluing," though true bluing encompasses several related processes. Because the coating is thin (on the order of a micrometre), it is favored where dimensional tolerances must be preserved.
