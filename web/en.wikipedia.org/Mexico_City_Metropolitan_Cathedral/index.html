Mexico City Metropolitan Cathedral

The Metropolitan Cathedral of the Assumption of the Most Blessed Virgin Mary into Heaven (Catedral Metropolitana de la Asunción de la Santísima Virgen María a los cielos) is the seat of the Archdiocese of Mexico. It sits atop the former Aztec sacred precinct near the Templo Mayor on the northern side of the Zócalo, the main square of Mexico City.

Construction

Built in sections from 1573 to 1813, the cathedral blends Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque, and Neoclassical styles. Architect Manuel Tolsá completed the towers, dome, and facade in the early 19th century. It measures approximately 59 metres wide by 128 metres long, with two bell towers containing 25 bells.

Interior

The cathedral has five naves, 16 chapels, and several notable altarpieces, including the Altar of the Kings and the Altar of Forgiveness. Its sacristy holds colonial-era paintings by artists such as Cristóbal de Villalpando and Juan Correa. Beneath the cathedral are crypts containing the remains of archbishops and traces of pre-Hispanic structures, including a stone depicting the Aztec calendar.

Sinking and restoration

Because Mexico City is built on a drained lakebed, the cathedral has sunk unevenly for centuries; major engineering works from the 1990s to 2000s stabilised the structure. It is part of the Historic Centre of Mexico City UNESCO World Heritage Site, inscribed in 1987.
