Court of Appeal

TypeAppellate court
FunctionHearing appeals from lower courts
ExamplesEngland and Wales; US federal courts of appeals; New Zealand; Ontario

A court of appeal (also appeals court or appellate court) is a court that hears challenges to decisions of trial courts and other lower tribunals. Such courts generally do not conduct trials or receive new evidence; instead they review the record of the proceedings below for errors of law, jurisdiction, or procedure, and may affirm, reverse, or modify the decision, or remit the case for a new trial.

England and Wales

The Court of Appeal of England and Wales, established in 1875, is the second most senior court in the jurisdiction, below the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom. It has a Civil Division, headed by the Master of the Rolls, and a Criminal Division, headed by the Lord Chief Justice. It hears appeals from the High Court, county courts, Crown Court, and certain tribunals. Its judges are Lords Justices of Appeal and Lady Justices of Appeal.

United States

In the US federal system, the courts of appeals are the intermediate appellate courts, organised into thirteen circuits (eleven geographic, the D.C. Circuit, and the Federal Circuit). They hear appeals from the district courts and certain agencies; their decisions may be reviewed by the Supreme Court, usually by certiorari. Most US states also have intermediate appellate courts bearing names such as court of appeals or superior court.

Other jurisdictions

Courts of appeal exist throughout the common-law world, including Canada (provincial courts of appeal), Australia, New Zealand, Ireland, and many Commonwealth countries, as well as in some civil-law systems.
