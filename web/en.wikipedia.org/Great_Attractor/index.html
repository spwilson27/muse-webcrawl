The Great Attractor is a region of gravitational attraction in intergalactic space and the apparent central gravitational point of the Laniakea Supercluster of galaxies. Located at a distance of roughly 150 to 250 million light-years in the direction of the constellations Triangulum Australe and Norma, it was inferred from the peculiar velocities of galaxies: the Milky Way and its neighbours are moving toward it at about 600 km/s relative to the cosmic microwave background.

Discovery and explanation

Deviations from the smooth Hubble flow were noted in the 1970s and 1980s; in 1987 a group including Donald Lynden-Bell and Alan Dressler popularised the term "Great Attractor" for the mass concentration responsible. The region lies in the Zone of Avoidance, the part of the sky obscured by the Milky Way's dust and stars, which long hampered direct observation. X-ray and radio surveys have since revealed the Norma Cluster (ACO 3627), a massive galaxy cluster near the attractor's centre, and shown that the flow continues beyond it toward the even larger Shapley Supercluster. Modern interpretations treat the Great Attractor less as a single object than as the gravitational basin of the local supercluster complex.
