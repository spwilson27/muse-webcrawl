Active optics

Active optics is a technology used with reflecting telescopes developed in the 1980s, which actively shapes a telescope's mirrors to prevent deformation due to external influences such as wind, temperature, and mechanical stress. Without active optics, the next generation of larger telescopes of 8 metres and above could not be built.

Method

Active optics works on timescales of seconds or longer: a wavefront sensor measures distortions in the incoming light, a computer computes the correction, and actuators behind the primary mirror adjust its shape to compensate. Because the corrections are slow, the primary mirror can be made thin and lightweight (a meniscus mirror), supported at many points.

Comparison with adaptive optics

Active optics should not be confused with adaptive optics, which works on much shorter timescales (milliseconds) to correct atmospheric distortion using deformable secondary mirrors. Many modern observatories use both systems together.

Applications

The technology was pioneered by the European Southern Observatory's New Technology Telescope (NTT), which entered operation in 1989, and is now standard on large telescopes such as the Very Large Telescope and the Keck telescopes (in segmented form).
