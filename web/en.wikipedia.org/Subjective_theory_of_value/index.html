Subjective theory of value

The subjective theory of value is a theory of value in economics which holds that the value of a good is not determined by any inherent property of the good, nor by the amount of labor necessary to produce it, but instead by the importance an individual places on the good for the achievement of his or her ends. The theory was a central element of the marginal revolution of the 1870s and is foundational to the Austrian School and to modern neoclassical economics.

History

Ideas anticipating the theory appear in medieval scholastics and in 18th-century writers such as Ferdinando Galiani and Condillac. The theory was developed systematically and independently by Carl Menger in his Principles of Economics (1871), William Stanley Jevons, and Léon Walras. Menger's version explained value through the satisfactions a good provides, ranked by urgency — the germ of marginal utility.

Explanation

Under the theory, value is imputed by acting individuals to goods according to the goods' perceived capacity to satisfy wants. A famous consequence is the resolution of the paradox of value (the diamond–water paradox): water, though essential, is abundant, so its marginal unit is valued less than a marginal unit of the scarce diamond. Prices emerge from countless such subjective evaluations as buyers and sellers exchange.

Contrast with the labor theory

The subjective theory stands in contrast to the labor theory of value associated with Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and Karl Marx, which grounds value in the labor embodied in production. Menger reversed the direction of causation: consumer-goods values determine the values of the producer goods (factors of production) used to make them, through the process of imputation.
