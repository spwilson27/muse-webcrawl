Public international law

The Peace Palace, The Hague, home of the International Court of Justice.

Public international law (also simply international law) is the body of rules, norms, and standards that govern relations between sovereign states and other subjects of international law, including international organisations, and increasingly individuals. It is distinct from private international law (conflict of laws), which determines which national law applies to private disputes with cross-border elements. Because there is no world legislature or police force, international law rests largely on consent, custom, reciprocity, and reputation, though its rules are routinely applied by national and international courts.

Sources

Article 38 of the Statute of the International Court of Justice lists the classic sources: international conventions (treaties); international custom, evidenced by general practice accepted as law (opinio juris); and general principles of law recognised by nations. Judicial decisions and scholarly writings serve as subsidiary means for determining rules. Certain norms — the prohibitions of genocide, slavery, and aggressive war among them — are considered peremptory (jus cogens) and bind all states regardless of consent.

Fields and institutions

Modern public international law includes the law of treaties (codified in the Vienna Convention of 1969), the law of the sea (UNCLOS 1982), diplomatic and consular law, international human rights law, international humanitarian law governing armed conflict, international criminal law, international trade and investment law, and international environmental law. Its principal judicial organ is the International Court of Justice at The Hague, alongside specialised tribunals such as the International Criminal Court, the WTO dispute-settlement system, and arbitral bodies. The Charter of the United Nations (1945) supplies the system's constitutional frame, prohibiting the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity of states except in self-defence or under Security Council authorisation.

History

The discipline is conventionally traced to early-modern writers such as Francisco de Vitoria, Francisco Suárez, and above all Hugo Grotius, whose De jure belli ac pacis (1625) systematised the law of nations. The Peace of Westphalia (1648) is traditionally associated with the emergence of a system of sovereign, equal states, and the 19th and 20th centuries brought codification, the League of Nations, and the post-1945 expansion into human rights and decolonisation.
