Plague (disease)

Plague is an infectious disease caused by the bacterium Yersinia pestis. Symptoms include fever, weakness and headache. Usually this begins one to seven days after exposure. There are three forms of plague, each affecting a different part of the body and causing associated symptoms: pneumonic plague infects the lungs, causing shortness of breath, cough and chest pain; bubonic plague affects the lymph nodes, making them swell; and septicemic plague infects the blood and can cause tissues to turn black and die.

Transmission

The bubonic and septicemic forms are generally spread by flea bites or handling an infected animal, whereas pneumonic plague is generally spread person to person via infection in droplets from the air. The bacterium is maintained in wild rodent populations in endemic foci on several continents.

History

Plague has caused some of the most devastating pandemics in human history. The Plague of Justinian (541–549) struck the Byzantine Empire; the Black Death (1346–1353) killed an estimated 75–200 million people in Eurasia and North Africa, peaking in Europe in 1348–1350; and the third plague pandemic, beginning in China in the mid-19th century, spread worldwide through shipping. It was during the third pandemic, in Hong Kong in 1894, that Alexandre Yersin identified the causative bacterium.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is typically made by finding the bacterium in a sample of fluid from a lymph node, blood, or sputum. Plague is treatable with antibiotics such as streptomycin, gentamicin, and doxycycline; early treatment greatly improves survival. Without treatment, mortality is high, especially for the pneumonic and septicemic forms.

Modern occurrence

Plague remains endemic in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Americas, including Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Peru, and the southwestern United States, where a small number of human cases occur most years. The World Health Organization reports hundreds to thousands of cases globally per year.
