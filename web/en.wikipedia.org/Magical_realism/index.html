Magical realism

Magical realism (also magic realism) is a style of fiction in which magical or fantastical elements are woven into an otherwise realistic, mundane setting and narrated matter-of-factly, so that the supernatural is accepted as part of everyday life. The term, first used by German art critic Franz Roh in 1925 for painting, became associated above all with Latin American literature of the mid-20th century.

Characteristics

Magical realist works present an authoritative, realistic narrative voice, an abundance of vivid sensory detail, and a setting in which the phenomenal and the marvelous coexist without explanation or surprise. Time is often cyclical or distorted, myths and folklore are treated as lived reality, and political critique — especially of colonialism and authoritarianism — is frequently embedded in the fantastic. Unlike fantasy, the magical elements do not belong to a separate world; unlike surrealism, they are not framed as dreams.

Major writers

The foundational figure is Gabriel García Márquez, whose novel One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), set in the fictional town of Macondo, defined the mode for a global audience. Other key practitioners include Alejo Carpentier, who theorised lo real maravilloso ("the marvelous real"), Juan Rulfo, Miguel Ángel Asturias, Jorge Luis Borges, Isabel Allende, and Laura Esquivel. The style profoundly influenced writers worldwide, including Salman Rushdie (Midnight's Children), Toni Morrison (Beloved), and Haruki Murakami.

Criticism

Critics have debated whether the label is a marketing category for Latin American writing, and some authors resisted it; García Márquez himself insisted he merely described a reality in which the extraordinary was ordinary.
