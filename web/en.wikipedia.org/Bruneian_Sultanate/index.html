Bruneian Sultanate

The Bruneian Sultanate (Malay: Kesultanan Brunei) is the historical state centred on Brunei on the north coast of Borneo, and the predecessor of the modern nation of Brunei Darussalam. At its height in the 15th and 16th centuries it controlled much of Borneo and parts of the Philippines, including Manila.

Origins

Brunei's rulers trace their line to the 14th century. According to tradition, Awang Alak Betatar converted to Islam and became the first Sultan, Muhammad Shah, around 1368. In the early 15th century Brunei was recorded in Ming Chinese sources as a tributary trading power, and its fifth Sultan, Bolkiah (r. 1485–1524), presided over the empire's greatest territorial extent, earning the epithet "the singing captain" in local tradition.

Golden age and European contact

The port of Brunei prospered from trade in camphor, pepper, gold and forest products. When the survivors of Magellan's expedition visited in 1521, Antonio Pigafetta described a wealthy court city built partly on stilts over the Brunei River. Spain, coveting Brunei's influence over the Muslim sultanates of the Philippines, attacked in the Castilian War of 1578 and briefly occupied the capital.

Decline

Civil war in the 17th century, internal succession disputes and piracy weakened the sultanate. During the 19th century, the adventurer James Brooke acquired Sarawak, and Brunei's territory shrank steadily under pressure from Brooke's dynasty and the British North Borneo Company. In 1888 Brunei accepted British protection, and a British Resident administered the state from 1906.

Modern era

The discovery of oil at Seria in 1929 transformed the sultanate's finances. Brunei regained self-government in 1959 and full independence from the United Kingdom on 1 January 1984, remaining an absolute monarchy under Sultan Hassanal Bolkiah, the 29th ruler of the line.
