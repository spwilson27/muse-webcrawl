The Time Machine is a post-apocalyptic science fiction novella by English author H. G. Wells, published in 1895. The work is generally credited with the popularization of the concept of time travel by using a vehicle or device to travel purposely and selectively forward or backward in time. The term "time machine", coined by Wells, is now almost universally used to refer to such a vehicle or device.

Plot

The book's protagonist is a Victorian English scientist and gentleman inventor living in Richmond, Surrey, identified by a narrator simply as the Time Traveller. The Time Traveller explains to his dinner guests that he has discovered a way to travel through time, and later recounts his journey to the year AD 802,701. In that distant future, humanity has diverged into two species: the gentle, childlike Eloi, who live above ground in decaying splendour, and the brutish, ape-like Morlocks, who dwell underground and emerge at night to prey upon the Eloi.

After recovering his machine and travelling still further into the future, the Time Traveller witnesses the dying Earth under an enlarged, red sun before returning to his own time. His guests are sceptical, and he departs on a further journey from which he never returns.

Themes and legacy

Wells used the far future as a vehicle for social commentary, extrapolating the class divisions of Victorian Britain into the divergent evolution of the Eloi and Morlocks. The novella is considered a foundational work of science fiction and has been adapted into feature films in 1960 and 2002, as well as numerous other media. It has influenced countless writers and helped establish Wells's reputation as a pioneer of the genre. See also Time travel.
