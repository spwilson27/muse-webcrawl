Frying

Cooking food in hot oil or fat

Frying is the cooking of food in oil or another fat. One of the fastest and most widespread cooking methods, frying is attested in ancient Egypt and appears in the Roman cookbook Apicius. Food cooked this way develops a crisp, browned surface through rapid dehydration and Maillard reactions, while the interior cooks by conduction.

Techniques

Frying varies by the amount of fat used. In pan frying, food cooks in a shallow layer of oil in a pan and is turned once. Sautéing uses very little fat with small pieces kept moving; stir frying, developed in China, uses a hot wok with constant tossing. Deep frying submerges food entirely in hot oil (typically 175–190 °C), producing an evenly crisp crust, as in chips (french fries), fried chicken and doughnuts. Shallow frying and air frying — which circulates very hot air with little or no oil — are intermediate options.

Science and health

When food meets hot oil, surface water boils off as steam, which limits oil absorption while the crust sets; properly fried food absorbs less fat than commonly assumed, though battered and breaded items take up more. Repeatedly reheating oil degrades it, producing off flavours and potentially harmful compounds, so frying fats are chosen for high smoke points and stability. Fried foods are energy-dense, and dietary guidelines generally advise moderation.
