Perfect competition

In economics, perfect competition is an idealised market structure in which no participant is large enough to influence the market price of a product. Because its conditions are rarely met in full, perfect competition serves chiefly as a benchmark against which real market structures — monopoly, oligopoly and monopolistic competition — are compared.

Conditions

  • Many buyers and sellers, each too small to affect price; firms are price takers.
  • Homogeneous products: goods offered by different firms are perfect substitutes.
  • Perfect information: all participants know prices and product characteristics.
  • Free entry and exit: no barriers prevent firms entering or leaving the market.
  • No transaction costs and no externalities.

Equilibrium

In the short run, a perfectly competitive firm produces where price equals marginal cost and can earn abnormal profit or loss. In the long run, entry and exit drive economic profit to zero, so price equals minimum average total cost as well: P = MC = ATC. The result is both allocative and productive efficiency — goods are produced at the lowest possible cost and priced at their marginal cost of production.

Examples and criticism

Agricultural commodity markets and some financial markets approximate perfect competition, but no real market satisfies all conditions. Critics note that the model is static, ignores innovation and product differentiation, and treats the absence of profit as equilibrium even though real competition is a dynamic process of rivalry, discovery and creative destruction, as emphasised by Austrian and Schumpeterian economists.
