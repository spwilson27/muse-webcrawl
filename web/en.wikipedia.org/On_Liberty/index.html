On Liberty

On Liberty is a philosophical essay by the English philosopher John Stuart Mill, published in 1859. It applies Mill's utilitarian system to society and the state, and argues for limits on the power that society and government may legitimately exercise over the individual. It is one of the most influential defences of liberalism and of freedom of speech ever written.

The harm principle

Mill states his governing principle early in the work: "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others." Conduct that harms only the agent — "self-regarding" conduct — lies outside the legitimate reach of law and social coercion, with exceptions for children and, controversially in Mill's text, societies he deemed "backward".

Freedom of thought and discussion

The second chapter defends unrestricted free expression. Mill argues that a silenced opinion may be true; that even false opinions often contain a portion of truth; that unchallenged true opinions decay into "dead dogma"; and that doctrines must be contested to be understood in their living meaning. Suppressing discussion, he concludes, assumes infallibility on the part of the suppressors.

Individuality and applications

Later chapters defend individuality and "experiments of living" as essential to human progress, and apply the principles to cases such as trade, education, marriage and the limits of state action, warning against the growth of an intrusive bureaucracy. Mill dedicated the book to his late wife, Harriet Taylor Mill, whose influence on the work he described as profound.

Legacy

On Liberty became a canonical text of liberal political thought, shaping doctrines of free speech in jurisprudence and philosophy, and provoking critics — from James Fitzjames Stephen to modern communitarians and legal theorists such as Joel Feinberg — to refine the boundaries of the harm principle.
