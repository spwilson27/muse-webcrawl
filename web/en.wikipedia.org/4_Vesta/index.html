Vesta (minor-planet designation 4 Vesta) is one of the largest objects in the asteroid belt, with a mean diameter of 525 kilometres (326 mi). It was discovered by the German astronomer Heinrich Wilhelm Olbers on 29 March 1807 and is named after Vesta, the virgin goddess of home and hearth in Roman mythology.

Vesta is thought to consist of a metallic iron–nickel core, an olivine-rich rocky mantle and a basaltic crust, making it a differentiated protoplanet — a surviving remnant of the early Solar System. It is the second-most-massive body in the asteroid belt after Ceres, accounting for roughly nine per cent of the belt's total mass.

Geology

The southern hemisphere of Vesta is dominated by two enormous impact craters, Rheasilvia (about 505 km across) and the older Veneneia, whose collision events excavated roughly one per cent of the asteroid's volume. Debris from these impacts forms the Vesta family of asteroids, and fragments reaching Earth are known as HED meteorites (howardites, eucrites and diogenites).

Exploration

NASA's Dawn spacecraft orbited Vesta from July 2011 to September 2012, mapping its surface and confirming its differentiated interior, before departing for Ceres. Dawn's observations revealed a giant mountain near the south pole, among the tallest in the Solar System, and evidence of past volcanic activity and water-bearing minerals.
