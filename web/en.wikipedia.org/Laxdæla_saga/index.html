Laxdæla saga

Laxdæla saga (Old Norse pronunciation: [ˈlaksˌdɛːla ˈsaɣa]), also Laxdœla saga or The Saga of the People of Laxárdalur, is one of the most celebrated of the Icelandic family sagas (Íslendingasögur). Probably written around 1230–1260, it recounts the history of the people of Laxárdalur (Salmon River Dale) in western Iceland from the late ninth century to the early eleventh century. It is especially noted for its tragic love triangle involving Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir and the foster-brothers Kjartan Óláfsson and Bolli Þorleiksson, and is distinguished among the sagas by the prominence of its women characters.

Synopsis

The saga opens with Ketill Flatnose and his daughter Unn the Deep-Minded, who, after the deaths of her husband and son, sails to Iceland and claims land in the Laxá river district. The narrative follows her descendants through generations of settlement and feud: Höskuldr Dala-Kollsson, his son Óláfr pái ("the Peacock"), and Óláfr's son Kjartan. At its heart is the love of Kjartan and Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir, "the most beautiful woman in Iceland". Kjartan travels to Norway, is detained at the court of King Óláfr Tryggvason during Iceland's conversion to Christianity, and in his absence Guðrún is persuaded — falsely told Kjartan will marry the king's sister — to wed Kjartan's foster-brother Bolli. The resulting bitterness culminates in Bolli killing Kjartan in an ambush at Hafragil, and in Bolli's own death in revenge. Guðrún, who married four times, ends her days as Iceland's first known anchoress/nun at Helgafell; asked which of her husbands she loved most, she replies famously: "I treated worst the one I loved most."

Characters

  • Unn the Deep-Minded — matriarch and settler.
  • Óláfr pái — prosperous chieftain, son of the Irish slave-girl Melkorka, revealed as an Irish king's daughter.
  • Kjartan Óláfsson — the saga's hero, killed by his foster-brother.
  • Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir — central figure; beautiful, proud, and long-lived.
  • Bolli Þorleiksson — Kjartan's foster-brother and killer.
  • Snorri goði — the canny chieftain who engineers settlements.

Themes and style

Scholars remark on the saga's unusual interest in romance motives, fine clothing and wealth (suggesting aristocratic or French influence), its strong, complex women, and its awareness of fate and dreams — Guðrún's four dreams, interpreted by Gestr the Wise, foretell her four marriages. It also gives one of the fullest saga accounts of Iceland's conversion to Christianity around the year 1000.

Manuscripts

The saga survives principally in the fourteenth-century vellum Möðruvallabók and in a number of paper manuscripts. It has been translated into English many times, notably by Muriel Press (1964) and by Magnus Magnusson and Hermann Pálsson (1969).
