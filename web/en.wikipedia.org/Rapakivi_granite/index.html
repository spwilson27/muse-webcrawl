Rapakivi granite

Rapakivi granite is a hornblende-biotite granite characterised by large, round crystals (ovoids) of orthoclase feldspar, each mantled by a rim of plagioclase feldspar known as oligoclase or andesine. The name, first used in 1891 by Jakob Sederholm, derives from the Finnish rapa kivi, "crumbled stone" or "rotten rock", because the rock weathers readily into gravelly debris.

Occurrence

Classic rapakivi massifs occur in southern Finland — the Wiborg (Vyborg), Åland, Laitila and Vehmaa batholiths — and around the Baltic region. Similar Proterozoic anorogenic granites are found worldwide, including in Ukraine, Brazil, China and the mid-continental United States. The Finnish batholiths are about 1.6 billion years old, formed during a period of extensional tectonics rather than mountain building.

Texture and origin

The mantled ovoid texture is thought to record changing conditions in the magma chamber — for example magma mixing or decompression during ascent — which caused plagioclase to crystallise on pre-existing rounded potassium feldspar crystals. The variety with mantled ovoids is called wiborgite, and the unmantled variety pyterlite.

Use

Polished rapakivi granite is a popular ornamental building stone (sold under names such as "Baltic Brown"); it was used for parts of St Petersburg, including columns and embankments, and appears in buildings worldwide.
