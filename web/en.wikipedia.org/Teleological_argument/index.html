Teleological argument

The teleological argument (from Greek τέλος, telos, "end" or "purpose"), also known as the argument from design, is an argument for the existence of God that infers a designer from perceived evidence of deliberate design and purpose in the natural or physical world. It is one of the classical arguments of natural theology, alongside the cosmological and ontological arguments.

History

Ideas of design appear in Plato and in Aristotle's doctrine of final causes. Thomas Aquinas's fifth way argues from the directedness of natural bodies toward ends to the existence of an intelligent guide. The argument's most famous formulation is William Paley's watchmaker analogy in Natural Theology (1802): just as a watch found on a heath implies a watchmaker, so the intricate adaptations of organisms imply a designer. The Bridgewater Treatises of the 1830s elaborated the argument for Victorian science.

Criticism

David Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779) raised enduring objections: the analogy between artefacts and the universe is weak, order might arise from chance or necessity, and a designer would not by itself explain the designer's own origin. Immanuel Kant held the argument establishes at most an architect of matter, not a creator. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection (1859) provided a natural mechanism for biological adaptation, widely seen as undermining the biological version of the argument.

Modern versions

Contemporary defenders appeal to cosmology rather than biology: the fine-tuning of physical constants for life is presented as evidence of design, and the intelligent design movement of the 1990s argued that certain biological structures exhibit "irreducible" or "specified" complexity. Critics respond that fine-tuning may be explained by a multiverse or remain an open question, and that intelligent design has not gained acceptance as science.
