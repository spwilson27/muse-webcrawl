Communism

Communism is a political and economic ideology whose goal is the establishment of a communist society: a socioeconomic order centred on common ownership of the means of production, distribution, and exchange that allocates products in society based on need. A communist society would entail the absence of private property and social classes, and ultimately money and the state.

History

The modern communist movement emerged in the 19th century from the works of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, most famously The Communist Manifesto (1848) and Marx's Das Kapital. Marxists held that capitalism would be overthrown by a proletarian revolution, followed by a transitional workers' state ("dictatorship of the proletariat") and eventually a stateless, classless society.

The Russian Revolution of 1917 brought the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin to power, creating the Soviet Union, the first state ruled by a communist party. After the Second World War, communist governments were established across Eastern Europe and in China (1949), Cuba (1959), Vietnam, and elsewhere. The Cold War set the communist bloc against the capitalist West until the revolutions of 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991.

Variants

Major currents include Marxism–Leninism, Trotskyism, Maoism, council communism, anarcho-communism, and Eurocommunism. Communist parties continue to govern in China, Cuba, Vietnam, Laos, and North Korea, though several of these states have adopted substantial market mechanisms.

Criticism

Critics associate communist regimes with authoritarianism, political repression, and economic inefficiency; defenders argue that historical outcomes reflected particular circumstances and point to achievements in literacy, industrialisation, and social welfare. The scholarly debate over the human toll of 20th-century communist states remains contentious.
