Honeydew (secretion)

Honeydew is a sugar-rich sticky liquid, secreted by aphids, some scale insects, and many other true bugs (Hemiptera) as they feed on plant sap. When their mouthpart penetrates the phloem, the sugary, high-pressure liquid is forced out of the anus of the insect, allowing the insect to process the nutrients it needs from the dilute sap. Honeydew is particularly common as a secretion in hemipteran insects and is often troublesome to gardeners, because it coats leaves and surfaces beneath infested plants, encouraging the growth of sooty mold fungi.

Production

Phloem sap is rich in sugars but poor in amino acids. Insects that feed on it must process a large volume of sap to obtain sufficient protein, and the excess sugar and water are excreted as honeydew. The droplets may be flicked away from the insect, allowed to fall, or, in the case of many aphids, harvested by other animals.

Honeydew as food

Honeydew is collected by certain species of birds, wasps, stingless bees, and honey bees, which can convert it into a dark, strong honey called honeydew honey. This honey is highly prized in parts of Europe and New Zealand and is produced in forests where aphid populations feed on trees such as beech and fir. Ants commonly farm aphids, protecting them from predators and stroking them with their antennae to stimulate the release of honeydew, which the ants then consume — a classic example of mutualism.

Problems

The sugary coating promotes the growth of black sooty mold, which can reduce photosynthesis in heavily infested plants. Honeydew falling from trees can also coat cars, pavements, and outdoor furniture beneath. In ancient times, honeydew falling from trees was sometimes interpreted as a substance descending from the heavens; some scholars have suggested it as a possible natural explanation for the biblical manna.
