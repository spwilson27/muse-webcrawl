Dignāga

Dignāga (also written Diṅnāga; c. 480 – c. 540 CE) was an Indian Buddhist philosopher and logician. He is credited with laying the foundations of pramāṇa-samuccaya, a branch of Buddhist epistemology and logic. He is one of the most prominent figures in the development of Indian logic (hetu-vidyā), and his work influenced both Buddhist and non-Buddhist Indian philosophy for centuries.

Bornc. 480 CE, Simhavakta near Kanchipuram, South India
Diedc. 540 CE

Life

Traditional accounts say Dignāga was born into a Brahmin family near Kanchipuram in South India and ordained as a monk, initially in the Vatsiputriya school, before studying with the great Yogācāra master Vasubandhu. He spent considerable time in Odisha and debated rival Hindu philosophers, gaining fame as a dialectician. Little of his biography is historically certain; most information comes from later Tibetan and Chinese sources, including accounts by the pilgrim Xuanzang.

Philosophy

Dignāga held that there are only two means of valid cognition (pramāṇa): perception (pratyakṣa), which apprehends bare particulars (svalakṣaṇa) free of conceptual construction, and inference (anumāna), which deals with universals (sāmānyalakṣaṇa). He excluded scriptural testimony as an independent means of knowledge, treating it as reducible to inference.

His theory of inference is built around the trairūpya, the "three modes" that a valid reason (hetu) must satisfy: it must be present in the subject of the inference, present in similar cases, and absent in dissimilar cases. Dignāga illustrated the relations between reasons and properties with the hetucakra ("wheel of reasons"), a ninefold matrix considered an early achievement in formal logic.

In his philosophy of language, Dignāga developed the apoha (exclusion) theory: words refer not to real universals but to the exclusion of everything other than the referent. This nominalist semantics allowed Buddhist philosophers to explain how language and thought can function while denying the existence of real universals.

Works

Of Dignāga's many treatises, most survive only in Tibetan and Chinese translation; the Sanskrit originals are largely lost. His magnum opus, the Pramāṇasamuccaya, systematised his epistemology. Other works include the Hetucakraḍamaru, Nyāyamukha ("Introduction to Logic"), Ālambanaparīkṣā ("Examination of the Object of Cognition") and the Marma-pradīpa. His student Īśvarasena taught Dharmakīrti, who became the other giant of the Buddhist logical tradition.
