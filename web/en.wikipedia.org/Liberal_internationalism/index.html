Liberal internationalism

Liberal internationalism is a foreign policy doctrine and tradition in international relations that holds that peace and prosperity are best secured through international institutions, free trade, democracy, and the rule of law among nations.

Origins

The doctrine draws on the thought of Immanuel Kant, whose essay Perpetual Peace (1795) proposed that republics bound by a federation and international law would avoid war with one another. It was championed in the 20th century by U.S. President Woodrow Wilson, whose Fourteen Points (1918) called for a League of Nations, open diplomacy, and national self-determination.

The liberal international order

After the Second World War, the United States and its allies built institutions embodying liberal internationalist principles, including the United Nations, the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank, and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. Theorists such as G. John Ikenberry describe this "liberal international order" as open, rules-based, and anchored by alliances and multilateral cooperation.

Criticism and challenges

Realist scholars argue that power politics, not institutions, ultimately governs state behaviour, while critics on the left contend the order has served Western interests. In the 21st century the doctrine has faced challenges from rising powers, populist nationalism, and debate over intervention and sovereignty.
