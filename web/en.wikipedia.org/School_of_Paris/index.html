School of Paris

The School of Paris (French: École de Paris) refers to the French and émigré artists who worked in Paris in the first half of the 20th century. The term was coined by the art critic André Warnod in 1925. The School of Paris was not a single movement or institution, but rather describes the loose community of artists — many of them foreign-born — who made Paris the center of the art world between the two World Wars, first in Montmartre and then in Montparnasse.

Artists

Artists commonly associated with the School of Paris include Amedeo Modigliani, Marc Chagall, Chaïm Soutine, Jules Pascin, Pablo Picasso, Constantin Brâncuși, Tsuguharu Foujita, and Marie Laurencin. Many arrived in Paris from Eastern Europe and elsewhere, were trained in a variety of styles, and exhibited at the Salon d'Automne and Salon des Indépendants. Some, including many Jewish artists who met at the studios of La Ruche, were later gathered under the label "École de Paris" by critics and dealers.

Characteristics

Unlike movements such as Cubism or Fauvism, the School of Paris had no unified style. Its painters ranged from expressionist and lyrical abstraction to figurative portraiture and landscape, bound together mainly by place and period rather than by a program. After World War II, a "New School of Paris" (Nouvelle École de Paris) of lyrical abstractionists — including Pierre Soulages, Nicolas de Staël, and Jean Bazaine — carried the term forward.

Legacy

The concentration of artists in Paris declined after the Second World War as New York became the leading center of avant-garde art. The term School of Paris remains a useful label for the city's cosmopolitan interwar art scene.
