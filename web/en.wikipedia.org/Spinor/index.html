In geometry and physics, spinors are elements of a complex vector space that can be associated with Euclidean or Minkowski space and that transform in a distinctive way under rotations: a spinor requires a rotation of 720°, rather than 360°, to return to its original state. Spinors are the basic mathematical objects describing particles with intrinsic angular momentum (spin), such as electrons and quarks.

Overview

Spinors were introduced into geometry by Élie Cartan in 1913, though their physical importance emerged when Wolfgang Pauli used two-component spinors to describe electron spin (1927) and Paul Dirac's relativistic wave equation (1928) required four-component spinors. Unlike vectors and tensors, spinors transform under the double cover of the rotation group — the spin group Spin(n) — rather than the rotation group itself; this is why the sign ambiguity under a full 360° turn appears.

Types

  • Pauli spinors — two-component objects describing non-relativistic spin-1/2 particles.
  • Dirac spinors — four-component objects combining particle and antiparticle degrees of freedom in relativistic quantum mechanics.
  • Weyl spinors — two-component chiral spinors, used for massless particles.
  • Majorana spinors — real spinors that can describe particles that are their own antiparticles.

Mathematical setting

The rigorous theory of spinors uses Clifford algebras: spinors form representation spaces ("spin representations") of the spin groups associated with quadratic forms. The concept generalises to arbitrary dimensions and signatures, and spin structures on manifolds are central to modern differential geometry and the Atiyah–Singer index theorem.
