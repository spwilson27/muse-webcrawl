Human intelligence (espionage)

Human intelligence (abbreviated HUMINT and sometimes pronounced as hyoo-mint) is intelligence gathered by means of interpersonal contact, as opposed to technical collection disciplines such as signals intelligence (SIGINT) or imagery intelligence (IMINT). It includes both overt collection — by diplomats, military attachés, and debriefers — and clandestine espionage conducted by case officers running recruited agents.

Clandestine HUMINT

In clandestine operations, an intelligence officer (case officer) spots, assesses, recruits, and handles agents — people with access to desired information who report in secret. Handling involves secure communication, meetings, and tradecraft to protect the source. Agents may be motivated by money, ideology, compromise, or ego (summarized by the acronym MICE). Some sources are "walk-ins" or "volunteers" who offer information, while defectors are intelligence personnel who switch sides.

Overt and other HUMINT

Much HUMINT is collected openly: interrogation and debriefing of refugees, prisoners, and travellers; liaison exchanges with friendly services; and reporting by official personnel abroad. Specialized sub-disciplines include interrogation science, elicitation, and the handling of liaison relationships.

Challenges

HUMINT sources can fabricate information, serve as double agents, or be manipulated by hostile counterintelligence. Notable historical cases range from the Cambridge Five and Aldrich Ames to the flawed human sourcing behind claims about Iraqi weapons of mass destruction before the 2003 invasion of Iraq. Modern services balance HUMINT against technical collection, and debates continue over the ethics and legality of coercive interrogation.
