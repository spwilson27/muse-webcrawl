British West Indies

The British West Indies (BWI) were the British territories in the West Indies: Anguilla, the Cayman Islands, Turks and Caicos Islands, Montserrat, the British Virgin Islands, Jamaica, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Antigua and Barbuda, Barbados, Dominica, Grenada, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and Trinidad and Tobago, together with the Bahamas, British Honduras (Belize), and British Guiana (Guyana) on the adjacent mainland coasts, which were often included in the term. The colonies were established from the early seventeenth century, beginning with St Kitts in 1624 and Barbados in 1627.

History

Britain's Caribbean colonies were built on plantation agriculture — above all sugar — worked first by indentured labourers and then by enslaved Africans transported across the Atlantic in the hundreds of thousands. By the eighteenth century the sugar islands were among the most valuable possessions in the British Empire. The Slavery Abolition Act 1833 ended slavery throughout the British colonies (with full freedom after the "apprenticeship" period ended in 1838), after which planters turned to indentured labour from India and China.

The colonies were administered in groupings such as the British Leeward Islands and British Windward Islands. In 1958 most of them joined the short-lived West Indies Federation, an attempt at political union as a step towards independence; the federation dissolved in 1962 after Jamaica and Trinidad and Tobago withdrew and became independent. Most of the larger territories achieved independence during the 1960s–1980s, while Anguilla, the British Virgin Islands, the Cayman Islands, Montserrat, and the Turks and Caicos Islands remain British Overseas Territories.

Legacy

The British West Indies left a shared institutional and cultural heritage across the Anglophone Caribbean: English language, common law, parliamentary government, Anglican and other churches, and cricket. The West Indies cricket team, drawn from the region's nations, became one of the great forces in world sport, dominating Test cricket from the late 1970s through the early 1990s. The University of the West Indies, founded in 1948, remains a regional institution.
