Osceola

Osceola (1804 – January 30, 1838), born Billy Powell, became an influential leader of the Seminole people in Florida and led their resistance during the Second Seminole War (1835–1842), one of the longest and costliest of the United States' Indian wars.

Life

Born to a Muscogee (Creek) mother in Alabama, Powell moved with his family to Spanish Florida after the Red Stick War, joining the Seminoles. Under the name Asi-yahola ("black drink singer," anglicized as Osceola) he rose as a war leader opposing removal to the West under the Indian Removal Act and the Treaty of Payne's Landing, which he regarded as fraudulent. The war opened in December 1835 with the ambush of Indian agent Wiley Thompson and the Dade battle. Osceola's guerrilla campaigns frustrated successive US commanders in the Florida swamps.

Capture and death

In October 1837 Osceola was seized while under a flag of truce during negotiations near Fort Peyton — an act widely condemned in the American press at the time. He was imprisoned at Fort Marion in St. Augustine and then Fort Moultrie in Charleston, where the artist George Catlin painted his famous portrait. He died there on January 30, 1838, probably of malaria and complications of a throat infection. His capture in violation of a truce remains a notorious episode in US–Native American relations, and Osceola remains a celebrated symbol of Seminole resistance; many places and institutions in Florida bear his name, and he figures as the symbolic leader of Florida State University's Seminoles.
