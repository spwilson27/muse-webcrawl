Elisabeth Kübler-Ross

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross (July 8, 1926 – August 24, 2004) was a Swiss-American psychiatrist, a pioneer in near-death studies, and the author of the internationally best-selling book On Death and Dying (1969), in which she first discussed her theory of the five stages of grief — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance (DABDA). Her work transformed the care of terminally ill patients and helped found the modern hospice and palliative care movements. She was named by Time magazine as one of the 100 most important thinkers of the 20th century.

Early life and career

Kübler-Ross was born in Zurich, Switzerland, in 1926, one of triplets. Defying her father's wish that she become a secretary in his business, she worked in a hospital laboratory, volunteered with refugees after the Second World War, and visited the Majdanek concentration camp, an experience that deeply affected her thinking about death and compassion. She studied medicine at the University of Zurich, graduating in 1957, married the American physician Emanuel Ross, and moved to the United States, where she trained in psychiatry in New York and Colorado before joining the University of Chicago's Pritzker School of Medicine.

On Death and Dying and the five stages

In Chicago, Kübler-Ross was dismayed by the way dying patients were avoided and neglected by medical staff. She began interviewing terminally ill patients — famously holding seminars in which patients spoke to an audience of doctors, nurses, and students from behind a one-way mirror — and from these interviews distilled the pattern published in On Death and Dying. The five stages describe common responses of people confronting their own death; Kübler-Ross later emphasised, with co-author David Kessler, that the stages were never meant to be a rigid linear sequence, that not everyone experiences all of them, and that they apply also to grief after loss generally. The model became one of the most influential — and most debated — frameworks in thanatology: empirical studies have found only partial support for fixed stages, and grief researchers favour more flexible accounts, but the model's role in legitimising open discussion of death is undisputed.

Later work

Kübler-Ross wrote more than twenty books, including On Children and Death and The Wheel of Life, her memoir. She founded the Shanti Nilaya healing centre in California and later a centre in Virginia, conducted workshops on life, death, and transition around the world, and advocated for hospice care and for patients with AIDS in the 1980s, proposing a controversial AIDS hospice for abandoned infants that local opposition blocked. Her later interests in near-death experiences and out-of-body phenomena drew criticism from the scientific mainstream, which she accepted as the cost of following her patients' reports. After a series of strokes from 1995, she lived in Arizona and died in Scottsdale in 2004. Her papers are held at Stanford University.
