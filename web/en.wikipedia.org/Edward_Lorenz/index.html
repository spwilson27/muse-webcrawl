Edward Norton Lorenz (23 May 1917 – 16 April 2008) was an American mathematician and meteorologist who founded chaos theory. His discovery that tiny differences in initial conditions can produce wildly different outcomes in weather models became known as the butterfly effect.

Discovery of chaos

Lorenz, a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, was running a simplified computer model of atmospheric convection in 1961 when he restarted a calculation using rounded numbers from a printout (0.506127 instead of 0.506127...). The new run diverged completely from the original, showing that deterministic systems can be sensitive to arbitrarily small perturbations. His 1963 paper "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow" introduced the Lorenz attractor, a three-equation system whose solutions trace a butterfly-shaped strange attractor.

Legacy

His work reshaped meteorology, establishing intrinsic limits on weather forecasting (the two-week horizon is often attributed to him), and founded chaos theory, which spread through physics, biology, economics and popular culture. The term "butterfly effect" comes from his 1972 talk, "Does the flap of a butterfly's wings in Brazil set off a tornado in Texas?" Lorenz received the Kyoto Prize in 1991.
