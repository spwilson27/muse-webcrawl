Chondrodystrophy

Chondrodystrophy (from Greek chondros, cartilage, and dystrophy, disordered growth) is a group of disorders of cartilage development, particularly of endochondral ossification, that result in abnormal bone growth and typically disproportionate short stature. The term historically overlapped with what are now classified among the skeletal dysplasias.

Mechanism

In endochondral ossification, long bones lengthen at the epiphyseal (growth) plates, where cartilage is progressively replaced by bone. Disturbances of chondrocyte proliferation, hypertrophy, or matrix production — often from mutations in genes such as FGFR3, collagens (COL2A1), or cartilage matrix proteins — produce shortened limbs, altered skull and spine development, and characteristic radiographic findings.

Types

The best-known chondrodystrophy is achondroplasia, caused by a gain-of-function mutation in FGFR3 and characterized by rhizomelic limb shortening, macrocephaly, and midface hypoplasia. Related conditions include hypochondroplasia, thanatophoric dysplasia (usually lethal in the newborn period), and various metaphyseal and epiphyseal chondrodysplasias. In animal breeding, chondrodystrophy is deliberately selected in dog breeds such as the dachshund and corgi, where it also predisposes to intervertebral disc disease.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis combines clinical examination, skeletal radiographs, and genetic testing. Management is supportive: monitoring for complications such as spinal stenosis and hydrocephalus, orthopedic care, and in some cases growth-promoting therapies or limb-lengthening surgery.
