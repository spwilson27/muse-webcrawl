The Hadley cell, also known as the Hadley circulation, is a global-scale tropical atmospheric circulation in which air rises at the equator, flows poleward at a height of 10 to 15 kilometres above the Earth's surface, descends in the subtropics, and then returns equatorward near the surface. This circulation creates the trade winds, tropical rain belts and hurricanes, subtropical deserts and the jet streams.

Mechanism

Intense solar heating near the equator warms surface air, which becomes buoyant and rises in deep convective towers within the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ). As the air rises it cools, its water vapour condenses, and heavy tropical rainfall results. At the tropopause the air spreads poleward; conserving angular momentum, it accelerates eastward, feeding the subtropical jet streams. By about 30° latitude the air has cooled enough to sink back toward the surface, producing the subtropical highs — belts of high pressure associated with the world's great deserts, such as the Sahara and the Australian Outback. The returning surface flow is deflected by the Coriolis effect, becoming the easterly trade winds.

History

The circulation is named after George Hadley, an English lawyer and amateur meteorologist who proposed the mechanism in 1735 to explain the trade winds. Hadley's model imagined a single cell spanning from equator to pole; the modern three-cell model — Hadley, Ferrel and polar cells — refines this picture. In the 1920s and 1930s, work by Tor Bergeron and others placed the Hadley cell within the broader general circulation of the atmosphere.

Seasonal variation and climate change

The Hadley cells of the two hemispheres strengthen and shift with the seasons, following the latitude of maximum solar heating. Observations and model studies indicate that the Hadley circulation has widened poleward by roughly 0.1–0.5 degrees of latitude per decade since the late twentieth century, a change associated with poleward shifts of the subtropical dry zones and storm tracks.
