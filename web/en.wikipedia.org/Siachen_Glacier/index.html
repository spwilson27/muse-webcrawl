The Siachen Glacier is a glacier located in the eastern Karakoram range in the Himalayas, situated just northeast of the point NJ9842 where the Line of Control between India and Pakistan ends. At 76 km (47 mi) long, it is the longest glacier in the Karakoram and the second-longest in the world's non-polar areas. It falls from an altitude of 5,753 m (18,875 ft) above sea level at its head at Indira Col on the border with China to 3,620 m (11,875 ft) at its terminus. The entire Siachen Glacier, with all major passes, has been under the administration of India since 1984 (Operation Meghdoot). Pakistan controls areas to the west of the Saltoro Ridge.

The name Siachen comes from the Balti words sia (wild rose) and chen (place), meaning the place of wild roses. The glacier lies in the disputed Kashmir region, claimed by both India and Pakistan.

Geography

The glacier is fed by numerous tributary glaciers, including the Teram Shehr and the Lolofond, and drains into the Nubra River, a tributary of the Shyok and ultimately the Indus. The Saltoro Ridge runs along its western side, with passes such as Sia La and Bilafond La giving access to the glacier. The region is one of the most inhospitable inhabited places on Earth, with winter temperatures dropping to −50 °C (−58 °F) and blizzards and avalanches common.

The Siachen conflict

Ambiguity in the 1949 Karachi Agreement and the 1972 Simla Agreement — which delimited the Line of Control only as far as point NJ9842, "thence north to the glaciers" — allowed competing interpretations of sovereignty over the glacier. In April 1984, India launched Operation Meghdoot and airlifted troops to occupy the key passes of the Saltoro Ridge. Pakistan attempted to dislodge them, most notably in 1987, but failed. The resulting confrontation has been called the world's highest battleground, with posts located above 6,000 metres.

More soldiers have died from the extreme weather and terrain than from combat; avalanches, such as the 2012 Gayari avalanche that killed 140 Pakistani soldiers and civilians, have caused the heaviest losses. A ceasefire along the Actual Ground Position Line has held since 2003, though both armies maintain large deployments. The military presence has caused significant environmental damage, including waste accumulation on the glacier, which is a vital water source for the Indus basin.

Exploration history

The glacier was first documented by the explorer Charles Granville Bruce and others in the early 20th century; the Workman couple explored it extensively in 1911–1912, naming features such as Rose Glacier. The first winter crossing and systematic surveys were made by later expeditions, and mountaineering parties visited regularly before the militarisation of 1984.
