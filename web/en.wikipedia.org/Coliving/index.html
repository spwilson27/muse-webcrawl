Coliving

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coliving is a type of shared housing in which residents — typically young professionals, digital nomads or students — rent private bedrooms (sometimes private apartments) while sharing kitchens, living spaces, workspaces and amenities, under arrangements managed by an operator that provides cleaning, utilities, internet and organised community activities bundled into a single payment. Coliving emerged in its modern commercial form in the 2010s in expensive cities and technology hubs, and is considered part of the sharing economy.

Characteristics

Coliving spaces emphasise convenience and community over the informal arrangements of traditional flatshares. Features typically include:

  • Flexible, short leases (often monthly) with furnished rooms and all bills included;
  • Large shared common areas, coworking spaces and sometimes gyms, cinemas or roof terraces;
  • A community manager who organises events and mediates between residents;
  • Application or interview processes intended to curate a compatible resident mix.

History

Shared living has long precedents — boarding houses, residential hotels, kibbutzim, communes and student dormitories — but the branded coliving model developed around 2013–2016 in cities such as San Francisco, New York and London, driven by high rents and a mobile workforce. Operators including WeLive (from WeWork), Common, Ollie, The Collective (London) and Shanti House raised substantial investment; several ventures later failed, most visibly The Collective's administration in 2021 and the collapse of WeWork's expansion, while the sector consolidated and expanded into purpose-built developments. The COVID-19 pandemic, remote work and the growth of digital-nomad visas renewed demand, especially in lower-cost destinations in Europe, Latin America and Southeast Asia.

Criticism

Commentators have criticised coliving for charging premium prices for small private spaces, for marketing communal living as a lifestyle product, and for precarious, hotel-like tenure. Supporters argue it combats urban loneliness, offers flexibility impossible in conventional rentals, and uses urban housing stock more efficiently.
