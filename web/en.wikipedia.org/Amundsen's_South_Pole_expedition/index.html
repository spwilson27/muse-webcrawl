Amundsen's South Pole expedition

The Norwegian flag planted at the South Pole on 14 December 1911.

The first expedition to reach the geographic South Pole was led by Norwegian explorer Roald Amundsen. He and four others — Olav Bjaaland, Helmer Hanssen, Sverre Hassel, and Oscar Wisting — arrived at the pole on 14 December 1911, five weeks ahead of a British party led by Robert Falcon Scott as part of the Terra Nova Expedition. Amundsen and his team returned safely to their base, and later learned that Scott and his four companions had died on their return journey.

Background

Amundsen had originally planned to conquer the North Pole, but after Frederick Cook and Robert Peary both claimed to have reached it in 1909, he secretly redirected his expedition southward. He used Nansen's ship Fram, established his base camp, Framheim, at the Bay of Whales on the Ross Ice Shelf — some 100 km closer to the pole than Scott's base at McMurdo Sound — and relied on skis and dog sledges, with carefully placed supply depots marking the route across the barrier.

The polar journey

After a false start in September 1911, the polar party set out on 19 October with four sledges and 52 dogs. They ascended the previously unknown Axel Heiberg Glacier to the polar plateau, and reached the pole on 14 December, where they spent three days taking observations to fix their position, leaving a tent — Polheim — and a letter for Scott. The party returned to Framheim on 25 January 1912, after 99 days and roughly 3,000 km (1,860 mi), with 11 dogs surviving. Amundsen announced the achievement publicly on 7 March 1912 from Hobart, Tasmania.
