Rock cycle

Rock cycle
ConceptContinuous transformation of rocks between the three main classes
Key processesMelting, cooling, weathering, erosion, compaction, cementation, heat and pressure
First describedJames Hutton, late 18th century

The rock cycle is the fundamental concept in geology describing how the three main types of rock — igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic — transform into one another over geological time through processes driven by plate tectonics, the Earth's internal heat, and surface weathering powered by the Sun and gravity.

The cycle

Magma cools and solidifies into igneous rock. Exposed at the surface, any rock is weathered and eroded into sediment, which is transported, deposited, buried, compacted and cemented into sedimentary rock. Under the heat and pressure of deep burial or mountain building, sedimentary or igneous rock becomes metamorphic rock. Buried still deeper, any rock may melt back into magma, completing the cycle. Short cuts are possible at every stage: metamorphic rock can weather directly to sediment, and the great ranges built of recycled crust — such as the Rocky Mountains — show the cycle in action.

History and significance

The cyclical view of Earth processes is associated with James Hutton, the 18th-century founder of modern geology, whose observation of unconformities at places such as Siccar Point revealed "no vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end." The rock cycle frames modern understanding of how continents grow, why sedimentary basins host oil, gas and groundwater, and how the long-term carbon cycle — through silicate weathering — regulates climate.
