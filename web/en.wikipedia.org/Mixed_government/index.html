Mixed government

Mixed government, or the mixed constitution, is a form of government that integrates elements of democracy, aristocracy, and monarchy into a single system. The theory, developed by Greek and Roman thinkers, holds that blending the rule of the one, the few, and the many produces stability, because each element checks the degeneration of the others.

Classical theory

Aristotle analysed constitutions and praised politeia, a blend of oligarchy and democracy. The fullest classical statement comes from Polybius, whose Histories attributed Rome's success to its mixture: the consuls supplied the monarchic element, the Senate the aristocratic, and the popular assemblies and tribunes the democratic. Cicero endorsed the mixed constitution in De re publica as the best practical form of government.

Later influence

The idea influenced medieval and early modern political thought, including the English constitution as analysed by Montesquieu, in which King, Lords, and Commons balanced one another. The framers of the United States Constitution drew on mixed-government theory, though they recast it through the separation of powers and federalism rather than through estates of the realm. John Adams was a prominent advocate of the balanced constitution in his Defence of the Constitutions of Government (1787).

Criticism

Critics from Machiavelli — who praised Rome's productive tumults — to modern democratic theorists have questioned whether institutionalising the power of social orders entrenches inequality. Nevertheless, the mixed constitution remains a foundational concept in the history of republican thought.
