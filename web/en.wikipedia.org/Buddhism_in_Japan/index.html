Buddhism in Japan

Introduced6th century CE, via Korea
Major schoolsZen (Sōtō, Rinzai), Pure Land (Jōdo, Jōdo Shinshū), Nichiren, Shingon, Tendai
Key figuresPrince Shōtoku, Kūkai, Saichō, Dōgen, Hōnen, Shinran, Nichiren

Buddhism in Japan began in the 6th century CE, when the Korean kingdom of Baekje presented the Japanese court with a statue of the Buddha and sutras, traditionally dated to 538 or 552 CE. Championed by Prince Shōtoku, Buddhism became intertwined with the Japanese state and culture, developing distinctive Japanese schools and deeply shaping the country's art, architecture and literature.

Early history

During the Nara period, the state sponsored great temples such as Tōdai-ji in Nara, with its colossal Great Buddha consecrated in 752. In the Heian period, Saichō founded the Tendai school on Mount Hiei and Kūkai founded the esoteric Shingon school on Mount Kōya, both drawing on teachings brought from China.

Kamakura Buddhism

The Kamakura period (1185–1333) saw a flowering of new movements emphasising simple, accessible practice: Hōnen and Shinran taught Pure Land devotion to Amida Buddha; Nichiren preached the Lotus Sutra; and Zen, transmitted by Eisai and Dōgen, found favour with the samurai class and gave rise to a distinctive aesthetic culture including the tea ceremony and ink painting.

Later history

Under the Tokugawa shogunate, temples were used in a registration system controlling the population and suppressing Christianity. After the Meiji Restoration, Buddhism briefly faced suppression in favour of State Shinto, but survived. Today most Japanese participate in Buddhist funeral and memorial customs alongside Shinto practices, and Japan's historic temples remain major cultural treasures.
