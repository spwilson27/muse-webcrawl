Biomimetics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biomimetics or biomimicry is the emulation of the models, systems and elements of nature for the purpose of solving complex human problems. Living organisms have evolved well-adapted structures and materials over geological time through natural selection, and biomimetics applies these strategies to engineering, materials science, architecture and design. The term biomimicry was popularised by Janine Benyus in her 1997 book Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature.

Examples

Perhaps the most famous example is Velcro, invented by George de Mestral after noticing how burdock burrs clung to his dog's fur. Other examples include: the "lotus effect" self-cleaning surfaces inspired by the water-repellent microstructure of lotus leaves; drag-reducing surfaces modelled on shark skin; the kingfisher-beak profile used to redesign the nose of the Japanese Shinkansen bullet train, reducing tunnel sonic booms; termite-mound ventilation principles applied to buildings such as Eastgate Centre in Harare; and gecko-inspired adhesives based on the setae of gecko feet. Neural networks and evolutionary algorithms apply biological principles to computation.

Approaches

Practice ranges from direct copying of form, through abstraction of underlying processes (such as photosynthesis for artificial leaves), to emulating whole ecosystems, for example in industrial symbiosis. Researchers distinguish "biology to design" — starting from an organism's solution — from "challenge to biology", which begins with an engineering problem and searches nature for analogous solutions.
