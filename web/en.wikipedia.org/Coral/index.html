Corals are colonial marine invertebrates within the class Anthozoa in the phylum Cnidaria. They typically form compact colonies of many identical individual polyps. Coral species that build reefs are known as hermatypic, or "hard", corals that extract calcium carbonate from seawater to create a hard, durable exoskeleton that protects their soft, sac-like bodies. Other species of corals that are not involved in reef building are known as "soft" corals.

Biology

Each polyp is a few millimetres in diameter, with a mouth surrounded by tentacles armed with stinging cells (nematocysts). Many reef-building corals harbour photosynthetic dinoflagellate algae called zooxanthellae in their tissues, which supply nutrients and give corals much of their colour; the expulsion of these symbionts under stress causes coral bleaching. Corals reproduce both sexually, through mass spawning events in which gametes are released simultaneously, and asexually by budding and fragmentation.

Reefs

Over thousands of years, coral skeletons accumulate into coral reefs, among the most biodiverse ecosystems on Earth, supporting an estimated quarter of all marine species. Major reef systems include the Great Barrier Reef off Australia and the reefs of the Coral Triangle and the Caribbean.

Threats

Corals are threatened by ocean warming and acidification from climate change, pollution, overfishing, destructive fishing practices, and disease. Mass bleaching events have become more frequent, causing widespread reef decline.

Human uses

Precious corals, especially red and pink Corallium species, have been harvested for jewellery since antiquity. Coral skeletons have also been used as building material and in traditional medicine.
