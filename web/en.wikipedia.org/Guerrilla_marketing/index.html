Guerrilla marketing

Guerrilla marketing is an advertisement strategy in which a company uses surprise or unconventional interactions to promote a product or service. The term was popularised by Jay Conrad Levinson's 1984 book Guerrilla Marketing, borrowing the idea of irregular tactics used by small forces from guerrilla warfare.

Principles

Guerrilla marketing substitutes imagination, energy and time for a large advertising budget, aiming to create memorable, buzz-worthy encounters that consumers share by word of mouth or social media. It is especially associated with small businesses competing against larger rivals, but major brands also mount large-scale stunts.

Types

Common forms include ambient marketing (ads in unusual places), ambush marketing (associating a brand with an event it does not sponsor), stealth marketing (promoting without revealing the commercial intent), street marketing, flash mobs and viral marketing campaigns designed for online sharing.

Examples and risks

Famous examples include the Blair Witch Project's fabricated mythology campaign (1999) and the Red Bull Stratos space jump (2012). Guerrilla tactics can also backfire or break the law: a 2007 promotion in Boston using battery-powered LED placards was mistaken for explosive devices, causing a major security alert and legal consequences for the marketers involved. Regulators in many jurisdictions require advertising — however unconventional — to be identifiable and lawful.
