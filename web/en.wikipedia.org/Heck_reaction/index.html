Heck reaction

The Heck reaction (also called the Mizoroki–Heck reaction) is a palladium-catalysed chemical reaction that couples an unsaturated halide (or triflate) with an alkene in the presence of a base to form a substituted alkene, creating a new carbon–carbon bond. Developed by Tsutomu Mizoroki in 1971 and independently by Richard F. Heck in 1972, it was one of the first palladium-catalysed cross-coupling reactions and remains a workhorse of organic synthesis. Heck shared the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for palladium-catalysed cross couplings.

Mechanism

The catalytic cycle begins with oxidative addition of the aryl or vinyl halide to Pd(0), giving an organopalladium(II) intermediate. The alkene coordinates and undergoes migratory insertion into the palladium–carbon bond; the regiochemistry of this step determines whether the linear or branched product predominates. Beta-hydride elimination then releases the substituted alkene, and the base (commonly triethylamine or an acetate) regenerates Pd(0) from the resulting hydridopalladium species. Ligands such as triphenylphosphine, and additives like tetrabutylammonium salts under "Jeffery conditions", tune rate and selectivity.

Scope and variants

The reaction tolerates many functional groups and is used to make styrenes, dienes and cinnamates, and in the industrial and laboratory synthesis of pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals — examples include steps toward the asthma drug montelukast and the herbicide prosulfuron. Variants include the intramolecular Heck reaction, valuable for building rings in natural-product total synthesis; reductive Heck reactions, which intercept the insertion product with hydride to give saturated compounds; and asymmetric versions using chiral ligands to set stereocentres during migratory insertion, pioneered by Shibasaki and Overman.

History and recognition

Mizoroki reported the arylation of alkenes with aryl iodides under palladium catalysis in 1971; Heck, working at the University of Delaware, generalised the method in the 1970s using organomercury and then halide substrates. Together with the Suzuki and Negishi couplings, the Heck reaction earned the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Heck, Ei-ichi Negishi and Akira Suzuki.
