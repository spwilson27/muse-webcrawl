Potential theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Equipotential lines visualise a harmonic potential in two dimensions.

In mathematics and mathematical physics, potential theory is the study of harmonic functions and of the potentials associated with them. The subject grew out of 19th-century physics, particularly the study of gravitation and electrostatics, in which forces could be represented as the gradient of a scalar potential satisfying Laplace's equation. The name "potential" derives from the fact that, in classical physics, the potential at a point in space is the potential energy per unit charge (or mass) at that point.

Potential theory

Field: Mathematical analysis, mathematical physics

Central objects: Harmonic and subharmonic functions, Green's functions

Key equation: Laplace's equation, Δu = 0

Overview

A harmonic function is a twice-differentiable function u satisfying Laplace's equation Δu = 0. Potential theory studies the properties of such functions: the mean value property, the maximum principle, and boundary-value problems such as the Dirichlet problem, which asks for a harmonic function inside a domain taking prescribed values on its boundary. Closely related notions include subharmonic and superharmonic functions, the Perron method for solving the Dirichlet problem, and the theory of capacity, which measures the "size" of a set in terms of its electrostatic properties.

Applications

Beyond its origins in gravitation and electrostatics, potential theory underlies parts of fluid dynamics (potential flow), probability theory — through the deep connection between harmonic functions and Brownian motion, since the probability that a Brownian particle exits a domain through a given part of the boundary is a harmonic function — and complex analysis, where the real and imaginary parts of holomorphic functions are harmonic. Modern ("nonlinear") potential theory extends these ideas to p-harmonic functions and to metric spaces.
