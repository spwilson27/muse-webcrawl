The Gutenberg Bible, also known as the 42-line Bible (from the number of lines on most pages) or the Mazarin Bible, was the earliest major book printed in Europe using mass-produced movable metal type. It was printed by Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz, Germany, in the mid-1450s, and marked the start of the "Printing Revolution" and the age of the printed book in the West. The Bible is a version of the Latin Vulgate; copies were sold unbound and unrubricated, with decoration and initials added by hand according to each buyer's taste and budget.

Production

Gutenberg's workshop printed about 180 copies over roughly two to three years, about a quarter of them on vellum and the rest on high-quality Italian paper. Each two-volume set contains about 1,282 pages. Analysis of the type shows work proceeded with several presses and compositors in parallel, and that the number of lines per page was increased from 40 to 42 early in production to save paper. The enterprise was financed by loans from Johann Fust; in 1455 Fust sued Gutenberg and took over much of the workshop's equipment and stock, completing remaining work with Peter Schöffer.

Surviving copies and value

Forty-nine copies survive in whole or in part; 21 are complete. Major collections are held by the British Library (two complete copies), the Library of Congress, the Bibliothèque nationale de France, the Gutenberg Museum in Mainz, and the Morgan Library, among others. Complete copies are among the most valuable printed books in the world: the last single volume sold at auction, in 1987, fetched $5.4 million, and a complete copy has been estimated to be worth tens of millions of dollars. No complete copy has changed hands since 1978.

Legacy

The Gutenberg Bible is prized both as a technological landmark and as a finely printed book rivalling the best manuscripts. The movable-type system Gutenberg perfected — hand mould, metal type, oil-based ink and screw press — spread to hundreds of European towns within decades, vastly reducing the cost of books and reshaping scholarship, religion and literacy. The Bible is listed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register.
