Crimean Khanate

Crimean Khanate
Status: Khanate; Ottoman vassal (1478–1774)
Capital: Bakhchysarai
Established: 1441
Annexed by Russia: 1783
Common languages: Crimean Tatar, Ottoman Turkish
Religion: Sunni Islam
Ruling house: Giray dynasty

The Crimean Khanate, self-defined as the Great Horde and Desht-i Kipchak, and in old European historiography and geography known as Little Tartary, was a Crimean Tatar state existing from 1441 to 1783, the longest-lived of the Turkic khanates that succeeded the empire of the Golden Horde. Established by Hacı I Giray in 1441, it was regarded as the direct heir of the Golden Horde and of Desht-i-Kipchak.

History

In 1475 the Ottoman Empire conquered the Genoese colonies on the southern Crimean coast, and from 1478 the Khanate was an Ottoman protectorate, although its Giray khans retained considerable autonomy and supplied cavalry for Ottoman campaigns. The Khanate's territory included the Crimean Peninsula and the steppes between the Dniester and the Don.

The Khanate was a major military power in Eastern Europe and conducted frequent slave raids into Ruthenian, Polish and Muscovite lands; the trading centre of Kaffa (Feodosia) was a major slave market. Crimean forces famously burned Moscow in 1571, though they were repelled the following year at the Battle of Molodi.

Decline and annexation

As Russian power grew in the 18th century, the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774) ended Ottoman suzerainty and made the Khanate nominally independent, a prelude to its annexation by the Russian Empire under Catherine the Great in 1783, the year the last khan, Şahin Giray, abdicated.

Society and culture

The khan's palace at Bakhchysarai (Hansaray) was the political and cultural centre. The population included Crimean Tatars, Greeks, Armenians, Karaites, Jews and others, and the Khanate's lands supported agriculture, trade and crafts alongside the nomadic steppe economy.
