Anemia

Anemia (also spelled anaemia; from Ancient Greek ἀναιμία, "lack of blood") is a blood disorder in which the blood's ability to carry oxygen is reduced, typically because of a lower-than-normal number of red blood cells or a reduction in the amount of haemoglobin in them. By World Health Organization criteria, anemia is defined by haemoglobin below 13 g/dL in men and 12 g/dL in non-pregnant women. It develops slowly or rapidly and may cause fatigue, weakness, pale skin, shortness of breath, dizziness and headaches; severe anemia can lead to heart failure. Globally, roughly a quarter of the world's population — about two billion people — is affected, with iron deficiency the single most common cause.

Causes and classification

Anemias are classified by red cell size (mean corpuscular volume): microcytic (small cells, e.g. iron deficiency, thalassaemia), normocytic (e.g. acute blood loss, anemia of chronic disease, aplastic anemia) and macrocytic (large cells, e.g. vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, alcohol use). Mechanistically they arise from blood loss, decreased red cell production, or increased red cell destruction (haemolysis), as in sickle cell disease, hereditary spherocytosis and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency.

Iron-deficiency anemia

Iron deficiency — from inadequate diet, malabsorption, menstruation, pregnancy or chronic gastrointestinal blood loss — is responsible for roughly half of all cases worldwide. It produces small, pale red cells and may cause pica (craving non-food substances), brittle nails and restless legs. Treatment is oral or intravenous iron plus management of the underlying source of loss.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on a full blood count showing low haemoglobin, supplemented by red cell indices, reticulocyte count, blood film examination and iron studies, vitamin levels or haemoglobin electrophoresis as indicated. In severe acute anemia, transfusion may be required; otherwise treatment targets the cause.

Epidemiology

Anemia disproportionately affects young children, women of reproductive age and people in low-income countries, where nutritional deficiency, malaria and intestinal parasites are common. It contributes to maternal and child mortality and impairs childhood cognitive development.
