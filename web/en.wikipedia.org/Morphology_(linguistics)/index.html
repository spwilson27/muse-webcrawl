Morphology (linguistics)

Analysis of the word "unbreakable" into the morphemes un- + break + -able.

In linguistics, morphology (from Greek μορφή morphḗ, "form, shape") is the study of words, including the principles by which they are formed, and how they relate to one another within a language. It analyses the structure of words and parts of words, such as stems, root words, prefixes, and suffixes. Morphology also looks at parts of speech, intonation and stress, and the ways context can change a word's pronunciation and meaning.

Morphemes

The smallest unit of meaning or grammatical function is the morpheme. Morphemes may be free (able to stand alone, like cat) or bound (affixes like un-, -ed). A single word may consist of one morpheme (dog) or many (unbelievability). The varying realisations of a morpheme in different environments are its allomorphs, as with the English plural pronounced /s/, /z/, or /ɪz/.

Inflection and derivation

Inflectional morphology modifies a word to express grammatical categories — tense, case, number, aspect — without changing its core lexical category (walk/walked, cat/cats). Derivational morphology creates new words or changes category, as in happy → happiness or teach → teacher. Compounding joins whole words (blackbird). Languages differ in how much they rely on these processes: analytic languages like English use relatively little inflection, while agglutinative languages like Turkish and fusional languages like Latin build long, morphologically rich words.

Approaches and history

The discipline descends from the ancient Indian grammatical tradition of Pāṇini and classical Greek and Latin grammar. Modern models include item-and-arrangement, item-and-process, and word-and-paradigm approaches; key 20th-century figures include Leonard Bloomfield and, later, generative morphologists such as Mark Aronoff. Computational morphology underlies spell-checkers, search engines, and machine translation.
