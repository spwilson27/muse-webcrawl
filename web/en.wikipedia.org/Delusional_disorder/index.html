Delusional disorder

Delusional disorder, traditionally known as paranoid disorder, is a mental illness in which a person has delusions but with no accompanying prominent hallucinations, thought disorder, mood disorder, or significant flattening of affect. Delusions are a specific symptom of psychosis. They can be bizarre or non-bizarre in content; non-bizarre delusions are fixed false beliefs that involve situations which could potentially occur in real life, such as being harmed or poisoned. Apart from their delusion(s), people with delusional disorder may continue to socialize and function normally, and their behavior does not necessarily seem odd.

Signs and symptoms

The primary feature is the presence of one or more delusions lasting a month or longer. Types are classified by their predominant theme: erotomanic, grandiose, jealous, persecutory, somatic, mixed, and unspecified. For example, in the persecutory type the person believes they are being conspired against or mistreated; in the jealous type, that a partner is unfaithful without evidence.

Causes and diagnosis

The exact cause is unknown, but genetic, biochemical, and environmental factors are believed to play a role. Diagnosis, per DSM-5 criteria, requires that the person has never met criteria for schizophrenia, that functioning is not markedly impaired apart from the impact of the delusions, and that the symptoms are not attributable to another medical condition, substance, or mental disorder.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment typically involves psychotherapy and, where accepted, antipsychotic medication. A major challenge is that people with the disorder often do not consider themselves ill and may not seek treatment. The prognosis varies; some recover fully, while in others the condition is chronic but stable.
