ClassBarbiturate

Thiopental, sold as sodium thiopental or Sodium Pentothal, is a short-acting barbiturate general anaesthetic. It was discovered in the early 1930s by Ernest Volwiler and Donalee Tabern at Abbott Laboratories and entered clinical use in 1934. Given intravenously, it produces unconsciousness within about 30 to 45 seconds, and a single dose lasts only five to ten minutes because the drug rapidly redistributes from the brain into fatty tissues.

Medical uses

Thiopental has been widely used for induction of general anaesthesia, for treating refractory status epilepticus, and for reducing intracranial pressure in neurosurgery. It acts by potentiating the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA at the GABAA receptor. Its use has declined in many countries in favour of propofol, partly because of supply problems: it is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

"Truth serum" and executions

Thiopental gained popular fame as a supposed "truth serum"; in sub-anaesthetic doses it lowers inhibitions and makes subjects talkative, but statements made under its influence are unreliable. In the United States it was long the first drug in the three-drug lethal-injection protocol, until manufacturers ceased supplying it for executions and states turned to alternatives.
