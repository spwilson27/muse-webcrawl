Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) is a molecular cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescent probes that bind to only particular parts of a nucleic acid sequence with a high degree of sequence complementarity. It was developed by biomedical researchers in the early 1980s to detect and localize the presence or absence of specific DNA sequences on chromosomes. Fluorescence microscopy can be used to find out where the fluorescent probe is bound to the chromosomes.

FISH
FieldMolecular cytogenetics
TargetDNA or RNA sequences in situ
DetectionFluorescence microscopy
UsesKaryotyping, cancer diagnostics, species identification, gene mapping

Probes

Probes are short lengths of single-stranded DNA or RNA, typically 20–1000 nucleotides, labelled with fluorophores. Whole-chromosome "paints", locus-specific probes, and repetitive-sequence probes are used for different purposes. Samples are denatured so the probe can hybridize to its complementary target, then washed to remove unbound probe before imaging.

Applications

FISH is widely used in clinical genetics and oncology: it detects the HER2 amplification used to select breast cancer therapy, the BCR-ABL fusion of chronic myeloid leukaemia, and chromosomal deletions and duplications in prenatal and developmental diagnosis. In microbiology, FISH with ribosomal RNA probes identifies and counts specific microorganisms in environmental and clinical samples without culturing them. Variations include multiplex FISH (M-FISH), spectral karyotyping, and RNA FISH for gene expression.
