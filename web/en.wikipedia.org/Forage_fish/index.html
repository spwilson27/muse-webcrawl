Forage fish

ExamplesAnchovies, sardines, herring, menhaden, capelin, sand lance
HabitatCoastal and open-ocean surface waters
RoleTransfer energy from plankton to larger predators

Forage fish, also called prey fish or bait fish, are small pelagic fish which are preyed on by larger predators for food. Predators include other larger fish, seabirds and marine mammals. Typical ocean forage fish feed near the base of the food chain on plankton, often by filter feeding. They include particularly fishes of the family Clupeidae (herrings, sardines, shad and menhaden), but also other small fish, including halfbeaks, and certain small schooling predatory fish such as anchovies and sardines.

Ecology

Forage fish compensate for their small size by forming large schools. They occupy a central position in marine food webs: they convert the production of phytoplankton and zooplankton into food for tuna, billfish, cod, seals, whales and seabirds. This mid-trophic role is sometimes called the "wasp-waist" of the ecosystem, because a relatively small number of species channel most energy flow.

Fisheries

Forage fish account for roughly a third of the world's total wild fish catch by weight. The Peruvian anchoveta fishery is among the largest single-species fisheries on Earth. Most of the catch is not eaten directly by people but reduced to fishmeal and fish oil, used in aquaculture feed, livestock feed and nutritional supplements. A smaller share is canned or sold fresh, as with sardines and herring.

Conservation

Because so many predators depend on them, scientists and conservation groups have argued for precautionary catch limits that leave enough forage fish in the water. Populations of forage species can fluctuate dramatically with ocean conditions such as El Niño, which has repeatedly crashed anchoveta stocks off Peru.
