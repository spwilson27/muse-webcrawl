Glucose-alanine cycle

The glucose–alanine cycle shuttles nitrogen from muscle to liver.

The glucose–alanine cycle, also called the Cahill cycle after George Cahill, is a series of reactions in which amino groups and carbons from skeletal muscle are transported to the liver. It is analogous in purpose to the Cori cycle but carries nitrogen as well as carbon: it allows the muscle to dispose of potentially toxic ammonia in the safe, non-toxic form of the amino acid alanine, while the liver converts the carbon skeleton back into glucose.

Mechanism

In muscle, amino acids are broken down for energy, especially during exercise and fasting. Their amino groups are transferred by transamination to pyruvate (itself derived from glycolysis), forming alanine. Alanine is released into the bloodstream and taken up by the liver, where alanine aminotransferase converts it back to pyruvate. The amino group enters the urea cycle for excretion as urea, while the pyruvate is used in gluconeogenesis to make glucose, which can be exported back to the muscle for energy.

Significance

The cycle therefore does two jobs at once: it transports nitrogen to the liver for disposal without releasing free ammonia into the blood, and it recycles the carbon skeletons of amino acids as glucose, helping maintain blood glucose during prolonged exercise and starvation at the cost of hepatic energy expenditure.
