Oneiroi

In Greek mythology, the Oneiroi (Greek: Ὄνειροι, "Dreams") were the personifications of dreams. They were the sons of Nyx (Night), born without a father, or in other accounts the children of Hypnos (Sleep) — and in Cicero's telling, the offspring of Erebus and Nyx. According to Hesiod's Theogony, the Oneiroi were among the many children of Night, siblings of such figures as Moros (Doom), Thanatos (Death), and the Keres.

Names and roles

Although the Oneiroi were usually an undifferentiated host of dream-spirits, later authors gave three of them individual names and functions:

  • Morpheus — the most famous of the Oneiroi, who took on human shapes in dreams and was the chief messenger of dream-visions to kings and heroes.
  • Phobetor (also called Icelus) — who appeared in dreams in the form of animals and other living creatures.
  • Phantasos — who produced dreams of inanimate objects, such as rocks, water, and trees.

Ovid describes the three in the eleventh book of the Metamorphoses, where they dwell with their father Somnus (Hypnos) in a dark cave in the land of the Cimmerians, near the stream of Lethe, where poppies grow and eternal dusk prevails.

In epic poetry

Homer presents dreams as passing through two gates in the underworld: true dreams issue from a gate of horn, while deceptive dreams come through a gate of ivory — a famous passage in Book 19 of the Odyssey, explained by Penelope. The Oneiroi were said to dwell beyond the streams of Oceanus, in the far west, near the borders of Hades. In Virgil's Aeneid, Aeneas likewise exits the underworld through the gate of ivory.

Cultural legacy

The Oneiroi survive in modern culture chiefly through Morpheus, who has lent his name to the drug morphine and appears as the character Dream (Morpheus) in Neil Gaiman's comic series The Sandman. The word "oneiric", meaning dreamlike, derives directly from the Greek oneiros.
