Compact space

In mathematics, and more specifically in general topology, compactness is a property that seeks to generalize the notion of a subset of Euclidean space being closed (containing all its limit points) and bounded (having all its points lie within some fixed distance of each other). A topological space is called compact if every open cover of the space has a finite subcover.

Definitions

A subset K of a topological space X is compact if, whenever K is covered by a collection of open sets, finitely many of those sets suffice to cover K. Equivalent characterizations in metric spaces include sequential compactness — every sequence has a convergent subsequence whose limit lies in the set. The Heine–Borel theorem states that a subset of Euclidean space is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

Properties

Compactness is preserved under continuous maps: the continuous image of a compact space is compact. Consequently, a continuous real-valued function on a compact space attains its maximum and minimum (the extreme value theorem). Closed subsets of compact spaces are compact, and compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed. The product of compact spaces is compact, a result known as Tychonoff's theorem.

Examples

Closed bounded intervals [a, b] of real numbers, spheres, and finite spaces are compact. The open interval (0, 1) and the whole real line are not compact. Compactness plays a fundamental role in analysis, geometry, and mathematical physics.
