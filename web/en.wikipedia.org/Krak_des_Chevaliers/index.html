Krak des Chevaliers (French pronunciation: [kʁak de ʃ(ə)valje]; Arabic: قلعة الحصن, Qalʿat al-Ḥiṣn) is a medieval crusader castle in Syria and one of the most important preserved medieval castles in the world. Perched on a hill some 650 metres above sea level near the Homs Gap — the strategic corridor between inland Syria and the Mediterranean — it is widely considered the archetypal Crusader castle, embodying the mature concentric design of 13th-century military architecture.

History

A first fortress was built on the site in 1031 by the Mirdasid emir of Aleppo. Crusaders of Raymond of Toulouse camped there in 1099, and in 1142 Raymond II of Tripoli granted the castle to the Knights Hospitaller, who rebuilt and vastly expanded it over the following century, after an earthquake in 1170 prompted major reconstruction. From Krak, a garrison of up to about 2,000 men controlled the surrounding countryside. The castle repelled attacks by Nur al-Din and Saladin, but in 1271 the Mamluk sultan Baibars took it after a siege — reportedly hastened by a forged letter ordering the garrison to surrender. The Mamluks repaired and used the fortress, after which it gradually declined into a village stronghold.

Architecture

Krak des Chevaliers consists of two concentric lines of defence: a high inner ward with massive round towers, a chapel (later converted to a mosque), a great hall, and deep cisterns, ringed by a sloped outer wall and glacis separated by a water-filled moat. Its storerooms could sustain the garrison through a siege of years. T. E. Lawrence called it "the finest castle in the world," and it was inscribed, jointly with the Citadel of Salah Ed-Din, on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2006. The castle sustained damage during the Syrian civil war, including airstrikes in 2013, but remains largely intact.
