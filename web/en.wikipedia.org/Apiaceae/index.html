Apiaceae, also called Umbelliferae, is a family of mostly aromatic flowering plants named after the type genus Apium. It includes many familiar herbs, spices, and vegetables—carrot, parsley, celery, parsnip, coriander (cilantro), cumin, fennel, dill, caraway, chervil, and anise—as well as deadly poisonous species such as poison hemlock (Conium maculatum), water hemlock, and the phototoxic giant hogweed.

Characteristics

Members of the family are mostly herbaceous plants with hollow stems, alternate and usually highly divided leaves with sheathing bases, and—most distinctively—inflorescences in the form of umbels: flower stalks radiating from a single point like umbrella ribs, often in compound arrangements. The small five-petalled flowers produce dry fruits (schizocarps) that split into two one-seeded mericarps, frequently ribbed and containing aromatic oil canals.

Distribution and uses

The family is distributed worldwide but centred in temperate regions, particularly Eurasia and North America. Economically it is among the most important plant families, supplying vegetables (carrot, parsnip, celery), culinary herbs (parsley, coriander, dill), spices (cumin, caraway, ajwain), and traditional medicines. Because edible and poisonous members can look similar, the family is a classic cautionary example in plant identification—the poison hemlock that reputedly killed Socrates resembles several harmless relatives.
