Mound

A mound is a heaped pile of earth, gravel, sand, rocks, or debris. Mounds may be natural features, formed by erosion, deposition or animal activity, but the term is most often used for artificial structures raised by human beings. Artificial mounds have served as burial places, platforms for buildings and temples, defensive works, ceremonial sites and boundary markers in cultures across the world.

Burial mounds

Burial mounds, known as barrows in Britain, tumuli in classical contexts and kurgans on the Eurasian steppe, cover graves or cremation deposits. They range from small heaps a metre across to vast monuments such as Silbury Hill in Wiltshire, England, the largest prehistoric artificial mound in Europe. In North America, the peoples collectively called the Mound Builders raised thousands of mounds in the Mississippi and Ohio valleys between roughly 3500 BCE and the sixteenth century CE, including effigy mounds shaped like animals and flat-topped platform mounds that carried temples and elite residences; Cahokia's Monks Mound is the largest pre-Columbian earthwork north of Mexico.

Other uses

Platform mounds supported public buildings in Mesoamerica, Southeast Asia and the ancient Near East, where ziggurats raised temples above the plain. Defensive mounds, such as the motte of Norman motte-and-bailey castles, carried a fortified tower. In sport, the pitcher's mound in baseball and the tee mound in golf are modest modern survivals of the term.

Natural mounds

Natural mounds include termite and ant mounds, the Mima mounds of North American prairies, and spring and mud-volcano mounds; the distinction between natural and artificial mounds is a recurring problem in archaeology, often resolved only by excavation.
