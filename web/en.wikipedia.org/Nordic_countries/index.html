The Nordic countries are a geographical and cultural region in Northern Europe and the North Atlantic comprising Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway and Sweden, together with their associated territories: Greenland, the Faroe Islands and Åland. The five states and three autonomous territories share extensive historical, cultural and linguistic ties, high levels of social welfare, and a tradition of intergovernmental cooperation formalised in the Nordic Council and the Nordic Council of Ministers.

Terminology

"Scandinavia" in English usage most often denotes Denmark, Norway and Sweden, whereas "the Nordic countries" (from Norden, "the North") covers the wider five-state group plus the autonomous territories. The Finnish language is not related to the North Germanic Scandinavian languages, but Finland's history under Swedish rule and shared institutions place it firmly within the region.

History

The region was united in part during the Viking Age and under the Kalmar Union (1397–1523). Sweden and Denmark-Norway were rival powers for centuries; Finland passed from Sweden to Russia in 1809 and became independent in 1917, while Iceland gained independence from Denmark in 1944. After the Second World War the states built a distinctive welfare model — the Nordic model — combining free-market economies with comprehensive welfare states, and created a passport union (1954) enabling free movement.

Politics and economy

The Nordic states are parliamentary democracies with proportional representation and frequent coalition governments. Denmark, Finland and Sweden are members of the European Union; Norway and Iceland belong to the European Economic Area. The countries consistently rank near the top of indices for press freedom, low corruption, gender equality and reported happiness.
