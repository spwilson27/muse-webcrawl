Siege of Alesia

The siege of Alesia was the climactic battle of the Gallic Wars, fought in September 52 BC around the Gallic oppidum of Alesia in modern France. Julius Caesar's Roman army besieged the Gallic forces of Vercingetorix, constructing an elaborate double ring of fortifications to starve the defenders while holding off a massive Gallic relief army. The Roman victory effectively ended organized Gallic resistance to Roman rule.

Background

Vercingetorix, king of the Arverni, had united many Gallic tribes in revolt against Rome. After a failed engagement at Gergovia and subsequent manoeuvring, Caesar pursued Vercingetorix's army to Alesia, a hilltop fortress of the Mandubii tribe, where the Gallic force took refuge with its cavalry and infantry.

The siege works

Caesar surrounded Alesia with a circumvallation about 16 km (10 miles) long — walls, towers, trenches, pits with sharpened stakes, and traps such as lilia and cippi — facing inward against the besieged. When a huge Gallic relief army approached, he built a second, outward-facing line, the contravallation, about 21 km long, placing his army between the two.

The battle

Over several days, the besieged Gauls and the relief army launched coordinated attacks on the Roman lines, most dangerously in the northwest sector where the terrain made the fortifications weakest. Caesar personally led reserves to the crisis points. When the attacks failed and supplies inside Alesia ran out, Vercingetorix surrendered; according to tradition, he rode out and laid his weapons at Caesar's feet.

Aftermath

The victory secured Roman control of Gaul, which became a Roman province. Vercingetorix was held prisoner in Rome for six years and executed after Caesar's triumph in 46 BC. The siege is described in Caesar's Commentarii de Bello Gallico and is considered a classic example of military engineering.
