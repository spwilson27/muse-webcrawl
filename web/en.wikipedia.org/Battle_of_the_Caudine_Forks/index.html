Battle of the Caudine Forks

Date321 BCE
LocationNear Caudium, Samnium, Italy
ResultSamnite victory; Roman army trapped and forced to surrender
BelligerentsRoman Republic vs. Samnites
CommandersConsuls Titus Veturius Calvinus and Spurius Postumius Albinus vs. Gaius Pontius

The Battle of the Caudine Forks (Latin: Furculae Caudinae) was an event of the Second Samnite War in 321 BCE in which a Roman army was trapped in a mountain defile near Caudium by the Samnites under Gaius Pontius and forced to surrender without a battle. The humiliation of the Romans, who were made to pass "under the yoke", became proverbial in Roman memory.

The defile where two Roman legions were trapped without a fight.

Background

Rome and the Samnite tribes of the Apennines were contesting control of Campania. In 321 BCE the consuls Veturius Calvinus and Postumius Albinus marched to relieve a supposed Samnite siege of Luceria in Apulia, taking a shortcut through a narrow valley near Caudium. The intelligence was a Samnite ruse: Pontius had planted disguised shepherds to mislead the Romans.

The trap

The valley had two narrow passes; when the Romans entered, they found the far exit blocked by felled trees and rocks, and turning back, found the entrance similarly sealed, with Samnite troops on the heights. Unable to fight or escape, and starving, the Romans surrendered. On the advice of his father Herennius, Pontius — rejecting both the options of releasing them unharmed to win friendship or massacring them to destroy Rome's power — humiliated them: the soldiers were stripped of their arms and made to pass under a yoke of spears, and the consuls agreed terms, giving 600 knights as hostages.

Aftermath

The Roman Senate refused to ratify the agreement, arguing that the consuls had no authority to bind the state without its consent, and handed the consuls over to the Samnites — who refused to accept them. Hostilities resumed, and Rome eventually won the Samnite Wars, but the disgrace of the Caudine Forks remained a byword for military humiliation; "to pass under the yoke" entered the language as a symbol of submission.
