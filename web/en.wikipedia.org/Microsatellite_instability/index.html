Microsatellite instability

Microsatellite instability (MSI) is the condition of genetic hypermutability (predisposition to mutation) that results from impaired DNA mismatch repair (MMR). The presence of MSI represents phenotypic evidence that MMR is not functioning normally.

Mechanism

Microsatellites are short, repeated sequences of DNA (such as CA repeats) that are prone to slippage errors during replication. A working mismatch repair system — involving proteins such as MLH1, MSH2, MSH6 and PMS2 — corrects these errors. When MMR is defective, insertion and deletion errors accumulate and microsatellites change in length from cell to cell, producing instability and a high overall mutation burden.

Cancer association

MSI is best known in colorectal cancer, where about 15 per cent of tumours are MSI-high. It occurs both sporadically (usually through silencing of the MLH1 gene by methylation) and in the hereditary condition Lynch syndrome, caused by inherited mutations in mismatch repair genes; Lynch syndrome also raises the risk of endometrial and other cancers. Tumours are classified by testing as MSI-high (MSI-H), MSI-low or microsatellite stable (MSS).

Clinical importance

MSI testing guides prognosis and treatment. MSI-high colorectal cancers have a somewhat better prognosis stage-for-stage but respond poorly to 5-fluorouracil chemotherapy. Because their many mutations generate neoantigens, MSI-high tumours are particularly responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitors such as pembrolizumab — the first cancer treatment approved by the US FDA on the basis of a genetic feature (MSI/dMMR status) rather than tumour location.
