Military
Also called: Armed forces
Function: Defence and warfare on behalf of a state
Typical branches: Army, navy, air force

A military, also known collectively as armed forces, is a heavily armed, highly organised force primarily intended for warfare. It is typically authorised and maintained by a sovereign state, with members identifiable by uniform, rank structure and discipline. Beyond war-fighting, militaries may support civil authorities in emergencies, conduct peacekeeping, and deter aggression.

Organisation

Modern armed forces are usually divided into service branches: an army for land warfare, a navy for maritime warfare, and an air force for air and space operations; some states add marines, coast guards, space forces or gendarmeries. Personnel may be professionals (volunteers), conscripts, or a mixture, supplemented by reserves. Command authority normally runs from a civilian head of government through a defence ministry to professional service chiefs.

History

Organised warfare is as old as civilisation, from the phalanxes of Greece and the legions of Rome to the gunpowder armies of early modern Europe. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries brought mass conscription, industrialised logistics and total war; the Cold War added nuclear deterrence. Today's largest militaries include those of China, India and the United States, and global military expenditure exceeds two trillion US dollars annually.

Society and law

Military service is governed by its own legal codes and by international humanitarian law, including the Geneva Conventions. The relationship between armed forces and civilian society — and the danger of military coups — is a central topic of civil-military relations.
