Noun incorporation

Noun incorporation is a grammatical process in which a noun forms a compound together with a verb, creating a complex verb. It is found in many polysynthetic languages, including languages of North America such as the Iroquoian languages, and also in languages such as Ainu, Chukchi, and some Austronesian languages. A classic example is a single-word verb meaning "meat-buy" or "house-build," in which the incorporated noun corresponds to what would be a direct object in an analytic construction.

Functions

Noun incorporation can background old or predictable information, allow new participants to be introduced with other arguments, or create conventionalized expressions for habitual activities. Linguist Marianne Mithun classified noun incorporation into four functional types, ranging from lexical compounding to the manipulation of discourse structure.

Analysis

The syntactic analysis of noun incorporation has been debated: some linguists treat it as a morphological compounding process, while others, following work by Mark Baker, analyse it as involving syntactic movement of a noun head into the verb. The phenomenon has been central to debates about the relationship between morphology and syntax.

Distribution

Noun incorporation is prominent in many Indigenous languages of the Americas, Siberia, and the Pacific, but is rare or absent in most European languages, where similar meanings are expressed by ordinary noun phrases or light-verb constructions.
