The Trades Union Congress (TUC) is a national trade union centre, a federation of trade unions in England and Wales, most of which are themselves "affiliated" to it. There are around 48 affiliated unions, with a total of about 5.5 million members. (Scotland has its own body, the Scottish Trades Union Congress.) The TUC's decision-making body is the annual Congress, held each September, and between congresses it is governed by a General Council.

History

The TUC was founded at a meeting in the Mechanics' Institute, Manchester, in June 1868, initially to coordinate campaigning for the legalisation of trade unions, achieved in 1871. It became the central voice of British labour, instrumental in creating the Labour Representation Committee in 1900 — the origin of the Labour Party — and in organising the General Strike of 1926, called in support of locked-out miners. In the postwar decades the TUC was a pillar of Britain's corporatist settlement, consulted by governments of both parties; its influence declined sharply under the Thatcher governments' union legislation and falling membership after 1979.

Role

The TUC represents its affiliates to government and employers, campaigns on pay, rights at work, and public services, coordinates disputes involving multiple unions, and provides education and research. Its headquarters, Congress House in Bloomsbury, London, opened in 1958 and is noted for the Jacob Epstein sculpture above its entrance commemorating the war dead of the trade union movement.
