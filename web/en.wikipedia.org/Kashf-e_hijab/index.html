Kashf-e hijab

Kashf-e hijab (Persian: کشف حجاب, lit. "unveiling") was a decree issued by Reza Shah, the Shah of Iran, on 8 January 1936, which banned the wearing of Islamic headscarves and veils (hijab and chador) in public. The policy, part of Reza Shah's programme of forced modernisation and Westernisation of Iran, remained in force until his abdication in 1941.

Implementation

Under the decree, police were ordered to remove veils by force from women who wore them in public, and veiled women could be barred from public places and government offices. Many religious women chose to stay at home rather than appear unveiled; some were humiliated or assaulted. The policy was deeply unpopular with the Shia clergy and conservative society, though it was welcomed by some urban, educated women and women's rights activists of the period.

Aftermath

After Reza Shah's abdication in 1941, the ban effectively lapsed and many women resumed veiling, making the veil a lasting symbol of religious and political identity in Iran. The decree is often contrasted with the mandatory veiling imposed after the Iranian Revolution of 1979, when the Islamic Republic compelled women to wear the hijab — the opposite form of state control over women's dress.
