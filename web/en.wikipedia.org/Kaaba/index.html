The Kaaba (Arabic: الكعبة, al-Kaʿba, 'the Cube'), also spelled Ka'ba or Kabah, sometimes referred to as al-Kaʿba al-Musharrafa, is a stone building at the centre of Islam's most important mosque, the Masjid al-Haram in Mecca, Saudi Arabia. It is the most sacred site in Islam and is regarded by Muslims as the Bayt Allāh ('House of God').

Religious significance

The Kaaba is the qibla — the direction Muslims face during prayer (salah) anywhere in the world. Pilgrims performing Hajj or ʿUmrah circumambulate the Kaaba seven times counterclockwise in the ritual of tawaf. Islamic tradition holds that the Kaaba was built or rebuilt by the prophet Ibrahim (Abraham) and his son Ismail (Ishmael), and that the Black Stone (al-Ḥajar al-Aswad), set into its eastern corner, was given to Ibrahim by the angel Jibril.

History

In pre-Islamic Arabia the Kaaba was a shrine housing idols of Arabian deities. After the Conquest of Mecca in 630 CE, Muhammad removed the idols and rededicated the building to the worship of God alone. The structure has been rebuilt several times — by the Quraysh before Islam, by Abdullah ibn al-Zubayr in the 7th century, and comprehensively under the Ottoman sultan Murad IV in 1631 after flood damage.

Architecture and features

The building is roughly cuboid, about 13.1 m high, with sides about 11.0 × 12.9 m, built of granite from the hills near Mecca on a marble base. It is draped in the kiswa, a black silk cloth embroidered with gold Quranic calligraphy, replaced annually. Other notable features include the golden door (Bab ar-Rahmah), the Mizab (gold waterspout), the semi-circular Hijr Ismail wall, and the Station of Ibrahim nearby.
