Two-phase commit protocol

The coordinator solicits votes, then broadcasts the decision
FieldDistributed computing, databases
PurposeAtomic commitment of distributed transactions
PhasesPrepare (voting), Commit (completion)

In transaction processing, databases, and computer networking, the two-phase commit protocol (2PC) is a type of atomic commitment protocol. It is a distributed algorithm that coordinates all the processes that participate in a distributed atomic transaction on whether to commit or abort (roll back) the transaction. This protocol achieves its goal even in many cases of temporary system failure, and is thus widely used. However, it is not resilient to all possible failure combinations, and in rare cases manual intervention is needed to remedy an outcome.

Algorithm

The protocol proceeds in two phases. In the commit-request phase (or voting phase), the coordinator asks all participating sites to prepare to commit the transaction and to vote yes or no. Each participant executes the transaction up to the point where it can be committed, writes the necessary records to its log, and replies with its vote. In the commit phase, if all participants voted yes, the coordinator instructs all of them to commit; if any voted no, it instructs all of them to abort. Participants then complete the operation and acknowledge it.

Disadvantages

The protocol is a blocking protocol: if the coordinator fails permanently after participants have voted and are waiting for the decision, those participants must hold locks and remain in doubt until the coordinator recovers. This can reduce availability. Variants include the three-phase commit protocol, which reduces blocking at the cost of additional messages, and Paxos-based commit protocols.
