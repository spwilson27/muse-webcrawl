Denim

Denim is a sturdy cotton warp-faced textile in which the weft passes under two or more warp threads. This twill weaving produces a diagonal ribbing that distinguishes it from cotton duck. Denim is available in a range of colours, but the most commonly found is indigo denim, in which the warp thread is dyed while the weft thread is left white — giving blue jeans their characteristic look, blue on the outside and pale on the inside.

The diagonal twill weave and indigo warp of classic denim

Etymology

The name derives from the French serge de Nîmes, "serge from Nîmes", the city where the fabric was produced; the related word jean comes from Genoa, whose sailors wore a similar cloth.

History

Denim's modern story begins in the 19th-century United States. In 1873, Jacob W. Davis and Levi Strauss received a patent for rivet-reinforced work trousers made of denim, and Levi's jeans became the standard garment of miners, cowboys, and labourers. After the Second World War, jeans spread worldwide as casual wear and a symbol of youth culture, and denim became one of the most ubiquitous fabrics on earth.

Manufacture and dyeing

Traditional denim is dyed with indigo, historically from plants and now almost entirely synthetic; because indigo coats the yarn surface rather than penetrating it, denim fades distinctively with wear. Weaving was traditionally done on shuttle looms producing selvedge denim, now prized by enthusiasts, though most output comes from faster projectile looms.

Environmental concerns

Denim production is water-intensive, from cotton irrigation to dyeing and finishing treatments such as stonewashing and sandblasting; the industry has faced criticism over water use, chemical discharge, and worker health, prompting moves toward laser finishing and recycled cotton.
