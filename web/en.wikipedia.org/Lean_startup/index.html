Lean startup

The lean startup methodology is an approach to building businesses and products that aims to shorten product development cycles and rapidly discover whether a proposed business model is viable. It does so through a combination of business-hypothesis-driven experimentation, iterative product releases, and validated learning. Developed by entrepreneur Eric Ries, drawing on lean manufacturing, Steve Blank's customer development method, and agile software development, the methodology was popularized by Ries's 2011 book The Lean Startup.

Core principles

Lean startup treats a startup's products, markets and strategy as untested hypotheses. Instead of executing a fixed business plan, the company quickly builds a minimum viable product (MVP) — the smallest version of the product that allows a full turn of the Build–Measure–Learn loop — and uses measured customer feedback to decide whether to persevere or pivot (make a structured course correction). Ries distinguishes vanity metrics from actionable metrics and proposes "innovation accounting" to track progress.

Origins

Ries developed the approach while working at the social-virtual-world startup IMVU (founded 2004), where rapid release cycles and continuous deployment replaced long development phases. He began blogging the method in 2008 and coined the term "lean startup" by analogy with lean manufacturing. Steve Blank, Ries's mentor and investor, contributed the customer development framework, which his student formalized as "get out of the building" and later codified in The Startup Owner's Manual.

Adoption

The method spread widely in Silicon Valley and beyond; companies such as Dropbox (whose explainer-video MVP is a canonical case study), Zappos and IMVU are frequently cited examples. Lean startup concepts have been adopted by large corporations ("intrapreneurship"), government innovation programs (e.g., the U.S. NSF I-Corps), nonprofits and business schools, where they are taught in entrepreneurship curricula.

Criticism

Critics argue the methodology can encourage short-term thinking, may be ill-suited to heavily regulated or capital-intensive industries, and is sometimes reduced to slogans (e.g., that an MVP is simply a poor-quality first release). Others note that many cited success stories are retrospective and that the evidence base for the method's superiority is thin. Defenders respond that lean startup is a framework for reducing waste under extreme uncertainty, not a guarantee of success.
