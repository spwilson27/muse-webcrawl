Notre Dame school

Periodc. 1160 – 1250
LocationNotre-Dame Cathedral, Paris
Key figuresLéonin, Pérotin
GenresOrganum, conductus, motet

The Notre Dame school refers to the group of composers working at or near the Cathedral of Notre-Dame in Paris from about 1160 to 1250, whose innovations gave rise to the first great body of written polyphony in Europe. Their music is sometimes grouped under the label ars antiqua.

The Magnus liber and its masters

According to the English theorist Anonymous IV, the singer Léonin compiled the Magnus liber organi, a cycle of two-part organa for the liturgical year, and Pérotin revised and expanded it, composing organa in three and four parts for the cathedral's great feasts. In these works the chant is stretched into long sustained tenor notes beneath melismatic upper voices, or broken into measured discant sections using the rhythmic modes.

Techniques and influence

The Notre Dame composers pioneered measured notation, in which rhythm is specified by note shapes grouped in modal patterns, freeing composers to coordinate up to four independent voices. Their genres — organum, conductus, and the emerging motet — spread across Europe through manuscripts copied as far away as Scotland and Spain, and their methods shaped sacred music composition for centuries.
