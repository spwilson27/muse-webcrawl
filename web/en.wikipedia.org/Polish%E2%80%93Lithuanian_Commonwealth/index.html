Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth

Years1569–1795
CapitalKraków, later Warsaw
GovernmentHereditary and elective monarchy with a parliament (Sejm)
LanguagesPolish, Latin, Lithuanian, Ruthenian, others

The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, formally known as the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, was a bi-confederal state, sometimes called a federation, of Poland and Lithuania ruled by a common monarch in real union, who was both King of Poland and Grand Duke of Lithuania. It was one of the largest and most populous countries of 16th- to 17th-century Europe.

Established by the Union of Lublin in 1569, the Commonwealth was notable for its political system, often called the Golden Liberty, in which the nobility (szlachta) elected the king and exercised extensive rights, including the liberum veto.

History

At its peak in the early 17th century, the Commonwealth covered almost 1 million square kilometres and sustained a multi-ethnic population of about 12 million. It weakened during the mid-17th-century Deluge and was gradually partitioned by Prussia, Austria, and Russia, disappearing from the map in 1795 with the Third Partition.

Legacy

The Commonwealth is remembered for religious tolerance, codified by the Warsaw Confederation of 1573, and for its early experiment in constitutional monarchy.
