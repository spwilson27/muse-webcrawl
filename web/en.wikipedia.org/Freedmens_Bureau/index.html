The Freedmen's Bureau, formally the Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands, was a United States federal agency established on 3 March 1865, in the final weeks of the Civil War, to aid freed slaves (freedmen) and war refugees in the former Confederate states during Reconstruction. Headed by General Oliver Otis Howard, it was one of the federal government's first large-scale social welfare efforts.

Activities

The Bureau issued food and medical aid, operated hospitals, reunited families separated by slavery, legalized marriages, and helped freedpeople negotiate labour contracts with employers. It supervised abandoned and confiscated lands, though President Andrew Johnson's restoration of land to pardoned former owners largely ended hopes of land redistribution ("forty acres and a mule"). Bureau courts provided a measure of legal protection where state courts discriminated against Black litigants.

Education

Its most enduring achievement was education. Working with Northern aid societies and Black communities, the Bureau built or supported thousands of schools and helped found institutions that became historically Black colleges and universities, including Howard University (named for the commissioner), Fisk University, and Hampton Institute. By 1870 more than 1,000 Bureau-supported schools served roughly 150,000 pupils.

Opposition and end

The Bureau faced violent white resistance, chronic underfunding, and few agents on the ground. Johnson vetoed bills extending it (Congress overrode the veto in 1866), and its operations were wound down by 1869–1872. Historians regard the Bureau as an unprecedented but ultimately constrained experiment in federal protection of civil rights.
