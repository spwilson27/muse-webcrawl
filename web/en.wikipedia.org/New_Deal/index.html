New Deal

President Franklin D. Roosevelt signing legislation in 1933

The New Deal was a series of programs, public work projects, financial reforms, and regulations enacted by President Franklin D. Roosevelt in the United States between 1933 and 1938, with the goal of providing relief, recovery, and reform during the Great Depression. It responded to needs for relief of the unemployed and poor, recovery of the economy, and reform of the financial system to prevent a repeat depression.

Roosevelt's first term, known for the "First New Deal" (1933–1934) and the "Second New Deal" (1935–1936), produced a wide array of federal programs and agencies such as the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), the Civil Works Administration (CWA), the Farm Security Administration (FSA), the National Industrial Recovery Act of 1933 (NIRA), and the Social Security Administration (SSA). The programs gave direct relief to the unemployed in the form of jobs in public work projects, aid to farmers and rural communities, and financial reforms including the Emergency Banking Act, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), and the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC).

The First Hundred Days

Roosevelt took office on March 4, 1933, amid a banking crisis in which thousands of banks had failed. He declared a national "bank holiday" and convened Congress in a special session. During the famous "First Hundred Days," Congress passed a flurry of legislation, including the Emergency Banking Act, the Economy Act, and laws creating the CCC, the Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA), the Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), and the Public Works Administration (PWA). These measures aimed to stabilize the financial system, put people to work, and stimulate industrial and agricultural recovery.

The Second New Deal

Beginning in 1935, the Second New Deal shifted emphasis toward social welfare and labor rights. Key measures included the Works Progress Administration (WPA), which employed millions of people on public works projects; the National Labor Relations Act (the Wagner Act), which guaranteed workers the right to organize and bargain collectively; and the Social Security Act, which created old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and aid to dependent children. In 1938 the Fair Labor Standards Act established a minimum wage and maximum hours, prohibited most child labor, and required overtime pay.

Opposition and legacy

The New Deal drew opposition from business leaders, conservative Democrats, and Republicans, who criticized its expansion of federal power and deficit spending. The Supreme Court struck down several early programs, prompting Roosevelt's controversial 1937 "court-packing" plan, which failed but was followed by a shift in the Court's jurisprudence. Economists and historians continue to debate the New Deal's effectiveness in ending the Depression—unemployment remained high until wartime mobilization—but it fundamentally reshaped the role of the federal government, creating the modern American welfare and regulatory state whose programs, including Social Security and deposit insurance, endure today.
