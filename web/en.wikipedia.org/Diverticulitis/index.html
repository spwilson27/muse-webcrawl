Diverticulitis

SpecialtyGastroenterology, general surgery
SymptomsAbdominal pain, fever, nausea, diarrhoea, constipation, blood in the stool
ComplicationsAbscess, perforation, fistula, peritonitis
Risk factorsObesity, lack of exercise, smoking, family history, low-fibre diet

Diverticulitis is a digestive disease characterised by inflammation of abnormal pouches—diverticula—that develop in the wall of the large intestine. Symptoms typically include abdominal pain of sudden onset, commonly in the lower left side, although the pain may be in the lower right side, particularly in people of Asian descent. Other symptoms may include fever, nausea, diarrhoea or constipation, and blood in the stool. Complications can include abscess formation, perforation of the bowel, fistulas and peritonitis.

The presence of diverticula without inflammation is known as diverticulosis. Diverticula are believed to form at weak points in the colonic wall under increased pressure, and diverticulitis occurs when one or more of these pouches becomes inflamed, possibly due to obstruction by faecal material and subsequent bacterial overgrowth or microperforation.

Signs and symptoms

The most common symptom is abdominal pain, classically in the left lower quadrant in Western populations. The pain is usually constant and may be accompanied by fever, raised white blood cell count, nausea, and a change in bowel habit. In older patients the presentation may be more subtle. Complicated diverticulitis may present with signs of sepsis or an acute abdomen.

Causes and risk factors

Diverticular disease becomes more common with age in Western countries. Factors associated with diverticulitis include obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, a family history of the disease, and the use of certain medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids and opioids. Diets low in fibre and high in red meat have long been associated with the condition, although the role of dietary nuts, seeds and corn—once thought to lodge in diverticula—is no longer considered significant.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually suggested by the symptoms and physical examination and is confirmed by computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis, which can show bowel wall thickening, inflamed diverticula and complications such as abscesses. Blood tests typically show raised inflammatory markers. Colonoscopy is generally avoided during an acute episode because of the risk of perforation, but may be performed weeks later to exclude other conditions such as colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease.

Treatment

Mild, uncomplicated diverticulitis may be treated with rest, dietary modification and observation; antibiotics, once used routinely, are increasingly reserved for selected patients. Severe or complicated attacks require hospitalisation, intravenous fluids and antibiotics, and abscesses may be drained under imaging guidance. Surgery—such as a Hartmann procedure or primary resection with anastomosis—is required for free perforation, generalised peritonitis, or failure of medical management, and may be considered electively after recurrent attacks.

Epidemiology

Diverticulosis affects a large proportion of older adults in Western countries, and roughly 4–5% of people with diverticulosis develop diverticulitis in their lifetime. The condition is less common in rural Africa and Asia, where high-fibre diets predominate, though rates are rising worldwide as populations age and adopt Western lifestyles.
