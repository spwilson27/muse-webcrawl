Theoretical physics

Theoretical physics is a branch of physics that employs mathematical models and abstractions of physical objects and systems to rationalise, explain and predict natural phenomena. This is in contrast to experimental physics, which uses experimental tools to probe these phenomena. Theoretical physicists use mathematics and concepts abstracted from observation and experiment to construct theories — systems of ideas, often expressed in equations — whose predictions can then be tested.

Overview

The progress of science generally depends on the interplay between experimental studies and theory. Mainstream theories include classical mechanics, electromagnetism, statistical mechanics, thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, relativity and quantum field theory. Theoretical work ranges from the nearly empirical modelling of complex systems (for example in condensed matter physics) to speculative frameworks such as string theory and quantum gravity that attempt to unify the fundamental forces and reconcile quantum mechanics with general relativity.

History

Physics became increasingly mathematical from the time of Newton and Leibniz. The nineteenth century brought Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism; the twentieth, Einstein's special and general theories of relativity and the quantum mechanics of Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger and Dirac. Celebrated theorists such as Feynman, Landau and Hawking combined new mathematics with physical intuition, while theory sometimes ran far ahead of experiment — antimatter, the Higgs boson and gravitational waves were all predicted theoretically decades before their detection.
