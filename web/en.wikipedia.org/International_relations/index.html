International relations (IR) is the study of the relationships among countries, the roles of sovereign states, intergovernmental organizations, non-governmental organizations, and multinational corporations. It is an academic and public policy field, and can be either positive or normative as it both seeks to analyze as well as formulate the foreign policy of particular states.

History

The modern state system is often traced to the Peace of Westphalia of 1648, which established principles of territorial integrity and state sovereignty. International relations emerged as a distinct academic discipline after the First World War, with the founding of the first chair in international politics at Aberystwyth University in 1919. The devastation of the Second World War and the onset of the Cold War spurred the growth of the field, particularly in the United States.

Major theories

Realism emphasizes the anarchic structure of the international system and the pursuit of power and security by states; prominent realists include Hans Morgenthau and Kenneth Waltz, whose neorealism focused on the distribution of capabilities. Liberalism stresses cooperation, international institutions, democracy, and economic interdependence as paths to peace. Constructivism, associated with Alexander Wendt, argues that international politics is socially constructed through ideas, norms, and identities. Other approaches include Marxism, feminism, and the English School.

Key topics

The field covers diplomacy, international law, war and peace, international political economy, globalization, human rights, environmental politics, and security studies. Levels of analysis range from the individual decision-maker, to the state, to the international system as a whole.
