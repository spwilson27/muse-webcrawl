Language of thought

The language of thought hypothesis (LOTH), also called the mental language hypothesis, is a view in philosophy of mind and cognitive science holding that thought occurs in a mental language, often called Mentalese, which has a compositional, language-like structure. The hypothesis was developed most prominently by the philosopher Jerry Fodor, beginning with his 1975 book The Language of Thought.

The hypothesis

According to LOTH, mental representations are not images or raw neural activations but symbols structured like sentences: they have constituents combined according to syntactic rules, and their meanings are determined compositionally — the meaning of a complex representation is a function of the meanings of its parts and their mode of combination. Thinking is then a kind of computation: causal processes operate on mental sentences in virtue of their syntactic form, mirroring how a computer manipulates symbols.

Arguments

Fodor offered several classic arguments. Productivity: a finite mind can entertain infinitely many distinct thoughts, which is best explained by a finite stock of primitive concepts combined recursively. Systematicity: anyone who can think "John loves Mary" can also think "Mary loves John," suggesting thoughts share common constituents. The hypothesis also explains how non-linguistic animals and pre-linguistic infants might think, since Mentalese is not a natural language but a more basic representational medium into which natural languages are learned and translated.

Criticisms

Connectionist models of cognition challenge LOTH by proposing that cognition arises from distributed patterns of activation in neural networks without discrete, sentence-like symbols. Philosophers have also questioned whether the systematicity and productivity arguments are decisive, whether Mentalese generates a regress (a language for interpreting Mentalese), and whether concepts really have the classical definitional structure Fodor assumed — an issue Fodor himself later pressed with his informational atomism.
