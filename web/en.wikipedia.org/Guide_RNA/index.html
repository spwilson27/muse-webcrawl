Guide RNA

Guide RNA (gRNA) is a small RNA molecule that directs a protein — typically a nucleic-acid-modifying enzyme — to a specific target sequence. The term is used in two main contexts: the RNA editing of kinetoplastid protozoa, where guide RNAs were first discovered, and CRISPR-based systems, where a guide RNA steers the Cas9 nuclease to a chosen DNA sequence.

In RNA editing

Guide RNAs were identified in the early 1990s in the mitochondria (kinetoplasts) of trypanosomes such as Trypanosoma brucei. There, many messenger RNAs are extensively edited by insertion and deletion of uridine residues. Short guide RNAs, encoded largely by minicircles of kinetoplast DNA, base-pair with the pre-edited mRNA and specify exactly where uridines are added or removed by the editosome enzyme complex, thereby creating the mature, translatable message.

In CRISPR systems

In the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 immune system, the guide consists of two RNAs: a CRISPR RNA (crRNA), whose spacer sequence matches the target, and a trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA) that scaffolds binding to Cas9. For laboratory use, the two are fused into a single guide RNA (sgRNA), a key simplification in the 2012 work of Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier, and colleagues. By simply changing the roughly 20-nucleotide spacer sequence, researchers can programme Cas9 to cut almost any DNA site adjacent to a short PAM motif, making guide RNA design the central step in genome-editing experiments. Related usages include guide RNAs for Cas12 and Cas13 enzymes, prime editing (where the pegRNA also encodes the desired edit), and RNA-targeting systems.
