Spinal cord injury

A spinal cord injury (SCI) is damage to the spinal cord that causes temporary or permanent changes in its function. Symptoms may include loss of muscle function, sensation, or autonomic function in the parts of the body served by the spinal cord below the level of the injury.

Causes

The majority of spinal cord injuries are caused by trauma to the vertebral column, including motor vehicle accidents, falls, acts of violence (mainly gunshot wounds), and sports injuries. Non-traumatic causes include tumors, infections, and degenerative diseases.

Classification

Injuries may be complete, in which all sensation and ability to control movement are lost below the level of injury, or incomplete, in which some function remains. Damage to the cord at the cervical level can cause tetraplegia (quadriplegia); injuries below that can cause paraplegia. The American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale grades severity from A (complete) to E (normal).

Treatment

Treatment begins with restricting further motion of the spine and maintaining adequate blood pressure. Rehabilitation focuses on maximizing independence and managing complications such as pressure sores, infections, and spasticity. Research into stem cells, neuroprotection, and brain–computer interfaces continues.

Epidemiology

Globally, between 250,000 and 500,000 people suffer a spinal cord injury each year. Males are affected at least twice as often as females.
