Coronary angiography

Coronary angiography is a procedure that uses X-ray imaging to see the heart's coronary arteries. A catheter is threaded through the radial or femoral artery to the origins of the coronary arteries, and a radiocontrast agent is injected so that the arteries can be visualised by fluoroscopy. The resulting images, called coronary angiograms, reveal narrowing (stenosis) or blockage caused by atherosclerosis and guide treatment decisions such as angioplasty with stenting or coronary artery bypass surgery.

Procedure

The procedure is usually performed under local anaesthesia with light sedation in a catheterisation laboratory. Access is most commonly gained through the radial artery in the wrist or the femoral artery in the groin. A thin catheter is advanced under X-ray guidance to the aortic root and selectively engaged with each coronary ostium. Contrast injections are filmed in several projections to map the arterial tree; pressures may be measured and left ventriculography may be performed to assess ventricular function.

History and uses

Selective coronary angiography was pioneered by Mason Sones at the Cleveland Clinic, after an accidental selective injection in 1958 demonstrated that the coronary arteries could be safely opacified; Melvin Judkins subsequently developed a percutaneous femoral approach. The technique remains the reference standard for diagnosing coronary artery disease, though CT coronary angiography increasingly serves as a non-invasive alternative for lower-risk patients. Risks include bleeding at the access site, contrast-induced kidney injury, arrhythmia, stroke, and very rarely artery dissection or death.
