Minneapolis sound

The Minneapolis sound is a subgenre of funk rock with elements of synth-pop and new wave that was pioneered by the musician Prince and other artists in Minneapolis, Minnesota, in the late 1970s and 1980s. According to the Rolling Stone Album Guide, the Minneapolis sound "loomed over mid-1980s R&B and pop, not to mention the next two decades' worth of electro, house, and techno."

Characteristics

While many of Prince's black contemporaries were moving toward the smoother sounds of quiet storm and urban contemporary, the Minneapolis sound blended funk's rhythmic syncopation with rock guitar, synthesizers, and drum machines. Signature features included synthesized horn lines in place of a live horn section, prominent use of the Linn LM-1 drum machine, falsetto vocals, and a fusion of black and white musical influences reflecting the racially mixed culture of the Twin Cities scene.

History

The sound emerged from Minneapolis's north side music community, where bands such as Flyte Tyme, Mind & Matter, and Grand Central — Prince's early band — performed. Prince's breakthrough albums, especially 1999 (1982) and Purple Rain (1984), brought the style to international attention. His protégé acts, including The Time, Vanity 6, Apollonia 6, Sheila E., and The Family, spread the sound widely. The production team of Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, fired by Prince from The Time in 1983, went on to define the sound of Janet Jackson's Control (1986) and Rhythm Nation 1814 (1989), as well as hits for Alexander O'Neal and the S.O.S. Band.

Influence

The Minneapolis sound influenced contemporary R&B, pop, and dance music, and remains associated with artists such as Ready for the World, Jesse Johnson, and later performers who drew on its synth-funk vocabulary.
