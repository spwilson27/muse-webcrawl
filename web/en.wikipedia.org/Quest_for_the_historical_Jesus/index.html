Quest for the historical Jesus

The quest for the historical Jesus consists of attempts by scholars to reconstruct the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth as a historical figure, distinguishing the Jesus recoverable by historical-critical methods from the "Christ of faith" presented in Christian doctrine. Scholars generally agree on a small core of facts — that Jesus was baptised by John the Baptist, preached in Galilee, and was crucified in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate — while debating nearly everything else about his message and self-understanding.

The first quest

The quest is conventionally dated from Hermann Samuel Reimarus, whose fragments, published posthumously by Lessing in the 1770s, argued that Jesus was a failed Jewish revolutionary and that his resurrection was invented by his disciples. Nineteenth-century "lives of Jesus", such as those of David Friedrich Strauss (1835), who treated the miracles as myth, and Ernest Renan (1863), followed. In 1906 Albert Schweitzer's The Quest of the Historical Jesus surveyed this literature and concluded that the liberal lives of Jesus were projections of their authors; Schweitzer himself presented Jesus as an apocalyptic prophet.

Later phases

Rudolf Bultmann's form criticism inaugurated a period of scepticism (sometimes called the "no quest"), holding that the Gospels are theological documents from which little biography can be recovered. A "new quest" from the 1950s, associated with Ernst Käsemann, sought continuity between the historical Jesus and the preaching of the early church using criteria such as embarrassment and multiple attestation. A "third quest" from the 1980s, including scholars such as E. P. Sanders, John P. Meier and the Jesus Seminar, placed Jesus firmly within Second Temple Judaism, though its portraits — apocalyptic prophet, social reformer, wisdom teacher — continue to diverge.

Sources and methods

The principal sources are the Synoptic Gospels and the Gospel of John, supplemented by the letters of Paul, the works of Josephus, and non-canonical texts such as the Gospel of Thomas. The historicity of Jesus himself is accepted by nearly all scholars of antiquity, with the Christ myth theory regarded as a fringe position.
