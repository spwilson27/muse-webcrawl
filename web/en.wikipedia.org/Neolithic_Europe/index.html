Neolithic Europe

Neolithic Europe is the period when Neolithic technology — farming, herding, pottery and polished stone tools — spread across Europe, roughly from 7000 BCE in the southeast to about 1700 BCE in the far north. The "Neolithic package" of domesticated wheat, barley, sheep, goats and cattle originated in the Fertile Crescent of southwest Asia and was carried into Europe by migrating farmers whose ancestry is now traceable through ancient DNA.

Spread of farming

Farming reached Greece and the Balkans by about 7000–6500 BCE, producing cultures such as Starčevo and Vinča. Two main routes carried it further: a Danubian route into central Europe, where the Linear Pottery culture (LBK, c. 5500–4500 BCE) built longhouse villages on fertile loess soils, and a Mediterranean route westward associated with Cardial Ware pottery, reaching Iberia and southern France by about 5400 BCE. Agriculture arrived in Britain and Ireland around 4000 BCE and in Scandinavia, with the Funnelbeaker culture, around the same time.

Society and monuments

Neolithic communities built some of Europe's most enduring monuments: megalithic tombs such as Newgrange in Ireland (older than the Egyptian pyramids), the stone circles of Stonehenge and Avebury, the alignments of Carnac in Brittany, and Malta's temple complexes. Settled life brought pottery traditions, polished stone axe exchange networks, early copper metallurgy in the Balkans, and — by the late Neolithic — the wheel, the plough and secondary products such as milk and wool in what Andrew Sherratt called the Secondary Products Revolution.

Genetics

Ancient DNA research since 2010 has shown that Europe's early farmers were largely descended from Anatolian populations and mixed only gradually with indigenous hunter-gatherers. A further major migration from the Pontic steppe around 3000 BCE, associated with the Corded Ware culture, transformed the genetic and probably the linguistic landscape of Europe.
