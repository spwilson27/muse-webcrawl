Reservation in India

Reservation in India is a system of affirmative action in which a percentage of seats in government jobs, public universities, and legislatures are reserved for historically disadvantaged groups — principally the Scheduled Castes (SC), Scheduled Tribes (ST), and Other Backward Classes (OBC), and, since 2019, the Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) among the general category.

History

Forms of reservation existed in princely states during the British colonial period; the princely state of Kolhapur under Chhatrapati Shahu Maharaj introduced reservations for non-Brahmin and backward classes in 1902. After independence, the Constitution of India (1950) provided reservations for SCs and STs in legislatures, education and public employment. The Mandal Commission recommended extending reservations to OBCs, implemented in central government jobs in 1990 amid major public debate.

Structure

In central government institutions the quotas are approximately 15% for SC, 7.5% for ST, 27% for OBC, and 10% for EWS, with the Supreme Court having generally capped total reservation near 50% (the "creamy layer" of the OBC category is excluded). Individual states set their own quotas, some exceeding these figures.

Debate

Supporters argue reservation remedies centuries of caste-based discrimination and ensures representation; critics argue it compromises merit, entrenches caste identities, and benefits relatively advantaged members of the reserved groups. Reservation remains a central and contentious issue in Indian politics.
