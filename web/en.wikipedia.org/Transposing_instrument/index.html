Transposing instrument

Transposing instrument
DefinitionInstrument whose written and sounding pitches differ
ExamplesClarinet in B♭, horn in F, trumpet in B♭, English horn
OppositeNon-transposing ("in C") instrument

A transposing instrument is a musical instrument for which the written pitch in notation differs from the pitch that actually sounds. When a clarinettist reads a written C, for example, a clarinet in B♭ sounds B♭. Instruments described as being "in" a key sound that key's tonic when the player reads a written C: a horn in F sounds a perfect fifth below its written notes, and a trumpet in B♭ sounds a major second below.

Common transpositions

Many orchestral and band instruments transpose. The clarinet family includes instruments in B♭ and A (sounding a major or minor third below written pitch), the soprano saxophone in B♭ and alto saxophone in E♭ (a major sixth below), the English horn in F (a fifth below), the horn in F (a fifth below), and trumpets in B♭, C, D and other keys. Some instruments transpose by an octave: the double bass and guitar sound an octave below written pitch, the piccolo an octave above, and the contrabassoon and bass recorder also displace by octaves. Octave-transposing instruments are sometimes treated separately, since their parts use the same staff positions as non-transposing ones.

Reasons for transposition

Transposition developed for practical reasons. Historically, brass instruments without valves could play only the natural harmonic series of one key; crooks of tubing changed the instrument's key, and parts were written in C so that players could read the same part regardless of the crook fitted. For woodwind families built in several sizes, transposing parts let a player switch between instruments — such as clarinets in B♭ and A — while using identical fingerings for identically written notes. Transposition also keeps parts within comfortable staff ranges and avoids excessive ledger lines and difficult key signatures.

Reading at concert pitch

Conductors' scores traditionally present transposing parts as written, so a conductor must mentally transpose when reading them; many modern scores are written "in C", with all parts shown at sounding (concert) pitch. Orchestral players are expected to transpose some older parts at sight — horn players, for instance, routinely read parts written for horns in keys other than F. In jazz and popular music, chord charts and lead sheets are commonly produced in separate editions for B♭, E♭ and C instruments.
