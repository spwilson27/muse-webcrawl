Replication crisis

Replication crisis
FieldMetascience; psychology, medicine, social sciences
Key studyOpen Science Collaboration (2015), Science
CausesPublication bias, p-hacking, low statistical power, HARKing

The replication crisis (also called the reproducibility crisis) is an ongoing methodological crisis in which many scientific studies — particularly in psychology, medicine and the social sciences — have proven difficult or impossible to reproduce or replicate. Because replication is a cornerstone of the scientific method, the finding that a large fraction of published results do not hold up has raised fundamental questions about research practices and incentives.

Background

Concerns crystallised in the early 2010s. In 2005, John Ioannidis published the influential essay "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False". In 2011, a paper claiming evidence for precognition, together with high-profile fraud cases, galvanised psychologists. That same year researchers demonstrated how undisclosed flexibility in data analysis — "researcher degrees of freedom" — lets scientists present false positives as significant findings.

Evidence

The landmark Open Science Collaboration study (2015) attempted to replicate 100 studies from leading psychology journals: while 97% of the originals reported significant results, only about 36% of the replications did, and replication effect sizes were on average half the originals'. Similar projects found modest replication rates in experimental economics (about 61%) and in social science studies published in Nature and Science (about 62%). In preclinical cancer biology, an industry-led effort found that many landmark findings could not be reproduced.

Causes

Contributing factors include publication bias toward positive results, low statistical power, p-hacking (exploiting analytic flexibility to reach significance), HARKing (hypothesising after results are known), selective reporting, perverse incentives rewarding novel results over careful ones, and a historical lack of prestige attached to replication work itself.

Reforms

The crisis has driven the open science movement: preregistration of hypotheses and analyses, registered reports (peer review before data collection), mandatory data and code sharing, larger sample sizes, and journals and funders that explicitly value replication. Whether these reforms are reversing the problem remains an active area of metascience research.
