The Italian War of 1521–1526, sometimes called the Four Years' War, was a conflict in the Italian Wars between France under Francis I and an alliance of the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, Spain, the Papal States, and England. It centred on control of the Duchy of Milan and dominance in Italy, and ended with a crushing Imperial victory at the Battle of Pavia and the capture of the French king.

Origins

Charles V's election as Holy Roman Emperor in 1519 encircled France with Habsburg territories — Spain, the Low Countries, Austria, and Naples. When Imperial and papal forces expelled the French from Milan in late 1521, Francis I resolved to recover the duchy, France's longstanding Italian claim, and the war widened: England joined the anti-French alliance in 1522, and fighting extended to the French frontiers in Picardy, Navarre, and Provence.

Course of the war

French attempts to retake Milan failed at Bicocca (April 1522), where Swiss mercenaries in French pay attacked Imperial arquebusiers and Landsknechte behind fieldworks and were slaughtered — a landmark demonstration of the growing power of firearms and defensive positions. The defection of the Constable Charles de Bourbon to the Emperor in 1523 further weakened France, and an Imperial invasion of Provence in 1524 was repulsed at Marseille. In October 1524 Francis personally led a large army over the Alps, retook Milan, and laid siege to Pavia.

Battle of Pavia

On 24 February 1525 an Imperial relief army under Charles de Lannoy attacked the French besiegers. In a battle decided by Spanish arquebusiers and Landsknecht pikemen, the French army was destroyed: much of the French nobility was killed, and Francis I was captured on the field — remarking, in the famous letter to his mother, that "of all things nothing remains to me but honour and life, which is safe".

Treaty of Madrid

Francis was taken to Madrid and, in January 1526, signed the Treaty of Madrid, renouncing French claims to Milan, Naples, Flanders, and Artois and agreeing to restore Burgundy to Charles, in exchange for his release. Once free, he repudiated the treaty as signed under duress, and the Italian states, alarmed at Habsburg predominance, formed the League of Cognac with France, setting the stage for the next phase of the wars in 1526.
