Current account (balance of payments)

The current account is one of the two main components of a country's balance of payments (the other being the capital and financial account). It records the flow of goods, services, primary income (such as investment earnings and wages), and secondary income (transfers such as foreign aid and remittances) between a country and the rest of the world over a given period.

Components

The current account comprises four sub-balances: (1) trade in goods — exports minus imports of merchandise, often called the trade balance; (2) trade in services — transport, travel, finance, software, and other services; (3) primary income — dividends, interest, and profits earned abroad minus those paid to foreigners, plus compensation of employees; and (4) secondary income — current transfers with no quid pro quo, including workers' remittances.

Surpluses and deficits

A current account surplus means a nation is a net lender to the rest of the world; a deficit means it is a net borrower, in the sense that the deficit must be financed by net capital inflows. National accounting identities show the current account equals national saving minus domestic investment, so deficits can reflect low saving, high investment, or fiscal deficits. Economists debate when deficits are problematic: persistent large deficits may create external debt vulnerabilities, while surpluses (as in China, Germany, and oil exporters at various times) imply corresponding deficits elsewhere and can fuel trade tensions.

Examples

The United States has run current account deficits almost continuously since the early 1980s, peaking near 6% of GDP in 2006. Japan and Germany have been famous for large surpluses, and commodity exporters' balances swing with energy prices. Statistics are compiled under standards set by the IMF's Balance of Payments Manual.
