Autochthon (mythology)

Erichthonius of Athens, born from the earth itself, was the archetypal autochthon of Greek myth.

In Greek mythology and historiography, an autochthon (Greek: αὐτόχθων, from autos, "self", and chthōn, "earth") was a person or people sprung directly from the soil of their land — "earth-born" — rather than having migrated there. The concept was central above all to Athens, whose citizens claimed to have inhabited Attica since the beginning and to be literally born from its earth.

Athenian autochthony

The mythic kings Cecrops and Erichthonius were said to have been born from the soil of Attica: Cecrops was half man, half serpent, and Erichthonius sprang from the earth after Hephaestus's seed fell upon it. Athenian funeral orations and political rhetoric celebrated autochthony as proof of the purity, antiquity and unity of the citizen body, and as justification for Athenian claims against other Greeks. Plato's Menexenus and the funeral speech attributed to Pericles both deploy the idea.

Other traditions

Similar earth-born peoples appear elsewhere in Greek tradition: the Theban Spartoi ("sown men") grew from the dragon's teeth planted by Cadmus, and Arcadians prided themselves on being older than the moon. In a wider comparative sense, many mythologies include earth-born first humans, from Prometheus's clay figures to traditions of emergence from the land itself.

Political significance

Modern scholars, notably Nicole Loraux, have analysed Athenian autochthony as an ideology that imagines the citizen body as a single family born of one mother — the earth — while excluding women, slaves and foreign residents from full membership of the community. The term has since passed into general use for indigenous inhabitants, in phrases such as "autochthonous peoples".
