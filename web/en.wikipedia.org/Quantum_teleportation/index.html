Quantum teleportation

Quantum teleportation
Proposed1993 (Bennett, Brassard, Crépeau, Jozsa, Peres, Wootters)
First demonstrated1997 (photons; Innsbruck and Rome groups)
Resources usedOne entangled pair (ebit) + 2 classical bits per qubit teleported
FieldQuantum information

Quantum teleportation is a technique for transferring quantum information from a sender at one location to a receiver at another location. While the name evokes science-fiction teleportation, the process does not transport matter or energy, nor does it permit faster-than-light communication. What is transferred is the exact quantum state of a particle, using a previously shared pair of entangled particles together with two bits of classical information. The original state is destroyed in the process, consistent with the no-cloning theorem.

The standard teleportation protocol: Alice and Bob share an entangled pair

The protocol

The protocol was published in 1993 by Charles Bennett, Gilles Brassard, Claude Crépeau, Richard Jozsa, Asher Peres, and William Wootters. Its standard steps are:

  1. Alice and Bob share a pair of particles in an entangled Bell state. Alice also holds a third particle in an unknown state to be teleported.
  2. Alice performs a joint Bell-state measurement on her two particles and obtains one of four equally likely outcomes — two classical bits of information.
  3. Alice sends the two bits to Bob over an ordinary classical channel.
  4. Depending on the bits received, Bob applies one of four simple operations (or none) to his particle, which transforms it into an exact replica of the original unknown state.

Because the classical message cannot travel faster than light, teleportation respects special relativity. Alice's measurement destroys the original state, so the quantum information is moved rather than copied.

Experiments

The first experimental realizations, in 1997, teleported the polarization state of photons. Teleportation has since been demonstrated with atoms, ions, superconducting circuits, and solid-state defects. In 2017, the Chinese Micius satellite was used to teleport photon states from the ground to orbit over distances of up to 1,400 km, a step toward a global quantum network.

Applications

Teleportation is a basic primitive of quantum information science. It underlies proposals for quantum networks and a future "quantum internet," gate teleportation in fault-tolerant quantum computing, and quantum repeaters that extend the range of quantum key distribution. A related technique, entanglement swapping, teleports entanglement itself between particles that have never interacted.
