Rimfire ammunition

Rimfire ammunition is a type of firearm cartridge in which the priming compound is distributed inside the hollow rim of the case base. When the firing pin strikes and crushes the rim, the primer ignites the propellant. The design contrasts with centerfire ammunition, which uses a separate primer in the centre of the case head.

A .22 Long Rifle cartridge, the most common rimfire round in the world.

History

Rimfire ignition was developed in France in the 1840s; the Flobert cartridges of 1845 were the first metallic cartridges of the type. The system powered many early cartridge arms of the American Civil War era, including the Henry rifle's .44 Henry round. Because the thin case head must be soft enough to be crushed by the firing pin, rimfire cases cannot withstand high pressures, limiting the system to relatively low-powered cartridges—though it once served in surprisingly large calibres.

Modern cartridges

The dominant rimfire cartridge today is the .22 Long Rifle, used for training, target shooting, hunting small game and Olympic competition; billions are produced annually because they are cheap and have negligible recoil. More powerful modern rimfires include the .22 Winchester Magnum Rimfire, .17 Hornady Magnum Rimfire and .17 Winchester Super Magnum. Rimfire cases are not practically reloadable, since the priming is integral to the drawn case.
