John Stuart Mill

John Stuart Mill (20 May 1806 – 7 May 1873) was an English philosopher, political economist, politician, and civil servant. One of the most influential thinkers in the history of liberalism, he contributed widely to social theory, political theory, and political economy. Dubbed "the most influential English-speaking philosopher of the nineteenth century" by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, he conceived of liberty as justifying the freedom of the individual in opposition to unlimited state and social control.

Education and early career

Mill was educated rigorously by his father, James Mill, learning Greek at age three and Latin, mathematics, and logic in childhood. He worked for the British East India Company from 1823 to 1858. A mental crisis in his twenties led him to question strict utilitarian upbringing and to value poetry and emotion, recounted in his Autobiography (1873).

Philosophy and major works

Mill was a proponent of utilitarianism, an ethical theory developed by his predecessor Jeremy Bentham. His Utilitarianism (1863) refined the doctrine, emphasizing qualitative differences among pleasures. In On Liberty (1859), he articulated the harm principle: that the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. A System of Logic (1843) laid out methods of inductive reasoning known as Mill's methods. His Principles of Political Economy (1848) was the leading economics textbook of the era.

Women's rights and politics

Mill was an early advocate of women's equality. His essay The Subjection of Women (1869), written with reference to his wife Harriet Taylor Mill, argued for legal and social equality of the sexes. As Member of Parliament for Westminster (1865–1868), he proposed women's suffrage in debate on the Reform Bill of 1867.
