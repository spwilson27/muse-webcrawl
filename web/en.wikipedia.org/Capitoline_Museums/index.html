The Capitoline Museums (Italian: Musei Capitolini) are a group of art and archaeological museums in Piazza del Campidoglio, on top of the Capitoline Hill in Rome, Italy. The historic seats of the museums are the Palazzo dei Conservatori and the Palazzo Nuovo, facing each other across Michelangelo's piazza, with galleries also in the Palazzo Senatorio and the Galleria Lapidaria connecting them beneath the square.

The museums trace their origin to 1471, when Pope Sixtus IV donated a group of ancient bronzes — including the Capitoline Wolf — to the people of Rome. This nucleus makes the Capitoline arguably the oldest public museum collection in the world; Pope Clement XII formally opened the museums to the public in 1734.

Collections

The Palazzo dei Conservatori holds the great bronze sculptures: the Capitoline Wolf suckling Romulus and Remus; the Spinario, a boy pulling a thorn from his foot; fragments of the colossal statue of Constantine, including the enormous head and hand; and, in a purpose-built glass hall on the site of the ancient Temple of Jupiter, the original gilded bronze equestrian statue of Marcus Aurelius, removed from the piazza for conservation in 1981. The palace also contains the Capitoline Pinacoteca, with paintings by Caravaggio (The Fortune Teller and John the Baptist), Titian, Veronese, Rubens, Van Dyck and Guercino, whose vast Burial of Saint Petronilla dominates one gallery.

The Palazzo Nuovo is devoted mainly to ancient marble sculpture: the Dying Gaul, the Capitoline Venus, the Faun in Rosso Antico, the busts of Roman emperors and Greek philosophers arranged in the Hall of the Emperors and Hall of the Philosophers, and Egyptian and sarcophagus collections. The Tabularium gallery offers celebrated views over the Roman Forum.

Branches

The museum system also includes the Centrale Montemartini, a former power station on the Via Ostiense opened in 1997, where classical statues are displayed among the turbines and diesel engines of Rome's first public electricity plant.
