Polyphemus (Greek: Πολύφημος, "many-voiced" or "much spoken of") is the one-eyed giant son of Poseidon and Thoosa in Greek mythology, one of the Cyclopes described in Homer's Odyssey. His best-known episode is his encounter with Odysseus, in which he imprisons the hero and his men in his cave, eats several of them, and is blinded and outwitted by Odysseus — a defeat that brings Poseidon's wrath down on Odysseus for the rest of his voyage home. Polyphemus also appears in a contrasting, romantic role in later literature as the unrequited lover of the sea nymph Galatea.

In the Odyssey

In Book 9 of the Odyssey, Odysseus and twelve of his men land on the island of the Cyclopes — later identified with Sicily, beneath Mount Etna — and enter the cave of Polyphemus, a lawless shepherd who lives alone with his flocks. The giant returns, rolls a huge stone across the entrance, and, defying the laws of hospitality (xenia), devours two of the men. Odysseus gets him drunk on strong wine; asked his name, Odysseus replies "Nobody" (Outis). While Polyphemus sleeps, the Greeks drive a heated, sharpened olive-wood stake into his single eye. When the blinded giant cries for help, the other Cyclopes hear him shout that "Nobody" is harming him and go away. Odysseus and his remaining men escape by clinging to the undersides of the sheep as they are let out to pasture. As the ship sails away, Odysseus fatally reveals his true name, allowing Polyphemus to pray to his father Poseidon to curse Odysseus's homecoming.

Polyphemus and Galatea

Hellenistic and Roman poets developed a very different Polyphemus: the lovesick shepherd of Theocritus's Idylls, singing clumsily on the shore to the sea nymph Galatea, who spurns him. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, Galatea loves the youth Acis, and the jealous Polyphemus crushes Acis with a rock; Galatea transforms Acis's blood into a river. This version inspired later works including Handel's masque Acis and Galatea.

Legacy

The cave episode became one of the most famous tales of trickery in Western literature, and the giant's name passed into zoology — the American paddlefish has a namesake genus, and a large hawkmoth is named Antheraea polyphemus for its eyespots.
