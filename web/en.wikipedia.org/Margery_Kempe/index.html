Margery Kempe

Bornc. 1373, Bishop's Lynn (now King's Lynn), Norfolk, England
DiedAfter 1438
OccupationMystic, pilgrim, author
Known forThe Book of Margery Kempe, the earliest surviving autobiography in English

Margery Kempe (c. 1373 – after 1438) was an English Christian mystic and pilgrim from King's Lynn in Norfolk. She is known as the author of The Book of Margery Kempe, dictated late in her life, which recounts her spiritual experiences, extensive pilgrimages, and conflicts with church authorities, and is regarded as the earliest surviving autobiography in the English language.

Life

Margery was born Margery Brunham, daughter of John de Brunham, a merchant who served as mayor of Lynn and as a member of Parliament. Around 1393 she married John Kempe, with whom she had fourteen children. After the birth of her first child she suffered a severe mental breakdown, during which she experienced visions; recovering, she later undertook brewing and milling businesses that failed, which she interpreted as punishments for worldly ambition. Following a visitation from Christ, she devoted herself to a life of prayer and sought — eventually successfully — to persuade her husband to agree to a chaste marriage.

Kempe travelled extensively on pilgrimage: to Jerusalem and Rome in 1413–1415, to Santiago de Compostela in 1417, to shrines in England including Canterbury, York, and Norwich — where she consulted the anchoress Julian of Norwich, who counselled her on her visions — and late in life to Norway and the Baltic, visiting Gdańsk (Danzig) in 1433. She was conspicuous for her loud, copious weeping during Mass and at the sight of suffering, which attracted both devotion and hostility; she was several times accused of Lollardy (heresy) and examined before church authorities, including Archbishop Thomas Arundel, but was never convicted.

The Book

In the 1430s the illiterate Kempe dictated her life story to scribes, producing The Book of Margery Kempe. The sole surviving manuscript, copied around 1450, was long lost and known only from extracts printed by Wynkyn de Worde and Henry Pepwell in the early sixteenth century — which presented Kempe as a conventional devout anchoress. The full manuscript was rediscovered in 1934 in a country house library and published soon after, transforming scholarly understanding of medieval women's religious life.

Reputation

Kempe's Book is valued as a vivid record of late medieval piety, of a laywoman's negotiation with ecclesiastical authority, and of everyday life — childbirth, business failure, travel, and social scorn — in fifteenth-century England. Scholars debate her sanity, her orthodoxy, and the literary shaping of her self-presentation, but her historical importance is secure: she is recognised in the Church of England's calendar of saints with a commemoration on 9 November.
