Soba

Soba (そば or 蕎麦) are Japanese noodles made of buckwheat flour, or a combination of buckwheat and wheat flour. They are roughly as thick as spaghetti and are served either chilled with a dipping sauce (tsuyu) or hot in a noodle soup. The word soba also refers to the buckwheat plant itself. In Okinawa, "Okinawa soba" is a different dish, made entirely of wheat.

Zaru soba — chilled noodles on a bamboo sieve

History

Buckwheat was cultivated in Japan from prehistoric times, but soba noodles as a distinct dish developed in the Edo period (1603–1868), when soba shops proliferated in the city of Edo (Tokyo). Buckwheat grew well in poor mountain soils, and regional styles developed, such as those of Nagano (Shinshu soba) and Izumo. Handmade noodles of pure buckwheat (jūwari soba, "100 percent") are prized but fragile; most commercial soba blends in wheat as a binder.

Serving styles

Common cold preparations include mori soba and zaru soba, eaten by dipping noodles in a chilled, concentrated sauce of dashi, soy sauce, and mirin, with wasabi and scallions. Hot soba is served in a lighter broth, often topped with tempura (tempura soba), duck (kamo nanban), or a raw egg (tsukimi soba). Eating soba on New Year's Eve — toshikoshi soba, "year-crossing noodles" — symbolizes longevity and letting go of the year's hardships.
