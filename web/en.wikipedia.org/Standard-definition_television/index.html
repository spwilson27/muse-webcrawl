Standard-definition television

Standard-definition television (SDTV) is a television system that uses a resolution that is not considered to be either high or enhanced definition. Standard refers to the fact that it is the prevalent specification for broadcast (and later, cable) television prior to the emergence of high-definition television (HDTV) in the late 1990s and early 2000s.

The two common SDTV signal types are 576i, with 576 interlaced lines of resolution, derived from the European-developed PAL and SECAM systems, and 480i, with 480 interlaced lines, based on the American NTSC system. SDTV refresh rates are generally 25, 29.97, 30 or 50–60 frames (or fields, in interlaced systems) per second.

Pixel aspect ratio

Unlike high-definition formats, standard-definition signals do not necessarily use square pixels. The 4:3 and anamorphic 16:9 versions of 576i and 480i share the same horizontal resolution but use different pixel aspect ratios, so a 720×576 image may be displayed in either aspect ratio depending on signalling.

Digital SDTV

Digital television transitions carried SDTV formats in standards such as DVB, ATSC and ISDB, and on DVDs. As HDTV became standard in developed markets during the 2010s, broadcasters progressively reduced or ended SDTV simulcasts, though SDTV remains common in many regions and in archive material.
