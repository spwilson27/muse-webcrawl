Medicine in the medieval Islamic world

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the history of medicine, medicine in the medieval Islamic world refers to the science of medicine developed in the Islamic world during the Islamic Golden Age (roughly the 8th to 13th centuries) and written in Arabic, then the lingua franca of Islamic civilisation. Islamic medicine preserved, systematised and extended the knowledge of classical Greek and Roman medicine, as well as Persian and Indian traditions.

Institutions

The Islamic world developed the bimaristan, a hospital institution that provided treatment, medical training and in some cases licensing examinations for physicians. Large hospitals in Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo included wards for different diseases, pharmacies and libraries.

Major figures

Notable physicians included al-Razi (Rhazes), whose works distinguished smallpox from measles; Ibn Sina (Avicenna), whose Canon of Medicine remained a standard text in Europe for centuries; Ibn al-Nafis, who described the pulmonary circulation of blood; and al-Zahrawi (Abulcasis), a pioneer of surgery whose encyclopedia described many surgical instruments and procedures.

Contributions

Islamic scholars advanced pharmacology, ophthalmology, anatomy and hospital organisation, and their translations and commentaries transmitted both ancient knowledge and new observations to medieval Europe.
