Biafra, officially the Republic of Biafra, was a partially recognized secessionist state in West Africa that declared independence from Nigeria in May 1967. It took its name from the Bight of Biafra, the Atlantic bay to its south. Its attempted secession triggered the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970), one of the defining African conflicts of the 20th century.

Background

The mainly Igbo Eastern Region of Nigeria proclaimed secession after waves of anti-Igbo pogroms in the north in 1966 killed tens of thousands and drove over a million refugees east, and after the failure of the Aburi Accord between Lt. Col. Yakubu Gowon's federal government and Eastern governor Lt. Col. Chukwuemeka Odumegwu Ojukwu. On 30 May 1967 Ojukwu declared the Republic of Biafra.

The war

Only a handful of states formally recognized Biafra (including Tanzania, Gabon, Ivory Coast, Zambia, and Haiti), though France, Israel, and others provided covert support. Federal forces, backed by Britain and the Soviet Union, gradually compressed the enclave. Nigeria's blockade produced a catastrophic famine — images of starving children with kwashiorkor galvanized global public opinion and spurred the modern humanitarian airlift, inspiring the founding of Médecins Sans Frontières. Estimates of deaths, mostly civilian, range from about one to three million.

Collapse and aftermath

By late 1969 Biafra was a starving pocket around Owerri and Umuahia. Ojukwu fled to Ivory Coast on 11 January 1970, and his deputy Philip Effiong surrendered days later. Gowon proclaimed a policy of "no victor, no vanquished", and Biafra was reintegrated into Nigeria. The name has been revived in the 21st century by independence movements such as MASSOB and IPOB, whose agitation Nigeria has treated as a security threat.
