Primary succession

Primary succession is the process by which an ecological community develops on a surface that was previously uninhabited and lacks soil, such as newly cooled lava, exposed rock, or a retreating glacier's till. It contrasts with secondary succession, which occurs where a disturbance has removed a community but left the soil intact.

Process

Pioneer species such as lichens and mosses colonize the bare surface first, weathering rock and accumulating organic matter that begins soil formation. Grasses, shrubs, and eventually trees follow over decades to centuries, leading toward a relatively stable climax community.

Examples

Classic study sites include Glacier Bay in Alaska, where succession follows glacial retreat, and the volcanic island of Surtsey, Iceland, formed in 1963. Primary succession is typically much slower than secondary succession because soil must be created from scratch.
