Engine control unit

An engine control unit (ECU), also called an engine control module (ECM), is an electronic control unit that manages the operation of an internal combustion engine. It is one of the most important embedded systems in a modern vehicle. Using input from numerous sensors, the ECU controls actuators to determine fuel injection quantity and timing, ignition timing, idle speed, variable valve timing, turbocharger boost, and other parameters, balancing performance, fuel economy, and exhaust emissions in real time.

Function

The ECU continuously reads sensors such as the oxygen (lambda) sensor in the exhaust, the mass airflow or manifold pressure sensor, throttle position sensor, crankshaft and camshaft position sensors, and coolant temperature sensor. From stored calibration maps ("fuel maps" and "ignition maps") plus real-time correction algorithms, it computes injector pulse width and spark timing for each cylinder. Closed-loop control using the oxygen sensor keeps the air–fuel ratio near the stoichiometric value so that the catalytic converter works efficiently.

History

Electronic engine management became widespread in the late 1970s and 1980s as emissions and fuel-economy regulations tightened; early systems such as Bosch's Motronic combined fuel injection and ignition in one unit. Since the 1990s, on-board diagnostics standards (OBD-II in the United States) have required ECUs to monitor emissions-related components and store fault codes readable through a standard connector.

Tuning and related systems

ECU software can be modified ("remapped" or "chipped") to change performance characteristics, a common practice in motorsport and aftermarket tuning, sometimes with legal and warranty consequences. In modern vehicles the engine control unit communicates over the CAN bus with other controllers, including the transmission control unit; where both functions are combined the unit may be called a powertrain control module (PCM).
