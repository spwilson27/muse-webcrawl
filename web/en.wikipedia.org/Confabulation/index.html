Confabulation

Confabulation involves honestly reporting memories that never occurred.

In neuroscience and psychology, confabulation is a memory error defined as the production of fabricated, distorted, or misinterpreted memories about oneself or the world, without the conscious intention to deceive. People who confabulate present incorrect memories ranging from subtle alterations of real events to entirely bizarre inventions, and they are generally confident that their recollections are correct despite contradictory evidence.

Causes

Confabulation is associated with brain damage, particularly to the frontal lobes and the basal forebrain, and is a classic feature of Korsakoff's syndrome, a disorder caused by thiamine deficiency, often related to alcohol misuse. It also occurs in Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, after anterior communicating artery aneurysm rupture, in schizophrenia, and in split-brain patients, whose left-hemisphere "interpreter" constructs explanations for actions initiated by the disconnected right hemisphere. Mild confabulation—filling gaps in memory with plausible material—can occur in healthy people, especially children and older adults.

Types

Researchers distinguish provoked confabulations, produced in response to questioning and resembling ordinary memory errors, from spontaneous confabulations, which are unprovoked, often acted upon, and linked to failures of reality monitoring and temporal context: patients mistake memories of imagined or past events for current reality. Theories attribute the symptom to deficits in memory retrieval, source monitoring, and executive evaluation of candidate memories.

Management

There is no specific cure; management focuses on treating the underlying condition, providing orientation and memory aids, and helping patients and families understand that the false statements are symptoms, not lying. Cognitive rehabilitation targeting self-monitoring may reduce confabulation in some patients.
