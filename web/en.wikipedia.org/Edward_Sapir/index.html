Edward Sapir (26 January 1884 – 4 February 1939) was an American anthropologist and linguist, widely considered one of the founders of modern linguistics in the United States. A student of Franz Boas at Columbia University, Sapir made fundamental contributions to the description and classification of the Indigenous languages of North America, to phonemic theory, and to the study of the relationship between language, thought and culture.

Career

Sapir was born in Pomerania and emigrated with his family to the United States as a child, growing up in New York. After fieldwork on languages of the Pacific Northwest and California — documenting Takelma, Yana and other languages, sometimes working with the last fluent speakers — he spent 1910 to 1925 in Ottawa as chief of anthropology for the Geological Survey of Canada. He then moved to the University of Chicago and, in 1931, to Yale, where he founded its linguistics programme.

Ideas

Sapir argued that language shapes habitual thought — the idea later popularised as the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis of linguistic relativity, developed with his student Benjamin Lee Whorf. His classification of North American languages into a small number of large families, though revised, was a landmark of historical linguistics. His 1921 book Language remains a classic introduction, and his work on the phoneme helped establish structural linguistics. Sapir also wrote poetry and essays on music and psychiatry.
