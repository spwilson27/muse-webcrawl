Sherman's March to the Sea


Sherman's troops destroying a railroad near Atlanta, Georgia.
Date: 15 November – 21 December 1864
Location: Georgia, Confederate States of America
Commander: Maj. Gen. William Tecumseh Sherman
Result: Union victory; Savannah captured

Sherman's March to the Sea was a military campaign conducted through Georgia from November 15 until December 21, 1864, by Major General William Tecumseh Sherman of the Union Army during the American Civil War. After capturing Atlanta, Sherman's force of about 62,000 men marched some 285 miles (458 km) to the port of Savannah, living off the land and destroying military targets, industry, infrastructure, and civilian property along the way.

Background

Following the Atlanta Campaign, Sherman concluded that the Union could only win by breaking the Confederacy's economic and psychological capacity for war. Having secured Grant's approval, he abandoned his supply lines and set out across Georgia, leaving Hood's Confederate army behind in Tennessee, where it was destroyed at the battles of Franklin and Nashville.

The march

The army advanced in two wings along parallel routes, foraging widely across a swath up to 60 miles (97 km) wide. Soldiers known as "bummers" seized food, livestock, and valuables; organized parties destroyed railroads — heating rails and twisting them around trees as "Sherman's neckties" — burned cotton gins, warehouses, mills, and factories, especially those serving the Confederate war effort. Resistance from Georgia militia and Wheeler's cavalry was sporadic and ineffective. Sherman estimated the damage at $100 million.

Capture of Savannah

Reaching the coast in mid-December, Sherman's forces captured Fort McAllister and invested Savannah. Confederate general Hardee evacuated his garrison across the Savannah River on 20 December, and on 21 December 1864 Sherman occupied the city, telegraphing President Abraham Lincoln: "I beg to present you as a Christmas gift the City of Savannah."

Legacy

The march is a famous application of scorched-earth and total war strategy, intended to show ordinary Southerners that their government could not protect them. It remains celebrated in the North and bitterly remembered in Georgia, and it set the stage for Sherman's subsequent Carolinas Campaign in early 1865.
