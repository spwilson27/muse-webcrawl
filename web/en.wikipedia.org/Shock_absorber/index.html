Shock absorber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A shock absorber or damper is a mechanical or hydraulic device designed to absorb and damp shock impulses. It does so by converting the kinetic energy of the shock into another form of energy (typically heat), which is then dissipated. Most shock absorbers are a form of dashpot — a damper that resists motion via viscous friction.

Function

In a vehicle, shock absorbers reduce the effect of traveling over rough ground, leading to improved ride quality and vehicle handling. While shock absorbers serve the purpose of limiting excessive suspension movement, their intended sole purpose is to damp spring oscillations. Shock absorbers use valving of oil and gasses to absorb excess energy from the springs. Spring rates are chosen by the manufacturer based on the weight of the vehicle. If the controlling value of the shock absorber is not correct, the ride may be either overly harsh or excessively bouncy.

Types

  • Twin-tube dampers — the most common automotive design, with inner and outer cylinders.
  • Monotube dampers — a single tube with a floating piston separating oil and a high-pressure gas charge; responsive and resistant to fade.
  • MacPherson struts — a structural assembly combining a shock absorber with the suspension's locating function.
  • Air and gas-charged shocks — use compressed gas to reduce foaming of the hydraulic fluid.
  • Adjustable and adaptive dampers — allow valving to be changed manually or electronically, sometimes in real time using magnetorheological fluid.

Applications

Beyond automobiles and motorcycles, shock absorbers are used in aircraft landing gear, industrial machinery, bridges and buildings (for seismic protection), and railway vehicles.
