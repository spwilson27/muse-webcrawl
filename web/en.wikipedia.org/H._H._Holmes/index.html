H. H. Holmes


Holmes in 1895

Herman Webster Mudgett (May 16, 1861 – May 7, 1896), better known as Dr. Henry Howard Holmes or H. H. Holmes, was an American con artist and serial killer active between 1891 and 1894. By the time of his execution in 1896, Holmes had confessed to 27 murders, though some he claimed to have killed were still alive and the true number is unknown. He is often called one of America's first documented serial killers.

Early life

Mudgett was born in Gilmanton, New Hampshire, and studied medicine at the University of Michigan, where he stole cadavers to commit insurance fraud by disfiguring corpses and claiming they were insured accident victims. He moved through several cities working as a pharmacist and swindling creditors before settling in Chicago in 1886, taking the name H. H. Holmes.

The "Murder Castle"

In Chicago's Englewood neighbourhood, Holmes built a large three-storey building mixing shops and hotel rooms—later sensationalised in the press as the "Murder Castle"—during the run-up to the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893. The upper floors contained labyrinthine, windowless rooms, doors that could be locked from outside, chutes, and a kiln; Holmes employed a rotating staff of workers so none would know the whole plan. Several employees, guests, and associates disappeared. Modern historians caution that newspaper accounts greatly embellished the building's features, but it plainly served his fraud schemes and at least some murders.

Capture and trial

Holmes fled Chicago after the fair, conducting frauds across the United States and Canada with accomplice Benjamin Pitezel, whom he murdered in Philadelphia in 1894 for insurance money; he also murdered three of Pitezel's children. Arrested in Boston in November 1894, he was tried in Philadelphia for Pitezel's murder, convicted, and hanged at Moyamensing Prison on May 7, 1896. His case inspired Erik Larson's bestseller The Devil in the White City (2003).
