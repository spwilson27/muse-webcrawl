Test cross

Punnett square for a monohybrid test cross

In genetics, a test cross, first introduced by Gregor Mendel, is the breeding of an individual with a phenotypically recessive individual, in order to determine the genotype (zygosity) of the first individual.

Principle

An organism showing a dominant trait may be either homozygous dominant (AA) or heterozygous (Aa); the two cannot be distinguished by appearance alone. Crossing it with a homozygous recessive (aa) partner reveals the answer in the offspring: if the tested individual is homozygous dominant, all offspring show the dominant phenotype; if it is heterozygous, roughly half the offspring will show the recessive phenotype.

Use

Test crosses were a foundational tool of classical genetics, used by Mendel with pea plants to verify his laws of segregation and independent assortment. The analogous experiment in which an F1 hybrid is crossed back to either parent is called a backcross. Test crosses remain standard practice in plant and animal breeding and in genetic analysis of model organisms, although molecular genotyping now often replaces them where DNA testing is available.
