Crochet (UK: /ˈkroʊʃeɪ/, US: /kroʊˈʃeɪ/; French: [kʁɔʃɛ]) is a process of creating textiles by using a crochet hook to interlock loops of yarn, thread, or strands of other materials. The name derives from the French term crochet, meaning "hook".

Unlike knitting, which keeps many stitches open on needles, crochet completes each stitch before moving to the next, with only one active loop on the hook. Basic stitches — chain, slip stitch, single (UK double) crochet, double (UK treble) crochet and treble — are combined into fabrics, lace, garments, blankets (including the granny square), toys (amigurumi) and doilies.

History

Crochet as known today developed in 19th-century Europe; Irish crochet lace became an important cottage industry during and after the Great Famine of the 1840s. The first printed patterns appeared in the 1820s–40s, and Mlle. Riego de la Branchardière popularised the craft in Britain. Because it requires only a hook and yarn, crochet spread worldwide and saw major revivals in the 1970s and again in the 2010s–2020s through social media.
