Tetrapod (structure)

A tetrapod is a type of structure used in coastal engineering, a four-legged concrete structure designed to dissipate the energy of incoming waves by allowing water to flow around rather than against it, and to interlock when piled together. Tetrapods were originally developed in France in 1950 by Pierre Danel and Paul Anglès d'Auriac of the Neyrpic laboratory at Grenoble, who received a patent for the design.

Design

The tetrapod has four legs radiating from a central point in the directions of the vertices of a tetrahedron. Because of this geometry, a tetrapod always rests stably on three legs with the fourth pointing upward, whatever its orientation. When dumped in large numbers along a shoreline or around a breakwater, the units interlock into a porous, flexible layer that resists displacement, absorbs wave energy, and reduces erosion of the material behind it.

Use

Tetrapods are used extensively in Japan, where they line a large fraction of the country's coastline, as well as in breakwaters and seawalls worldwide. They range from small units of a few hundred kilograms to pieces weighing tens of tonnes. Their use is not without controversy: they are visually intrusive, can be hazardous to swimmers and marine life, and may alter natural sediment transport and coastal ecosystems.

Related structures

Other armour units with similar purposes include dolosse, accropodes, cubes, and core-locs, all classed as armourstone alternatives or concrete armour units for rubble-mound breakwaters.
