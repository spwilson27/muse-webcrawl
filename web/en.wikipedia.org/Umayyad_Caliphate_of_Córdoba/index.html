The Caliphate of Córdoba (Arabic: خلافة قرطبة) was an Islamic state ruled by the Umayyad dynasty from 929, when Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed himself caliph, until 1031. It encompassed most of the Iberian Peninsula (al-Andalus) and parts of North Africa, with its capital at Córdoba, then one of the largest and most advanced cities in the world.

History

The Umayyads had ruled al-Andalus as an independent emirate since 756, when Abd al-Rahman I, a refugee from the Abbasid overthrow of the Umayyads in Damascus, established himself in Córdoba. In 929 Abd al-Rahman III assumed the caliphal title to counter the rival Fatimid caliphate in North Africa and the Abbasids in Baghdad. Under him and his son al-Hakam II, Córdoba became a centre of learning, with a great library and flourishing scholarship, medicine, agriculture and trade. From 976 the powerful chamberlain Almanzor (al-Mansur) dominated the state and campaigned extensively against the Christian kingdoms of the north.

Decline and fall

After Almanzor's death in 1002, succession disputes plunged the caliphate into civil war (the fitna of al-Andalus). Rival claimants and Berber, Arab and Slav factions devastated Córdoba, and in 1031 the caliphate was abolished, fragmenting into the independent taifa kingdoms.

Culture

The period saw the enlargement of the Great Mosque of Córdoba, the palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra, advances in irrigation and the introduction of new crops, and a celebrated (if uneven) coexistence of Muslim, Christian and Jewish communities.
