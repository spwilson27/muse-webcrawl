Civilian review board

A civilian review board (also called a civilian review commission or police review board) is a body of civilians, independent of a police department, that reviews complaints against police officers and, in varying degrees, investigates misconduct, recommends discipline, and advises on policy.

Models

Oversight systems are commonly grouped into models: review models, in which a board examines completed internal investigations; investigative models, in which civilian staff conduct independent investigations, sometimes with subpoena power; and auditor or monitor models, which review the quality of the department's own processes. Powers differ widely: many boards are advisory, while some, such as New York City's Civilian Complaint Review Board (established in its modern form in 1993), employ civilian investigators and prosecute substantiated cases in administrative trials.

History and debate

Calls for civilian review in the United States date to the early 20th century and gained force during the civil rights era. Supporters argue independent review increases legitimacy and public trust; critics, including many police unions, contend that civilians lack the expertise to judge split-second decisions and that review can duplicate internal affairs. Research on effectiveness is mixed, and outcomes depend heavily on independence, funding, and authority.

Around the world

Analogous bodies exist internationally, including police ombudsmen and independent complaints commissions in the United Kingdom, Canada, and elsewhere, some with statutory power to investigate deaths in custody.
