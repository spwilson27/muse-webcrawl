Orpheus

Orpheus charms the underworld with his lyre.

Orpheus (Ancient Greek: Ὀρφεύς) is a legendary musician, poet, and prophet in ancient Greek religion and myth. Said to be the son of the Muse Calliope (and, in some traditions, of Apollo or the Thracian king Oeagrus), he possessed music of such power that it could charm animals, trees, and even stones.

The descent to the underworld

The most famous Orpheus myth tells of his wife Eurydice, who died of a snakebite on their wedding day. Orpheus descended alive into Hades and, by his music, moved the gods of the dead to let him lead her back — on the single condition that he not look back at her until both had reached the upper world. At the threshold, fearing he was being deceived, he turned; Eurydice was lost to him a second and final time. The story is told at length by Virgil in the Georgics and by Ovid in the Metamorphoses.

Other myths and death

Orpheus sailed with Jason and the Argonauts, saving the crew from the Sirens by outsounding them. After losing Eurydice he shunned the company of women and was torn apart by Thracian Maenads; his severed head, still singing, floated down the Hebrus to Lesbos, where it became an oracle. His lyre was placed among the stars as the constellation Lyra.

Orphism

A body of religious teachings and poems — the Orphic hymns, theogonies, and gold tablets buried with initiates — was attributed to Orpheus. Orphic religion taught the immortality and transmigration of the soul and the need for purification, influencing Pythagoreanism and later Greek thought. The myth has inspired countless works, from Monteverdi's L'Orfeo (1607), often called the first great opera, to Gluck, Offenbach, and modern retellings.
