Walter Burley Griffin (November 24, 1876 – February 11, 1937) was an American architect and landscape architect, best known for designing Canberra, the capital of Australia. He is associated with the Prairie School of architecture and developed the L-shaped floor plan and an early use of reinforced concrete construction, including the "Knitlock" modular block building system.

Early career

Griffin graduated in architecture from the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign in 1899 and worked in Frank Lloyd Wright's Oak Park studio from 1901 to 1905, becoming one of the leading figures of the Prairie School. In 1911 he married Marion Mahony, a talented architect and delineator who had been the first woman licensed as an architect in Illinois; her renderings were central to their joint practice.

Canberra

In 1912 the Griffins won the international competition to design Australia's new federal capital, Canberra. Griffin's plan — geometric axes aligned to the surrounding hills, a central artificial lake, and radiating avenues — was adopted, and he was appointed Federal Capital Director of Design and Construction. Years of bureaucratic conflict followed, and he resigned in 1920 with the plan substantially altered, though its core geometry survives in today's Canberra. Lake Burley Griffin, completed in 1963, bears his name.

Later work

The Griffins designed the Sydney suburb of Castlecrag, the Newman College building at the University of Melbourne, and numerous houses in Australia. In 1935 they moved to India, where Griffin designed libraries and exhibition buildings; he died of peritonitis in Lucknow in 1937.
