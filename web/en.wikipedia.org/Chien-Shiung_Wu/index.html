Chien-Shiung Wu (Chinese: 吳健雄; 1912–1997) was a Chinese-American experimental physicist who made significant contributions in nuclear and particle physics. She worked on the Manhattan Project, helping develop a process for separating uranium isotopes by gaseous diffusion, and is best known for the Wu experiment (1956), which demonstrated that parity is not conserved in weak interactions.

Life and career

Educated in China and at the University of California, Berkeley (PhD 1940), Wu taught at Smith College and Princeton before joining Columbia University in 1944, where she remained for her career. Her precision measurements of beta decay resolved the "tau–theta puzzle" context in which Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang proposed parity violation; her experiment, using polarised cobalt-60 at near absolute zero, confirmed it. Lee and Yang received the 1957 Nobel Prize; Wu's exclusion became a famous case of a woman scientist being overlooked, though she later received many honours, including the inaugural Wolf Prize in Physics in 1978.

Legacy

Wu also confirmed Fermi's theory of beta decay with key experiments and investigated the conserved vector current hypothesis. She has been nicknamed the "First Lady of Physics" and the "Chinese Marie Curie." She died in New York City in 1997, and in 2021 the United States Postal Service issued a stamp in her honour.
