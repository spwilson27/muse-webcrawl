Grijalva River

Native nameRío Grijalva (Spanish)
LocationMexico (Chiapas, Tabasco)
LengthApprox. 480 km
MouthGulf of Mexico, near Frontera, Tabasco

The Grijalva River (Spanish: Río Grijalva) is a river in southeastern Mexico, one of the country's largest by discharge. It flows for about 480 kilometres from the highlands of Chiapas through the state of Tabasco to the Gulf of Mexico. The river is named after the Spanish explorer Juan de Grijalva, who in 1518 led the first European expedition to its mouth.

Course

The Grijalva rises in the Sierra Madre de Chiapas near the Guatemalan border and descends through the Central Depression of Chiapas, passing the state capital Tuxtla Gutiérrez, where it cuts the spectacular Sumidero Canyon. It then crosses the plains of Tabasco, passing the city of Villahermosa, before joining the Gulf of Mexico near the port of Frontera. Its drainage basin, combined with the neighbouring Usumacinta River, forms the most important river system in Mexico and northern Central America.

Hydroelectric development

A cascade of major dams on the upper river — Angostura (Belisario Domínguez), Chicoasén (Manuel Moreno Torres), Malpaso (Nezahualcóyotl) and Peñitas — makes the Grijalva one of Mexico's principal sources of hydroelectric power. The Chicoasén dam, at the end of the Sumidero Canyon, is one of the tallest rock-fill dams in the world.

Flooding

The Tabasco floodplain is prone to severe inundation. In October–November 2007 the Grijalva and its tributaries flooded catastrophically, affecting over a million people and submerging much of Villahermosa in one of Mexico's worst natural disasters. Further major flooding occurred in 2020, prompting renewed debate over dam management, deforestation and flood-control works on the lower river.
