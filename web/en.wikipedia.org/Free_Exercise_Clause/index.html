Free Exercise Clause

The Free Exercise Clause is the portion of the First Amendment to the United States Constitution that prohibits the government from interfering with the free exercise of religion. Together with the Establishment Clause, which bars government establishment of religion, it forms the First Amendment's religion clauses: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof..."

Doctrine

The U.S. Supreme Court applied the clause against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in Cantwell v. Connecticut (1940). In Sherbert v. Verner (1963), the Court held that laws substantially burdening religious practice must satisfy strict scrutiny — a compelling government interest pursued by the least restrictive means. That standard was narrowed in Employment Division v. Smith (1990), which held that neutral, generally applicable laws do not require a religious exemption even if they burden religious practice.

Congress responded to Smith with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA), which restored the strict scrutiny test by statute for federal action, and many states enacted their own versions. Important free exercise cases include Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972), protecting Amish parents' right to withdraw children from formal schooling; Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993), striking down ordinances targeting Santería animal sacrifice; Burwell v. Hobby Lobby (2014), decided under RFRA; and Fulton v. City of Philadelphia (2021), concerning a Catholic foster care agency.
