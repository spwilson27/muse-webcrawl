Photograph

A photograph: an image fixed by the action of light on a sensitive surface.

A photograph is an image created by light falling on a light-sensitive surface, usually photographic film or an electronic image sensor. The word derives from the Greek phōtós (light) and gráphō (to draw): "drawing with light." Photographs are used in art, science, journalism, commerce, and personal memory-keeping worldwide.

History

The chemical basis emerged from observations that silver salts darken in light. In 1826 or 1827 Nicéphore Niépce made the earliest surviving camera photograph, a view from his window at Le Gras, using a bitumen-coated pewter plate and an exposure of hours. Louis Daguerre's daguerreotype (1839) and William Henry Fox Talbot's calotype paper negative made photography practical; George Eastman's roll film and Kodak cameras (1888) brought it to the mass market.

Technology

A camera lens focuses the scene's light onto the sensitive surface. Film records it in silver halide grains, developed into a permanent image; digital sensors convert photons to electrical signals stored as image files. Exposure is governed by aperture, shutter speed, and sensitivity (ISO). Colour photography, commercialized through processes such as Autochrome and Kodachrome, became dominant in the twentieth century before digital photography displaced film in the 2000s — the phonograph's optical cousin (see Phonograph) among media transformed by digitization.
