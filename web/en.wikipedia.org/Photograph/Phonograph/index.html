Phonograph

A phonograph, later called a gramophone (as a trademark since 1887, as a generic name in the UK since 1910), and since the 1940s a record player, or more recently a turntable, is a device for the mechanical and analogue reproduction of recorded sound. The sound vibration waveforms are recorded as corresponding physical deviations of a helical or spiral groove engraved, etched, incised, or impressed into the surface of a rotating cylinder or disc, called a phonograph record. To recreate the sound, the surface is rotated while a playback stylus traces the groove and is therefore vibrated by it, reproducing the recorded sound.

History

The phonograph was invented in 1877 by Thomas Edison. Phonograph cylinders were the earliest commercial medium for recording and reproducing sound, from the late 1880s. Emile Berliner's gramophone, which used flat discs, gradually supplanted the cylinder during the 1890s and 1900s; disc records became the dominant format for most of the 20th century, first as 78 rpm shellac discs and later as vinyl LPs and singles.

Alexander Graham Bell's Volta Laboratory made improvements in the 1880s, producing the graphophone, which used wax-coated cardboard cylinders. Early phonographs were sold with blank cylinders on which customers could record their own voices; prerecorded entertainment cylinders developed later.

Late 20th century and revival

The phonograph was the most common device for playing recorded music from the 1870s through the 1980s, when it was largely replaced by the cassette tape and then the compact disc. Vinyl records nevertheless experienced a significant revival in the 21st century, with turntable and record sales growing steadily.
