Securities Exchange Act of 1934

The 1934 Act brought the secondary trading of securities, including on the New York Stock Exchange, under federal regulation.

The Securities Exchange Act of 1934 is a United States federal law governing the secondary trading of securities — stocks, bonds and debentures — and the markets and financial professionals who participate in them. Enacted in the wake of the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the Great Depression, it was signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on June 6, 1934. Whereas the Securities Act of 1933 regulates the initial issuance of securities, the 1934 Act regulates trading after issuance.

Provisions

The Act created the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) to enforce the federal securities laws. It requires public companies to register their securities and file periodic reports — annual reports on Form 10-K, quarterly reports on Form 10-Q, and current reports on Form 8-K — so that investors have a continuous stream of information. It regulates stock exchanges, brokers and dealers, and transfer agents, and authorizes the Federal Reserve to set margin requirements for credit used to purchase securities.

Anti-fraud and market rules

Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5, adopted under it, prohibit fraud in connection with the purchase or sale of any security and form the basis of most securities-fraud litigation, including insider trading cases. Section 9 outlaws market manipulation. The Act also governs proxy solicitations, tender offers (expanded by the Williams Act of 1968), and beneficial-ownership reporting by corporate insiders under Section 16.

Amendments and legacy

The Act has been amended many times, notably by the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, which added internal-control and executive-certification requirements after the Enron scandal, and by the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010. Together with the 1933 Act it remains the foundation of United States securities regulation.
