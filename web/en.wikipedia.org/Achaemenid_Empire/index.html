Achaemenid Empire

Period: c. 550 – 330 BC
Capital: Babylon, Pasargadae, Susa, Persepolis, Ecbatana
Greatest extent: c. 5.5 million km² under Darius I
Common languages: Old Persian, Imperial Aramaic, Elamite, others

The Achaemenid Empire (c. 550–330 BC) was an ancient Iranian empire founded by Cyrus the Great. At its greatest extent under Darius I it stretched from the Balkans and Eastern Europe in the west to the Indus Valley in the east, encompassing some 5.5 million square kilometres — the largest empire the world had yet seen — and perhaps 40–50% of the world's population at the time.

History

Cyrus II of Persia united the Persian tribes, defeated the Median Empire, then conquered Lydia (546 BC) and the Neo-Babylonian Empire (539 BC). His son Cambyses II added Egypt. Darius I (522–486 BC) reorganised the empire into provinces (satrapies) governed by satraps, built an extensive road system including the Royal Road, standardised coinage and law, and moved into Thrace and Macedonia. His invasion of Greece was defeated at Marathon (490 BC); his son Xerxes I's larger expedition (480 BC) also failed at Salamis and Plataea.

The empire endured for two more centuries before falling to Alexander the Great, whose armies won at Issus (333 BC) and Gaugamela (331 BC); the last king, Darius III, died in 330 BC.

Administration and culture

The Achaemenids ruled a multiethnic empire with notable religious tolerance, repatriating exiled peoples — most famously allowing the Jews to return from Babylon and rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem. Imperial Aramaic served as a lingua franca; royal inscriptions such as the Behistun Inscription were carved in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian. Persepolis, founded by Darius, served as the ceremonial capital, and Zoroastrianism was the faith of the ruling house.
