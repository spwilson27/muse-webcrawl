Beta function (physics)

In theoretical physics, specifically quantum field theory, a beta function, β(g), encodes the dependence of a coupling parameter g on the energy scale μ of a physical process, as described by the renormalization group. It is defined by

β(g) = ∂g / ∂(ln μ).

The sign and zeros of the beta function determine how interactions strengthen or weaken with energy and whether a theory possesses fixed points.

Quantum chromodynamics

The beta function of quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is negative for few enough quark flavors: the coupling decreases at higher energies, a phenomenon known as asymptotic freedom, discovered by David Gross, Frank Wilczek, and H. David Politzer in 1973 (Nobel Prize 2004). Conversely, the coupling grows at low energies, consistent with the confinement of quarks and gluons inside hadrons. In quantum electrodynamics the beta function is positive: the electric charge is screened at long distances and grows at short distances.

Fixed points and phase transitions

Zeros of the beta function define scale-invariant fixed points. The idea, developed through the work of Murray Gell-Mann and Francis Low (1954) and the renormalization group equations of Callan and Symanzik, also underlies Kenneth Wilson's theory of critical phenomena: near a second-order phase transition, couplings flow to a fixed point characterized by critical exponents. The epsilon expansion exploits the beta function near four dimensions.

Exact results

In supersymmetric theories, the NSVZ beta function gives an exact relation between the beta function and anomalous dimensions. Conformal field theories are precisely those theories whose beta functions vanish identically.
