Island biogeography (or insular biogeography) is a field within biogeography that examines the factors affecting the species richness and diversification of isolated natural communities. The theory was originally developed to explain the pattern of species richness on true islands, but it has since been extended to habitat "islands" surrounded by inhospitable terrain — mountain tops, lakes, nature reserves, and forest fragments.

The MacArthur–Wilson equilibrium model

In 1967 Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson published The Theory of Island Biogeography, proposing that the number of species on an island reflects a balance between two processes. The immigration rate of new species is highest when the island is empty and declines as species accumulate and fewer arrivals are new. The extinction rate is low when few species are present and rises as crowding, competition, and small population sizes take their toll. Where the curves cross, the island sits at a dynamic equilibrium: the species count stays roughly constant even as the composition keeps changing — a prediction called the turnover effect.

Area and distance effects

The model explains two robust empirical patterns. First, larger islands have more species than smaller ones (the species–area relationship): larger islands offer more habitats and larger populations, lowering extinction rates. Second, islands nearer a mainland have more species than remote ones (the distance effect), because immigration is easier. MacArthur and Wilson also predicted refinements such as the "rescue effect", in which continued immigration shores up populations on near islands and lowers their extinction rates.

Evidence and tests

Natural experiments supported the theory: after the 1883 eruption of Krakatoa sterilized its islands, birds and plants recolonized toward equilibrium numbers within decades. In a famous manipulative test, Wilson and Daniel Simberloff fumigated small mangrove islands in Florida Bay and recorded arthropods recolonizing to approximately their original species numbers, though with different composition — direct evidence of equilibrium turnover.

Applications

Island biogeography strongly influenced conservation biology, particularly in reserve design — the "SLOSS" debate over whether a single large or several small reserves protect more species — and in understanding habitat fragmentation, mountaintop ("sky island") communities threatened by climate change, and the biotas of lakes and caves. Island evolution itself produces characteristic phenomena studied by the field, including adaptive radiation (as in Darwin's finches and Hawaiian honeycreepers), island gigantism and dwarfism, and the loss of dispersal ability in island species.
