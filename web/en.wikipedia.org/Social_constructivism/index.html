Social constructivism

Social constructivism is a sociological and psychological theory of knowledge according to which human development is socially situated and knowledge is constructed through interaction with others. It holds that individuals construct meaning in collaboration with peers, teachers and their culture, rather than discovering an objective reality entirely on their own.

Origins

Social constructivism draws heavily on the work of the Soviet psychologist Lev Vygotsky (1896–1934), who argued that cognitive development is fundamentally shaped by social interaction and culture. Vygotsky introduced the concept of the zone of proximal development — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can achieve with guidance from a more knowledgeable other. His work was published in the West only decades after his death, notably in Mind in Society (1978) and Thought and Language.

Key ideas

  • Social interaction precedes development: higher mental functions appear first between people (interpsychological) and only later inside the individual (intrapsychological).
  • Language as a tool: speech and other cultural sign systems mediate thought and learning.
  • Scaffolding: temporary support provided by teachers or peers enables learners to perform tasks beyond their independent ability.
  • Communities of practice: learning is participation in shared activities, as developed by Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger.

In education

In classrooms, social constructivism underpins collaborative learning, peer tutoring, problem-based learning and discussion-based teaching. Teachers act as facilitators rather than transmitters of information, designing tasks that require students to articulate, negotiate and defend ideas together. Assessment tends to emphasise process and authentic performance over rote recall.

Relation to other theories

Social constructivism is related to, but distinct from, the constructivism of Jean Piaget, which focuses on the individual's active construction of knowledge through stages of development, and from social constructionism in sociology, which examines how social phenomena and categories themselves are created through collective processes.
