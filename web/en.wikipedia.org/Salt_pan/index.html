Salt pan

The white crust of a natural salt pan.

A salt pan forms when a pool of water evaporates faster than it is replenished, leaving behind a flat expanse covered with salt and other minerals. Natural salt pans occur in deserts and arid basins where drainage has no outlet to the sea, while artificial salt pans — also called salt evaporation ponds — are constructed to harvest salt from seawater or brine.

Natural pans

Famous natural salt pans include the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia, the largest salt flat in the world at over 10,000 km²; the Makgadikgadi Pan in Botswana; Etosha Pan in Namibia; and Badwater Basin in Death Valley, California. Their extremely flat, reflective surfaces are used to calibrate satellites, and the lithium-rich brines beneath some South American salars are a major source of lithium.

Artificial pans

In salt works (salinas), seawater is channelled through a sequence of shallow ponds where sun and wind progressively concentrate the brine until salt crystallises and can be raked up. Such ponds, often coloured pink or red by halophilic algae and bacteria, also provide important habitat for shorebirds and brine shrimp.

Hazards

The hard crust of a salt pan can conceal soft mud beneath, trapping vehicles; the deceptive surface has long been a danger to travellers crossing desert playas.
