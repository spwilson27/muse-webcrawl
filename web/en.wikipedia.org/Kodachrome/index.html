Kodachrome

ManufacturerEastman Kodak
Introduced1935 (16 mm film); 1936 (35 mm stills)
Discontinued2009 (processing ended 2010)
TypeColour reversal film

Kodachrome was the first successful mass-market colour photographic film, introduced by Eastman Kodak in 1935. A subtractive colour reversal film, it became famous for its rich, saturated colours, fine grain, and exceptional archival stability, and was the film of choice for generations of professional photographers, photojournalists, and filmmakers.

History

Kodachrome was invented by Leopold Mannes and Leopold Godowsky Jr., two professional musicians and amateur chemists who worked with Kodak's research laboratories. It launched first as 16 mm movie film in 1935, followed by 35 mm and sheet formats for still photography in 1936. Unlike later films, Kodachrome contained no dye couplers in its emulsion; the dyes were introduced during the complex, tightly controlled K-14 processing sequence, which only Kodak and a few licensed laboratories could perform.

Characteristics and legacy

Kodachrome was noted for its sharpness, warm colour palette, and dark-storage stability measured in many decades. Iconic images shot on it include much of National Geographic's mid-century colour photography and Steve McCurry's "Afghan Girl" portrait (1984). Speeds ranged from the slow original emulsions to Kodachrome 200; Kodachrome 25 and 64 were especially prized. Paul Simon's 1973 hit song "Kodachrome" cemented the film's place in popular culture.

Discontinuation

Declining sales in the digital era led Kodak to end production in 2009. The last roll was processed at Dwayne's Photo in Parsons, Kansas, on 30 December 2010, ending 75 years of Kodachrome photography.
