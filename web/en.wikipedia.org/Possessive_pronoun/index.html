Possessive pronoun

English possessive pronouns
Independent formsmine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs
Dependent forms (determiners)my, your, his, her, its, our, their
FunctionIndicate possession or association in place of a noun phrase

A possessive pronoun is a pronoun that expresses possession, ownership, or close association, standing in place of a noun phrase: for example, "This book is mine" or "The choice is yours." In English these independent possessive pronouns are mine, yours, his, hers, ours, and theirs (the independent form of its is rare).

Pronouns versus determiners

English also has a set of dependent possessive forms — my, your, his, her, its, our, their — which must accompany a noun ("my book"). Traditional grammar called these "possessive adjectives"; modern linguistics classifies them as possessive determiners, since they function like articles rather than adjectives. The distinction matters because only the independent forms can stand alone as a full noun phrase ("Hers is the red one"), whereas determiners cannot.

Grammatical features

Possessive pronouns encode the person and number of the possessor, not of the thing possessed: our remains our whether one thing or many is owned. Third-person singular forms also mark the gender or animacy of the possessor (his, hers, its). Unlike the possessive determiners, the independent pronouns (except his) do not take the apostrophe — a frequent source of confusion with the contraction it's ("it is"). Several possessive pronouns double as possessive forms in double genitive constructions, as in "a friend of mine."

In other languages

Many languages inflect possessive pronouns for the gender, number, and case of the possessed noun, as French le mien, la mienne or German meiner, meine, meines. Others, such as Japanese, have no possessive pronouns at all, using a possessive particle with ordinary pronouns instead. Some languages also distinguish alienable from inalienable possession within their possessive systems.
