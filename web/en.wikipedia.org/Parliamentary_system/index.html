Parliamentary system

Parliamentary system Head of government: Prime minister (or chancellor/equivalent)
Head of state: Separate — president or monarch
Key mechanism: Executive depends on legislative confidence
Examples: United Kingdom, Germany, India, Japan, Canada, Australia

A parliamentary system is a form of government in which the executive branch derives its democratic legitimacy from its ability to command the support ("confidence") of the legislature (parliament), to which it is accountable. In contrast to a presidential system, the executive and legislative branches are interconnected: the head of government — usually a prime minister — is normally a member of the legislature and must resign, with the cabinet, if it loses a vote of no confidence.

Characteristics

Parliamentary systems have a distinct head of state, who is either a president (in parliamentary republics such as Germany, India, Italy, and Israel) or a hereditary monarch (in constitutional monarchies such as the United Kingdom, Spain, the Netherlands, and Japan's system in effect). The head of state normally performs ceremonial functions and appoints the head of government, while executive power is exercised by the prime minister and cabinet. Because the government depends on parliamentary support, elections may be called early when confidence collapses, though many countries (Germany, and the UK between 2011 and 2022) have used fixed-term rules or require a constructive vote of no confidence that simultaneously elects a successor.

Varieties

The Westminster system, inherited from the United Kingdom across much of the Commonwealth, features adversarial debate, single-party majority governments under first-past-the-post elections in many cases, and a loyal opposition. Consensus systems, typical of continental Europe, use proportional representation and coalition governments. A semi-presidential system, such as France's, combines a directly elected president with significant powers alongside a prime minister accountable to parliament, and is sometimes classed separately.

Advantages and criticisms

Defenders argue that parliamentary systems avoid the deadlock and rival legitimacies of separated presidential systems, allow rapid removal of failing governments, and ensure continuous accountability. Critics point to instability under fragmented parliaments (the French Fourth Republic and Italy are common examples), executive dominance where a disciplined majority makes the prime minister quasi-presidential, and the weaker separation of powers. Political scientists such as Juan Linz famously argued that presidentialism is more prone to democratic breakdown, a thesis debated ever since.
