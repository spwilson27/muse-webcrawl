Germination

Germination is the process by which an organism grows from a seed or spore. The term is most commonly applied to the sprouting of a seedling from the seed of an angiosperm or gymnosperm. Germination begins with the uptake of water by the dormant seed (imbibition) and ends with the emergence of the embryonic root, the radicle, which anchors the seed and absorbs water for the growing plant.

Requirements

Viable seeds require water, oxygen, and a suitable temperature to germinate; some species additionally require particular light conditions or dormancy-breaking treatments such as cold stratification or scarification of the seed coat. During imbibition the seed swells, respiration increases, and stored food reserves — starch, proteins, and oils stored in the endosperm or cotyledons — are mobilised by enzymes such as amylase, under the control of plant hormones including gibberellins; abscisic acid, by contrast, maintains dormancy.

Types

In epigeal germination, as in beans, the hypocotyl elongates and lifts the cotyledons above the soil surface. In hypogeal germination, as in peas and maize, the cotyledons remain below ground and the epicotyl emerges. Some plants, such as mangroves, show vivipary, in which the seed germinates while still attached to the parent plant.

Germination rate and testing

Agriculturists and gardeners measure seed quality by the germination rate — the percentage of seeds in a lot that germinate under standard conditions — and by seedling vigour. Many jurisdictions require commercial seed lots to meet minimum germination standards.
