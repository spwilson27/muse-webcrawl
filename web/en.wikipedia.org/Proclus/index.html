Proclus Lycius (Greek: Πρόκλος Λύκιος; 8 February 412 – 17 April 485), called Proclus the Successor (Πρόκλος ὁ Διάδοχος, Próklos ho Diádokhos), was a Greek Neoplatonist philosopher, one of the last major classical philosophers of Antiquity. He set forth one of the most elaborate and fully developed systems of Neoplatonism and, through translations and commentaries, exerted a profound influence on Islamic and medieval Christian philosophy. He headed the Platonist school at Athens for nearly 50 years.

Life

Proclus was born in Constantinople to wealthy parents from Lycia and studied rhetoric and philosophy at Alexandria before moving to Athens, where he studied under Plutarch of Athens and Syrianus, whom he succeeded as head (scholarch) of the Academy around 437. His pupil Marinus wrote a biography describing his extraordinary productivity — allegedly 700 lines a day — his ascetic devotion to pagan worship, and his political involvement in Athens. He died in 485 and was succeeded by Marinus.

Works and philosophy

His Elements of Theology presents Neoplatonic metaphysics as a rigorously deductive system of 211 propositions, describing the procession of all reality from the One through Intellect and Soul. The Platonic Theology systematically expounds the hierarchy of gods. Proclus also wrote extensive commentaries on Plato's Timaeus, Republic, Alcibiades and Parmenides, a commentary on Euclid's Elements that is a major source for the history of Greek mathematics, and works on astronomy and on Providence and evil. His thought influenced Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite and, via the Arabic Book of Causes tradition, medieval scholasticism.
