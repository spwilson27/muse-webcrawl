Osteocyte

SystemSkeletal
LocationWithin bone matrix (lacunae)

An osteocyte, a type of bone cell, is the most commonly found cell in mature bone tissue. Osteocytes may live as long as the organism itself; the adult human skeleton is estimated to contain about 42 billion of them. They are former osteoblasts that became trapped in the bone matrix they secreted, residing in small spaces called lacunae and communicating through slender processes running in channels called canaliculi.

Structure

The cell body of an osteocyte is roughly 15–30 μm across and star-shaped, with numerous dendritic processes extending through the canaliculi. Through gap junctions at the ends of these processes, osteocytes form a connected network — the lacuno-canalicular system — that links them to each other, to surface osteoblasts and to the vasculature.

Function

Osteocytes act as the principal mechanosensors of bone: they detect strains and microdamage and orchestrate the response of osteoclasts and osteoblasts in bone remodelling. They secrete sclerostin, an inhibitor of the Wnt signalling pathway that restrains bone formation, and FGF23, a hormone regulating phosphate metabolism. Osteocytes can also remove and replace bone immediately around their lacunae, a process called osteocytic osteolysis.

Clinical significance

Osteocyte death, which increases with age and after glucocorticoid treatment, is associated with bone fragility and impaired repair of microdamage. Mutations affecting osteocyte products such as sclerostin cause high-bone-mass disorders like sclerosteosis, while the anti-sclerostin antibody romosozumab is used to treat osteoporosis.
