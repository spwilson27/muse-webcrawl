History of rail transport in Japan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Shinkansen, introduced in 1964, symbolized Japan's postwar technological resurgence.

The history of rail transport in Japan began in the Meiji period, when Japan's first railway opened in 1872 between Shimbashi in Tokyo and Yokohama, built with British engineering assistance. The line used the narrow gauge of 1,067 mm (3 ft 6 in), which became the standard for most of Japan's conventional network, a decision that shaped Japanese railways ever after.

Expansion and nationalization

During the following decades both the government and private companies built lines rapidly. The first line on Hokkaido opened in 1880, and by the late 1880s the Tokaido Main Line connected Tokyo with Kobe. Under the Railway Nationalization Act of 1906, the government purchased the major private railways, creating a state network administered later by Japanese Government Railways and, after 1949, Japanese National Railways (JNR).

War and reconstruction

The network suffered extensive damage in the Second World War but was rebuilt under the occupation. Electrification and dieselization replaced steam traction during the 1950s and 1960s. Urban private railways and subways expanded greatly in Tokyo and Osaka; Tokyo's first subway line had opened in 1927.

The Shinkansen

On 1 October 1964, shortly before the Tokyo Olympic Games, the Tokaido Shinkansen opened between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka, the world's first high-speed railway, operating at 210 km/h on standard gauge. The network expanded with the Sanyo, Tohoku and Joetsu Shinkansen in the following decades, and trains eventually reached speeds of 300 km/h and beyond. The Shinkansen became famous for its punctuality and safety record.

Privatization and the modern network

In 1987 the heavily indebted JNR was divided and privatized into the six regional passenger companies and one freight company of the JR Group. Private railways, subways and newer transit systems such as monorails and automated guideway lines continue to serve Japan's dense metropolitan areas. Magnetic levitation (maglev) technology, tested for decades, underlies the Chuo Shinkansen under construction between Tokyo and Nagoya, designed for speeds of 500 km/h.
