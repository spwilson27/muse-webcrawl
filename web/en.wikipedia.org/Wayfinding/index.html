Wayfinding

Wayfinding (or way-finding) encompasses all the ways in which people orient themselves in physical space and navigate from place to place. The term is also used for the design discipline concerned with signs, maps, landmarks, and environmental information systems that help people find their way in buildings, transport networks, and cities.

Human wayfinding

People navigate using a combination of strategies: following known routes, using landmarks, reading signs, and forming cognitive maps — mental representations of an environment. Environmental psychologist Kevin A. Lynch, in The Image of the City (1960), identified paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks as the elements from which people build such images. Wayfinding behaviour varies with culture, familiarity, and ability, and traditional systems — such as Polynesian navigation by stars, waves, and birds — demonstrate sophisticated non-instrument navigation.

Design practice

In architecture and design, wayfinding refers to the deliberate planning of signage, naming, numbering, lighting, sightlines, and digital navigation aids in complex environments such as airports, hospitals, and campuses. Good wayfinding design aims to reduce stress and errors, supporting accessibility and legibility for first-time users, and overlaps with information design and environmental graphic design.
