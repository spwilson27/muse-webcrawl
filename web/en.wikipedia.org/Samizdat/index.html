Samizdat

Meaning: “Self-publishing” (Russian: сам = self, издат = publishing)

Region: Soviet Union and Eastern Bloc

Period: 1950s–1980s

Related forms: Tamizdat, magnitizdat, roentgenizdat

Samizdat (Russian: самиздат, “self-published”) was the clandestine copying and distribution of literature banned by the state in the Soviet Union and other Eastern Bloc countries. Dissidents reproduced uncensored texts by hand — most often laboriously retyped on manual typewriters with carbon paper, each typing producing only a handful of legible copies — and passed them from reader to reader, each recipient expected to make further copies. Samizdat became a central medium of the Soviet dissident movement and a byword for underground publishing under censorship.

History

Self-copying of forbidden texts has older precedents in Russia, but samizdat as a mass phenomenon dates from the Khrushchev Thaw of the 1950s, when poetry by repressed writers such as Anna Akhmatova and Osip Mandelstam, and prose by Boris Pasternak, began to circulate in typescript. After the 1966 trial of the writers Andrei Sinyavsky and Yuli Daniel, samizdat acquired a political dimension: stenograms of trials, open letters, petitions and human-rights bulletins circulated widely. From 1968 the Chronicle of Current Events, a meticulous record of arrests, trials and camp conditions, appeared in samizdat for fifteen years despite KGB efforts to suppress it.

Works by Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, including The Gulag Archipelago, circulated in samizdat before publication abroad. Related practices included tamizdat (“publishing over there”, i.e. works smuggled out and published in the West, then smuggled back), magnitizdat (tape-recorded songs of bard poets such as Bulat Okudzhava and Vladimir Vysotsky) and roentgenizdat (forbidden music cut onto discarded X-ray film).

Repression and significance

Possession and distribution of samizdat could bring dismissal, expulsion, imprisonment under anti-Soviet agitation laws, or confinement in psychiatric hospitals. Typewriters were registered and their typefaces sampled by the authorities so that typescripts could be traced to a machine. Nevertheless samizdat created an alternative public sphere, preserved a suppressed literary heritage, and communicated the human-rights movement’s record to the world. With glasnost in the late 1980s the banned texts were published officially and samizdat faded away, its archive now a key source for historians of the late Soviet period.
