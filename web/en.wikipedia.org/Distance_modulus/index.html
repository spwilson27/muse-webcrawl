Distance modulus

The distance modulus is a way of expressing interstellar and intergalactic distances in astronomy. It is usually used to express the distance to a galaxy or star cluster. It relates the apparent magnitude m and absolute magnitude M of an astronomical object:

μ = m − M = 5 log10(d / 10 pc)

where d is the distance in parsecs. By convention, the absolute magnitude is the apparent magnitude an object would have if it were located at a distance of 10 parsecs. A distance modulus of 0 thus corresponds to a distance of 10 parsecs; each increase of 5 in the modulus corresponds to a tenfold increase in distance.

Definition

The distance modulus μ is defined as the difference between the apparent magnitude m and the absolute magnitude M of an object. Solving for distance gives:

d = 10(μ/5) + 1 pc

For example, the Large Magellanic Cloud has a distance modulus of about 18.5, corresponding to roughly 50 kiloparsecs, while the Andromeda Galaxy has a distance modulus of about 24.4, corresponding to roughly 765 kiloparsecs.

Different kinds of distance modulus

Distance is not the only quantity that affects apparent magnitude. Absorption of light by interstellar dust (extinction) also dims objects, so the observed distance modulus exceeds the "true" distance modulus by the amount of extinction along the line of sight. Astronomers distinguish the true distance modulus μ0 from the observed modulus after correcting or not correcting for extinction A:

μ = μ0 + A

Usage

Distance moduli are central to the cosmic distance ladder and to standard-candle methods such as Cepheid variable stars, RR Lyrae variables, and Type Ia supernovae. Modern measurements report moduli with uncertainties of a few hundredths of a magnitude for nearby galaxies.
