Blowout (well drilling)

A blowout is the uncontrolled release of crude oil or natural gas from a well after pressure control systems have failed. In the early days of oil exploration blowouts were common and spectacular — gushers announced a rich strike — but in modern drilling a blowout is a serious accident that can kill workers, destroy rigs and cause major pollution.

Causes

Blowouts occur when formation pressure exceeds the hydrostatic pressure of the drilling mud column, producing an influx (a “kick”) that is not detected or controlled in time. Contributing factors include unexpectedly high-pressure zones, lost circulation, swabbing while tripping pipe, and failure of the blowout preventer (BOP), the stack of valves designed as the last line of defence.

Prevention and control

Modern practice relies on mud-weight management, kick detection, crew well-control training, and tested, redundant BOPs. Once a blowout occurs, options include capping the well, pumping in heavy “kill” mud, or drilling a relief well to intercept the borehole, a process that can take months.

Notable blowouts

Famous blowouts include the Spindletop gusher of 1901, the Ixtoc I spill in the Gulf of Mexico in 1979, and the Deepwater Horizon blowout of 20 April 2010, which killed eleven workers and produced the largest marine oil spill in United States history.
