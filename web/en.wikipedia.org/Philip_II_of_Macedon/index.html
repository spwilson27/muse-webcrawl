Philip II of Macedon

Philip II of Macedon (382–336 BC) was the king of the ancient Greek kingdom of Macedon from 359 BC until his assassination in 336 BC. He transformed Macedon from a weak peripheral state into the dominant power of Greece and laid the military and political foundations for the conquests of his son, Alexander the Great.

Rise to power

Philip became regent, then king, after his brother Perdiccas III was killed fighting the Illyrians. As a youth he had been a hostage in Thebes, where he observed the tactics of Epaminondas. He rebuilt the Macedonian army, introducing the sarissa — a pike of up to 6 metres — and the phalanx formation that bore his name, combined with elite Companion cavalry.

Conquest of Greece

Over two decades of warfare and diplomacy, Philip annexed Thessaly and Thrace, defeated Athens and Thebes at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BC, and organised the Greek states (except Sparta) into the League of Corinth under Macedonian leadership, preparing a campaign against the Persian Empire.

Assassination and legacy

Philip was assassinated at Aegae during the wedding of his daughter Cleopatra, killed by his bodyguard Pausanias. He was succeeded by his son Alexander. A tomb at Vergina discovered in 1977, with rich grave goods, is widely attributed to Philip.
