Indian termination policy

Indian termination was a policy of the United States government from the mid-1940s to the mid-1960s intended to assimilate Native Americans into mainstream society. It proposed ending the federal government's recognition of tribal sovereignty, dissolving reservations, and subjecting Native people to the same laws and taxes as other citizens. Formalised by House Concurrent Resolution 108 in 1953, the policy led to the termination of more than 100 tribes and bands and the loss of over a million acres of tribal land.

Termination was enacted through a series of tribe-specific statutes in the 1950s.

Implementation

Termination acts targeted tribes considered "ready" for assimilation, including the Menominee of Wisconsin and the Klamath of Oregon, both of whom held valuable timber resources; the results included poverty, loss of services, and land sales. Public Law 280 transferred criminal and civil jurisdiction over many reservations to states. A companion relocation programme encouraged Native people to move to cities such as Chicago, Denver, and Los Angeles.

End of the policy

Termination was widely judged a failure. President Richard Nixon formally repudiated it in 1970, and the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 inaugurated the modern era of tribal self-governance. Several terminated tribes, including the Menominee, later had their federal recognition restored.
