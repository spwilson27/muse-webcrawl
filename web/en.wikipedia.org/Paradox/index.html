Paradox

A paradox is a logically self-contradictory statement or a statement that runs contrary to one's expectation. It is a statement that, despite apparently valid reasoning from true or apparently true premises, leads to a seemingly self-contradictory or logically unacceptable conclusion. A paradox usually involves contradictory yet interrelated elements that exist simultaneously and persist over time.

Paradox
EtymologyFrom Greek paradoxos, "contrary to expectation"
FieldsLogic, philosophy, mathematics, physics, literature

Common themes

Many paradoxes arise from self-reference, in which a statement refers to itself (as in the liar paradox, "This statement is false"), from vagueness (as in the sorites paradox of the heap), or from conflicts between intuition and formal results. The philosopher W. V. O. Quine distinguished three classes: veridical paradoxes, which produce a result that seems absurd but is demonstrably true; falsidical paradoxes, which rest on a fallacy; and antinomies, which reach genuine contradictions and have forced revisions of foundational theories.

Famous paradoxes

  • Liar paradox — a sentence asserting its own falsehood.
  • Russell's paradox — the set of all sets that do not contain themselves; it overturned naive set theory.
  • Zeno's paradoxes — motion argued to be impossible; resolved by the mathematics of infinite series and calculus.
  • Grandfather paradox — a time traveller preventing their own existence.
  • Ship of Theseus — identity through gradual replacement of parts.
  • Banach–Tarski paradox — a ball decomposed and reassembled into two balls identical to the original, a veridical result of set theory.
  • Fermi paradox — the tension between the expected abundance of extraterrestrial life and the absence of evidence for it.
  • Paradox of thrift and other economic paradoxes, where individually rational saving harms the aggregate economy.

In logic and mathematics

Paradoxes have been engines of progress: Russell's paradox led to axiomatic set theory and type theory; the paradoxes of infinity reshaped analysis; and Gödel's incompleteness theorems grew from self-referential constructions akin to the liar paradox. In physics, paradoxes such as the twin paradox of special relativity and the black hole information paradox continue to probe the consistency of theories.

In literature

In rhetoric and literature, "paradox" also denotes a figure of speech — a seemingly contradictory statement that reveals a deeper truth, as in "less is more" or Oscar Wilde's witticisms. Such paradoxes are devices of poetry and drama rather than logical problems.
