Atchafalaya Basin

The Atchafalaya Basin, or Atchafalaya Swamp, is the largest wetland and swamp in the United States. Located in south-central Louisiana, it is a combination of wetlands and river delta area where the Atchafalaya River and the Gulf of Mexico converge. The basin contains about 260,000 hectares (nearly 1 million acres) of bottomland hardwood forest, swamps, bayous and backwater lakes, and is famous for its Cajun culture, crawfish industry, and remarkable wildlife.

Geology and hydrology

The basin is the floodway of the Atchafalaya River, a distributary of the Mississippi and Red rivers. Without human intervention the Mississippi would have shifted its main flow into the shorter, steeper Atchafalaya channel; the US Army Corps of Engineers' Old River Control Structure (completed 1963) limits the Atchafalaya to about 30% of the combined flow. Levees confine the floodway, and sediment delivered by the river is building new delta land at Atchafalaya Bay — one of the few places on the Louisiana coast gaining land.

Ecology

The basin supports bald cypress–tupelo swamps, hundreds of bird species including the roseate spoonbill, and fisheries for crawfish, catfish and bass. It hosts one of the largest nesting concentrations of wood storks and herons in North America. Threats include sedimentation of backwater lakes, saltwater intrusion, invasive species and the legacy of oil and gas canals.

People and culture

Acadian (Cajun) settlers and other communities adapted to the basin's seasonal flooding, living in houseboats and camps and harvesting its fish, crawfish, fur and timber. The basin's culture — French language, foodways and music — is celebrated worldwide; the region is also known for swamp tours and as the setting of numerous books and films.
