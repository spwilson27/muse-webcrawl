Impeachment

Impeachment
[Image: A legislature in session conducting an impeachment trial]
DefinitionFormal process by which a legislature charges a public official with misconduct
OriginMedieval England; adopted in many constitutions

Impeachment is a process by which a legislative body or other legally constituted tribunal initiates charges against a public official for misconduct. In most systems, impeachment itself is the bringing of charges — comparable to a criminal indictment — while removal from office follows only upon conviction, often by a separate vote or body. The word derives from the Latin impedicare, "to catch by the foot".

History

The procedure originated in England, where Parliament first used it in 1376 against Baron Latimer during the Good Parliament. British impeachment fell into disuse after the early 19th century (the last trial was that of Lord Melville in 1806), but the concept was carried into the United States Constitution and then into many other constitutions worldwide.

Practice in different countries

In the United States, the House of Representatives impeaches by majority vote and the Senate tries the case, requiring two-thirds to convict. Comparable mechanisms exist in many democracies: South Korea's National Assembly impeaches and its Constitutional Court decides removal (as with Presidents Roh Moo-hyun in 2004, reinstated, and Park Geun-hye, removed in 2017); Brazil removed President Dilma Rousseff in 2016; Lithuania removed President Rolandas Paksas in 2004. Grounds typically include treason, bribery, corruption, and grave violations of the constitution, though their interpretation is often intensely political.
