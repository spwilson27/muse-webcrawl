Haakon the Good

Haakon Haraldsson (c. 920–961), known as Haakon the Good (Old Norse: Hákon góði) and sometimes as Haakon Adalsteinfostre (Old Norse: Hákon Aðalsteinsfóstri) after his foster father, was the third king of Norway and the youngest son of Harald I of Norway, called Fairhair, and Thora Mosterstong.

Life

Haakon was fostered at the court of King Æthelstan of England, where he was raised a Christian — hence his byname Adalsteinfostre, "foster-son of Æthelstan". On Harald Fairhair's death, Haakon's half-brother Eric Bloodaxe took the throne and ruled harshly. Around 934 Haakon returned to Norway with English support, was acclaimed king, and Eric fled the country.

As king, Haakon attempted to introduce Christianity into Norway, building churches and bringing missionaries from England, but met strong resistance from pagan chieftains and was forced to compromise; according to the sagas he himself participated in pagan sacrifices under pressure. He is credited with organizing the leiðangr naval levy and establishing law codes, including revisions associated with the Gulating and Frostating assemblies.

Death

Haakon spent his reign fighting the sons of Eric Bloodaxe, who repeatedly invaded from Denmark with the support of Harald Bluetooth. He defeated them several times, but at the Battle of Fitjar on Stord (c. 961) he was mortally wounded while winning the battle. According to tradition, he died asking to be carried to Sæheimr. He was succeeded by Eric's son Harald Greycloak. The skald Eyvindr skáldaspillir composed the poem Hákonarmál in his honour, depicting his welcome into Valhalla.
