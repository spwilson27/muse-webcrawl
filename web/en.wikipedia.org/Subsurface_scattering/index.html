Subsurface scattering

Subsurface scattering (SSS), also known as subsurface light transport (SSLT), is a mechanism of light transport in which light that penetrates the surface of a translucent object is scattered by interacting with the material and exits the surface at a different point. The light will generally penetrate the surface and be reflected a number of times at irregular angles inside the material before passing back out of the material at a different angle than it would have had if it had been reflected directly off the surface.

Subsurface scattering is important for realistic 3D computer graphics, being necessary for the realistic rendering of materials such as marble, skin, leaves, wax, and milk.

Rendering techniques

Most materials in reality exhibit some amount of subsurface scattering. Efficient simulation is challenging because light can enter and exit at many different points. Approaches include the dipole approximation derived from the BSSRDF (bidirectional scattering-surface reflectance distribution function), texture-space diffusion methods used for real-time skin rendering, and Monte Carlo path tracing with volumetric transport. Henrik Wann Jensen's work on the BSSRDF in 2001 was a landmark, and the effect was widely popularized in film rendering after its use on characters such as Gollum.

Examples

Human skin is a classic example: light penetrates the outer layers and scatters within the dermis, giving skin its soft appearance and reddish tones where blood absorbs other wavelengths. Other examples include marble sculptures that glow subtly when backlit, candle wax, grapes, and jade.
