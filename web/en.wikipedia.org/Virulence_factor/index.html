Virulence factor

Virulence factors (in medicine, also referred to as pathogenicity factors) are cellular structures, molecules and regulatory systems that enable microbial pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, and prions) to achieve colonization of a niche in the host, immunoevasion, immunosuppression, entry into and exit out of cells, and obtaining nutrition from the host. They are often encoded on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids and bacteriophages, allowing them to spread between strains by horizontal gene transfer.

Categories

Adherence factors, such as pili and fimbriae, let bacteria attach to host tissues. Invasion factors and type III secretion systems inject effector proteins into host cells. Capsules resist phagocytosis, while M protein and protein A interfere with opsonization and antibody binding. Exotoxins damage host tissue directly: examples include diphtheria toxin, cholera toxin, and botulinum toxin; endotoxin (lipopolysaccharide) of Gram-negative bacteria provokes strong inflammatory responses. Siderophores allow pathogens to scavenge iron, otherwise scarce in the host.

Regulation

Virulence genes are frequently coordinated by two-component regulatory systems and quorum sensing, switching on only when bacterial density and host cues indicate a suitable environment. Clusters of virulence genes on the chromosome are termed pathogenicity islands.

Medical significance

Many vaccines and toxoids target virulence factors rather than the whole organism, and antivirulence drug strategies seek to disarm pathogens instead of killing them, potentially reducing selection for antibiotic resistance.
