Ancient Egyptian creation myths

Ancient Egyptian creation myths are the ancient Egyptian accounts of the creation of the world. The Pyramid Texts, tomb inscriptions, and writings dating from the Old Kingdom (c. 2700–2200 BCE) provide multiple creation myths, each associated with a different major cult centre. Despite their differences, the myths share common elements: the world emerged from the lifeless waters of chaos, called Nu or Nun, and the gods and the ordered cosmos arose from a primeval mound or through the self-generation of a creator god.

Heliopolitan myth

In the Heliopolitan creation myth, the creator god Atum emerged from the waters of Nun upon a primeval mound and, alone, produced the first divine pair: Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture). From them came Geb (earth) and Nut (sky), whose children were Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys — together with Atum forming the Ennead, the group of nine gods.

Memphite theology

The Memphite theology, recorded on the Shabaka Stone, presents Ptah, god of craftsmen, as the supreme creator who brought the world into being through his heart (thought) and tongue (speech): everything that exists was conceived in Ptah's heart and commanded into being by his word.

Hermopolitan and Theban myths

The Hermopolitan myth centres on the Ogdoad, four pairs of primordial deities representing the qualities of the chaos waters. In the Theban myth, Amun — a transcendent, hidden creator — was emphasized, especially during the New Kingdom, when Thebes was the capital.

Common themes

Across the variants, creation is not a single finished act but an ongoing process: the sun's daily rising re-enacts the first arising (the zep tepi, "first occasion"), and the ordered world (embodying maat, order and truth) must continually be maintained against the forces of chaos (isfet).
