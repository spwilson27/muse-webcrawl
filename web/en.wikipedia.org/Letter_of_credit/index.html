Letter of credit

Letters of credit rely on documents, such as bills of lading, rather than the goods themselves.

A letter of credit (LC), also known as a documentary credit, is a payment mechanism used in international trade. It is a written commitment by a bank, issued on behalf of a buyer (the applicant), to pay a seller (the beneficiary) a stated amount, provided the seller presents specified documents — typically evidence of shipment such as a bill of lading — within a stated period and in strict compliance with the credit's terms.

How it works

The buyer arranges for its bank (the issuing bank) to open the credit in favour of the seller. The issuing bank often asks a bank in the seller's country (the advising bank, which may also confirm the credit) to notify the seller. After shipping the goods, the seller presents the required documents to its bank; if the documents conform, the bank pays, accepts a draft, or negotiates the credit, and seeks reimbursement from the issuing bank, which in turn debits the buyer.

A key legal feature is the autonomy principle: banks deal in documents and not in goods, so the credit is independent of the underlying sales contract. Payment depends on the documents appearing on their face to comply — a standard known as strict compliance.

Governing rules

Most letters of credit are issued subject to the Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits (UCP), published by the International Chamber of Commerce; the current version is UCP 600 (2007). Standby letters of credit, widely used in the United States as performance or payment guarantees, are often governed by the ISP98 rules.

Types

  • Revocable and irrevocable — under UCP 600 all credits are irrevocable.
  • Confirmed and unconfirmed — a confirming bank adds its own undertaking to pay.
  • Sight and usance — payable on presentation, or at a future date.
  • Transferable and back-to-back — allowing intermediaries to pass the credit to suppliers.
  • Standby letter of credit — payable on evidence of default rather than performance.

Risks and costs

Letters of credit reduce the risk that a seller will not be paid after shipping, and assure the buyer that payment follows proof of shipment. They involve bank fees and significant documentary risk: a large proportion of first presentations are rejected for discrepancies. Frauds involving forged documents are a recognised hazard because banks do not inspect the goods.
