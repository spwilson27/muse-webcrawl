Word order

In linguistics, word order (also called linear order) is the order in which words and phrases appear in a clause or sentence. Languages are commonly classified by the relative order of subject (S), verb (V) and object (O) in declarative sentences — a typology pioneered by Joseph Greenberg in 1963.

Types

Six logically possible orders occur, but two dominate: subject–verb–object (SVO), as in English, Mandarin and Spanish, and subject–object–verb (SOV), as in Japanese, Turkish, Hindi and Korean; together they account for the great majority of the world's languages. Verb–subject–object (VSO) order is found in Irish, Welsh, Classical Arabic and many Austronesian languages, while VOS, OVS and OSV are rare, occurring mainly in some Amazonian and Australian languages.

Flexibility and factors

Many languages do not have a single rigid order: case marking or agreement can free constituents to move for emphasis, topic or focus, as in Latin or Russian. Word order correlates with other typological features, such as the order of adpositions and nouns. In languages like English, which has little inflection, word order carries most of the burden of signalling grammatical relations, distinguishing "the dog bit the man" from "the man bit the dog."
