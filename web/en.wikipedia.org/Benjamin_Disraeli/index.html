Benjamin Disraeli, 1st Earl of Beaconsfield (1804–1881), was a British statesman, novelist, and twice Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. The dominant figure of the Conservative Party for three decades, he played a central role in creating the modern party, championed an expansive imperial foreign policy, and remains the only British prime minister of Jewish birth.

Early life and novels

Born in London to a family of Italian-Jewish descent, Disraeli was baptised into the Church of England as a boy. After unsuccessful ventures in publishing and heavy debts, he made his name as a fashionable novelist with works such as Vivian Grey (1826) and later the political novels Coningsby (1844) and Sybil (1845), which articulated his "Young England" vision of a united nation led by a paternalist aristocracy.

Political career

Elected to Parliament in 1837, Disraeli rose through brilliant parliamentary attacks — notably helping to destroy Sir Robert Peel's government over the Corn Laws in 1846. He served as Chancellor of the Exchequer three times under the Earl of Derby and piloted the Reform Act 1867, which greatly expanded the urban working-class franchise. He became prime minister briefly in 1868 and again, with a majority, from 1874 to 1880.

Second ministry

His second government passed significant social reforms (public health, factory, and artisans' dwellings legislation) and pursued an assertive foreign policy: purchasing the Khedive of Egypt's shares in the Suez Canal in 1875, proclaiming Queen Victoria Empress of India in 1876, and checking Russia at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, from which he claimed to bring "peace with honour". Defeated in the 1880 election by Gladstone's Liberals, he died the following year.
