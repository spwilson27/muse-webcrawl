De Morgan's laws

In propositional logic and Boolean algebra, De Morgan's laws are a pair of transformation rules relating conjunction and disjunction through negation. They are named after the nineteenth-century British mathematician Augustus De Morgan. In words: "the negation of a conjunction is the disjunction of the negations," and "the negation of a disjunction is the conjunction of the negations."

Formal statement

For propositions P and Q:

¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ (¬P) ∨ (¬Q)
¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ (¬P) ∧ (¬Q)

In set theory the same rules hold for complements of unions and intersections: the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and the complement of an intersection is the union of the complements.

History and applications

Versions of the laws were known to medieval logicians such as William of Ockham, but De Morgan stated them formally in the 1840s as part of his work on the algebra of logic, later taken up by George Boole. The laws are fundamental in digital electronics and computer programming: they allow logic circuits to be rewritten using only NAND or only NOR gates, and they guide the simplification of Boolean expressions and conditional statements in software.
