Isospin

Introduced byWerner Heisenberg (1932)
SymbolI, I₃
Applies toProtons/neutrons; up/down quarks
SymmetryApproximate SU(2) flavour symmetry

In nuclear and particle physics, isospin (also called isotopic spin) is a quantum number related to the up and down quark content of particles. It was introduced by Werner Heisenberg in 1932 to treat the proton and neutron as two states of a single particle, the nucleon, because the strong nuclear force is nearly independent of electric charge.

The nucleon doublet

In the isospin formalism the nucleon is assigned total isospin I = ½, with the proton having third component I₃ = +½ and the neutron I₃ = −½, in formal analogy to ordinary spin. The pions form an isospin triplet (I = 1), and the four Delta baryons a quartet (I = 3/2). Total isospin is approximately conserved in strong interactions, but not in electromagnetic or weak interactions, which distinguish between the charge states.

Quark model

In the quark model, isospin arises because the up and down quarks have very similar masses; isospin rotations swap them. The Gell-Mann–Nishijima formula relates charge to isospin and other flavour quantum numbers: Q = I₃ + (B + S)/2 for the original strangeness formulation. Isospin symmetry explains mass degeneracies within hadron multiplets and governs selection rules in pion–nucleon scattering.

Weak isospin

A distinct, exactly-gauged quantity called weak isospin underlies the electroweak theory: left-handed fermions form weak-isospin doublets (for example the electron and its neutrino) under the SU(2) group of the Standard Model. Despite the shared name, weak isospin is a separate concept from the strong-interaction isospin described above.
