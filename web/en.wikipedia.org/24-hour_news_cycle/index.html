The 24-hour news cycle (or 24/7 news cycle) is the continuous investigation, reporting and broadcasting of news events throughout the day and night. It emerged with the spread of cable and satellite television channels dedicated entirely to news, and was later intensified by the internet and social media. The cycle replaced the older rhythm in which newspapers published once a day and broadcast bulletins appeared at fixed hours.

A 24-hour television newsroom, staffed continuously to fill an unbroken broadcast schedule.

History

Although radio stations experimented with all-news formats in the 1960s, the modern 24-hour news cycle is generally dated to the launch of CNN on 1 June 1980, the first television channel to broadcast news around the clock. CNN's live coverage of events such as the Challenger disaster (1986) and the Gulf War (1991) demonstrated the commercial and journalistic power of continuous broadcasting. Competitors including Headline News, Fox News Channel and MSNBC (both 1996), and international services such as BBC World News, Sky News and Al Jazeera, extended the model worldwide.

From the mid-2000s, online news sites, blogs, Twitter and other social platforms compressed the cycle further. Stories could now break at any moment, be updated minute by minute, and be displaced within hours, leading commentators to speak of an "always-on" or even "minute-by-minute" news cycle.

Effects on journalism

The demand for a constant stream of content reshaped newsroom practice. Journalists face pressure to publish first and verify later, live blogs and rolling "breaking news" banners became standard, and airtime must be filled even when little new information exists — encouraging speculation, repetition and punditry. Critics argue the cycle rewards speed over depth, amplifies minor stories and gaffes into major controversies, and contributes to news fatigue and audience burnout.

Defenders respond that continuous news democratised access to information, allows rapid reporting during emergencies, and holds public figures accountable in real time.

Effects on politics

Political actors adapted by planning announcements, speeches and "photo opportunities" for the cycle, developing rapid-response teams, and attempting to set each day's agenda before opponents could. The cycle is often blamed for shortening political attention spans, privileging conflict and personality over policy, and forcing governments into permanent campaign mode. The phenomenon is closely related to the concepts of the "news cycle" in political communication and agenda-setting theory.
