Queueing theory

A queueing node with arrivals, a buffer, and servers

Queueing theory is the mathematical study of waiting lines, or queues. A queueing model is constructed so that queue lengths and waiting times can be predicted. Queueing theory is generally considered a branch of operations research because the results are often used when making business decisions about the resources needed to provide a service.

History

The field was founded by the Danish engineer Agner Krarup Erlang, who in 1909 published the first paper on what would now be called queueing theory, modelling the number of telephone calls arriving at an exchange by a Poisson process. His formulas for loss and waiting time, the Erlang B and Erlang C formulas, remain in use in telephony and call-centre design.

Kendall's notation

Queues are described by Kendall's notation, A/S/c/K/N/D, specifying the arrival process, service-time distribution, number of servers, system capacity, population size, and service discipline. Common disciplines include first in, first out (FIFO), last in first out, and processor sharing.

Single queueing nodes

The simplest models include the M/M/1 queue, with Poisson arrivals, exponential service times and a single server, and the M/G/1 queue, in which service times follow a general distribution and mean waiting time is given by the Pollaczek–Khinchine formula. Little's law relates the average number of customers in a system to the arrival rate and the average time spent in the system.

Networks of queues

More complex systems are modelled as queueing networks, in which customers move between nodes. Jackson networks admit a product-form solution for their equilibrium distribution, a property extended by the BCMP theorem.

Applications

Queueing theory is applied to telecommunications, traffic engineering, computing (scheduling and performance analysis), manufacturing, and the design of service systems such as hospital emergency departments and call centres.
