Bronchiolitis

Bronchiolitis is inflammation of the bronchioles, the smallest air passages in the lungs. It usually occurs in children under two years of age and presents with coughing, wheezing, and shortness of breath. This inflammation is usually caused by viruses; the most common agent is respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), responsible for the majority of cases. Other causes include rhinovirus, influenza, human metapneumovirus, adenovirus, and parainfluenza viruses.

Signs and symptoms

The illness typically begins with two to three days of runny nose, cough, and mild fever, followed by wheezing, rapid breathing, chest retractions, and difficulty feeding as the lower airways become obstructed by inflamed, swollen bronchiolar walls and debris. In severe cases the infant may become hypoxic or develop apnoea. Most cases are mild and resolve within one to two weeks, but bronchiolitis is the most common cause of hospitalisation of infants in developed countries, particularly during winter epidemics.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on age, season, and examination findings; routine laboratory tests and chest X-rays are not recommended in typical cases, though pulse oximetry guides the assessment of severity. Treatment is supportive: supplemental oxygen, assistance with feeding, and nasal suctioning. Antibiotics, bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and nebulised adrenaline have not been shown to provide consistent benefit and are not routinely recommended. Prevention includes hand hygiene and, for high-risk infants, immunoprophylaxis; maternal RSV vaccination and long-acting monoclonal antibodies such as nirsevimab were introduced in the 2020s to protect infants during their first RSV season.

Epidemiology

Nearly all children are infected with RSV by the age of two. Worldwide, RSV-associated lower respiratory infections cause substantial infant mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Risk factors for severe bronchiolitis include prematurity, chronic lung disease of prematurity, congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, young age (under 12 weeks), and exposure to tobacco smoke.
