A galaxy group or group of galaxies is an aggregation of galaxies comprising about 50 or fewer member galaxies, each at least as bright as the Milky Way, bound together by gravity. Groups are the most common structures of galaxies in the universe, containing the majority of all galaxies and galactic mass. They are intermediate in scale between individual galactic halos and the larger, hotter galaxy clusters, which contain hundreds to thousands of galaxies.

Characteristics

Groups typically span 1 to 2 megaparsecs and have total masses around 1013 solar masses, with velocity dispersions of only a few hundred kilometres per second. Unlike clusters, most groups contain little hot X-ray-emitting intergalactic gas, although the most massive groups show faint extended X-ray halos. Because member galaxies move slowly relative to one another, interactions and mergers are frequent in groups, making them important sites of galaxy evolution.

Compact groups

Compact groups are small, dense configurations of four to ten galaxies in close proximity, first systematically catalogued by Paul Hickson in 1982. Famous examples include Stephan's Quintet and Seyfert's Sextet. Their high densities and low velocity dispersions make them natural laboratories for studying tidal interactions and merger-driven transformation, though there is debate about how long such configurations can persist before coalescing.

The Local Group

The Milky Way belongs to the Local Group, a group about 10 million light-years across containing more than 80 known galaxies, dominated by the Milky Way, Andromeda (M31) and Triangulum (M33) spirals. The Local Group is itself part of the Virgo Supercluster within the Laniakea Supercluster.
