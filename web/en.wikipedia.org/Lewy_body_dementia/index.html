Lewy body dementia is associated with abnormal deposits of the protein alpha-synuclein in the brain.

Lewy body dementia (LBD) is an umbrella term for two related dementias: dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) and Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD). Both are characterized by abnormal deposits of the protein alpha-synuclein, called Lewy bodies, in the brain. It is one of the most common types of progressive dementia, second in prevalence only to Alzheimer's disease among neurodegenerative dementias.

Signs and symptoms

The core clinical features are progressive cognitive decline severe enough to interfere with daily life; fluctuating cognition with pronounced variations in attention and alertness; recurrent, complex visual hallucinations; and spontaneous features of parkinsonism such as slowness of movement, rigidity, and tremor. REM sleep behaviour disorder, in which people act out vivid dreams, often precedes the dementia by years. People with LBD can have severe sensitivity to antipsychotic (neuroleptic) medications, which may worsen symptoms and can be dangerous.

Cause and pathology

Lewy bodies are round aggregates of alpha-synuclein and other proteins that accumulate inside neurons in the cortex and brainstem. Named after Friederich Lewy, who described them in 1912, they are also the hallmark of Parkinson's disease. The two LBD diagnoses are distinguished by timing: in DLB, dementia appears before or within a year of parkinsonism; in PDD, dementia develops in established Parkinson's disease.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by biomarkers such as dopamine transporter imaging, which shows reduced uptake in DLB, and by the pattern of relatively preserved medial temporal lobe structure on MRI compared with Alzheimer's disease. Definitive diagnosis requires autopsy.

Treatment and prognosis

There is no cure. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as rivastigmine and donepezil can improve cognition and reduce hallucinations. Management emphasizes avoiding antipsychotics where possible, treating sleep and mood symptoms, and supporting caregivers. Survival after diagnosis is typically in the range of five to eight years, comparable to Alzheimer's disease.
