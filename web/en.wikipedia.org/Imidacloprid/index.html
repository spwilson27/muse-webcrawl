Imidacloprid

Imidacloprid, a systemic neonicotinoid insecticide.

Imidacloprid is a systemic insecticide that acts as an insect neurotoxin and belongs to a class of chemicals called the neonicotinoids, which act on the central nervous system of insects. It works by interfering with the transmission of stimuli in the insect nervous system, binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and causing paralysis and death. Patented in the 1980s and commercialised by Bayer in 1991, it became the most widely used insecticide in the world, applied as seed treatments, soil and foliar sprays, and in flea treatments for pets.

Environmental concerns

Because imidacloprid is systemic, it is taken up throughout treated plants, including into pollen and nectar, exposing pollinators. It is highly toxic to bees and other beneficial insects and to aquatic invertebrates, and it is persistent in soil and mobile in water. Imidacloprid is one of the substances investigated in connection with colony collapse disorder and wider declines in insect populations.

Regulation

The European Food Safety Authority concluded in 2013 that imidacloprid posed acute risks to bees, and the European Union first restricted and then, in 2018, banned all outdoor uses of imidacloprid and two related neonicotinoids. Several other countries and some US states have adopted partial restrictions, while the compound remains in widespread agricultural use elsewhere.
