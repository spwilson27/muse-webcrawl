Distance-regular graph

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a distance-regular graph is a regular graph such that for any two vertices v and w at distance i, the number of vertices adjacent to w and at distance i−1 from v, and the number of vertices adjacent to w and at distance i+1 from v, depend only on i and on the distance between v and w — not on the choice of the vertices themselves.

Definition

Formally, a connected graph Γ of diameter d is distance-regular if there exist integers b0, b1, …, bd−1 and c1, c2, …, cd such that for every pair of vertices x, y at distance i:

  • the number of neighbours of y at distance i−1 from x is ci;
  • the number of neighbours of y at distance i+1 from x is bi.

The sequence {b0, b1, …, bd−1; c1, …, cd} is called the intersection array of the graph. Every distance-regular graph is distance-transitive in structure of counts, though the converse is not required: every distance-transitive graph is distance-regular, but some distance-regular graphs are not distance-transitive.

Examples

  • Complete graphs and complete bipartite graphs Kn,n
  • Cycle graphs and odd graphs, including the Petersen graph
  • Cube graphs and the dodecahedron graph
  • The strongly regular graphs (the distance-regular graphs of diameter 2)
  • Johnson graphs and Hamming graphs
  • Moore graphs of diameter 2, such as the Hoffman–Singleton graph

Properties

Distance-regular graphs have only a limited number of distinct eigenvalues, at most the diameter plus one, and their spectra are completely determined by the intersection array. The adjacency algebra generated by the powers of the adjacency matrix coincides with the Bose–Mesner algebra of an associated association scheme, making these graphs a central object of algebraic graph theory. Feasibility conditions on intersection arrays, such as the Krein conditions and the absolute bound, heavily restrict which parameter sets can occur.

Classification results

Distance-regular graphs with large girth or large diameter have been extensively classified. It is known, for example, that there are only finitely many distance-regular graphs with a given valency greater than two — a result following from the classification of such graphs with diameter at least three — while the valency-3 (cubic) distance-regular graphs are completely known: there are exactly 13 of them.
