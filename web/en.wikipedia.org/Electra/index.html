Electra

In Greek mythology, Electra (Greek: Ἠλέκτρα, Ēléktra, "amber") was the daughter of King Agamemnon and Queen Clytemnestra of Mycenae, and the sister of Orestes and Iphigenia. She is best known for her role in avenging her father's murder.

Electra at the tomb of Agamemnon, a subject of classical Greek art and tragedy.

Myth

After Agamemnon returned from the Trojan War, he was murdered by Clytemnestra and her lover Aegisthus. Electra saved her young brother Orestes by sending him away, and years later, when Orestes returned, she helped him kill Clytemnestra and Aegisthus to avenge their father. In some versions Electra later married Pylades, Orestes' loyal companion.

In literature

Electra is the central figure of tragedies by all three great Greek tragedians: Aeschylus's Libation Bearers, Sophocles' Electra, and Euripides' Electra. Later treatments include plays by Voltaire and Jean Giraudoux, Eugene O'Neill's Mourning Becomes Electra, and Richard Strauss's opera Elektra (1909) with libretto by Hugo von Hofmannsthal.

Electra complex

Carl Jung coined the term "Electra complex" for a girl's psychosexual competition with her mother for her father's affection, as a counterpart to Freud's Oedipus complex.
