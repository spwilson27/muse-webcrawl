X-inactivation (also called Lyonization, after English geneticist Mary Lyon) is a process by which one of the copies of the X chromosome is inactivated in therian female mammals. The inactive X chromosome is silenced by being packaged into a transcriptionally inactive structure called heterochromatin. As nearly all female mammals have two X chromosomes, X-inactivation prevents them from having twice as many X-chromosome gene products as males, who have only one copy of the X chromosome (dosage compensation).

Mechanism

The choice of which X chromosome will be inactivated is random in placental mammals such as humans (in marsupials, by contrast, it is always the paternal X that is silenced). Once an X chromosome is inactivated it remains inactive throughout the lifetime of the cell and its descendants. X-inactivation is initiated early in embryonic development from the X-inactivation centre (Xic), which expresses the long non-coding RNA Xist; Xist RNA coats the future inactive X in cis and recruits silencing complexes, after which the chromosome is heavily methylated, hypoacetylated and condensed into the Barr body, visible at the nuclear periphery. A small number of genes — roughly 15 percent in humans, including those in the pseudoautosomal regions — escape inactivation and are expressed from both X chromosomes.

Consequences

Because the choice is random and clonally inherited, females are mosaics of cell populations expressing either the maternal or the paternal X. The classic visible example is the calico and tortoiseshell cat, in which an X-linked coat-colour gene produces patches of orange and black fur. X-inactivation also affects human disease: females heterozygous for X-linked recessive conditions such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy or haemophilia are usually unaffected carriers, but skewed (non-random) inactivation can produce symptoms, and the severity of some X-linked disorders in women depends on the pattern of inactivation in relevant tissues. Random X-inactivation is also used experimentally as a marker of clonality in tumours.
