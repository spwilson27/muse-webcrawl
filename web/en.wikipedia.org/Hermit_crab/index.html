Hermit crabs are decapod crustaceans of the superfamily Paguroidea, comprising more than 1,100 species. Most hermit crabs protect their soft, curved abdomens by occupying empty gastropod shells, which they carry with them and exchange as they grow.

Description

Unlike true crabs, hermit crabs have a soft, asymmetrical abdomen that curls to fit the spiral of a mollusc shell; the last pairs of legs are reduced and modified to grip the shell's columella. The left cheliped (claw) is usually larger than the right and serves as an operculum, blocking the shell entrance when the crab withdraws. Sizes range from a few millimetres (e.g. Pagurus minutus) to the coconut crab (Birgus latro), the largest terrestrial arthropod, which abandons shells as an adult. Other notable species include the common hermit crab Pagurus bernhardus of European waters and the Caribbean land hermit crab Coenobita clypeatus, popular in the pet trade.

Behaviour and ecology

Hermit crabs are mostly aquatic, living from intertidal rock pools to the deep sea, though the family Coenobitidae is terrestrial. As a crab grows it must find a larger shell; competition for shells can be intense, and crabs may form "vacancy chains" in which several individuals line up by size to exchange shells in sequence. Some species carry sea anemones on their shells, gaining protection while the anemone gains food scraps. Most are omnivorous scavengers. Females carry eggs attached to the pleopods; larvae pass through planktonic zoeal stages and a glaucothoe stage before settling and seeking a first shell.

Classification

Paguroid families include the Paguridae (right-handed hermit crabs), Diogenidae (left-handed), Coenobitidae (land hermit crabs and the coconut crab), Lithodidae (king crabs, which evolved from hermit-crab ancestors), Parapaguridae, Pylochelidae and Pylojacquesidae. Squat lobsters and porcelain crabs are close anomuran relatives.
