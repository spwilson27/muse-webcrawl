The Pauli equation or Schrödinger–Pauli equation is the formulation of the Schrödinger equation for spin-½ particles, which takes into account the interaction of the particle's spin with an external electromagnetic field. It is the non-relativistic limit of the Dirac equation and can be used where particles are moving at speeds much less than the speed of light, so that relativistic effects can be neglected. It was formulated by Wolfgang Pauli in 1927.

Equation

For a particle of mass m and charge q, in an electromagnetic field described by the magnetic vector potential A and electric scalar potential ϕ, the Pauli equation reads:

[ (1/2m) (σ·(p − qA))² + qϕ ] ψ = iħ ∂ψ/∂t

Here σ = (σx, σy, σz) are the Pauli matrices collected into a vector for convenience, and ψ is a two-component spinor wavefunction. Expanding the squared term yields the Schrödinger Hamiltonian plus a coupling of the spin to the magnetic field, −(qħ/2m) σ·B, which describes the interaction of the particle's intrinsic magnetic moment with the field.

Relation to the Stern–Gerlach experiment

The spin-interaction term explains the splitting of atomic beams in the Stern–Gerlach experiment and predicts a gyromagnetic ratio of g ≈ 2 for the electron: the magnetic moment associated with electron spin is twice what a classical rotating charge distribution would suggest, a value that emerges naturally from the Pauli (and Dirac) theory.

From the Dirac equation

The Pauli equation can be derived from the Dirac equation in the non-relativistic limit by separating the large and small components of the Dirac spinor. It retains spin as a two-state degree of freedom but treats it non-relativistically; fully relativistic corrections, such as spin–orbit coupling and the Darwin term, arise at higher order.
