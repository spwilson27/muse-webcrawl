Wallerian degeneration

Wallerian degeneration is an active process of degeneration that results when a nerve fibre is cut or crushed and the part of the axon distal to the injury (i.e., farther from the neuron's cell body) degenerates. The process is named after Augustus Volney Waller, who described it in 1850 in experiments on frogs' nerves. Degeneration usually begins within 24 hours of injury: the distal axon fragments, its myelin sheath breaks down, and the debris is cleared by Schwann cells and recruited macrophages. A related process, retrograde degeneration ("dying back"), can affect the proximal axon and, in severe cases, the cell body.

Mechanism

Severed from the cell body, the distal axon can no longer be supplied with the survival factor NMNAT2, a short-lived enzyme synthesized in the soma and transported along the axon. Its depletion activates the protein SARM1, which consumes NAD+ and triggers catastrophic energy failure and fragmentation of the axon—a programmed "axon death" pathway (sometimes called Wallerian-like degeneration when it occurs without physical cutting, as in some neuropathies and neurodegenerative diseases).

In the peripheral nervous system, Schwann cells dedifferentiate, proliferate, and align within the basal lamina tube to form bands of Büngner, which guide regenerating axons growing from the proximal stump at roughly 1 mm per day. In the central nervous system, debris clearance is far slower—oligodendrocytes do not play the same role and microglia clear myelin over months—one reason central axons regenerate poorly.

The Wlds mouse

The discovery of the Wallerian degeneration slow (Wlds) mutant mouse, in which severed axons survive for weeks instead of days, demonstrated that Wallerian degeneration is an actively regulated process rather than passive decay. The mutant gene encodes a fusion protein including NMNAT1, which substitutes for the lost NMNAT2 supply. Identifying SARM1 as the central executioner of the pathway has made it a drug target for preventing axon loss in peripheral neuropathy and other conditions.

Clinical significance

Wallerian degeneration is visible on MRI as signal changes along injured white-matter tracts and is a standard consideration in classifying nerve injuries (Seddon's and Sunderland's schemes), predicting whether spontaneous regeneration is likely or surgical repair is needed. Slowing or blocking the pathway experimentally preserves axons, an approach under investigation for diseases including motor neuron disease, glaucoma, and chemotherapy-induced neuropathy.
