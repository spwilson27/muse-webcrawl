The neutron is a subatomic particle, symbol n, which has no electric charge and a mass slightly greater than that of a proton. Protons and neutrons constitute the nuclei of atoms. Since protons and neutrons behave similarly within the nucleus, they are both referred to as nucleons. The neutron is composed of two down quarks and one up quark; its antimatter counterpart is the antineutron.

Discovery

The neutron's existence was hypothesised by Ernest Rutherford in 1920 to explain the discrepancy between atomic number and atomic mass. In 1932 James Chadwick at the Cavendish Laboratory demonstrated that the penetrating radiation produced when alpha particles struck beryllium (observed by Bothe, Becker, and Irène and Frédéric Joliot-Curie) consisted of uncharged particles of roughly proton mass — neutrons. Chadwick received the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

Properties and behaviour

Outside the nucleus, free neutrons are unstable and decay by beta emission into a proton, an electron and an electron antineutrino, with a mean lifetime of about 14.6 minutes. Within stable nuclei neutrons are stable. Because they carry no charge, neutrons penetrate matter readily, are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields, and interact with nuclei via the strong force — making them essential probes (neutron scattering and diffraction) and the sustaining agent of nuclear fission chain reactions in reactors and weapons. Slow ("thermal") neutrons are especially readily captured by many nuclei, driving nucleosynthesis processes such as the s-process in stars.
