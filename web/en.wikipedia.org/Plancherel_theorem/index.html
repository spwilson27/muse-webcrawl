In mathematics, the Plancherel theorem is a fundamental result in harmonic analysis, proven by Michel Plancherel in 1910. It states that the Fourier transform is an isometry — it preserves the L² norm — and therefore extends from integrable functions to a unitary map of the Hilbert space L²(ℝn) onto itself.

Statement

For a function f in both L¹ and L² on the real line, with Fourier transform f̂, the theorem asserts:

∫−∞∞ |f(x)|² dx = ∫−∞∞ |f̂(ξ)|² dξ

More generally, ∫ f(x) g(x)̄ dx = ∫ f̂(ξ) ĝ(ξ)̄ dξ (with the appropriate normalisation of the transform). Since functions in L¹ ∩ L² are dense in L², the transform extends uniquely to all square-integrable functions, even though the defining integral may no longer converge absolutely.

Significance

The identity expresses conservation of "energy" between a signal and its frequency spectrum, which is why engineers know the continuous result alongside its series analogue, Parseval's identity. In quantum mechanics it guarantees that a wavefunction has the same total probability in position and momentum representations. Abstractly, the theorem generalises to locally compact abelian groups, where the Fourier transform is a unitary map between L² of the group and L² of its dual group — a cornerstone of Pontryagin duality.

History

Plancherel, a Swiss mathematician at the University of Fribourg, developed the theory in work beginning in 1903 and reaching its full form in 1910, originally to settle questions about the convergence of Fourier integrals. The result completed a line of development running from Fourier's series through the Riesz–Fischer theorem.
