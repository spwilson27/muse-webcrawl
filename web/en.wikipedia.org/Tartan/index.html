Tartan

Tartan is a patterned woollen cloth consisting of criss-crossed horizontal and vertical bands in multiple colours, forming a repeating sequence of stripes called a sett. Tartan is particularly associated with Scotland and Scottish Highland dress — above all the kilt — and with the system of "clan tartans" identifying patterns with particular Scottish clans and families.

Weaving and design

Tartan is woven as a simple 2/2 twill: the warp and weft carry the same sequence of coloured threads, and where colours cross they mix optically to produce half-tone shades, giving the pattern its characteristic grid of solid blocks and blended squares. A pattern is specified by its "thread count" — the exact number of threads of each colour in the sett — recorded today by the Scottish Tartan Authority and the official Scottish Register of Tartans (established 2008).

History

Checked woollen cloth has been woven in Scotland since at least the third or fourth century CE (the Falkirk tartan is an early surviving fragment). By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, tartan was everyday wear in the Highlands; patterns were local and weaver-determined rather than clan-specific. After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the Dress Act of 1746 banned Highland dress for most men until its repeal in 1782. The nineteenth century saw a romantic revival — stage-managed by Sir Walter Scott for George IV's 1822 visit to Edinburgh and embraced by Queen Victoria at Balmoral — during which the clan-tartan system was systematised, partly on the basis of the dubious Vestiarium Scoticum (1842).

Modern use

Thousands of tartans are now registered: clan and family tartans, district and regimental tartans (such as the Black Watch "government sett"), commemorative and corporate designs, and fashion patterns such as Burberry's famous check (legally a tartan-style pattern). Tartan is worn at Highland games, weddings, and national celebrations, and remains a potent symbol of Scottish identity worldwide.
