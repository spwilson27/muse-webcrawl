Well logging, also known as borehole logging, is the practice of making a detailed record (a well log) of the geologic formations penetrated by a borehole. The log may be based either on visual inspection of samples brought to the surface (geological logs) or on physical measurements made by instruments lowered into the hole (geophysical logs).

History

The first electric log was recorded in 1927 in the Pechelbronn oil field in Alsace, France, by Conrad and Marcel Schlumberger, who measured electrical resistivity downhole. Log interpretation methods developed rapidly thereafter, and well logging became central to petroleum geology and reservoir evaluation.

Types of logs

Common measurements include electrical resistivity, natural gamma radiation, neutron and density porosity, acoustic (sonic) velocity, and nuclear magnetic resonance. Logs are run either on wireline after drilling, or with logging-while-drilling (LWD) sensors in the drill string. From the curves, petrophysicists infer lithology, porosity, fluid saturations, and permeability — the information used to decide whether a well can produce oil or gas economically.
