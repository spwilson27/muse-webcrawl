Mainstream economics

Mainstream economics is the body of knowledge, theories, and models of economics, as taught by universities worldwide, that governments and international institutions regard as the basis for their decisions. It is commonly associated with neoclassical economics and the post-war neoclassical synthesis that combined neoclassical microeconomics with Keynesian macroeconomics. The term is often used in contrast to heterodox economics — schools such as post-Keynesian, Marxian, Austrian, feminist, and ecological economics that criticise the mainstream's assumptions.

Supply and demand analysis is the canonical model of mainstream microeconomics.

Core tenets

Mainstream analysis typically begins from individuals and firms who optimise subject to constraints, interacting in markets that tend toward equilibrium, with prices adjusting to equate supply and demand. Rational choice theory, marginal analysis, and opportunity cost are foundational. Modern macroeconomics is dominated by New Keynesian DSGE (dynamic stochastic general equilibrium) models incorporating price stickiness, while microeconomics has expanded into game theory, information economics, and mechanism design. Since the 1980s–2000s, empirical methods — randomised experiments, natural experiments, and applied econometrics (the "credibility revolution") — have gained prominence, along with behavioural economics, which relaxes strict rationality while keeping mainstream modelling tools.

Development

The mainstream descends from the marginal revolution of the 1870s (Jevons, Menger, Walras), Marshall's synthesis, Keynes's macroeconomics (1936), and Samuelson's formalisation in Foundations of Economic Analysis (1947) and his textbook Economics (1948). Debates with monetarism (Friedman) and the rational-expectations critique (Lucas) reshaped macroeconomics in the 1970s–80s.

Criticism

Critics within and outside the profession charge the mainstream with over-reliance on unrealistic assumptions (perfect information, representative agents), excessive mathematisation, neglect of financial instability — exposed, they argue, by the 2008 financial crisis — and marginalisation of questions of distribution, power, and ecology. Defenders reply that the mainstream is pluralistic in practice, continuously absorbs successful heterodox insights, and that no rival approach matches its combination of rigour and policy usability.
