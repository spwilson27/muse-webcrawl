Blank verse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Blank verse is poetry written with regular metrical but unrhymed lines, almost always in iambic pentameter. It has been described as probably the most common and influential form of English poetry since the 16th century. The form was adapted from Italian versi sciolti ("freed verse") by Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, in his translation of Virgil's Aeneid (published 1554–1557).

History

Christopher Marlowe was the first English author to make full dramatic use of blank verse, and William Shakespeare developed it into the principal verse form of English drama, progressively loosening its regularity across his career. John Milton's Paradise Lost (1667) established blank verse as the medium of English epic poetry; his note on "The Verse" defended "English heroic verse without rhyme" against the "bondage of rhyming."

Of Man's first disobedience, and the fruit
Of that forbidden tree, whose mortal taste
Brought death into the world, and all our woe…
— Milton, Paradise Lost, Book I

Later use

The Romantic poets — Wordsworth, Coleridge, Shelley, and Keats — used blank verse for meditative and narrative poems such as The Prelude. Tennyson and Robert Browning carried it through the Victorian era, and in the 20th century it remained a major form for poets including Wallace Stevens and Robert Frost, whose colloquial blank verse shaped much modern American poetry.

Characteristics

Standard blank verse consists of ten-syllable lines of five iambic feet. Variations such as enjambment, caesura, feminine endings, and metrical substitution give the form flexibility approaching that of natural speech while retaining metrical order.
