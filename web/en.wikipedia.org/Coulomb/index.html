The coulomb (symbol: C) is the unit of electric charge in the International System of Units (SI). It is defined, since the 2019 revision of the SI, by taking the elementary charge e to be exactly 1.602176634×10−19 coulombs, so one coulomb is the charge of approximately 6.241509×1018 elementary charges. Equivalently, a coulomb is the charge transported by a constant current of one ampere in one second.

History

The unit is named after the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1736–1806), who formulated Coulomb's law describing the electrostatic force between charges. Before 2019 the coulomb was defined in terms of the ampere and the force between current-carrying wires; the 2019 redefinition fixed the elementary charge instead.

Usage

Battery capacity is often expressed in milliampere-hours; one ampere-hour equals 3,600 coulombs. The Faraday constant, the charge of one mole of electrons, is approximately 96,485 coulombs per mole.
