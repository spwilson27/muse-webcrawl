Ekman layer

The Ekman layer (friction layer) is the layer in a fluid where there is a force balance between pressure gradient force, Coriolis force and turbulent drag. It was first described by Vagn Walfrid Ekman.

History

Ekman developed the theory after studying the observations of Fridtjof Nansen, who noted during the Fram expedition (1893–1896) that icebergs drifted 20–40 degrees to the right of the prevailing wind direction. Nansen reasoned that this was caused by the Coriolis effect acting on a wind-driven current, and Ekman formalized the mathematics in his 1905 thesis.

The Ekman spiral

Within the layer, the flow velocity decreases with depth (or with height in the atmosphere) while its direction rotates, producing the distinctive Ekman spiral. The surface current flows at 45 degrees to the wind direction — to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere — and the net transport of the whole layer (Ekman transport) is 90 degrees to the wind.

Significance

Ekman transport drives major oceanographic phenomena: coastal upwelling, as along the Peruvian coast where nutrient-rich deep water supports rich fisheries; equatorial upwelling; and convergence and downwelling in ocean gyres. In the atmosphere, the frictional Ekman layer is the planetary boundary layer, where surface drag turns surface winds toward low-pressure centres.
