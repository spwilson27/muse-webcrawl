Thomas Paine (1737–1809)

Thomas Paine (born Thomas Pain; 9 February 1737 – 8 June 1809) was an English-born American Founding Father, political activist, philosopher and political theorist. He authored Common Sense (1776) and The American Crisis (1776–1783), two of the most influential pamphlets at the start of the American Revolution.

Early life

Paine was born in Thetford, Norfolk, and worked variously as a corset maker, schoolteacher and excise officer. In 1774 he emigrated to the British American colonies, arriving in Philadelphia with a letter of introduction from Benjamin Franklin.

Common Sense

Published anonymously in January 1776, Common Sense presented arguments for independence from Great Britain in clear, forceful prose accessible to ordinary readers. It spread the idea of American republicanism and sold an estimated 100,000 copies within months, an enormous figure relative to the colonial population.

Later work

During the French Revolution, Paine wrote Rights of Man (1791), defending the revolution against Edmund Burke's criticisms. He was elected to the French National Convention, but was imprisoned during the Reign of Terror. In The Age of Reason (1794–1807) he advocated deism and criticised institutional religion, which damaged his reputation in America.

Death and legacy

Paine died in New York City in 1809, largely ostracised; only six people attended his funeral. His remains were later exhumed by William Cobbett and taken to England, where they were eventually lost. His writings remain central to the traditions of republicanism and democratic radicalism.
