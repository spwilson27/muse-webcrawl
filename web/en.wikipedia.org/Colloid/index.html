Colloid

A colloid is a mixture in which one substance, consisting of microscopically dispersed insoluble particles, is suspended throughout another substance. The dispersed particles are larger than atoms or ordinary molecules — typically between about 1 nanometre and 1 micrometre in diameter — but small enough that they do not settle out under gravity and exhibit Brownian motion.

Types

Colloids are classified by the states of the dispersed phase and the continuous medium: aerosols (fog, smoke), foams, emulsions such as milk and mayonnaise (liquid in liquid), sols such as paint and blood (solid in liquid), and gels such as jelly. The term was introduced by Thomas Graham in 1861 from the Greek kolla, "glue".

Properties

Colloids scatter light, a phenomenon known as the Tyndall effect, which distinguishes them from true solutions. The particles often carry an electric charge, and the resulting repulsion helps stabilise the dispersion; adding electrolytes can neutralise the charge and cause coagulation. Protective agents such as gelatin can stabilise colloids against aggregation. Colloid science is important in food technology, pharmaceuticals, photography, water treatment and nanotechnology.
