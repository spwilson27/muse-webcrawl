Taurine

Taurine (2-aminoethanesulfonic acid) is an amino sulfonic acid that occurs naturally in the body. It is a major constituent of bile and can be found in the large intestine, and accounts for up to 0.1% of total human body weight. Taurine is named after taurus (Latin for bull or ox), as it was first isolated from ox bile in 1827 by German scientists Friedrich Tiedemann and Leopold Gmelin.

Unlike most amino acids, taurine is not incorporated into proteins. It exists freely in tissues and plays many roles in the body, including conjugation of bile acids, antioxidation, osmoregulation, membrane stabilization, and modulation of calcium signaling.

Physiological roles

Taurine is essential for cardiovascular function, and development and function of skeletal muscle, the retina, and the central nervous system. It is a conditionally essential nutrient for cats, in which deficiency causes retinal degeneration and dilated cardiomyopathy, and commercial cat food is supplemented with it. Taurine crosses the blood-brain barrier and has been implicated in a wide array of physiological phenomena, including inhibitory neurotransmission and protection against glutamate excitotoxicity.

Sources and synthesis

The body synthesizes taurine from the amino acid cysteine in the liver. Dietary sources include meat, fish, and shellfish; it is absent from most plant foods. Synthetic taurine is widely used as an ingredient in energy drinks, although the amounts used are far below those used in therapeutic research and its stimulating effects in such drinks are generally attributed to caffeine rather than taurine.
