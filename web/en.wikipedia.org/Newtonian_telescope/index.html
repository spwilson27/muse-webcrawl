Newtonian telescope

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Newtonian telescope, also called the Newtonian reflector, is a type of reflecting telescope invented by the English scientist Sir Isaac Newton (1643–1727), using a concave primary mirror and a flat diagonal secondary mirror. Newton's first reflecting telescope was completed in 1668 and is the earliest known functional telescope of its kind. The design was intended to solve the problem of chromatic aberration that plagued refracting telescopes of the era: because mirrors reflect all wavelengths equally, the image is free of colour fringing.

Design

Light enters the open tube and is reflected by the paraboloid (or spherical, in small instruments) primary mirror at the bottom back up the tube to a small flat secondary mirror, tilted at 45 degrees, which reflects the converging beam through the side of the tube into an eyepiece near the top. The observer thus looks into the side of the telescope. The focal ratio and aperture determine the instrument's magnification and light-gathering power.

Popularity

The Newtonian design remains the most popular type of telescope among amateur astronomers because it offers the largest aperture per unit cost, is relatively simple to manufacture and collimate, and can be built by hobbyists. Mounted on a simple altazimuth rocking-box mount, it becomes the Dobsonian telescope, which revolutionised amateur astronomy from the 1980s onward. Professional observatories use related reflector designs, though usually in the Cassegrain configuration rather than the Newtonian focus.
