Outer space

Outer space is the expanse that exists beyond Earth's atmosphere and between celestial bodies. It is not completely empty of matter but contains a tenuous plasma of hydrogen and helium, electromagnetic radiation, magnetic fields, cosmic rays, neutrinos, and dust. The baseline temperature of outer space, set by the cosmic microwave background radiation left over from the Big Bang, is about 2.7 kelvin.

Boundary

There is no clear boundary between Earth's atmosphere and space, since the atmosphere gradually thins with altitude. The Kármán line, at an altitude of 100 km (62 mi) above sea level, is conventionally used as the start of outer space in space treaties and for aerospace records keeping. The United States has historically used 80 km (50 mi) for awarding astronaut wings. In 2021 the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale retained the 100 km definition.

Regions

Geospace is the region surrounding Earth, including the magnetosphere and the orbits of artificial satellites, divided into low, medium, and geostationary Earth orbit. Beyond it lies interplanetary space within the Solar System, permeated by the solar wind, and interstellar space within the Milky Way, which the Voyager probes entered in 2012 and 2018. Intergalactic space, between galaxies, is emptier still and is being stretched by the expansion of the universe.

Exploration and law

Human spaceflight began with Yuri Gagarin's orbital flight in 1961. Activities in space are governed internationally by the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, which prohibits weapons of mass destruction in orbit and claims of national sovereignty over celestial bodies. Space is hazardous to humans because of vacuum, temperature extremes, and radiation; long-duration crews on the International Space Station experience bone and muscle loss. Uncrewed probes have visited all the planets of the Solar System.
