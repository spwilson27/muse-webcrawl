Bipolar disorder

Bipolar disorder, previously known as manic depression, is a mental disorder characterized by periods of depression and periods of abnormally elevated mood that each last from days to weeks. If the elevated mood is severe or associated with psychosis, it is called mania; if it is less severe and does not significantly impair functioning, it is called hypomania. During mania, an individual behaves or feels abnormally energetic, happy, or irritable, and they often make impulsive decisions with little regard for the consequences. There is usually also a reduced need for sleep.

Bipolar disorder involves swings between depressive and manic or hypomanic episodes.

Signs and symptoms

Depressive episodes involve low mood, loss of interest, fatigue, and sometimes suicidal thinking. Manic episodes involve elevated or irritable mood, increased energy, pressured speech, racing thoughts, distractibility, and risky behavior; severe episodes may include delusions or hallucinations. Mixed episodes combine features of both poles. Between episodes many people function well, though residual symptoms are common.

Causes

The cause is not clearly understood, but both genetic and environmental factors play a role. Twin studies suggest heritability of roughly 70–80%. Environmental risk factors include childhood adversity and long-term stress. Brain-imaging and neurochemical studies implicate circuits regulating mood and reward, but no single biomarker exists.

Diagnosis

Bipolar I disorder requires at least one manic episode; bipolar II requires at least one hypomanic and one major depressive episode without full mania. Cyclothymia involves milder, chronic fluctuations. Diagnosis is clinical and must exclude substance use, medication effects, and medical causes such as hyperthyroidism.

Management

Lifelong management typically combines mood-stabilizing medication — lithium is the classic agent — with anticonvulsants (such as valproate or lamotrigine), atypical antipsychotics, and psychotherapy including psychoeducation and cognitive behavioral therapy. Lithium also reduces the risk of suicide, which is markedly elevated in bipolar disorder.

Epidemiology

Bipolar disorder affects roughly 1–2% of the world's population, with onset typically in adolescence or early adulthood, and affects men and women at similar rates.
