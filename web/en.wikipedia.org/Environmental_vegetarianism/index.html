Environmental vegetarianism

Environmental vegetarianism is the practice of adopting a vegetarian or reduced-meat diet for environmental reasons. It is motivated by evidence that meat production — particularly beef production — requires substantially more land, water, and energy, and generates substantially more greenhouse gas emissions, than the production of plant foods delivering equivalent nutrition.

Livestock production is a major driver of land use change and deforestation

Environmental impacts of meat

A widely cited 2006 report by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, Livestock's Long Shadow, estimated that livestock production accounted for about 18 percent of global greenhouse gas emissions (a figure later revised to around 14.5 percent), as well as large shares of deforestation, water use, and nutrient pollution. Ruminant animals such as cattle produce methane through enteric fermentation, and manure management releases additional methane and nitrous oxide.

Arguments

Supporters argue that shifting diets toward plants is one of the most effective individual actions for reducing one's carbon footprint, and note that feed conversion is inherently inefficient: many calories of feed are required to produce one calorie of meat. Critics respond that some grazing land is unsuitable for crops, that well-managed livestock can play ecological roles, and that the type of meat matters greatly — beef typically has a far larger footprint than poultry or pork.

Related practices

Environmental vegetarianism overlaps with, but is distinct from, ethical vegetarianism motivated by animal welfare. Intermediate positions include flexitarianism, reducetarianism, and environmental veganism.
