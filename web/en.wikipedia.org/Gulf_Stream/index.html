The Gulf Stream is a powerful, warm ocean current that originates in the Gulf of Mexico, flows through the Straits of Florida along the eastern coast of North America, and then turns eastward across the North Atlantic. It is the western boundary current of the North Atlantic subtropical gyre and one of the strongest ocean currents in the world. Together with its extension, the North Atlantic Current, it transports warm tropical water toward north-western Europe, moderating the climate of countries such as the United Kingdom, Ireland and Norway.

Course and properties

Fed by the Florida Current, the Gulf Stream leaves the coast near Cape Hatteras, North Carolina, and meanders eastward in a series of loops and eddies. Its transport grows from about 30 sverdrups (30 million cubic metres per second) in the Florida Straits to well over 90 sverdrups downstream, largely through recirculation. The current is typically 80–150 km wide and can extend to depths of about 1,000 metres, with surface speeds reaching 2.5 metres per second.

History of study

The current was noted by Spanish explorers in the early 16th century, including Juan Ponce de León's expedition of 1513. In the 18th century Benjamin Franklin, working with mariner Timothy Folger, published one of the first charts of the current to help mail ships cross the Atlantic faster. Modern studies use satellite altimetry, drifters and moored instruments.

Climate influence

By carrying heat poleward, the Gulf Stream system contributes significantly to the mild winters of western Europe relative to similar latitudes in North America. Changes in the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation, of which the Gulf Stream is a component, are monitored closely for signs of weakening under climate change.
