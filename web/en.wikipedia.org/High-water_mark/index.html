High-water mark

A high-water mark is a point that represents the maximum rise of a body of water over land. Such a mark is often the result of a flood or a high tide. When not at sea, high-water marks may also relate to storm surges, seasonal rises in rivers and lakes, or deliberately surveyed reference points used in law and land measurement. Plaques, lines on buildings, and debris lines are all used to record high-water marks, which are valuable for flood-risk planning and insurance.

Legal meaning

In property law, especially in common-law jurisdictions, the (ordinary or mean) high-water mark commonly forms the legal boundary between privately owned land and public or Crown land along coasts and rivers; land seaward of the mark may be foreshore held by the state and subject to public rights of navigation and fishing.

Other meanings

The phrase has passed into general usage meaning the highest point achieved, as in military history (the "High-water Mark of the Confederacy" at the Battle of Gettysburg). In finance, a high-water mark is the highest peak value reached by an investment fund, used to calculate performance fees so that managers are not paid twice for the same gains. In computing, high-water mark systems track the largest value seen, for example in message sequencing and database replication.
