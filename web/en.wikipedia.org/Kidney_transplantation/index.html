Kidney transplantation or renal transplantation is the organ transplant of a kidney into a patient with end-stage kidney failure. It is generally the treatment of choice for suitable patients, offering longer survival and better quality of life than long-term dialysis. Kidneys come from living donors — often relatives or spouses, who can live healthily with one kidney — or from deceased donors allocated through national waiting-list systems.

History

Early attempts in the first half of the 20th century failed because of immunological rejection. The first successful kidney transplant was performed on 23 December 1954 at the Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston by a team led by Joseph Murray, transplanting a kidney between identical twins Ronald and Richard Herrick, so no immunosuppression was needed. Murray later shared the 1990 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. The introduction of azathioprine in the 1960s and ciclosporin in the late 1970s made transplants between unrelated people routinely feasible.

Procedure and matching

Donor and recipient are matched primarily by ABO blood group and by screening for donor-specific antibodies (crossmatching); HLA tissue matching influences long-term outcomes. In surgery, the donor kidney is usually placed in the recipient's iliac fossa in the pelvis, connected to the iliac artery and vein and to the bladder, while the patient's own failed kidneys are usually left in place. Paired kidney exchange programmes allow incompatible living donor–recipient pairs to swap donors.

Outcomes and complications

One-year graft survival now exceeds 90–95 per cent in most programmes, and living-donor kidneys typically function for 15–20 years or more. Recipients must take immunosuppressive drugs for the life of the graft, balancing the risks of acute and chronic rejection against infection and malignancy. More than 100,000 kidney transplants are performed worldwide each year, but demand greatly exceeds supply, making waiting lists and organ shortage persistent problems.
