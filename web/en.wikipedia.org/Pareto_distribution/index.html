Pareto distribution

The Pareto distribution, named after the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto, is a power-law probability distribution used in the description of social, quality control, scientific, geophysical, actuarial and many other types of observable phenomena. Originally applied to the distribution of wealth and income, it is exemplified by the Pareto principle (the "80–20 rule"), which states that roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes.

Definition

If X is a random variable with a Pareto (Type I) distribution, the probability that X is greater than some value x is given by a power law: Pr(X > x) = (xm/x)α for x ≥ xm, where xm is the (necessarily positive) minimum possible value and α is a positive parameter, the Pareto index. On logarithmic axes both the complementary cumulative distribution and the density are straight lines, the classic signature of a power law.

Properties

The distribution is heavy-tailed: its mean is infinite when α ≤ 1 and its variance is infinite when α ≤ 2, so extreme values dominate in ways unknown for the normal distribution. The Pareto distribution is related to the exponential distribution (the logarithm of a Pareto variable is exponential) and to Zipf's law for ranked data. Pareto himself observed that the fraction of the population with income above a level followed such a law with α near 1.5 in several countries.

Applications

  • Sizes of cities, files, and web traffic; word frequencies (Zipf's law).
  • Wealth and income distributions in economics.
  • Insurance: sizes of large claims and reinsurance pricing.
  • Geophysics: magnitudes of earthquakes (closely related to the Gutenberg–Richter law), oil field sizes.
  • Quality control and reliability, where a small number of defect types cause most failures.

Generalizations

Generalized Pareto distributions (Types II–IV) extend the family by adding location and additional shape parameters; the generalized Pareto distribution is central to extreme value theory and the peaks-over-threshold method. The log-normal and Pareto distributions are frequently compared when modelling skewed data such as incomes.
