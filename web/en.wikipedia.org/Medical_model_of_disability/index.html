Medical model of disability

The medical model of disability, or medical model, is based on a biomedical perception of disability. This model links a disability diagnosis to an individual's physical body. The model supposes that this disability may reduce the individual's quality of life and aims to diminish or correct this disability with medical intervention.

The medical model frames disability as a health condition to be treated or cured.

Description

In the medical model, disability is understood as a problem located within the individual: a disease, injury, or health condition that requires medical care, provided by professionals, in the form of treatment or rehabilitation. Management of the disability is aimed at a "cure", or at adjusting the individual so they can participate as fully as possible. Policy responses under this model focus on healthcare provision, rehabilitation services, and compensation for those deemed unable to work.

Criticism and alternatives

From the 1960s and 1970s, disabled people's movements and academics criticized the medical model for disempowering disabled people, defining them by their impairments, and ignoring the role of society in disabling them. The social model of disability, developed by the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS) and scholars such as Michael Oliver, distinguishes impairment (an individual condition) from disability (the disadvantage imposed by social barriers and attitudes). The World Health Organization's International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF, 2001) adopts an intermediate biopsychosocial model, combining medical and social perspectives.

Defenders of the medical model note that medical treatment genuinely relieves pain and restores function, and that many disabled people value diagnosis and intervention; critics of a purely social approach argue it neglects the realities of impairment itself.
