The United States secretary of labor is the head of the United States Department of Labor, a member of the Cabinet, and eleventh in the presidential line of succession. The position is one of the original cabinet posts created in connection with the organisation of the federal government, evolving from the combined Department of Commerce and Labor (est. 1903) when it was split in 1913 by President William Howard Taft, creating a separate Department of Labor explicitly "to foster, promote, and develop the welfare of the wage earners of the United States".

The secretary oversees agencies including the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the Wage and Hour Division, the Mine Safety and Health Administration, and the Employment and Training Administration. The department administers laws on minimum wage and overtime (the Fair Labor Standards Act), workplace safety, unemployment insurance jointly with the states, and union financial disclosure.

Notable secretaries

The longest-serving secretary was Frances Perkins (1933–1945), the first woman to hold a US cabinet post, who served throughout Franklin D. Roosevelt's presidency and was a central architect of the New Deal, including the Social Security Act and the Fair Labor Standards Act. Other notable holders include William B. Wilson, a former miners' union official and the first secretary; James P. Mitchell (1953–1961); Arthur Goldberg (1961–1962), later a Supreme Court justice; Robert Reich (1993–1997); Elaine Chao (2001–2009), the first Asian-American woman in a cabinet; Hilda Solis (2009–2013); Thomas Perez (2013–2017); and Marty Walsh (2021–2023), a former union leader who left to head the NHL players' association.
