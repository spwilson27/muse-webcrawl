Developmental psychology is the scientific study of how and why humans grow and change throughout life. Originally concerned with infants and children, the field has expanded to include adolescence, adult development, aging, and the entire lifespan. Developmental psychologists aim to explain how thinking, feeling, and behaviours change throughout life, in domains including motor skills, language, cognition, social attachment, personality and moral reasoning.

Major theories

Jean Piaget proposed that children pass through four qualitatively distinct stages of cognitive development — sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, and formal operational — constructing knowledge through assimilation and accommodation. Lev Vygotsky emphasised the social origins of thought, introducing the zone of proximal development and the role of language and culture. Erik Erikson described eight psychosocial stages spanning the whole lifespan, each defined by a crisis such as trust versus mistrust or identity versus role confusion.

Key areas

Attachment research, beginning with John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth's "strange situation" experiments, studies the bonds between infants and caregivers and their long-term effects. Lawrence Kohlberg modelled moral development as a sequence of stages of reasoning about justice. Studies of language acquisition debate the relative contributions of innate capacity (Chomsky's universal grammar) and social learning. Behavioural genetics, using twin and adoption studies, apportions individual differences between heredity and environment, while more recent epigenetic research shows the two interact throughout life.

Methods

The field relies on longitudinal studies that follow the same individuals over years, cross-sectional comparisons of different age groups, and sequential designs combining both, as well as experiments, naturalistic observation, and neuroimaging. Classic milestones of the discipline include the Minnesota longitudinal studies and long-running cohorts such as the Dunedin Study in New Zealand.

Applications

Developmental psychology informs parenting advice, curriculum design, child clinical practice, and policies on issues from nursery provision to retirement. Critiques of the field note that its foundational samples were disproportionately Western, educated and middle-class, prompting cross-cultural replication work.
