Stone–von Neumann theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Statement of the uniqueness of representations of the canonical commutation relations.

The Stone–von Neumann theorem, proved by Marshall Stone and John von Neumann in the early 1930s, is a foundational result of functional analysis and mathematical physics. It states that, for a system with finitely many degrees of freedom, all irreducible representations of the canonical commutation relations in Weyl form are unitarily equivalent.

Background

In quantum mechanics, position and momentum operators satisfy the commutation relation [Q, P] = iħ. Because these operators are unbounded, the relations are rigorously formulated through the unitary one-parameter groups they generate, the Weyl relations. Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave mechanics appeared to be different formulations of quantum theory; von Neumann's work showed they are the same representation.

Statement

Precisely: any irreducible, strongly continuous representation of the Weyl relations for finitely many degrees of freedom is unitarily equivalent to the Schrödinger representation on L²(Rⁿ). The result fails for infinitely many degrees of freedom, which is why quantum field theory admits unitarily inequivalent representations.

Related results

The theorem is closely connected with Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups and with the theory of the Heisenberg group, whose representation theory it essentially classifies.
