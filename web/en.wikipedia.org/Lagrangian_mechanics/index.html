Lagrangian mechanics is a reformulation of classical mechanics, introduced by the Italian-French mathematician and astronomer Joseph-Louis Lagrange in his 1788 work Mécanique analytique. In Lagrangian mechanics, the trajectory of a system of particles is found by solving the Euler–Lagrange equations, which are derived from a single scalar function called the Lagrangian. The Lagrangian formulation is especially powerful for systems with constraints, where it avoids the need to compute constraint forces explicitly.

The Lagrangian

For a system with kinetic energy T and potential energy V, the Lagrangian is defined as

L = T − V

The Lagrangian is expressed as a function of the generalized coordinates qi, their time derivatives (generalized velocities) q̇i, and possibly time t itself. The number of independent generalized coordinates equals the number of degrees of freedom of the system.

Euler–Lagrange equations

The equations of motion follow from Hamilton's principle: the actual path taken by the system makes the action functional stationary. The resulting Euler–Lagrange equations are

d/dt (∂L/∂q̇i) − ∂L/∂qi = 0

These are second-order differential equations for the generalized coordinates. When the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on a coordinate, that coordinate is called cyclic (or ignorable), and its conjugate momentum is conserved — a consequence closely related to Noether's theorem, which connects continuous symmetries of the Lagrangian to conservation laws.

Comparison with Newtonian mechanics

Unlike Newtonian mechanics, which works with vectors (forces and accelerations) in a chosen reference frame, Lagrangian mechanics is a scalar, energy-based approach. It handles holonomic constraints elegantly: rather than introducing unknown constraint forces, one simply chooses coordinates that already satisfy the constraints. The same framework generalizes naturally to relativistic mechanics and, through the Legendre transform to the Hamiltonian, to Hamiltonian mechanics and quantum mechanics.

Applications

Lagrangian methods are standard in celestial mechanics, robotics, molecular dynamics, and field theory. In modern physics, the Lagrangian density is the starting point for formulating theories such as the Standard Model of particle physics, where the Euler–Lagrange equations are applied to fields rather than particles.
