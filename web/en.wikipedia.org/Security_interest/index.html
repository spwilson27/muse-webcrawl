Security interest

Security interest
FieldCommercial law; property law
Key conceptsAttachment, perfection, priority, collateral
Governed by (US)Uniform Commercial Code, Article 9
RelatedMortgage, lien, pledge, secured transaction

A security interest is a legal claim on collateral that has been pledged, usually to secure the repayment of a loan or the performance of another obligation. If the debtor defaults, the secured party may take possession of and sell the collateral to satisfy the debt. Security interests reduce a lender's risk and thereby lower the cost of credit.

Creation and perfection

Under Article 9 of the Uniform Commercial Code in the United States, a security interest becomes enforceable — it "attaches" — when value has been given, the debtor has rights in the collateral, and the debtor has authenticated a security agreement describing the collateral. To be effective against third parties, the interest generally must also be "perfected," most often by filing a financing statement (UCC-1) in a public registry, or by taking possession or control of the collateral.

Priority

When several creditors claim the same collateral, priority rules determine who is paid first. As a general rule, the first creditor to file or perfect has priority. Purchase-money security interests (PMSIs) — taken by a seller or lender to secure the price of the collateral itself — enjoy special priority if perfected promptly.

Comparison with other systems

English law historically recognized distinct devices such as the mortgage, charge, pledge, and lien, rather than a single unitary security interest; reforms modelled on Article 9 and the Canadian and Australian Personal Property Securities Acts have introduced registration-based systems in many Commonwealth jurisdictions. Real property is usually excluded and governed by mortgage law instead. See also collateral and secured transaction.
