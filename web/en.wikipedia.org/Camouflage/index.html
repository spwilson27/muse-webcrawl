Camouflage

Camouflage is the use of any combination of materials, coloration, or illumination for concealment, either by making animals or objects hard to see (crypsis), or by disguising them as something else (mimesis).

In nature

Cryptic colouration is the commonest form: animals match their background in colour and pattern, break up their outline with disruptive markings, eliminate shadow by countershading (dark above, pale below — found from deer to sharks), and in some species actively change, as in chameleons, cuttlefish, and snow-adapted hares and ptarmigan. Mimesis imitates a specific object: stick insects resemble twigs, leaf insects resemble foliage, and the viceroy butterfly mimics the unpalatable monarch. Some animals use transparent bodies, silvering, or decoration with materials from the environment.

Military camouflage

Armies long wore bright uniforms; concealment spread only with accurate long-range rifles and aerial observation in the late 19th and 20th centuries. In the First World War, French camoufleurs — the word gave English "camouflage" — painted guns and positions, and navies adopted "dazzle" painting to confuse range-finding rather than to hide. The Second World War brought patterned uniforms and large-scale deception, from dummy armies to the British Middle East Camouflage Directorate. Modern military camouflage must also defeat infrared, thermal, and radar sensors, and pixelated digital patterns (such as CADPAT and MARPAT) have largely replaced older woodland designs.

Culture and fashion

Camouflage patterns entered civilian fashion from the 1960s onward, used by both protesters and mainstream designers, and remain common in streetwear and hunting clothing.
