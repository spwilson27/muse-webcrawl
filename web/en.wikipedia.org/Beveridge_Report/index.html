Beveridge Report

The Beveridge Report, officially titled Social Insurance and Allied Services, is a government report published in the United Kingdom on 1 December 1942 that influenced the founding of that nation's welfare state. It was authored by the economist and Liberal politician William Beveridge, who identified five "Giant Evils" in society: squalor, ignorance, want, idleness, and disease.

Proposals

Beveridge proposed a comprehensive system of social insurance, in which every person in work would pay a single weekly national insurance contribution in return for benefits protecting them against unemployment, sickness, and old age — "from the cradle to the grave". The plan rested on three assumptions: family allowances, a comprehensive national health service free at the point of use, and the maintenance of full employment.

Reception and legacy

The report became an unexpected bestseller, selling hundreds of thousands of copies, and was widely discussed among troops and civilians during the Second World War. The coalition government received it coolly, but after its landslide victory in 1945, Clement Attlee's Labour government implemented most of Beveridge's proposals: the National Insurance Act 1946, the National Health Service Act 1946 (creating the NHS in 1948), and an expanded system of family allowances and public housing.

The Beveridge Report remains a landmark in the history of social policy and is frequently described as the founding document of the modern British welfare state.
