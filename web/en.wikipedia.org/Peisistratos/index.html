Peisistratos


Peisistratos ruled Athens in the 6th century BCE

Peisistratos (c. 600 – 527 BCE) was a tyrant of ancient Athens whose rule, though it interrupted the city's early democratic development, brought Athens stability, prosperity, and cultural prominence. He seized power three times — in about 561/560, again after exile, and finally after victory at the Battle of Pallene in 546 BCE — and ruled until his death, leaving the tyranny to his sons Hippias and Hipparchus, the Peisistratids.

Rise to power

An aristocrat related to Solon's family, Peisistratos won fame as a general in war against Megara and built a popular faction, the "Hill" party, against the aristocratic factions of the Plain and the Coast. According to Herodotus, he wounded himself and claimed an assassination attempt to obtain a bodyguard, with which he seized the Acropolis. Twice exiled by his rivals, he returned a second time with the theatrical help of a tall woman dressed as Athena, and a third time with mercenaries financed by mining revenues and foreign allies, winning at Pallene in 546 BCE.

Rule

Peisistratos governed moderately and largely kept Solon's laws and constitution intact. He promoted agriculture with loans and travelling judges, taxed produce, encouraged the export of olive oil and Athenian painted pottery, built aqueducts and temples — including work on the Acropolis and the Olympieion — and enlarged festivals such as the Panathenaea and the City Dionysia. Tradition credits his family with commissioning a standard text of Homer's epics. His tyranny ended after Hipparchus was assassinated in 514 BCE and Hippias was expelled in 510 BCE, paving the way for Cleisthenes' democratic reforms.
