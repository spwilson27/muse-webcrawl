Long Walls

The Long Walls were defensive walls built in ancient Greece to connect a city with its port, guaranteeing access to the sea even when the city was besieged by land. The most famous example linked Athens with its harbours at Piraeus and Phalerum, a corridor of roughly 6–7 km.

Athens

Construction of the Athenian Long Walls began after the Persian Wars, around 461 BC, and was completed in the 450s BC. The walls turned Athens and Piraeus into a single fortress supplied by sea, a strategy associated with Pericles during the Peloponnesian War: the Athenians abandoned the Attic countryside to Sparta's army and relied on their navy and imported grain.

Other examples and fate

Similar walls were built at Corinth, Megara and Argos. The Athenian walls were demolished by the Spartans in 404 BC after Athens' defeat — to the music of flute girls, according to Xenophon — but were rebuilt with Persian funding by Conon in 393 BC. They gradually lost military value and were quarried over the following centuries.
