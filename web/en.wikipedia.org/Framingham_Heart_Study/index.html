Framingham Heart Study

The Framingham Heart Study has followed residents of Framingham, Massachusetts since 1948.

The Framingham Heart Study is a long-term, ongoing cardiovascular cohort study of residents of the city of Framingham, Massachusetts. Begun in 1948 under the U.S. National Heart Institute (now the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute) and Boston University, it is one of the most influential epidemiological studies ever conducted, and much of the modern understanding of heart disease derives from it.

Design

The original cohort comprised 5,209 men and women aged 30 to 62 who were examined every two years with detailed medical histories, physical examinations and laboratory tests. In 1971 the study enrolled a second generation — the children of the original participants and their spouses — and in 2002 a third generation joined, allowing family and genetic studies. Later cohorts (the Omni cohorts) were added to reflect the town's growing ethnic diversity.

Findings

Framingham researchers coined the term "risk factor" and demonstrated that high blood pressure, high blood cholesterol, cigarette smoking, obesity, diabetes and physical inactivity increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, while HDL cholesterol is protective. The study produced the famous Framingham Risk Score, which estimates an individual's 10-year risk of coronary heart disease from age, sex, cholesterol, blood pressure and smoking status, and has guided preventive cardiology worldwide. Beyond the heart, the cohort has contributed to knowledge of stroke, dementia, osteoporosis, arthritis, hearing loss and genetics; thousands of scientific papers have been based on its data.
