Spoiler effect

The spoiler effect is the effect of vote splitting on an election, where a candidate who draws support from a more closely-aligned, major candidate may cause the major candidate to lose to an opposing candidate with quite different views. It is most pronounced in plurality voting systems, under which a minority candidate "spoils" the chances of a similar majority-preferred candidate while having almost no chance of winning themselves.

Mechanism

In a first-past-the-post election, votes for a minor candidate are effectively wasted if that candidate cannot win; if those voters would otherwise have supported a similar major candidate, the divided vote can hand the election to a candidate opposed by the combined majority. The spoiler effect is related to the violation of the independence of clones criterion and independence of irrelevant alternatives, and it encourages strategic voting, in which voters abandon a preferred candidate who seems unable to win. Duverger's law attributes two-party systems in part to this pressure. Electoral systems such as ranked-choice (instant-runoff) voting, approval voting, and proportional representation are designed to reduce or eliminate spoiling.

Examples

Frequently cited examples from United States elections include Ralph Nader's Green Party candidacy in the 2000 presidential election, widely argued to have drawn votes from Al Gore in Florida, which George W. Bush won by 537 votes; and the 1912 election, where Theodore Roosevelt's Progressive candidacy split the Republican vote and allowed Woodrow Wilson to win. Similar dynamics are discussed in many national and primary elections worldwide.
