Dover Bronze Age Boat

The preserved remains of the Dover Boat in Dover Museum

The Dover Bronze Age Boat is one of the oldest known seagoing vessels in the world, dating to about 1575–1520 BC (the Middle Bronze Age). Discovered in 1992 during roadworks in Dover, Kent, England, the boat is a sewn-plank vessel constructed of oak planks stitched together with yew withies, with the seams caulked with moss and sealed with beeswax. About 9.5 metres of the boat's length survived; it originally measured roughly 15–16 metres and could have carried a crew and cargo across the English Channel.

Discovery and excavation

The boat was found six metres below the modern street level by archaeologists of the Canterbury Archaeological Trust, working ahead of construction of the A20 road scheme. Waterlogged, anaerobic conditions had preserved the timbers. Because only a few days were available before construction resumed, the boat was cut into 32 sections, lifted, and later reassembled after conservation.

Construction

The hull consists of four shaped oak strakes joined edge-to-edge with yew withies passing through holes along the plank edges; transverse timbers (cleats) were left standing proud of the planks and wedged. The bottom was made of two planks joined by a central rail. There is no evidence for a mast or sail; the boat was probably paddled or rowed, and may have carried up to twenty paddlers and cargoes such as metalwork, pottery or livestock.

Significance and display

The Dover Boat is rare direct evidence for cross-Channel trade and seafaring in Bronze Age Europe, complementing the older Ferriby boats from the Humber. In 2012 a half-scale reconstruction, named Ole Crumlin-Pedersen, was built and launched to test its seaworthiness. The original is displayed in the Bronze Age Boat Gallery of Dover Museum, which won awards for its presentation.
