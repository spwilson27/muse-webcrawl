Abstract object

In philosophy and metaphysics, an abstract object is an object that does not exist in space or time and is not causally efficacious. In contrast, concrete objects are located in space and time and can enter into causal relations. Commonly cited examples of abstract objects include numbers, sets, propositions, properties, and possible worlds. Examples of concrete objects include physical objects, events, and individual people.

The abstract–concrete distinction is a central topic in metaphysics and the philosophy of mathematics. Philosophers disagree both about how the distinction should be drawn and about whether abstract objects exist at all. Platonists hold that abstract objects exist independently of minds and language, while nominalists deny their existence and attempt to explain away apparent references to them.

Criteria

Several criteria have been proposed for distinguishing abstract from concrete objects. Abstract objects are often characterised as:

  • Non-spatiotemporal: they do not exist at any particular place or time.
  • Causally inert: they cannot cause or be affected by physical events.
  • Not perceivable: they cannot be directly observed with the senses.

Each criterion faces counterexamples: some philosophers hold that spacetime itself is an abstract object with spatial and temporal parts, while others treat the universe as a single concrete object that is not itself located in space or time.

In philosophy of mathematics

Mathematical objects such as the number 7 or the empty set are the standard examples of abstract objects. The existence and nature of such objects is the subject of debate between Platonism, nominalism, structuralism and fictionalism. The indispensability argument, associated with Willard Van Orman Quine and Hilary Putnam, argues that we should believe in mathematical objects because they are indispensable to our best scientific theories.
