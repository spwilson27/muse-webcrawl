State atheism

Anti-religious campaigns accompanied state atheism in the USSR.

State atheism or state-sponsored atheism is the incorporation of positive atheism into political regimes, particularly associated with communist states that sought to eliminate or strictly control religion. It differs from secularism and state neutrality toward religion, as it actively promotes non-belief and may involve persecution of religious institutions and adherents.

History

The French Revolution saw an early, brief experiment in state atheism with the Cult of Reason in 1793–94. The most extensive implementation came in the Soviet Union, officially an atheist state under Marxist-Leninist ideology, where the League of Militant Atheists campaigned against religion, churches were closed or demolished, and believers faced discrimination and at times violent repression. Albania under Enver Hoxha declared itself the world's first atheist state in 1967, banning religion outright. Revolutionary Mexico, Mongolia, and Khmer Rouge Cambodia also pursued strongly anti-religious policies, and China officially promotes atheism among Communist Party members while recognizing five religions under state supervision.

Contemporary status

Since the fall of communism in Eastern Europe, no state formally professes atheism as a founding doctrine, though some states remain officially committed to it in party ideology. Scholars distinguish state atheism from the high levels of religious non-affiliation found in many secular democracies, which arise without state compulsion.
