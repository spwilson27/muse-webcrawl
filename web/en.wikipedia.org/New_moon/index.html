In astronomy, a new moon is the first lunar phase, occurring when the Moon and the Sun have the same ecliptic longitude—that is, when the Moon lies between the Earth and the Sun as seen from Earth. At this alignment the sunlit hemisphere of the Moon faces away from Earth, so the Moon appears dark and is generally invisible to the naked eye, lost in the Sun's glare. The new moon marks the beginning of a lunation, and lunar age and phase counts are conventionally reckoned from it.

Timing and cycle

The interval between successive new moons—the synodic month or lunation—averages about 29.53 days. Because the Moon is usually slightly above or below the ecliptic at conjunction, a solar eclipse does not occur every month; eclipses happen only when the new moon coincides with the Moon's crossing of a node of its orbit. A thin waxing crescent becomes visible one to three days after the new moon, and the full cycle proceeds through first quarter, full moon, and last quarter back to new moon.

Calendars and culture

Many traditional calendars begin their months at or near the new moon. The Islamic calendar starts each month with the sighting of the young crescent (hilal) after the new moon, determining observances such as Ramadan and Eid. The Chinese and Hebrew calendars are lunisolar and likewise anchor months to new moons, with the Chinese New Year falling on the second new moon after the winter solstice (subject to rules). In Hindu calendars the new moon day, Amavasya, is a significant observance. Spring tides—exceptionally high and low tides—occur around new and full moons, when the tidal forces of Sun and Moon align.

Terminology

In modern usage "new moon" denotes the astronomical conjunction itself. Historically, and in some calendar traditions, it referred to the first visible crescent. The entirely dark disc occasionally can be seen faintly by earthshine, sunlight reflected from Earth onto the Moon, poetically called "the old moon in the new moon's arms".
