René Descartes

René Descartes (31 March 1596 – 11 February 1650) was a French philosopher, scientist and mathematician, widely considered a seminal figure in the emergence of modern philosophy and science. He is often called the father of modern philosophy, and also laid foundations of analytic geometry, linking algebra and geometry in what is now called the Cartesian coordinate system.

Life

Descartes was born in La Haye en Touraine (now named Descartes in his honour) and educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche. After briefly studying law and serving as a gentleman volunteer in Dutch and Bavarian armies, he settled in the Netherlands in 1628, where he wrote most of his major works in relative seclusion. In 1649 he accepted an invitation from Queen Christina of Sweden to join her court in Stockholm, where he died of pneumonia the following winter.

Philosophy

In the Discourse on the Method (1637) Descartes set out to rebuild knowledge on indubitable foundations, employing methodical doubt — doubting everything that could possibly be doubted. The one truth he held immune to doubt was expressed in the famous phrase cogito, ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"). He argued for a dualism of thinking substance (res cogitans) and extended substance (res extensa), and developed rationalist proofs for the existence of God and the external world that were debated by contemporaries including Hobbes, Arnauld and Gassendi.

Mathematics and science

The appendix La Géométrie to the Discourse introduced analytic geometry, using coordinates to translate geometric problems into algebra. Descartes also contributed to optics, formulating the law of refraction, and to physiology and mechanics, though his vortex theory of planetary motion was later superseded by Newton's gravitation.

Legacy

Cartesian rationalism shaped Spinoza, Leibniz and the mainstream of early modern philosophy, and his mind–body problem remains central to the philosophy of mind.
