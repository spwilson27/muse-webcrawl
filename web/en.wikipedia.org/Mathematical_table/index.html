Mathematical table

Mathematical tables are lists of numbers showing the results of a calculation with varying arguments. Trigonometric tables were used in ancient Greece and India for applications to astronomy and celestial navigation, and continued to be widely used until electronic calculators became cheap and plentiful, in order to simplify and drastically speed up computation. Tables of logarithms and trigonometric functions were common in math and science textbooks, and specialized tables were published for applications such as astronomy, celestial navigation, and statistics.

History and use

Before electronic calculators, tables were the principal means of evaluating functions such as logarithms because converting multiplication and division to addition and subtraction saved enormous amounts of time. Charles Babbage conceived his Difference Engine partly to mechanize the production of accurate tables, which were prone to human error. Famous collections include the tables of John Napier (1614), who introduced logarithms, the tables compiled by the French Bureau du Cadastre under Gaspard de Prony in the 1790s, and Abramowitz and Stegun's Handbook of Mathematical Functions (1964).

Simple example

To compute the sine of 75 degrees 9 minutes 50 seconds using a table of sines like the one created by Regiomontanus, one might round up to 75 degrees 10 minutes and then find the value shown. Interpolation between neighbouring entries improved accuracy for values not printed directly.
