Tokugawa Ieyasu

Tokugawa Ieyasu (31 January 1543 – 1 June 1616) was the founder and first shōgun of the Tokugawa shogunate of Japan, which ruled from 1603 until the Meiji Restoration in 1868. He is regarded as one of the three "Great Unifiers" of Japan, along with his former lord Oda Nobunaga and his fellow subordinate Toyotomi Hideyoshi.

Early life

Ieyasu was born Matsudaira Takechiyo in Mikawa Province, the son of a minor local warlord. He spent much of his childhood as a hostage, first to the Oda clan and then to the powerful Imagawa clan, an experience that taught him patience and political caution. After the Imagawa were defeated at the Battle of Okehazama in 1560, Ieyasu allied with Oda Nobunaga and gradually consolidated his hold on Mikawa and neighbouring provinces.

Rise to power

After Nobunaga's death in 1582, Ieyasu became one of the most powerful daimyō in Japan, rivalling Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Following Hideyoshi's victory over the Hōjō in 1590, Ieyasu was transferred to the Kantō region, where he built his castle town at Edo (modern Tokyo). After Hideyoshi's death in 1598, Ieyasu led a coalition of eastern daimyō to victory at the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, effectively seizing control of the country.

Shogunate

In 1603, Ieyasu received the title of shōgun from Emperor Go-Yōzei and established the Tokugawa bakufu. He retired in 1605 in favour of his son Hidetada but retained real power, and in 1614–1615 he eliminated the last Toyotomi resistance at the Siege of Osaka. He died in 1616 and was enshrined as a deity at Nikkō Tōshō-gū. The system he built governed Japan for more than 260 years of relative peace.
