In mathematics, the harmonic series is the infinite series

Σn=1∞ 1/n = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1/5 + …

It is named for the concept of harmonics in music. Despite its terms approaching zero, the series diverges — its partial sums grow without limit, although so slowly that over 1043 terms are needed for the sum to exceed 100.

Divergence

The divergence was first proved in the 14th century by Nicole Oresme, who grouped the terms as 1 + 1/2 + (1/3 + 1/4) + (1/5 + … + 1/8) + …, each group exceeding 1/2, so the partial sums exceed any bound. Pietro Mengoli and the Bernoulli brothers Johann and Jacob later gave further proofs. The integral test gives another demonstration, since the integral of 1/x diverges.

Partial sums

The partial sum Hn = 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/n is called the n-th harmonic number. The difference Hn − ln n converges to the Euler–Mascheroni constant γ ≈ 0.57721. No harmonic number beyond H1 is an integer, since among 1…n there is a unique multiple of the largest power of 2 not exceeding n.

Related series

Replacing the exponent 1 by p gives the p-series, convergent exactly when p > 1; the harmonic series is the boundary case p = 1 and corresponds to ζ(1), the pole of the Riemann zeta function. The alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + … converges conditionally to ln 2. Deleting terms whose denominators contain a given digit yields the convergent Kempner series, while summing only over primes still diverges, like log log n.

Applications

Harmonic numbers appear in the analysis of algorithms (the expected cost of quicksort is proportional to n log n via harmonic sums), in the coupon collector's problem, in the book-stacking (leaning tower of Lire) puzzle, where a stack of n books can overhang by Hn/2 book lengths, and in the worm on a rubber band paradox.
