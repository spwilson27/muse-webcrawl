Consumer activism

Consumer activists use boycotts and public protest to pressure companies.

Consumer activism is activism undertaken on behalf of consumers, or by consumers themselves, to change the behaviour of businesses and governments. It encompasses boycotts, buycotts (deliberately buying from favoured firms), shareholder campaigns, litigation, lobbying for consumer protection legislation, and the work of consumer organisations.

History

Consumer protest has a long pedigree: colonial Americans boycotted British goods before independence, and nineteenth-century campaigners organised against adulterated food and exploitative "sweated" labour. The modern consumer movement took shape in the twentieth century with the founding of testing organisations such as Consumer Reports in the United States and the Consumers' Association in Britain, and with Ralph Nader's 1965 book Unsafe at Any Speed, which helped make product safety a central political issue.

Methods and issues

Prominent campaigns have targeted infant-formula marketing in developing countries (the Nestlé boycott), apartheid-era South Africa, sweatshop labour in the garment industry, animal testing, tobacco marketing, and, more recently, data privacy practices and greenwashing. The rise of social media has made it easier to organise rapid consumer pressure, while certification schemes such as Fairtrade convert consumer preference into market incentives.

Critics argue that consumer activism can be an inefficient substitute for regulation and that ethical consumption remains a minority pursuit; supporters reply that market pressure often moves companies faster than legislation can.
