Kibble–Zurek mechanism

The Kibble–Zurek mechanism (KZM) describes the non-equilibrium dynamics of a system driven through a continuous phase transition. It predicts how the density of topological defects—such as vortices, domain walls, or cosmic strings—depends on the rate at which the transition is crossed.

Background

Tom Kibble proposed in 1976 that phase transitions in the early universe would leave behind topological defects whose density is set by the correlation length at the time different regions of space independently chose a broken-symmetry state. Wojciech Zurek extended the idea in 1985 to condensed-matter systems, arguing that when a system is cooled through its critical point at a finite rate, the dynamics "freeze out" near the transition because the relaxation time diverges (critical slowing down).

Prediction

The mechanism predicts a power-law scaling of defect density with quench rate, with an exponent determined by the equilibrium critical exponents of the transition. The scaling has been tested in liquid crystals, superfluid helium, superconductors, Bose–Einstein condensates, trapped ions, and multiferroics, and is a cornerstone of non-equilibrium statistical physics.
