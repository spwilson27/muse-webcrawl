Bosque Redondo (Spanish for "round grove") was a reservation established by the United States government in eastern New Mexico Territory in the 1860s, around Fort Sumner on the Pecos River. It became the destination of the forced removal known as the Long Walk, in which thousands of Navajo people were marched from their homelands in 1864 under the military campaign led by Kit Carson.

The internment

By 1865 more than 8,500 Navajo and about 500 Mescalero Apache were confined on the 40-square-mile reservation. The experiment failed: alkaline water, scarce firewood, crop failures from cutworms and floods, disease, and raids by Comanches made survival precarious; thousands died. The Mescalero escaped in 1865. Costs of supplying the camp far exceeded expectations.

Treaty and return

The Treaty of Bosque Redondo, signed 1 June 1868 by Navajo leaders including Barboncito and the peace commission led by General William T. Sherman, allowed the Navajo to return to a portion of their ancestral homeland — the origin of the modern Navajo Nation reservation. The site is now commemorated by the Bosque Redondo Memorial at Fort Sumner Historic Site.
