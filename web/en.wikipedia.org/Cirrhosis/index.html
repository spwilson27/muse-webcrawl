Cirrhosis, also known as liver cirrhosis or hepatic cirrhosis, and end-stage liver disease, is a chronic liver disease characterised by replacement of normal liver tissue with scar tissue (fibrosis) and regenerative nodules, leading to loss of liver function. The disease typically develops slowly over months or years.

The most common causes are alcohol-related liver disease, viral hepatitis B and hepatitis C, and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (formerly non-alcoholic fatty liver disease). Other causes include autoimmune diseases, haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, and primary biliary cholangitis.

Signs and symptoms

Early on, there are often no symptoms. As the disease worsens, a person may become tired, weak, and itchy, with swelling in the lower legs, yellow skin (jaundice), easy bruising, and fluid build-up in the abdomen (ascites). Complications include portal hypertension, bleeding from oesophageal varices, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome, and liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on blood tests, medical imaging, and liver biopsy. Non-invasive assessment of fibrosis, such as transient elastography (FibroScan) and serum scores, is increasingly used. Severity is graded with the Child–Pugh score and the MELD score.

Treatment

Damage to the liver is generally irreversible, so treatment focuses on preventing progression and managing complications: abstinence from alcohol, antiviral therapy for hepatitis, weight management for fatty liver disease, vaccination, diuretics for ascites, and beta-blockers or banding for varices. In advanced cases, liver transplantation may be an option. Cirrhosis caused around 1.3 million deaths worldwide in recent estimates, making it a leading cause of death globally.
