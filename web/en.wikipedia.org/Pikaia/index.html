Pikaia

Pikaia gracilens is an extinct chordate animal known from the Middle Cambrian Burgess Shale of British Columbia, Canada. Averaging about 5 centimetres (2 inches) in length, this small, flattened, eel-like creature swam above the seafloor. It is famous as one of the earliest known animals with the defining features of the chordates — the group that includes all vertebrates, and therefore humans.

Discovery

Pikaia was collected by Charles Doolittle Walcott during his Burgess Shale expeditions and described in 1911; Walcott classified it as a polychaete worm. Its significance was recognised in the 1970s when Simon Conway Morris re-examined the fossils and identified chordate features. It is named after Pika Peak in Alberta.

Anatomy

Pikaia had a distinct head with two tentacle-like appendages and a series of short appendages bearing gill slits, a notochord running the length of the body, a dorsal nerve cord, and V-shaped muscle blocks (myomeres) visible as zigzag segments in fossils. These features mark it as a basal chordate, though whether it had a true cranium or vertebral elements is debated.

Significance

Popularised by Stephen Jay Gould in Wonderful Life (1989), Pikaia became an icon of the Cambrian explosion and of contingency in evolution: Gould argued that the survival of this unassuming chordate lineage made possible the later evolution of all vertebrates.
