Helvetism

A Helvetism (German: Helvetismus) is a word, expression or usage that is characteristic of the variety of a language spoken in Switzerland and differs from the standard usage of the same language elsewhere. The term is most often applied to Swiss Standard German, where Helvetisms are officially recognised features of the Swiss national standard, but it can also describe Swiss French and Swiss Italian usages.

Swiss German Helvetisms

Swiss Standard German differs from the German of Germany and Austria in vocabulary, some aspects of pronunciation and spelling, and a number of loanwords from French, Italian and Romansh. Familiar examples include Velo for bicycle (instead of Fahrrad), Billett for ticket, Natel for mobile phone, Rüebli for carrot, and parkieren for to park. Borrowings from French include Poulet (chicken) and Glace (ice cream, from French glace). In spelling, Swiss German famously does not use the letter ß, writing ss instead.

Status and codification

Helvetisms are not considered errors in Switzerland: they are used in federal administration, law, newspapers and schools, and are recorded in reference works such as the Variantenwörterbuch des Deutschen, which documents standard vocabulary across the German-speaking countries. German-language style authorities such as Duden mark many of them explicitly as Swiss ("schweizerisch"). The recognition of Helvetisms reflects the pluricentric nature of German, with national standard varieties in Germany, Austria and Switzerland.

French and Italian

Swiss French has its own regionalisms, sometimes also called Helvetisms, such as septante and nonante for seventy and ninety (rather than soixante-dix and quatre-vingt-dix), natel for a mobile phone and déjeuner, dîner and souper for breakfast, lunch and dinner respectively. Swiss Italian likewise features usages influenced by the country's other languages and by regional dialects.
