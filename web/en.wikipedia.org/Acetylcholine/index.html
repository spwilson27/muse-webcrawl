Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine (ACh) is an organic compound that functions in the brain and body of many types of animals, including humans, as a neurotransmitter. It is the chemical that motor neurons release to activate muscles, and it also plays a major role in the parasympathetic nervous system and in brain functions such as attention, learning and memory. It was the first neurotransmitter to be identified, in experiments by Henry Dale and Otto Loewi, who shared the 1936 Nobel Prize for the discovery.

Function

At the neuromuscular junction, acetylcholine released from motor nerve terminals opens nicotinic receptors on muscle fibers, triggering contraction. In the autonomic nervous system it is the transmitter of all preganglionic neurons and of parasympathetic postganglionic neurons, where muscarinic receptors slow the heart, stimulate digestion and constrict the pupils. In the central nervous system, cholinergic projections from the basal forebrain modulate arousal, attention and memory formation.

Synthesis and breakdown

Acetylcholine is synthesized by the enzyme choline acetyltransferase from choline and acetyl-CoA. After release it is rapidly hydrolyzed in the synaptic cleft by acetylcholinesterase, one of the fastest enzymes known. Drugs that inhibit this enzyme — including medicines for Alzheimer's disease and myasthenia gravis, as well as organophosphate insecticides and nerve agents — raise acetylcholine levels, with effects ranging from therapeutic to lethal.

Clinical relevance

Loss of cholinergic neurons is associated with Alzheimer's disease; botulinum toxin acts by blocking acetylcholine release; and the autoimmune disease myasthenia gravis targets its receptors. Anticholinergic drugs that block muscarinic receptors are used for conditions from motion sickness to overactive bladder.
