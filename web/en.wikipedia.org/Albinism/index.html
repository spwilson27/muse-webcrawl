Albinism

Albinism
SpecialtyDermatology, medical genetics, ophthalmology
CauseGenetic mutations affecting melanin production
TypesOculocutaneous (OCA), ocular (OA), syndromic forms

Albinism is a group of inherited genetic conditions characterised by the absence or severe reduction of the pigment melanin in the skin, hair, and eyes. It results from mutations in genes involved in producing or distributing melanin. Albinism occurs in all racial and ethnic groups worldwide and also widely in other animal species.

Signs and symptoms

People with oculocutaneous albinism have very pale skin that burns easily in sunlight and is prone to skin cancers, white or light hair, and light-coloured eyes. Because melanin is required for normal development of the optic nerves, albinism is consistently associated with eye problems: reduced visual acuity, nystagmus (involuntary eye movement), photophobia, and misrouting of optic nerve fibres at the chiasm. Ocular albinism mainly affects the eyes.

Genetics and management

Most forms are inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern; ocular albinism type 1 is X-linked. Several genes are involved, including TYR (encoding tyrosinase) in OCA1 and OCA2 in the commonest form in people of African descent. There is no cure; management centres on sun protection, corrective lenses, low-vision aids, and monitoring for skin cancer. In some regions, notably parts of sub-Saharan Africa, people with albinism face severe stigma and even violence linked to superstition.
