James Stirling (mathematician)

James Stirling (May 1692, Garden, Stirlingshire – 5 December 1770, Edinburgh) was a Scottish mathematician. He was nicknamed "the Venetian" because, while studying in Venice, he discovered and revealed the trade secrets of the glass-makers there, and was forced to flee for his life. He is best known for Stirling's approximation, a formula for approximating factorials, and for his work on infinite series.

Born: May 1692, Garden, Stirlingshire, Scotland

Died: 5 December 1770, Edinburgh, Scotland

Known for: Stirling's approximation, Stirling numbers

Fields: Mathematics

Life

Stirling studied at the University of Glasgow and then at Balliol College, Oxford, where he held a Snell Exhibition. During the Jacobite rising of 1715 his family was implicated, and Stirling lost his exhibition for refusing to swear an oath of allegiance. He travelled to Italy, where he worked in the glass industry in Venice. Around 1725 he returned to London, where he worked as a teacher of mathematics while maintaining contact with Isaac Newton. In 1730 he published his most important work, the Methodus differentialis. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1723. In 1735 he returned to Scotland to manage a lead-mining company at Leadhills, a post he held for the rest of his life.

Work

Stirling's Methodus differentialis (1730) treated infinite series and their summation. It contains the asymptotic expansion for the logarithm of the factorial function, from which Stirling's approximation follows: n! is approximately √(2πn) (n/e)n. The constants in the formula were later refined by Abraham de Moivre. Stirling also gave his name to the Stirling numbers of the first and second kinds, which count permutations by cycles and set partitions respectively. In 1717 he published Lineae tertii ordinis Neutonianae, a work on cubic curves that extended Newton's classification.

Legacy

The University of Stirling, founded in 1967, is named after the county rather than the man, but Stirling's name remains firmly attached to several fundamental results in combinatorics and analysis.
