Independent agency of the United States government

An independent agency of the United States government is an agency of the executive branch that is structured to operate with a degree of independence from direct presidential control. Typically, independent agencies are headed by a multi-member commission or board whose members serve fixed, staggered terms, are often required to be bipartisan in composition, and may be removed by the President only "for cause", rather than at will.

Examples

Well-known independent agencies include the Federal Reserve, the Securities and Exchange Commission, the Federal Trade Commission, the Federal Communications Commission, the Environmental Protection Agency (independent but headed by a single administrator serving at the President's pleasure in many respects), the National Labor Relations Board and the Central Intelligence Agency.

Constitutional debates

The independence of such agencies has long been debated. In Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935) the Supreme Court upheld for-cause removal protections for multimember commissions; more recent decisions, including Seila Law v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2020), have curtailed protections for agencies headed by a single director, reflecting a broader contest over the "unitary executive" theory.
