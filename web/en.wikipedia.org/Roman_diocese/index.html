In the later Roman Empire, a diocese (Latin: dioecesis, from Greek διοίκησις, "administration") was a large territorial administrative unit grouping together several provinces, governed by a vicarius acting as deputy to a praetorian prefect. Created under the reforms of the emperor Diocletian at the end of the 3rd century AD, the dioceses formed the middle tier of the late Roman administrative hierarchy — above the provinces and below the praetorian prefectures. The ecclesiastical use of the word "diocese" for a bishop's territory derives directly from this late Roman civil geography.

Creation under Diocletian

Before Diocletian, the term had been used for smaller districts, notably the tax and assize districts of Asia Minor and, earlier still, for the financial administration inherited from the Hellenistic world. As part of his sweeping reorganisation of the empire (from AD 293), Diocletian roughly doubled the number of provinces by subdivision and grouped them into twelve dioceses: in the East, Oriens, Pontica, Asiana, Thracia, Moesia, and Pannonia; in the West, Britannia, Gallia, Viennensis, Italia, Hispania, and Africa. Each diocese was administered by a vicarius, who supervised the provincial governors, heard appeals, and oversaw taxation and the annona (grain supply), while military command remained with separate duces and comites under the emperor.

Constantine and the prefectures

Constantine and his successors grouped the dioceses into great praetorian prefectures — Gaul, Italy, Illyricum, and the East — whose prefects became the empire's senior civil officials. The number of dioceses later rose: Dacia and Macedonia were split from Moesia, Egypt was separated from Oriens, and Italy was divided into two vicariates (Italia Annonaria at Milan and Italia Suburbicaria at Rome). The Notitia Dignitatum (c. AD 400–420) lists about fifteen dioceses, and the system endured in the East into the 7th century, when the Byzantine theme system superseded it.

Ecclesiastical adoption

As the Church organised itself along civil lines, bishops' territories, metropolitan provinces, and patriarchates were mapped onto the Roman framework, and the word diocese passed permanently into church usage — long after the civil dioceses themselves disappeared. The correspondence was deliberate: the Council of Chalcedon (451) ruled that church organisation should follow the civil divisions of the empire.

List of dioceses (c. AD 400)

East: Asiana, Pontica, Oriens, Egypt, Thracia, Macedonia, Dacia. West: Pannonia, Italia Annonaria, Italia Suburbicaria, Africa, Hispania, Septem Provinciae (Viennensis), Gallia, Britannia. Capitals included Ephesus (Asiana), Alexandria (Egypt), Thessalonica (Macedonia), Milan (Italia Annonaria), Carthage (Africa), and Trier (Gallia).
