Gamma ray logging

FieldPetrophysics; well logging
MeasuresNatural gamma radiation of formations
UnitsAPI units (GAPI)
Main sourcesPotassium-40; uranium and thorium decay series
Primary useDistinguishing shale from sandstone/limestone; depth correlation

Gamma ray logging is a method of measuring naturally occurring gamma radiation to characterize the rock or sediment in a borehole. As a wireline logging method, it is used in mineral exploration, hydrocarbon exploration, and geotechnical work. The log is normally presented in API units on a scale from roughly 0 to 150, recorded against depth.

Principle

Most natural gamma radiation from rock comes from potassium-40 and from the uranium and thorium decay series. Shales contain abundant clay minerals rich in potassium and adsorb uranium and thorium, so they read "hot" (high API), while clean sandstones, limestones, and coal typically read low. A scintillation detector — classically a sodium iodide crystal — counts gamma events as the tool is drawn up the hole.

Applications

Because the gamma response so reliably flags shale, the log is the standard first-pass lithology indicator and is used to compute shale volume (Vshale), a key petrophysical parameter. Gamma logs can be run through casing, making them essential for depth correlation between wells and for positioning perforations. Spectral gamma logging separately measures potassium, uranium, and thorium contributions, aiding clay typing and fracture identification.
