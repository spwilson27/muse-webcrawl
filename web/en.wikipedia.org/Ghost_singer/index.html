Ghost singer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A ghost singer is a singer who records vocal performances that are presented to the public as the work of another person, usually an actor, a celebrity, or a more marketable performer. The practice is distinct from both ordinary session singing and playback singing in film industries where it is openly credited: ghost singing is deliberately uncredited, and the audience is intended to believe that the named star is singing.

Film and television

Ghost singing was widespread in classical Hollywood, when studios routinely dubbed the singing voices of stars whose own voices were considered inadequate. The most celebrated ghost singer was Marni Nixon, whose voice was heard coming from Deborah Kerr in The King and I (1956), Natalie Wood in West Side Story (1961), and Audrey Hepburn in My Fair Lady (1964) — work for which she initially received neither screen credit nor royalties, before eventually winning recognition and back payments. Other well-known cases include India Adams dubbing Joan Crawford and Cyd Charisse, and Bill Lee singing for John Kerr and Christopher Plummer (in The Sound of Music, 1965).

Popular music

In pop and dance music, ghost singers have supplied the actual vocals for manufactured acts. The most notorious case is Milli Vanilli, whose 1990 Grammy Award for Best New Artist was revoked when it emerged that Rob Pilatus and Fab Morvan had not sung on their records at all. Dance acts of the late 1980s and 1990s frequently paired uncredited studio vocalists — such as Martha Wash, whose voice powered hits by Black Box and C+C Music Factory — with models presented as the singers. Wash's lawsuits over credit and royalties helped change industry practice on vocal attribution.

Attitudes and exposure

As credits have become more transparent and recording technology has changed, undisclosed ghost singing has become less respectable but has not disappeared; it survives in some celebrity recordings, in voice-matched replacements within groups, and in controversies over uncredited vocals later revealed by litigation, memoirs, or the ghost singers' own advocacy. The term is also used more loosely for any vocalist who knowingly records "in the style of," or as a stand-in for, another artist.
