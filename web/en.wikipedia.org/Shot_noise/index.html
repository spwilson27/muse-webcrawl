Shot noise


Shot noise arises from the discrete nature of electric charge.

Shot noise or Poisson noise is a type of noise which can be modelled by a Poisson process. In electronics it arises from the discrete nature of electric charge: because current consists of individual electrons arriving at random, independent times, the current fluctuates even under perfectly steady conditions. The effect was first described in 1918 by Walter H. Schottky, who studied current fluctuations in vacuum tubes and coined the name from the German Schrot, likening the effect to the patter of small shot.

Properties

For a mean current I, the root-mean-square shot-noise current in a bandwidth Δf is given by Schottky's formula, in = √(2qIΔf), where q is the elementary charge. Shot noise is white (its power spectral density is independent of frequency) up to very high frequencies, and its magnitude grows only as the square root of the current, so the signal-to-noise ratio improves with increasing current. Correlations between charge carriers can suppress shot noise below the Poisson value, an effect measured by the Fano factor and used to probe fractional charge in the fractional quantum Hall effect.

Optical shot noise

The same statistics apply to photons: light arriving at a detector is a stream of discrete photons, so measurements of intensity fluctuate with Poisson statistics. Photon shot noise sets a fundamental limit on precision in photometry, optical communication, microscopy, and gravitational-wave detectors such as LIGO, where it dominates the noise at high frequencies and motivates the use of squeezed light to reduce it.

Occurrence

Shot noise is significant in semiconductor devices — diodes, transistors, and photodiodes — at low currents, and in low-light imaging, where the small number of photoelectrons per pixel makes Poisson fluctuations visible as grain. It is distinct from thermal (Johnson–Nyquist) noise, which exists even at zero net current, and from flicker (1/f) noise, which dominates at low frequencies.
