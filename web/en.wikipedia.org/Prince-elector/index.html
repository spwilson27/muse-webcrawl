Prince-elector

GermanKurfürst (pl. Kurfürsten)
FunctionElecting the Holy Roman Emperor
Fixed byGolden Bull of 1356
NumberSeven (1356); later eight, then nine, briefly ten

The prince-electors (German: Kurfürsten) were the members of the electoral college that elected the Holy Roman Emperor. From the thirteenth century a small group of the empire's most powerful princes monopolised this right, and their number and identity were definitively fixed by the Golden Bull of 1356 issued by Emperor Charles IV.

The seven electors

The Golden Bull named seven electors: three ecclesiastical — the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier — and four secular: the King of Bohemia, the Count Palatine of the Rhine, the Duke of Saxony, and the Margrave of Brandenburg. Each electorate carried a great office of the imperial court, such as arch-chancellor or arch-steward, and the electors enjoyed privileges including indivisibility of their territories and primogeniture in succession.

Later changes and abolition

During the Thirty Years' War the Palatine electorate was transferred to Bavaria (1623) and a new, eighth electorate was created for the restored Palatinate in 1648; a ninth was added for Hanover in 1692. The electoral college ceased to function with the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806.
