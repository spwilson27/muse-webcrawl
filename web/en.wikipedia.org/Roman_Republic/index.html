The Roman Republic (Latin: Res publica Romana) was the era of classical Roman civilization beginning with the overthrow of the Roman Kingdom (traditionally dated to 509 BC) and ending in 27 BC with the establishment of the Roman Empire following the War of Actium. During this period, Rome's control expanded from the city's immediate surroundings to hegemony over the entire Mediterranean world.

Roman society at the time was primarily a cultural mix of Latin and Etruscan societies, as well as of Sabine, Oscan, and Greek cultural elements. The Republic's government was headed by two consuls, elected annually by the citizens and advised by a senate composed of appointed magistrates. Over time, plebeians won political equality through the Conflict of the Orders, gaining their own tribunes and eventually access to all magistracies.

History

Early Republic (509–264 BC)

According to tradition, the last king, Tarquin the Proud, was expelled after the outrage against Lucretia, and annual magistrates replaced kingship. Rome gradually conquered the Italian peninsula, defeating the Samnites and, in the Pyrrhic War, the Greek cities of the south, becoming master of Italy south of the Rubicon by 275 BC.

Middle Republic (264–133 BC)

The Punic Wars against Carthage (264–146 BC) destroyed Rome's great rival and brought overseas provinces in Sicily, Spain, and Africa. Victory over the Hellenistic kingdoms in the Macedonian Wars made Rome dominant in the eastern Mediterranean as well. The year 146 BC saw the destruction of both Carthage and Corinth.

Late Republic and fall (133–27 BC)

Land inequality and political violence — beginning with the tribunes Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus — destabilized the system. The era saw Marius's military reforms, Sulla's dictatorship and proscriptions, the slave revolt of Spartacus, the First Triumvirate of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus, Caesar's conquest of Gaul and civil war, his assassination in 44 BC, and the Second Triumvirate. Octavian's victory over Mark Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BC left him sole ruler; in 27 BC he took the name Augustus, conventionally marking the start of the Empire.

Government

Polybius famously described the constitution as a mixture of monarchy (the consuls), aristocracy (the Senate), and democracy (the assemblies). Magistrates included quaestors, aediles, praetors, consuls, and censors; tribunes of the plebs could veto acts of the Senate and magistrates. In emergencies a dictator could be appointed for six months. Roman law and citizenship policy — extending citizenship in graded forms to conquered peoples — were central instruments of integration.
