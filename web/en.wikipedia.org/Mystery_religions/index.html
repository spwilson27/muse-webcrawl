Mystery religions

The sanctuary at Eleusis, home of the oldest Greek mysteries

Mystery religions, mystery cults, sacred mysteries or simply mysteries were religious schools of the Greco-Roman world in which participation was reserved to initiates (mystai). The main characterization of this religion is the secrecy associated with the particulars of the initiation and the ritual practice, which may not be revealed to outsiders. The most famous mysteries of Greco-Roman antiquity were the Eleusinian Mysteries, which predated the Greek Dark Ages.

The mystery schools flourished in late antiquity; among the most prominent were the cults of Isis, Mithras, Cybele and Attis, Dionysus, and the Great Gods of Samothrace. Some scholars have suggested that early Christianity shared features with the mystery religions — secret initiation (baptism), a communal sacred meal, and a saviour figure — though the extent of direct influence remains debated.

Definition and etymology

The term "mystery" derives from Latin mysterium, from Greek mysterion (usually as the plural mysteria Μυστήρια), in this context meaning "secret rite or doctrine". An individual who followed such a rite was a mystes, "one who has been initiated", from myein "to close, shut", a reference to secrecy (the initiates were sworn not to disclose what they had experienced). The term was projected back onto earlier history by later authors and scholars; in antiquity the schools were generally known by the names of their deities and sanctuaries.

General characteristics

Mystery religions formed one of three types of Hellenistic religion, the others being the imperial cult and the popular or civic religions, and philosophical schools. In a mystery religion the believer joined voluntarily, could hold membership alongside public cults, and expected personal salvation through initiation and secret knowledge. Many had an outer circle of attendants and inner circles of full initiates, with grades of initiation through which one could progress.

Individual mysteries

Eleusinian Mysteries

The Mysteries of Eleusis, centred on the goddesses Demeter and Persephone, were celebrated annually for almost two millennia. Initiates believed they would enjoy a better lot in the afterlife. The rites — things shown, things said, and things done — were never revealed in writing and remain largely unknown.

Mysteries of Isis

The Egyptian goddess Isis was worshipped throughout the Roman world. Her mysteries, described in fictional form in Apuleius's The Golden Ass, involved initiation through death-like experiences and rebirth into the goddess's protection.

Mithraic mysteries

Mithraism was a Roman mystery religion centred on the god Mithras, popular among soldiers from the 1st to the 4th century CE. Its worship took place in cave-like temples (mithraea), and initiates passed through seven grades.

Other cults

Others included the mysteries of Dionysus (Bacchic mysteries), the cult of Cybele and Attis with its March festival cycle, the Samothracian mysteries promising safety at sea, and in late antiquity various syncretic and Gnostic movements.

Decline

The mysteries were suppressed in the 4th and 5th centuries as the Christian emperors of Rome closed pagan sanctuaries; the sanctuary at Eleusis was destroyed in 396 CE during the invasion of Alaric. Knowledge of many rites vanished with their last initiates.
