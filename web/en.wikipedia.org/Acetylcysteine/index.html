Acetylcysteine

Other names: N-acetylcysteine, NAC
Routes: Oral, intravenous, inhaled
Uses: Paracetamol overdose; mucolytic
Legal status: Prescription and OTC (varies)

Acetylcysteine, also known as N-acetylcysteine (NAC), is a medication and dietary supplement derived from the amino acid cysteine. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines, principally as the specific antidote for paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose, and is also used as a mucolytic to loosen thick mucus in respiratory conditions.

Medical uses

Paracetamol overdose

In paracetamol poisoning, the liver's glutathione stores are depleted, allowing the toxic metabolite NAPQI to damage hepatocytes. Acetylcysteine acts as a precursor for glutathione synthesis, detoxifying NAPQI. Given intravenously or orally within about 8–10 hours of ingestion it can prevent serious liver injury almost entirely; it remains beneficial even later, and is standard treatment in established liver failure from overdose.

Mucolytic

Inhaled or orally administered acetylcysteine breaks disulfide bonds in mucus glycoproteins, reducing viscosity. It has been used in cystic fibrosis, chronic bronchitis, and COPD, though evidence for long-term benefit in chronic lung disease is mixed.

Other uses and research

Because it replenishes glutathione and modulates glutamate, NAC has been investigated in psychiatry (including schizophrenia, addiction, and obsessive-compulsive related disorders) and as an antioxidant supplement, though it is not approved for these indications. It is sometimes used to reduce the risk of contrast-associated kidney injury, with equivocal evidence.

Side effects

Common effects include nausea, vomiting, and a characteristic rotten-egg odour from its sulfur content. Intravenous use can cause anaphylactoid reactions with flushing and bronchospasm, usually managed by slowing the infusion.
