Charles II (29 May 1630 – 6 February 1685) was King of England, Scotland, and Ireland from 1660 until his death. Known as the "Merry Monarch," he restored the monarchy after the English Civil War and the Interregnum under Oliver Cromwell, presiding over a court famous for its wit, theatre, and scientific curiosity.

Exile and Restoration

After his father Charles I's execution in 1649, Charles was proclaimed king in Scotland and crowned at Scone in 1651, but his invasion of England ended in defeat at Worcester, from which he escaped to France after famously hiding in an oak tree. Following Cromwell's death and the collapse of the Commonwealth, General Monck engineered the Restoration; Charles issued the Declaration of Breda and was crowned at Westminster Abbey in April 1661.

Reign

Charles's reign saw the Great Plague of 1665 and the Great Fire of London in 1666, two Anglo-Dutch Wars, and fierce religious and political conflict: the Clarendon Code penalized nonconformists, the fictitious Popish Plot of 1678 sparked anti-Catholic hysteria, and the Exclusion Crisis attempted to bar his Catholic brother James from the succession. By the secret Treaty of Dover (1670) Charles took French subsidies from Louis XIV, partly to free himself from Parliament, which he dissolved altogether for his final years. He chartered the Royal Society, patronized Christopher Wren's rebuilding of London, and founded the Royal Hospital Chelsea and the Royal Observatory at Greenwich.

Personal life and death

His marriage to Catherine of Braganza produced no surviving children, but Charles acknowledged at least twelve illegitimate children by mistresses including Nell Gwyn and Barbara Villiers. On his deathbed he converted to Catholicism. He was succeeded by his brother, James II.
