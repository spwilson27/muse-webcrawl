Hydrophobe

In chemistry, a hydrophobe is a molecule or molecular fragment that is repelled by water — from the Greek hydro- (water) and phobos (fear). Hydrophobic substances are typically nonpolar and therefore mix poorly with water while dissolving readily in oils and other nonpolar solvents; oils, fats, alkanes, and fluorocarbons are classic examples. The opposite property is hydrophilicity ("water-loving").

The hydrophobic effect

The apparent repulsion between water and hydrophobes arises not from direct repulsion but from the hydrophobic effect: water molecules form ordered, hydrogen-bonded cages around nonpolar surfaces, incurring an entropic penalty, so the system minimizes energy by minimizing the exposed nonpolar area — driving oil droplets to coalesce and amphiphilic molecules to self-assemble into micelles and lipid bilayers. This effect is a principal force in protein folding, where hydrophobic amino acid side chains typically bury themselves in the protein's interior.

Measurement and surfaces

Wettability is quantified by the contact angle of a water droplet: surfaces with contact angles above 90° are hydrophobic, and those above about 150° are termed superhydrophobic — a state achieved by combining low surface energy with micro- and nano-scale roughness, as on the lotus leaf, whose self-cleaning behaviour is known as the lotus effect. Hydrophobic coatings and treatments are applied in textiles, anti-fouling and anti-icing surfaces, and chromatography, where hydrophobic interaction chromatography separates proteins by their surface hydrophobicity.
