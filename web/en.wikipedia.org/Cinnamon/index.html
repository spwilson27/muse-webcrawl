Cinnamon

Cinnamon is a spice obtained from the inner bark of trees in the genus Cinnamomum. It is used mainly as an aromatic condiment and flavouring in a wide variety of cuisines, sweet and savoury dishes, breakfast cereals, snacks, and traditional medicines. "True cinnamon" comes from Cinnamomum verum (Ceylon cinnamon), while much commercial cinnamon derives from related species collectively called cassia, including C. cassia (Chinese cinnamon) and C. burmannii.

Production

The bark is harvested by coppicing the trees: outer bark is stripped, the inner bark loosens and is rolled into long quills ("cinnamon sticks"), which are then dried. Ceylon cinnamon quills are thin, soft, and layered; cassia bark is thicker and harder, with a stronger, hotter flavour.

History

Cinnamon was imported into Egypt as early as 2000 BC and was so prized in the ancient world that its sources were kept secret by traders. It was among the spices that drove European exploration, and Ceylon's cinnamon coast was successively contested by the Portuguese, Dutch, and British. Today major producers include Sri Lanka, Indonesia, China, and Vietnam.

Flavour and safety

Cinnamon's flavour is due chiefly to the aromatic compound cinnamaldehyde. Cassia varieties contain significant coumarin, which can be harmful to the liver in large doses; food safety authorities have set tolerable daily intakes, making Ceylon cinnamon preferable for heavy consumption.
