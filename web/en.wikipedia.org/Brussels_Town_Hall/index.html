The Brussels Town Hall (French: Hôtel de Ville; Dutch: Stadhuis) is the central edifice on the Grand-Place/Grote Markt in Brussels, Belgium. It was built in several stages between 1402 and 1455 in the Brabantine Gothic style, and is the only remaining medieval building of the Grand-Place. The Town Hall is home to the municipal administration of the City of Brussels and remains a working civic building as well as a major tourist landmark.

The oldest part of the building, the east wing, was begun in 1402. The west wing followed from 1444, and the famous tower — designed by Jan van Ruysbroek — was completed by 1455. The 96-metre tower is topped by a gilded statue of Saint Michael, the patron saint of Brussels, slaying a dragon. A notable feature of the building is its slight asymmetry: the tower is not exactly in the middle, and the two wings differ in the number of their arches, a quirk around which local legends have grown.

History

In 1695, during the Nine Years' War, French artillery under Marshal de Villeroy bombarded Brussels, destroying most of the Grand-Place; the Town Hall's interior burned, though its masonry shell and tower survived. The building was subsequently restored and enlarged in the 18th century. In the 19th century, the facade was adorned with numerous statues of dukes, duchesses, and notable figures from Brussels' history.

Architecture

The building exemplifies Brabantine Gothic architecture, with an arcaded ground floor, tall windows, rich sculptural decoration, and an ornate tower with an openwork spire. Inside, notable rooms include the Council Chamber, the Aldermen's Room, and the Maximilian Room, decorated with tapestries and paintings relating to the history of Brussels and Brabant.
