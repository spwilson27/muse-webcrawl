Thoracic diaphragm

The thoracic diaphragm, or simply the diaphragm, is a sheet of internal skeletal muscle in humans and other mammals that extends across the bottom of the thoracic cavity. It separates the thoracic cavity, containing the heart and lungs, from the abdominal cavity and performs the most important work of breathing: as the diaphragm contracts, the volume of the thoracic cavity increases and air is drawn into the lungs.

Structure

The diaphragm is a double-domed sheet of muscle and fibrous tissue. Its peripheral part consists of muscular fibers that converge on a central tendon. It has three major openings: the caval opening for the inferior vena cava, the esophageal opening for the esophagus, and the aortic hiatus for the aorta, thoracic duct, and azygos vein. The diaphragm is innervated by the phrenic nerve, arising from cervical spinal roots C3, C4, and C5.

Function

Contraction of the diaphragm flattens its domes and increases the vertical diameter of the thorax, producing inhalation. Relaxation allows the elastic recoil of the lungs to drive exhalation. The diaphragm also helps increase intra-abdominal pressure during actions such as vomiting, defecation, and lifting heavy objects. Involuntary spasmodic contractions of the diaphragm produce hiccups.

Clinical significance

Diaphragmatic disorders include paralysis from phrenic nerve injury, hernias such as the congenital diaphragmatic hernia and hiatal hernia, and referred pain to the shoulder tip from irritation of the phrenic nerve's sensory distribution.
