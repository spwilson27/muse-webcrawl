Sephardic Jews

Sephardic Jews trace their origins to the Jewish communities of the Iberian Peninsula.

Sephardic Jews (Hebrew: סְפָרַדִּים, Sefaraddim), also known as Sephardi Jews or Sephardim, are a Jewish diaspora population associated with the Iberian Peninsula (Spain and Portugal). The term derives from Sepharad, a biblical location identified in medieval times with Spain. The Sephardic diaspora developed after the expulsion of Jews from Spain in 1492 under the Alhambra Decree and the forced conversions in Portugal in 1497.

History

Jewish communities existed in Iberia from Roman times. Under Muslim rule in al-Andalus, Sephardic culture flourished during a "Golden Age" that produced figures such as the philosopher and physician Maimonides, the poet Judah Halevi, and the statesman Hasdai ibn Shaprut. Jewish life in Christian Spain deteriorated in the later Middle Ages, culminating in the pogroms of 1391, waves of forced conversion that created the converso class, and the establishment of the Spanish Inquisition in 1478.

The Alhambra Decree of 31 March 1492 ordered practising Jews to leave Castile and Aragon or convert to Christianity. Tens of thousands fled to Portugal, North Africa, Italy, and the Ottoman Empire, whose Sultan Bayezid II welcomed them. Major Sephardic centres developed in Salonica, Constantinople, Safed, Jerusalem, Fez, and later in Amsterdam, London, Livorno, and the Americas.

Language

The traditional language of Sephardim is Ladino, also called Judaeo-Spanish, a Romance language derived from Old Spanish and written in Hebrew characters, which preserved many archaic features of 15th-century Castilian mixed with Hebrew, Turkish, Greek, and other local borrowings. After the Holocaust destroyed the great Ladino-speaking communities of the Balkans, the language became endangered, though it survives among elderly speakers in Israel and Turkey and in a modest cultural revival.

Religious customs

Sephardic religious practice follows the rulings codified by Joseph Karo in the Shulchan Aruch (16th century), though local customs (minhagim) vary between Moroccan, Yemenite-influenced, Balkan, and Western Sephardic communities. Distinctive features include liturgical melodies of Middle Eastern character, Passover customs permitting rice and legumes (kitniyot), and naming children after living grandparents.
