Michael Barnsley

Michael Fielding Barnsley (born 1946) is an English mathematician, researcher and entrepreneur who has worked on fractal compression; he holds several patents on the technology. He received his Ph.D. in theoretical chemistry from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1972 and joined the faculty of the Georgia Institute of Technology in 1979. He is best known for popularising the use of iterated function systems in image compression and for the fractal fern that bears his name.

Career

Barnsley earned his undergraduate degree in mathematics from the University of Oxford and his doctorate in theoretical chemistry from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1972. In 1979 he took up a position at the Georgia Institute of Technology, where he became a professor of mathematics. In 1987 he founded Iterated Systems Incorporated, a company devoted to commercialising fractal image compression, and he served as its lead scientist. The company's technology was used in products such as Microsoft's Encarta encyclopedia, which stored thousands of images using fractal compression.

He later joined the Mathematical Sciences Institute at the Australian National University, where he continued research on fractals, super-fractals and fractal transforms.

Fractal image compression

Barnsley's central insight was that natural images contain self-similar structure that can be exploited by iterated function systems (IFS). In the collage theorem, he showed that if an image can be approximated well by applying a set of contractive affine transformations to copies of itself, then the attractor of that IFS is close to the image. This gave a practical route to encoding images as small sets of transformation parameters rather than as pixel grids, achieving high compression ratios at the cost of slow encoding times.

Barnsley fern

The Barnsley fern is a fractal generated by an iterated function system that closely resembles the black spleenwort fern. It is produced by four affine transformations applied with specified probabilities, and became one of the most widely recognised examples of a mathematically generated plant form. Barnsley introduced it in his book Fractals Everywhere (1988), which also became a standard textbook on fractal geometry.
