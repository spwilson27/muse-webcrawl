Aperiodic tiling

An aperiodic tiling is a non-periodic tiling of the plane generated by an aperiodic set of prototiles: a set of shapes that admit tilings, but none of them periodic (invariant under a translation). Aperiodic tilings arise in the study of quasicrystals and mathematical logic.

History

In 1961 Hao Wang conjectured that if a set of tiles can tile the plane, it can do so periodically. His student Robert Berger disproved this in 1964, constructing an aperiodic set of over 20,000 tiles in work connected to the undecidability of the domino problem. Raphael Robinson reduced the set to six tiles in 1971, and Roger Penrose found sets of two tiles in the 1970s.

Penrose tiling

The best-known example is the Penrose tiling, using pairs of shapes such as "kites and darts" or thin and thick rhombs, often with matching rules. Penrose tilings have fivefold symmetry and local self-similarity, and their structure underlies the mathematics of quasicrystals, discovered in alloys in 1982.

Einstein problem

A long-standing question asked whether a single shape (an "einstein", from German ein Stein, one tile) could force aperiodicity. In 2023 David Smith and collaborators announced the "hat" polykite, a single tile that tiles only aperiodically, followed by the chiral "spectre" tile.
