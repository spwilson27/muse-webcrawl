Paramecium

Paramecium is a genus of single-celled ciliates, commonly studied as a representative of the ciliate group. Paramecia are widespread in freshwater, brackish, and marine environments and are often abundant in stagnant basins and ponds. Their slipper-like shape gives them the common name "slipper animalcules".

A paramecium's body, typically 50 to 350 micrometres long, is covered in thousands of hair-like cilia whose coordinated beating propels the cell through water and sweeps bacteria and other food particles into its oral groove. Food is enclosed in food vacuoles, and excess water is expelled by contractile vacuoles. Uniquely among eukaryotes, paramecia possess two kinds of nuclei: a large macronucleus that controls everyday functions and a small micronucleus used in reproduction.

Reproduction

Paramecia reproduce asexually by binary fission. Under suitable conditions they may also undergo conjugation, a sexual process in which two cells temporarily join and exchange micronuclear material, and autogamy, a form of self-fertilisation. The species Paramecium aurelia became famous in genetics through Tracy Sonneborn's studies of cytoplasmic inheritance and the "killer" trait conferred by bacterial endosymbionts.

Research

Because they are large, easy to culture, and have a complex cell organisation, paramecia have been model organisms for cell biology and genetics since the eighteenth century, when they were among the first microorganisms observed under early microscopes.
