Motor racing, also known as motorsport, is a competitive sport involving the racing of motor vehicles, primarily automobiles and motorcycles, against one another or the clock. Beginning in the 1890s almost as soon as the automobile existed, it has grown into a global industry and entertainment, encompassing purpose-built circuits, street courses, ovals, and off-road terrain, and ranging from amateur karting to the commercial summit of Formula One.

History

The first organised motoring competition is generally considered the 1894 Paris–Rouen trial organised by the newspaper Le Petit Journal; city-to-city races across France followed, culminating in events such as the Paris–Bordeaux–Paris and, after fatal accidents, the ill-fated 1903 Paris–Madrid race, which spurred the shift to closed circuits. The Gordon Bennett Cup, the Targa Florio (1906), the Indianapolis 500 (1911), the Monaco Grand Prix (1929), and the 24 Hours of Le Mans (1923) established the classic calendar of the sport. Grand Prix racing was unified in 1950 into the Formula One World Championship, which today is the most-watched motor racing series in the world.

Categories

  • Open-wheel (single-seater): Formula One, IndyCar, and feeder formulae, with exposed wheels and heavy aerodynamic downforce.
  • Sports car and endurance: prototypes and GT cars racing for up to 24 hours (Le Mans, Daytona, Spa), now headed by the Hypercar class of the World Endurance Championship.
  • Touring cars and stock cars: production-based saloons (BTCC, Supercars) and American stock car racing under NASCAR.
  • Rallying: point-to-point timed stages on public or closed roads, crowned by the World Rally Championship.
  • Drag racing: straight-line acceleration over a quarter-mile or 1,000 feet.
  • Motorcycle racing: MotoGP, Superbike, motocross, and road racing such as the Isle of Man TT.
  • Electric racing: Formula E and related series for battery-electric vehicles.

Organisation and safety

International car racing is governed by the Fédération Internationale de l'Automobile (FIA), founded in 1904, with national bodies running domestic series. A race weekend typically comprises practice, qualifying to set the grid, and the race itself, under a system of flags and penalties. Safety has been transformed since the deadly decades of the 1950s–1970s — the 1955 Le Mans disaster killed more than 80 spectators — through circuit design, deformable structures, the HANS device, and the halo cockpit protector, drastically reducing fatalities.
