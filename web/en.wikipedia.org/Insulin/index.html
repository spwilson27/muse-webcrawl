Insulin

Insulin (from Latin insula, "island") is a peptide hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans. It is the body's principal anabolic hormone: it regulates the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats and protein by promoting the absorption of glucose from the blood into liver, fat and skeletal muscle cells, and by stimulating the storage of glucose as glycogen and fat. When insulin is absent (type 1 diabetes) or tissues respond poorly to it (type 2 diabetes), blood glucose rises with serious consequences.

Structure and synthesis

Human insulin consists of 51 amino acids in two chains — the A chain (21 residues) and the B chain (30) — linked by two disulfide bonds. It is synthesized as preproinsulin, processed to proinsulin, and cleaved into insulin and C-peptide within secretory granules, where it is stored as zinc-coordinated hexamers. It was the first protein whose amino-acid sequence was fully determined (by Frederick Sanger, Nobel Prize 1958) and one of the first proteins produced by recombinant DNA technology (1978–1982).

Physiology

Rising blood glucose after a meal triggers insulin secretion. Insulin binds its receptor — a receptor tyrosine kinase — on target cells, moving GLUT4 glucose transporters to the cell surface in muscle and fat, activating glycogen synthesis in the liver, promoting lipogenesis, and inhibiting gluconeogenesis and lipolysis. Its counter-regulatory hormone is glucagon, which raises blood glucose between meals.

Discovery

Insulin was isolated at the University of Toronto in 1921 by Frederick Banting and Charles Best, working in J.J.R. Macleod's laboratory, with James Collip purifying the extract. In January 1922 the extract saved the life of 14-year-old Leonard Thompson, a dying diabetic patient — the first successful treatment of a disease that had been a death sentence. Banting and Macleod received the 1923 Nobel Prize; Banting famously shared his prize money with Best.

Medical use

Insulin therapy is essential for all people with type 1 diabetes and many with type 2. Modern preparations are engineered analogues with different time profiles: rapid-acting (aspart, lispro, glulisine), short-acting (regular), intermediate (NPH), and long-acting basal analogues (glargine, detemir, degludec). Delivery is by syringe, pen injector, pump, or increasingly automated "closed-loop" systems pairing a pump with a continuous glucose monitor. The main risks are hypoglycemia and weight gain. Insulin appears on the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines, though its price — especially in the United States — has been the subject of sustained public controversy.
