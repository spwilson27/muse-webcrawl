Ape

The chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes), along with the bonobo, is the closest living relative of humans.

Apes (superfamily Hominoidea) are a group of Old World simian primates native to Africa and Southeast Asia. They are distinguished from monkeys by, among other traits, the complete absence of a tail, a more mobile shoulder joint adapted for swinging and suspension, a larger brain relative to body size, and a Y-5 pattern on the lower molar teeth. Apes are divided into two families: the great apes (Hominidae) — orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, bonobos and humans — and the lesser apes (Hylobatidae), the gibbons and siamangs.

Characteristics

Apes range in size from the small gibbons of about 4 kg to male gorillas of 200 kg or more. Compared with monkeys, apes have broader chests, no external tail (the tail vertebrae are reduced to the coccyx), an appendix, and more complex social behaviour and cognition. Their diets are generally omnivorous with an emphasis on fruit; gorillas are largely herbivorous. Most apes are endangered owing to habitat loss, hunting and disease.

Evolution

The hominoid lineage diverged from the Old World monkeys roughly 25–30 million years ago. Gibbons branched off first, followed by orangutans, then gorillas, and finally the split between the human lineage (hominins) and the chimpanzee–bonobo lineage about 6–7 million years ago. Genetically, humans share about 98.8% of their DNA with chimpanzees and bonobos.

Classification

  • Hylobatidae — gibbons and siamangs (about 20 species).
  • Hominidae — orangutans (Pongo), gorillas (Gorilla), chimpanzees and bonobos (Pan), and humans (Homo).
