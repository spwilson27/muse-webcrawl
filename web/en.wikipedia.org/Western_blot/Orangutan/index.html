Orangutans are great apes native to the rainforests of Indonesia and Malaysia, now found only on the islands of Borneo and Sumatra. Three species are recognised: the Bornean orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus), the Sumatran orangutan (P. abelii) and the Tapanuli orangutan (P. tapanuliensis), described in 2017. Their name derives from the Malay words orang ("person") and hutan ("forest").

Description

Orangutans have long, shaggy reddish-brown hair, long powerful arms — with a span of up to 2.2 m — and short legs. Males weigh 50–100 kg and females 30–50 kg. Dominant adult males develop broad cheek pads ("flanges") and a throat sac used for long calls. They are the most arboreal of the great apes and among the most solitary, with mothers and dependent offspring forming the principal social unit; infants may nurse for six years or more, giving orangutans the longest interbirth interval of any land mammal.

Behaviour and intelligence

Orangutans are highly intelligent: they use tools, including leaves as gloves and umbrellas, build elaborate night nests, and show evidence of cultural traditions and foresight. Their diet is mainly fruit (especially figs and durians), supplemented by leaves, bark, insects and occasionally small vertebrates. They are important seed dispersers in their forest ecosystems.

Conservation

All three species are Critically Endangered. Deforestation for palm oil plantations and timber, fires, and hunting have reduced the Bornean population by more than half in recent decades; the Tapanuli orangutan numbers fewer than 800 individuals and is the most threatened great ape in the world.
