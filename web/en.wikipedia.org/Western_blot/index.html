Western blot

The western blot (also called immunoblotting) is a widely used analytical technique in molecular biology and immunology to detect specific proteins in a sample of tissue homogenate or extract. The name is a play on the Southern blot (for DNA, named after Edwin Southern), with related techniques named northern blot (RNA) and eastern blot by analogy.

Procedure

Proteins are first separated by size using gel electrophoresis, usually SDS-PAGE, which denatures them and gives them a uniform negative charge. The separated proteins are then transferred ("blotted") onto a membrane of nitrocellulose or PVDF. After blocking unoccupied sites on the membrane, it is incubated with a primary antibody specific to the target protein, then with a secondary antibody — linked to a reporter enzyme (such as horseradish peroxidase) or fluorophore — that binds the primary antibody. Substrate addition produces a chemiluminescent or colour signal at the position of the target, whose band size confirms the protein's molecular weight and whose intensity roughly reflects its amount.

Applications

Western blotting is used in research to confirm protein expression, size and post-translational modification, and in diagnostics — historically as a confirmatory test for HIV antibodies and for Lyme disease, and in testing for BSE ("mad cow disease"). Because detection depends on antibody specificity, results require careful controls; quantitative conclusions often need loading controls such as housekeeping proteins (β-actin, GAPDH) and normalisation. Related dot-blot and slot-blot formats skip the electrophoresis step when size information is not needed.

History

The technique was developed in 1979 in the laboratories of Harry Towbin (at the Friedrich Miescher Institute) and George Stark (at Stanford), and popularised by W. Neal Burnette, who coined the name in 1981. It remains a staple of cell and molecular biology laboratories worldwide.
