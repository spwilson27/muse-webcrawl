The chimpanzee (Pan troglodytes), also known as the common chimpanzee, is a species of great ape native to the forest and savannah of tropical Africa. It has four confirmed subspecies and a fifth proposed one. The chimpanzee and the closely related bonobo form the genus Pan; these two species are the closest living relatives of humans, sharing about 98.8% of their DNA.

Description

Chimpanzees are covered in coarse black hair, with a bare face, fingers, toes, palms and soles. Males weigh 40–70 kg and females 27–50 kg; standing height is roughly 100–170 cm. Their arms are longer than their legs, suiting both knuckle-walking on the ground and climbing in trees. Life expectancy in the wild is about 40 years, and longer in captivity.

Behaviour

Chimpanzees live in fission–fusion communities of 15 to 150 members, with complex hierarchies, alliances and social grooming. They are renowned for tool use — termite fishing with sticks, nut cracking with stones, and leaf sponges — first documented by Jane Goodall at Gombe Stream in the 1960s, and for cultural variation between communities. They hunt cooperatively, sometimes preying on red colobus monkeys, and conflicts between communities can be lethally violent. Their diet is mainly fruit, supplemented by leaves, seeds, insects and meat.

Conservation

The chimpanzee is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List. Populations, estimated at 170,000–300,000 in the wild, are threatened by habitat loss and fragmentation, poaching for bushmeat, and infectious diseases such as Ebola.
