Henry Hudson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henry Hudson (c. 1565 – disappeared 23 June 1611) was an English sea explorer and navigator of the early 17th century, best known for his exploration of present-day Canada and parts of the northeastern United States in search of a northern passage to Asia. The Hudson River, Hudson Strait, and Hudson Bay in North America are named after him.

Voyages

Little is known of Hudson's early life; he emerges in the records in 1607, when the Muscovy Company of London hired him to find a route to the Pacific across the North Pole. Sailing the Hopewell, he reached the east coast of Greenland and Spitsbergen before ice forced him back; a second attempt in 1608, aiming for a passage east of Novaya Zemlya, also failed.

In 1609 the Dutch East India Company employed him to seek a northeast passage. When ice again blocked that route, Hudson — contrary to his instructions — crossed the Atlantic and explored the American coast. In September 1609 he sailed the Halve Maen ("Half Moon") up the river that now bears his name as far as present-day Albany, establishing the Dutch claim to New Netherland. He then explored Delaware Bay and Chesapeake Bay before returning to England.

Final voyage and mutiny

In 1610, backed by English investors, Hudson sailed the Discovery through Hudson Strait into the vast bay he took to be the Pacific. After wintering trapped in ice in James Bay, where scurvy, cold, and dwindling rations embittered the crew, Hudson insisted on continuing the search when the ice broke in spring 1611. In June the crew mutinied, led by Henry Greene and Robert Juet; Hudson, his teenage son John, and seven loyal or sick men were set adrift in a small open boat (a shallop) and were never seen again. The surviving mutineers reached England, where — remarkably — they largely escaped punishment, and their reports guided later voyages, including the discovery of Hudson Bay's true geography by Thomas Button.

Legacy

Hudson's voyages laid foundations for Dutch colonisation of the Hudson valley and English claims in Canada. His fate — cast adrift by his own crew — became one of the enduring legends of the age of exploration, retold in literature and art, including John Collier's painting The Last Voyage of Henry Hudson (1881).
