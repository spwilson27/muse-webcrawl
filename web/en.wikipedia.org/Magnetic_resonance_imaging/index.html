Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a medical imaging technique used in radiology to form pictures of the anatomy and the physiological processes of the body. MRI scanners use strong magnetic fields, magnetic field gradients, and radio waves to generate images of the organs in the body. MRI does not involve X-rays or the use of ionizing radiation, which distinguishes it from CT and PET scans.

MRI is based on the physics of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR): hydrogen nuclei (protons), abundant in body water and fat, align with the scanner's powerful static field. Radiofrequency pulses tip this magnetization, and as the protons relax back — with time constants (T1 and T2) that differ by tissue — gradient fields encode their positions so that a computer can reconstruct cross-sectional images with excellent soft-tissue contrast. Contrast agents based on gadolinium can further highlight blood vessels and abnormal tissue.

History

The phenomenon of NMR was described by Isidor Rabi (1938) and demonstrated in bulk matter by Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell (1946), who shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics. In the 1970s Paul Lauterbur introduced spatial gradients to create images, and Peter Mansfield developed the mathematics and the fast echo-planar technique; Raymond Damadian built one of the first whole-body scanners and produced the first human scan in 1977. Lauterbur and Mansfield received the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Applications

MRI is the imaging method of choice for the brain and spinal cord, joints and soft tissues, the heart, and many abdominal and pelvic organs; specialised techniques include functional MRI (fMRI) for brain activity, diffusion-weighted imaging for acute stroke, MR angiography, and spectroscopy. Limitations include long scan times, sensitivity to motion, loud gradient noise, claustrophobia, and safety hazards from ferromagnetic objects and some implants drawn to the powerful magnet.
