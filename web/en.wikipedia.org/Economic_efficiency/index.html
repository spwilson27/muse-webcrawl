Economic efficiency

Economic efficiency describes how well an economy allocates scarce resources to satisfy people's wants. A situation is economically efficient when no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off (Pareto efficiency), or when resources produce the maximum possible value of output.

Economic efficiency
ConceptsPareto efficiency, allocative efficiency, productive efficiency, dynamic efficiency
MeasuresProduction–possibility frontier; cost per unit output
ObstaclesMarket failure, externalities, monopoly, information asymmetry

Types of efficiency

Productive efficiency means goods are produced at the lowest possible cost, on the production–possibility frontier. Allocative efficiency means resources go to the goods and services people value most, so that price equals marginal cost. Dynamic efficiency concerns innovation and optimal investment over time.

Pareto efficiency

Named for Vilfredo Pareto, a Pareto-efficient allocation cannot be changed to benefit one person without harming another. The first fundamental theorem of welfare economics states that competitive market equilibria are Pareto efficient under standard assumptions; critics note that efficiency says nothing about the fairness of distribution.

Efficiency and policy

Cost–benefit analysis applies a weaker criterion (Kaldor–Hicks efficiency): a policy is efficient if winners could in principle compensate losers. Deadweight loss — the efficiency cost of taxes, tariffs and monopoly pricing — is a central measure in public economics.
