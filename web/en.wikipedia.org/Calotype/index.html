Calotype

Calotype (Talbotype)
InventorWilliam Henry Fox Talbot
IntroducedAnnounced 1839; patented 1841
MediumSilver iodide on paper (negative); salted paper prints
Key advantageReproducible — many prints from one negative
Superseded byCollodion (wet plate) process, 1850s

The calotype, also called the Talbotype, was an early photographic process invented by William Henry Fox Talbot and introduced in 1839–1841. It produced a paper negative from which multiple positive prints could be made, establishing the negative–positive principle on which photography rested for more than a century.

Process

Talbot coated writing paper with silver nitrate and potassium iodide to form light-sensitive silver iodide, then sensitised it further with "gallo-nitrate of silver" immediately before exposure. After an exposure of a minute or more in the camera, the latent image was developed in the same solution, fixed (eventually with sodium thiosulfate, "hypo"), washed and dried. The resulting negative, its image softened by the paper fibres, was contact-printed onto a second sheet of salted paper in sunlight.

History

Talbot had experimented with "photogenic drawing" from 1834 and rushed to publish after hearing of Daguerre's daguerreotype in 1839. He patented the improved calotype in 1841 and enforced the patent in England, which slowed its adoption there, though it flourished in Scotland — where David Octavius Hill and Robert Adamson produced celebrated portraits — and among travelling photographers documenting the Mediterranean and Middle East. Talbot's The Pencil of Nature (1844–46), the first commercially published book illustrated with photographs, showcased the process. The calotype was largely displaced in the 1850s by Frederick Scott Archer's sharper wet-collodion glass negatives.
