Narratology is the study of narrative and narrative texts, including the structures, devices, and conventions by which stories are told, and the theoretical models used to describe them. The term was coined by Tzvetan Todorov in 1969. Narratology emerged from Russian Formalism and French structuralism, and it seeks general principles of narrative across media — novels, films, drama, oral storytelling, and increasingly video games and digital media.

Foundations

Russian Formalists distinguished fabula (the chronological sequence of events) from sjuzhet (the events as arranged and presented in the work). Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) reduced folktales to 31 narrative functions and seven character roles. Gérard Genette's Narrative Discourse (1972), a landmark of "classical" narratology, systematised the analysis of tense (order, duration, frequency), mood (distance and focalisation), and voice (narrator type and narrative levels: extradiegetic, intradiegetic, metadiegetic).

Developments

Seymour Chatman recast the distinction as story versus discourse; Mieke Bal integrated narratology with cultural analysis. Since the 1990s, "postclassical" narratology has expanded the field toward cognitive, rhetorical, feminist, transmedial, and unnatural-narrative approaches. Narratology remains a core discipline in literary studies and a foundation for narrative theory in film studies and game studies (where it contrasts with ludology).
