Quapaw language

Quapaw, or Arkansas, is a Siouan language of the Dhegiha branch, historically spoken by the Quapaw people in the area at the mouth of the Arkansas River and later in northeastern Oklahoma, where the tribe was removed in the 19th century. The language is closely related to Omaha–Ponca, Kansa and Osage, the other Dhegiha languages, and more distantly to the wider Siouan family.

History and status

Quapaw was documented by missionaries and linguists from the 19th century onward, and extensive fieldwork was carried out in the 20th century, notably by Robert L. Rankin, whose recordings and notes with the last fluent speakers form the principal basis of modern documentation. The last fully fluent first-language speaker died in the 1980s, and the language is classified as dormant or extinct as a native tongue, though heritage speakers and learners remain.

Revitalisation

The Quapaw Nation has undertaken language revitalisation work drawing on the archival record, including dictionaries, texts and audio recordings, and offers community classes and online learning materials. Because of the close relationship among the Dhegiha languages, materials in Omaha–Ponca, Kansa and Osage also support reconstruction and teaching.

Phonology and grammar

Quapaw has a consonant inventory typical of Dhegiha Siouan, including aspirated and ejective stops, and a system of five oral vowels with contrastive nasal vowels. The language is verb-final (subject–object–verb), marks participants on the verb with pronominal prefixes, and uses postpositions rather than prepositions. Like its relatives, it has a set of positional verbs used in describing the shape and posture of objects.
