Puʻukoholā Heiau National Historic Site

Location: Kawaihae, Hawaiʻi Island, Hawaii, United States
Built: 1790–1791
Administered by: National Park Service
Area: about 86 acres

Puʻukoholā Heiau National Historic Site is a United States National Historic Site on the island of Hawaiʻi, near Kawaihae. It preserves the ruins of the last major ancient Hawaiian temple, or heiau, built by Kamehameha I, and the site is associated with the unification of the Hawaiian Islands.

History

Following a prophecy by the kahuna Kapoukahi that he would conquer all the islands if he built a great temple to the war god Kūkāʻilimoku on Puʻukoholā ("Hill of the Whale"), Kamehameha ordered construction in 1790. Thousands of men formed a human chain some 25 miles long to pass water-worn stones hand to hand from Pololū Valley; no mortar was used. The massive stone platform, roughly 224 by 100 feet, was completed in the summer of 1791 and dedicated with the sacrifice of Kamehameha's rival Keōua. Within a few years, Kamehameha had united most of the archipelago under his rule.

The site

The park also includes the earlier Mailekini Heiau below the hill, the submerged Hale o Kapuni Heiau said to be dedicated to shark gods, the royal compound at Pelekane, and the homestead site of John Young, Kamehameha's British adviser. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1962 and authorised as a National Historic Site in 1972.
