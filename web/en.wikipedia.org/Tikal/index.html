Tikal (modern Mayan orthography: Tik'al) is the ruin of an ancient city which was likely called Yax Mutal, found in a rainforest in northern Guatemala, in the department of Petén. It is one of the largest archaeological sites and urban centers of the pre-Columbian Maya civilization. Tikal was one of the most powerful kingdoms of the ancient Maya, dominating much of the Maya region politically, economically, and militarily during the Classic period.

History

Occupied from the Middle Preclassic period, Tikal rose to prominence under dynasties interacting — and sometimes in conflict — with the great central Mexican city of Teotihuacan. In 562 CE Tikal was defeated by Calakmul and entered a long hiatus, but it revived under the king Jasaw Chan K'awiil I, whose victory over Calakmul in 695 restored Tikal's power. His funerary pyramid, Temple I (the Temple of the Great Jaguar), faces Temple II across the Great Plaza. Like other lowland Maya centers, Tikal declined in the 9th–10th centuries during the Classic Maya collapse.

The site

The mapped core covers some 16 square kilometres with thousands of structures: temple-pyramids (Temples I–VI, the tallest, Temple IV, reaching about 70 m), the North Acropolis, palace complexes such as the Central Acropolis, ball courts, causeways (sacbeob), and reservoirs. Tikal National Park protects the ruins within the Maya Biosphere Reserve and was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.
