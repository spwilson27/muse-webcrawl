The Liberty Bell, an enduring symbol of American independence.

The Liberty Bell, previously called the State House Bell or Old State House Bell, is an iconic symbol of American independence, located in Philadelphia. Commissioned in 1751 by the Pennsylvania Provincial Assembly for the Pennsylvania State House (now Independence Hall), it was cast by the Whitechapel Bell Foundry in London and cracked soon after its arrival; it was recast in 1753 by Philadelphia founders John Pass and John Stow, whose names appear on the bell.

History

The bell originally summoned lawmakers and alerted citizens to public meetings. It later became famous—though the story is probably apocryphal—for supposedly ringing on 8 July 1776 to announce the first public reading of the Declaration of Independence. In 1777, as British troops approached Philadelphia, the bell was hidden in Allentown to prevent it being melted down for munitions. Its distinctive large crack developed by the mid-19th century; the bell was last rung for George Washington's birthday in 1846, when the crack was judged to have made it unringable.

Symbolism

The name "Liberty Bell" was popularized by abolitionists in the 1830s, who adopted the bell and its biblical inscription as a symbol of the anti-slavery movement. It later travelled the country for expositions and patriotic gatherings and was embraced by suffragists and the civil rights movement. The bell now rests in the Liberty Bell Center in Independence National Historical Park, across from Independence Hall, and is owned by the City of Philadelphia.
