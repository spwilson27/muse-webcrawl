In particle physics, a fermion is a particle that follows Fermi–Dirac statistics and possesses half-integer spin. Fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle: no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. They contrast with bosons, which have integer spin and may share states.

Elementary fermions

The Standard Model contains twelve elementary fermions — the matter particles — divided into six quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom) and six leptons (electron, muon, tau, and their neutrinos), each with a corresponding antiparticle. All have spin 1/2. Unlike bosons, which mediate forces, elementary fermions constitute matter.

Composite fermions

Composite particles made of an odd number of fermions are themselves fermions: protons and neutrons (three quarks each), atomic nuclei with odd mass number such as helium-3, and atoms with an odd total of constituent fermions. Baryons are the best-known composite fermions.

Consequences of Fermi statistics

The exclusion principle explains the structure of the periodic table, the stability of matter, electron degeneracy pressure in white dwarfs, and neutron degeneracy pressure in neutron stars. At low temperatures, systems of fermions form a Fermi sea; in some materials, pairing of fermions produces superconductivity and superfluidity.
