Neon sign

A neon sign is an electric sign lit by long, luminous gas-discharge tubes that contain rarefied neon or other gases. Neon tubes glow orange-red when filled with neon; other colours are produced by other gases (such as argon, which gives lavender-blue), by coloured glass, or by phosphor coatings inside the tube. Neon signs are most commonly used for advertising storefronts, theatres, hotels, and bars, and became an iconic feature of 20th-century cityscapes, from Times Square to Las Vegas and Hong Kong.

History

The technology descends from the Geissler tube of the 1850s. The French engineer Georges Claude demonstrated the first neon lamp in Paris in 1910, exploiting the neon gas newly available from his air-liquefaction company. Neon signs spread to the United States in the 1920s — the first American neon sign is often said to have advertised a Packard car dealership in Los Angeles in 1923 — and proliferated through the interwar and post-war decades.

Manufacture

Sign tubes are hand-crafted by glassblowers who heat and bend glass tubing over patterns, then evacuate the tube, fill it with gas, and seal in electrodes. A high-voltage transformer (typically 2,000–15,000 volts) ionises the gas, producing the characteristic glow. Skilled neon bending remains a specialised craft.

Decline and revival

From the 1960s onward, fluorescent-lit plastic signs and later LED lighting displaced neon in much commercial signage because of lower cost and easier maintenance. Since the 2010s, genuine neon has enjoyed a revival as a retro design element, and LED "neon flex" products imitate its appearance. Historic signs are preserved by institutions such as the Neon Museum in Las Vegas.
