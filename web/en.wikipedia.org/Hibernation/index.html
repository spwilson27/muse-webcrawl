Hibernation

Groundhogs are true hibernators

Hibernation is a state of minimal activity and metabolic depression undergone by some animal species. Hibernation is a seasonal heterothermy characterised by low body temperature, slow breathing and heart rate, and low metabolic rate. It most commonly occurs during winter months to conserve energy when sufficient food is unavailable.

Physiology

During deep hibernation, an animal's body temperature may fall to near ambient — in the Arctic ground squirrel, even slightly below freezing — and heart rate can drop from hundreds of beats per minute to as few as two or three. To prepare, hibernators accumulate fat reserves, often in the form of brown adipose tissue, whose rapid oxidation also powers periodic arousals in which the animal briefly warms up. Animals wake from hibernation in spring, timed by circannual rhythms, temperature and photoperiod.

Hibernating animals

True hibernators include ground squirrels, marmots, groundhogs, dormice, hamsters, hedgehogs, bats, and some marsupials and birds; the common poorwill is the only bird known to hibernate for extended periods. Bears undergo a shallower form of winter dormancy — their body temperature falls only a few degrees — often described as torpor or "winter sleep", though many biologists classify them as hibernators. Related states include aestivation (summer dormancy in hot or dry conditions) and brumation in reptiles.

Human interest

Understanding hibernation has medical implications, from organ preservation and treatment of trauma to proposed "torpor" states for long-duration spaceflight, because hibernators avoid muscle and bone loss despite months of inactivity.
