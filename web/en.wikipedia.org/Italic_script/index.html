Italic script

Italic script, also known as chancery cursive, is a form of calligraphy and handwriting developed in Renaissance Italy. It is a slightly slanted, flowing cursive style — distinct from the italic type used in typography, though the latter was modelled on it — and remains a standard hand taught to calligraphers.

History

The script evolved from humanist minuscule in the circle of Niccolò Niccoli in early 15th-century Florence, whose rapid, slanted handwriting became the basis of the papal chancery's cursive (cancelleresca). It spread across Italy and then Europe as the standard cursive for correspondence and documents.

Characteristics

Italic is written with a broad-edged pen held at an angle, producing characteristic thick-and-thin strokes. Letters are narrow and elliptical, slant slightly forward, and are usually written with simple entry and exit strokes and minimal loops; ascenders and descenders are modest. Modern revivals for handwriting instruction emphasize its legibility and speed.

In typography

The first italic type, cut by Francesco Griffo for Aldus Manutius in Venice in 1500–1501, imitated this hand, compressing text to fit Aldus's portable octavo editions. True italics in modern fonts remain distinct from merely sloped ("oblique") roman letters.
