Hero's journey

Also called: The monomyth
Proposed by: Joseph Campbell (1949)
Source: The Hero with a Thousand Faces

The hero's journey, or the monomyth, is a common narrative pattern said to underlie many myths and stories from around the world. It was articulated by American mythologist Joseph Campbell in his 1949 book The Hero with a Thousand Faces. In Campbell's formulation, a hero ventures from the ordinary world into a region of supernatural wonder, wins a decisive victory in confrontation with fabulous forces, and returns home empowered to bestow boons on others.

Structure

Campbell divided the journey into three acts — Departure, Initiation, and Return — comprising seventeen stages, including the Call to Adventure, Refusal of the Call, Supernatural Aid, Crossing the First Threshold, the Belly of the Whale, the Road of Trials, Atonement with the Father, Apotheosis, the Ultimate Boon, the Magic Flight, and Mastery of the Two Worlds. Not every story contains every stage, and Campbell acknowledged wide variation.

Influence

The monomyth strongly influenced modern storytelling. George Lucas credited Campbell's work as an inspiration for Star Wars. Screenwriting teacher Christopher Vogler condensed the model into twelve stages in The Writer's Journey (1992), which became a standard reference in Hollywood. Writers such as Neil Gaiman and filmmakers across the world have used or reacted against the template.

Criticism

Folklorists and myth scholars have criticised the monomyth for cherry-picking examples, flattening cultural differences, and treating a mutable pattern as universal. Some note that Campbell's scheme privileges male heroes and individualistic quests, and that many traditions emphasise communal, cyclical, or trickster-centred stories that fit poorly. Nevertheless, the hero's journey remains a popular analytical and creative tool.
