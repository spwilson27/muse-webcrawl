Opposition to the Vietnam War

Opposition to the Vietnam War refers to the broad social and political movement in the United States and other countries that opposed American involvement in the Vietnam War. Beginning in the early 1960s and intensifying after the escalation of 1965, the antiwar movement grew into one of the largest protest movements of the twentieth century, drawing together students, civil rights activists, clergy, veterans, and ordinary citizens.


Antiwar march on Washington, 1967

History

Early opposition came largely from pacifist organizations, religious groups, and the emerging student movement. The Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) organized the first national march on Washington in April 1965, drawing some 20,000 demonstrators. Teach-ins at universities, draft-card burnings, and the growth of resistance to conscription made the war a central domestic issue.

The Tet Offensive of January 1968 shattered public confidence in official claims of progress and marked a turning point in American opinion. Later that year, violent clashes between police and demonstrators outside the Democratic National Convention in Chicago were broadcast nationally. In October 1969 the Moratorium to End the War drew millions of participants in coordinated protests across the country, and the November Mobilization brought roughly half a million people to Washington.

Key events

  • 1965: First national antiwar march on Washington organized by SDS.
  • 1967: March on the Pentagon; Martin Luther King Jr. publicly denounces the war.
  • 1968: Tet Offensive; protests at the Democratic National Convention in Chicago.
  • 1969: Moratorium and Mobilization marches, among the largest protests in U.S. history.
  • 1970: Invasion of Cambodia sparks campus strikes; four students killed at Kent State University by Ohio National Guardsmen.
  • 1971: Publication of the Pentagon Papers; Vietnam Veterans Against the War stage Dewey Canyon III in Washington.

Impact

The movement is generally credited with constraining the Johnson and Nixon administrations, hastening the policy of Vietnamization, and contributing to the end of the draft in 1973. It reshaped American politics, deepened generational and cultural divisions, and left a lasting legacy in debates over the war powers of presidents and the role of protest in a democracy.
