Cyclic AMP

Cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP, cyclic AMP, or 3′,5′-cyclic adenosine monophosphate) is a second messenger important in many biological processes. It is a derivative of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and is used for intracellular signal transduction in many organisms, conveying the effect of hormones such as adrenaline and glucagon that cannot pass through the cell membrane.

Synthesis and breakdown

cAMP is synthesised from ATP by the enzyme adenylyl cyclase, located at the cell membrane, which is activated or inhibited by G proteins coupled to membrane receptors. It is degraded by phosphodiesterases into AMP. Caffeine and several drugs act by inhibiting phosphodiesterases, thereby prolonging cAMP signals.

Function

In eukaryotes, cAMP works mainly by activating protein kinase A, which phosphorylates target proteins and regulates metabolism, gene expression, and ion channels. It also activates exchange proteins (Epac) and cyclic nucleotide-gated channels, important in smell and vision. In bacteria, cAMP binds the catabolite activator protein to control genes for using alternative sugars, as in the lac operon of E. coli. In slime moulds, cAMP acts outside the cell as a chemoattractant signal.

History

Earl Sutherland discovered cAMP while studying how adrenaline stimulates glucose release in the liver, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1971. Its discovery established the general concept of second messengers in cell signalling.
