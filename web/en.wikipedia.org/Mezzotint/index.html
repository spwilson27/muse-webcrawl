Mezzotint

Mezzotint is a printmaking process of the intaglio family, used to produce prints with soft gradations of tone. It was invented in the 17th century — Ludwig von Siegen produced the first known mezzotint in 1642 — and became highly popular in 18th-century England for reproducing portraits and paintings, being prized for its rich, velvety blacks.

Technique

The artist roughens the entire surface of a copper or steel plate with a tool called a rocker, raising a uniform burr that, if inked, would print solid black. The image is then created by smoothing and burnishing areas of the plate with a scraper: the smoother the surface, the less ink it holds and the lighter the printed tone. Mezzotint thus works from dark to light, unlike most intaglio methods.

History and use

Mezzotint became the standard method for reproducing oil paintings before photography, with English practitioners such as John Raphael Smith producing celebrated portrait plates. Its main drawback is that the delicate burr wears quickly, limiting edition sizes. The technique survives in contemporary printmaking as a specialised art form.
