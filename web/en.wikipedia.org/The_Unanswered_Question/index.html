The Unanswered Question is a musical work by American composer Charles Ives, composed primarily in 1908 and revised in the 1930s. Written for trumpet, four flutes (or pairs of flutes, oboes and clarinets), and strings, it is one of the earliest examples of collage and layered, independent musical planes in modernist music. Along with Central Park in the Dark, it forms the set Two Contemplations. Leonard Bernstein took his title for his famous 1973 Norton Lectures at Harvard from Ives's piece.

Structure and meaning

Ives assigns symbolic roles to the three layers. The strings, playing very quietly throughout and often placed offstage, represent "The Silence of the Druids — who Know, See and Hear Nothing," a slow, serene, consonant chorale in G major. Against this, a solo trumpet intones "The Perennial Question of Existence" — a short atonal phrase — seven times. The flutes, standing for "Fighting Answerers," attempt to answer, becoming more agitated, dissonant, and frantic, until they give up in a "secret conference" of mocking dissonance. The trumpet asks the question one final time; only the silence of the strings remains. The question is never answered.

Performance history

The work was not performed until 1946, nearly four decades after its composition, when it was premiered in New York by students of the Juilliard School conducted by Edgar Schenkman. It is now among Ives's best-known works and is often programmed as a meditation on the limits of human knowledge.
