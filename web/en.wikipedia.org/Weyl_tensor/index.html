In differential geometry, the Weyl curvature tensor, named after Hermann Weyl, is a measure of the curvature of spacetime or, more generally, of a pseudo-Riemannian manifold. Like the Riemann curvature tensor, the Weyl tensor expresses the tidal force that a body feels when moving along a geodesic. The Weyl tensor differs from the Riemann curvature tensor in that it does not convey information on how the volume of the body changes, but rather only how the shape of the body is distorted by the tidal force.

Definition

The Weyl tensor C is obtained from the Riemann tensor R by subtracting all of its traces, built from the Ricci tensor and scalar curvature. In n ≥ 4 dimensions, schematically:

C = R − (terms in the Ricci tensor) − (terms in the scalar curvature)

with coefficients 1/(n−2) and 1/((n−1)(n−2)) respectively. It shares the algebraic symmetries of the Riemann tensor (antisymmetry in each index pair, symmetry under exchange of pairs, and the first Bianchi identity) but is completely trace-free: every contraction of the Weyl tensor vanishes.

Properties

The Weyl tensor vanishes identically in dimensions two and three, where curvature is entirely determined by the Ricci tensor. In four dimensions it has ten independent components. It is conformally invariant: rescaling the metric by a smooth positive function leaves the Weyl tensor (with one index raised) unchanged, and a manifold is locally conformally flat if and only if its Weyl tensor vanishes (in dimension ≥ 4).

Role in general relativity

In general relativity the Riemann tensor splits into the Ricci part, determined locally by matter through the Einstein field equations, and the Weyl part, which describes the free gravitational field — curvature that exists in vacuum, such as that outside a star or in a gravitational wave. In vacuum the Ricci tensor vanishes and the Weyl tensor equals the Riemann tensor. Roger Penrose's Weyl curvature hypothesis proposes that the Weyl curvature was very small near the Big Bang, offering an explanation of the arrow of time.

Electric and magnetic parts

Relative to an observer, the Weyl tensor decomposes into an "electric" part (responsible for tidal forces) and a "magnetic" part (responsible for frame-dragging effects). This decomposition underlies the Petrov classification of spacetimes by the algebraic structure of the Weyl tensor.
