Savings and loan association

A savings and loan association (S&L), or thrift institution, is a financial institution that specializes in accepting savings deposits and making mortgage and other loans. The terms "S&L" or "thrift" are mainly used in the United States; similar institutions in the United Kingdom, Ireland, and some Commonwealth countries include building societies and trustee savings banks. They were often legally differentiated from commercial banks in that they could have different regulatory structures, although in most modern contexts the distinction has largely disappeared.

History

The first American savings and loan was the Oxford Provident Building Association, founded in Frankford, Pennsylvania, in 1831, modelled on British building societies: members pooled savings and took turns using the fund to build or buy homes. During the Great Depression the federal government built a regulatory framework around the industry—the Federal Home Loan Bank system (1932), federal charters (1933), and federal deposit insurance through the FSLIC (1934)—to channel credit into housing. For decades S&Ls operated under interest-rate ceilings (Regulation Q) and a simple formula remembered as "3-6-3": pay 3% on deposits, lend at 6%, and be on the golf course by 3.

Savings and loan crisis

Rising interest rates in the late 1970s left thrifts paying more for deposits than their long-term fixed-rate mortgages earned. Deregulation in 1980–82 let S&Ls offer adjustable rates and invest in commercial real estate and junk bonds, but it also enabled speculative lending, fraud, and catastrophic losses. Roughly a third of the nation's 3,234 savings and loans failed between 1986 and 1995 in the savings and loan crisis, costing taxpayers an estimated $124 billion. Congress responded with the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement Act of 1989 (FIRREA), which abolished the FSLIC and the Federal Home Loan Bank Board, created the Resolution Trust Corporation to dispose of failed thrifts' assets, and placed S&L supervision with the new Office of Thrift Supervision.

Modern thrifts

The number of S&Ls has fallen from over 4,000 in the 1960s to a few hundred today, through failures, mergers, and conversions to bank charters. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 abolished the Office of Thrift Supervision and folded its functions into the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the FDIC, and the Federal Reserve. Surviving thrifts remain required to keep a majority of assets in housing-related lending under the Qualified Thrift Lender test.
