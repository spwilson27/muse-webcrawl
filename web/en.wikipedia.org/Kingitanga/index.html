The Kīngitanga, or Māori King movement, is a movement that arose among some Māori iwi (tribes) of New Zealand in the 1850s to establish a monarchy able to deal with the New Zealand government on equal footing, halt land sales, and unite Māori in the face of colonization.

History

In 1858 the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero was installed as the first Māori King at Ngāruawāhia. His successor Tāwhiao led the movement through the Waikato War of 1863–1864 and decades of exile in the King Country after the Crown's invasion and confiscation of Waikato lands. Later monarchs included Mahuta, who entered colonial politics, and Te Rata, and the movement's influence was consolidated by Princess Te Puea Hērangi, who established Tūrangawaewae Marae as its home.

Modern era

Te Arikinui Dame Te Atairangikaahu reigned for forty years (1966–2006), the longest-serving Māori monarch, succeeded by her son Tūheitia Paki. After Tūheitia's death in 2024, his daughter Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō was selected as the eighth Māori monarch. The Kīngitanga has no constitutional role but holds major cultural and political significance, and the annual Koroneihana celebrations draw leaders from across New Zealand and the Pacific.
