A pre-main-sequence star (also known as a PMS star) is a star in the stage of stellar evolution between being a protostar and arriving on the main sequence. Such a star shines mainly by gravitational contraction as it slowly shrinks toward hydrostatic equilibrium, heating its core until sustained hydrogen fusion begins.

Evolution

After a protostar clears its natal envelope and becomes optically visible — the so-called "stellar birthline" — it contracts along the Hayashi track, staying nearly fully convective at roughly constant surface temperature while its luminosity decreases. Intermediate-mass stars then follow the Henyey track, developing a radiative core and moving to higher temperatures before reaching the main sequence. The duration of the phase depends strongly on mass: stars of several solar masses complete it in well under a million years, a Sun-like star takes tens of millions of years, and the lowest-mass stars can take hundreds of millions of years. During contraction, lithium is progressively destroyed in the interior, so lithium abundance can be used to estimate the ages of young stars.

Types

Low-mass PMS stars (below about 2 solar masses) are classified as T Tauri stars, subdivided into classical T Tauri stars, which are still accreting from a disk, and weak-lined T Tauri stars, whose disks have largely dissipated. Intermediate-mass PMS stars (about 2–8 solar masses) are called Herbig Ae/Be stars. Stars above roughly 8 solar masses have essentially no observable PMS phase: they begin hydrogen fusion while still deeply embedded in their birth clouds.

Observational properties

Pre-main-sequence stars are found in star-forming regions and young clusters such as the Orion Nebula and Taurus-Auriga. They commonly show infrared excess from circumstellar disks, strong and variable emission lines (notably H-alpha), jets and outflows, X-ray activity from magnetic flares, and irregular brightness variations caused by starspots and variable accretion. Their disks are the birthplaces of planets.
