The Battle of Flodden, fought on 9 September 1513 near Branxton in Northumberland, was the largest battle ever fought between England and Scotland by number of combatants. It ended in a decisive English victory and the death of King James IV of Scotland — the last British monarch to die in battle — along with much of the Scottish nobility.

Background

Henry VIII's invasion of France in 1513, as part of the War of the League of Cambrai, drew Scotland into the war under the Auld Alliance with France. James IV led a large army — perhaps 34,000 men, with heavy French-supplied artillery and long pikes — across the Tweed into England, taking Norham Castle. The English army of about 26,000, commanded by Thomas Howard, Earl of Surrey, marched north to meet him while Henry campaigned in France.

The battle

Surrey outmanoeuvred the strong Scottish position on Flodden Edge by crossing the River Till at Twizel Bridge and forcing James to turn and fight at Branxton Hill. In wet conditions the Scottish pike formations lost cohesion descending the slope toward a boggy valley; English billmen proved more effective in the close fighting, and English archers and artillery disrupted the Scottish divisions. James IV was killed close to Surrey's own position, and Scottish losses are estimated at 5,000–10,000 men, including the king, an archbishop, and a generation of nobles — commemorated in the lament "The Flowers of the Forest". English losses were far lighter, around 1,500.

Aftermath

The defeat left Scotland under the regency of Margaret Tudor for the infant James V and ended major Scottish intervention in Henry VIII's French war. The battle remains a central event in Scottish national memory.
