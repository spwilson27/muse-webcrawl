Ophthalmoplegia

Ophthalmoplegia (also called ophthalmoparesis in milder cases) is the paralysis or weakness of the eye muscles, restricting movement of one or both eyes. It may involve the external muscles that move the eyeball (external ophthalmoplegia) or the internal muscles controlling the pupil and lens accommodation (internal ophthalmoplegia), or both. Affected individuals typically present with double vision (diplopia), drooping eyelid (ptosis) and misalignment of the eyes (strabismus).

Causes

The extraocular muscles are supplied by the oculomotor (III), trochlear (IV) and abducens (VI) cranial nerves, so ophthalmoplegia can result from damage anywhere along those pathways: the muscle itself, the neuromuscular junction, the peripheral nerves, the brainstem nuclei or the supranuclear gaze centres. Causes include diabetes and other microvascular disease, thyroid eye disease, myasthenia gravis, mitochondrial disorders such as chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia (CPEO), multiple sclerosis, tumours, aneurysms, stroke and Miller Fisher syndrome, a variant of Guillain–Barré syndrome. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia results from a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus and is a classic sign of multiple sclerosis in young patients.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by neuro-ophthalmological examination of eye movements, imaging (MRI or CT), blood tests and, where indicated, antibody and genetic testing. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause — for example immunotherapy in myasthenia or Miller Fisher syndrome, control of diabetes, or neurosurgical treatment of aneurysm — while prisms, eye patching or strabismus surgery can relieve diplopia. Many vascular cases recover spontaneously over weeks to months.
