World map

World map
SubjectThe entire surface of the Earth
Common projectionsMercator, Robinson, Winkel Tripel, Mollweide

A world map is a map of most or all of the surface of the Earth. Because the Earth is spherical and a map is flat, every world map relies on a map projection, which inevitably distorts area, shape, distance or direction to some degree.

History

The earliest known world maps date to ancient Babylonian clay tablets of the 6th century BCE. Greek geographers such as Anaximander and Ptolemy produced increasingly systematic world maps, and Ptolemy's Geographia (2nd century CE) set out coordinates for thousands of places. In the medieval period, schematic T-and-O maps coexisted with sophisticated Islamic and Chinese cartography. The Age of Exploration transformed world maps, and Martin Behaim's globe of 1492 is the oldest surviving terrestrial globe.

Projections

The Mercator projection (1569), designed for navigation, preserves angles but greatly inflates high-latitude areas, making Greenland appear comparable to Africa. Twentieth-century alternatives include the Robinson projection and the Winkel Tripel projection, adopted by the National Geographic Society in 1998, which balance area and shape distortion. Equal-area projections such as the Gall–Peters and Mollweide preserve relative sizes of continents.

Uses

World maps may be political (showing countries and borders), physical (showing terrain and oceans) or thematic (showing climate, population, languages and other data). In the digital era, interactive world maps and virtual globes have largely superseded printed wall maps for reference use.
