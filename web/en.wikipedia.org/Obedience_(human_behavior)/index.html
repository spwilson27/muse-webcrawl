Obedience (human behavior)

Obedience, in human behaviour, is a form of social influence in which a person yields to explicit instructions or orders from an authority figure. It differs from compliance, which involves requests from peers, and from conformity, which involves matching group behaviour. Obedience is studied by social psychology principally because of its role in atrocities committed under orders.

Milgram experiment

The most famous research is Stanley Milgram's 1961–63 experiments at Yale, in which participants were instructed by an experimenter to deliver what they believed were increasingly severe electric shocks to a "learner". In the best-known condition, 65% of participants continued to the maximum 450 volts despite the learner's protests, a result Milgram interpreted through an "agentic state" theory: people transfer responsibility for their actions to the authority. The studies provoked lasting ethical controversy as well as debate over interpretation; partial replications, such as Jerry Burger's in 2009, found comparable levels of obedience.

Related research

The Stanford prison experiment (1971) is frequently cited alongside Milgram's work, though its methods and conclusions are heavily contested. Cross-cultural replications show high obedience rates in many countries, with moderating factors including the proximity of the victim, the proximity and legitimacy of the authority, and the presence of dissenting peers, which sharply reduces compliance.

Applications

Understanding obedience informs military and legal doctrines on unlawful orders — "just following orders" (the Nuremberg defence) is generally not accepted as an excuse — and training in medicine, aviation, and other fields where challenging authority can prevent disaster.
