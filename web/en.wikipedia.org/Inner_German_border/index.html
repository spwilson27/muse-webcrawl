The inner German border (German: innerdeutsche Grenze) was the border between the German Democratic Republic (East Germany) and the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) from 1949 to 1990. Running 1,393 kilometres from the Baltic Sea to Czechoslovakia, it was one of the most heavily fortified frontiers in the world and a defining symbol of the Iron Curtain and the Cold War. It is distinct from the Berlin Wall, which surrounded West Berlin.

Development

The border originated in the occupation zones established after the Second World War. East Germany began fortifying it in May 1952, creating a ploughed strip, fences, watchtowers, and a restricted zone. Over the following decades the fortifications were upgraded with anti-vehicle ditches, signal fences, minefields (later removed), and automatic firing devices.

Crossings and escapes

Legal crossings were limited to a small number of rail, road, and river checkpoints. Many East Germans attempted to escape across the border; hundreds died in the attempt, shot by border guards or killed by mines and other devices. The East German border troops operated under orders that permitted lethal force against escapees, a policy that became the subject of prosecutions after reunification.

Fall and legacy

When Hungary opened its border with Austria in 1989 and mass protests swept East Germany, the border became permeable; on 9 November 1989 the Berlin Wall fell and crossings along the inner German border opened soon after. The fortifications were largely dismantled by German reunification on 3 October 1990. Sections survive as memorials, and the former death strip is now part of the European Green Belt nature conservation project.
