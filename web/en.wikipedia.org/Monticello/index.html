Monticello

Monticello was the primary plantation of Thomas Jefferson, the third president of the United States, who began designing Monticello after inheriting land from his father at age 14. Located just outside Charlottesville, Virginia, in the Piedmont region, the plantation was originally 5,000 acres (20 km²), with extensive cultivation of tobacco and mixed crops by enslaved African Americans.

Architecture

Jefferson designed the main house in the neoclassical style, drawing on Andrea Palladio's work and on buildings he observed as ambassador to France. Construction began in 1768 and, after a major redesign and rebuilding between 1796 and 1809, produced the domed house familiar today — the first dome on an American house. The estate's image appears on the reverse of the United States nickel.

The west front of Monticello, Jefferson's self-designed home.

Plantation and slavery

Monticello's operations depended on enslaved labour; Jefferson enslaved more than 600 people over his lifetime, about 400 of them at Monticello. Mulberry Row, near the house, was the centre of the plantation's workshops and slave dwellings. Sally Hemings, an enslaved woman at Monticello, bore children fathered by Jefferson, a conclusion supported by DNA evidence and now presented in the site's interpretation.

After Jefferson

After Jefferson's death in 1826, the estate passed through several owners, including the Levy family, who preserved it for much of the 19th and early 20th centuries. The Thomas Jefferson Foundation purchased it in 1923 and operates it as a museum and educational institution. Together with the University of Virginia, Monticello was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.
