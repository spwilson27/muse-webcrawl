Just-in-time manufacturing

Just-in-time (JIT)
Origin: Toyota Production System, Japan
Key figures: Taiichi Ohno, Eiji Toyoda
Tools: Kanban, pull production, takt time

Just-in-time (JIT) manufacturing is a production methodology aimed at reducing flow times and inventory by producing and delivering goods only as they are needed in the production process. Developed within the Toyota Production System in postwar Japan, principally by engineer Taiichi Ohno, JIT became a cornerstone of lean manufacturing worldwide.

Principles

Under JIT, each workstation produces only what the next step has "pulled" from it, signaled traditionally by kanban cards that authorize the manufacture or movement of a fixed quantity of parts. Production is leveled to a steady rate (heijunka) matched to customer demand, known as takt time. Because inventories are kept deliberately minimal, defects and machine failures surface immediately rather than being hidden by stockpiles, forcing continuous improvement (kaizen) and close coordination with suppliers, who deliver small lots frequently.

Advantages and criticism

Benefits include lower warehousing costs, reduced waste, faster detection of quality problems, and shorter lead times. However, JIT systems hold little safety stock and are therefore vulnerable to supply disruptions; the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami and the COVID-19 pandemic exposed the fragility of long, tightly coupled supply chains, leading some firms to blend JIT with "just-in-case" stockpiling for critical components.
