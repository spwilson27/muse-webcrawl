Paradiso (Italian for "Paradise") is the third and final part of Dante Alighieri's epic poem the Divine Comedy, following the Inferno and the Purgatorio. It is an allegory telling of Dante's journey through Paradise, guided by Beatrice, who symbolises theology. In the poem, Paradise is depicted as a series of concentric spheres surrounding the Earth, corresponding to the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the Fixed Stars and the Primum Mobile, beyond which lies the timeless Empyrean, the abode of God.

Structure

Like the other two cantiche, the Paradiso contains 33 cantos (plus the introductory canto counted within the Inferno's total), written in terza rima. Dante ascends on Easter week of 1300, moving from sphere to sphere, meeting blessed souls and discussing theology, philosophy, astronomy and the corruption of the Church with figures including Thomas Aquinas, Saint Bonaventure, the Emperor Justinian, Cacciaguida (Dante's crusader ancestor) and Saint Peter, who examines Dante on faith.

The spheres

Each sphere houses souls distinguished by a dominant virtue — or, in the lower heavens, by a virtue once imperfectly practised: the inconstant in the Moon, the ambitious in Mercury, lovers in Venus, teachers of wisdom in the Sun, warriors of the faith in Mars, just rulers in Jupiter, contemplatives in Saturn, and the triumphant host of the Church in the Fixed Stars. From the Primum Mobile Dante looks back on the universe, before Beatrice leads him into the Empyrean, where he glimpses the Celestial Rose of the blessed and, in a final flash of insight, the vision of God — "the Love that moves the sun and the other stars".

Themes

The Paradiso is the most theologically dense part of the poem, grappling with free will, predestination, the justice of divine judgement and the limits of human language before the ineffable. Dante repeatedly stresses that the graduated heavens are a condescension to human understanding: all the blessed truly dwell with God in the Empyrean.
