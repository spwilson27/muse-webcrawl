English existential clauses

English existential clauses are clauses whose function is to assert or comment on the existence (or presence) of something. In English they are typically formed with the dummy subject there followed by a form of the verb be and a noun phrase, as in:

There is a book on the table.

There were two men at the door.

Structure

The construction consists of the expletive (or "dummy") subject there, the verb (usually be), and the "displaced subject" or pivot — the noun phrase whose existence is asserted. Despite there occupying the subject position, agreement is usually controlled by the displaced subject ("there is a book" vs. "there are two books"), although in informal speech "there's" is often used with plural pivots.

Types and related constructions

Existential clauses may be plain ("there is a problem") or extended with locative or other adjuncts. Related constructions include presentational clauses ("there came a knock at the door"), cleft-like uses, and the use of there with verbs other than be, such as exist, remain and arise.

Analysis

Linguists debate whether there is a true subject or an expletive inserted to satisfy the requirement that English clauses have an overt subject. The construction typically obeys the "definiteness effect": the pivot is normally indefinite ("there is a man outside", but oddly "*there is the man outside" in the pure existential reading).
