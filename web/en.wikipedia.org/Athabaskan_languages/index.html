Athabaskan languages

The Athabaskan languages (also spelled Athabascan, Athapaskan, or Athapascan) form a large family of Indigenous languages of North America, part of the wider Na-Dene family. They are spoken across a vast area: interior Alaska and northwestern Canada (Northern Athabaskan), the Pacific coast of Oregon and California (Pacific Coast Athabaskan), and the American Southwest (Southern Athabaskan, or Apachean, including Navajo and the Apache languages).

Characteristics

Athabaskan languages are famous for their complex verb morphology: a verb may consist of a long chain of prefixes encoding subject, object, mode, aspect and direction attached to a short stem, and many verbs classify objects by shape and consistency (the "classificatory verb" system). Most are tonal or pitch-accent languages that developed tone from earlier glottalic consonants. Edward Sapir's work on these languages helped establish the Na-Dene hypothesis, and the scholar Michael Krauss did much to document them.

Subdivisions

Northern Athabaskan languages (such as Gwich'in, Slavey, Dene Sųłiné/Chipewyan, and Carrier) are spoken in Alaska and Canada's northwest. Pacific Coast languages (such as Hupa and Tolowa) survive in small communities in Oregon and northern California. Southern Athabaskan languages arose from a migration to the Southwest within the last thousand years; Navajo is the most widely spoken Indigenous language in the United States, famous in popular culture for its unbroken use by World War II code talkers.

Endangerment and revitalisation

Most Athabaskan languages are endangered, some with only a handful of elderly speakers. Immersion schools, master-apprentice programmes, and tribal language departments in Alaska, Canada, and the Navajo Nation work to revitalise them.
