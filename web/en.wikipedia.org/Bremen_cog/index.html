The Bremen cog is a well-preserved wreck of a medieval cog, a single-masted cargo ship of the type used by Hanseatic merchants in northern European waters. Dated by dendrochronology to about 1380, it was discovered in 1962 during dredging in the River Weser at Bremen, Germany, and is now the centerpiece of the German Maritime Museum in Bremerhaven.


The restored hull displayed in Bremerhaven.

Discovery and preservation

Workers widening the harbour channel found the waterlogged oak timbers; over 2,000 individual pieces were recovered. Conservators spent years treating the wood with polyethylene glycol to prevent it collapsing as it dried, and the ship was reassembled in a purpose-built hall.

Description

The vessel measures roughly 24 metres long and 8 metres in beam, with a clinker-laid (overlapping) hull, a flush-laid bottom, a sternpost rudder, and a single square sail. It appears to have sunk while still unfinished or shortly after launching, before being fitted out completely.

Significance

Before its discovery, cogs were known mainly from pictures on city seals and written records. The Bremen cog gave archaeologists direct evidence of the construction of the Hanseatic League's standard cargo carrier, and full-size replicas have since been built and sailed.
