A timar was a land grant in the Ottoman Empire, by which the sultan assigned the tax revenues of a defined parcel of state (miri) land to a cavalryman (sipahi) in return for military service. It was the smallest of the three grades of such grants: timars yielded less than 20,000 akçe per year, zeamets between 20,000 and 100,000, and has grants over 100,000.

Function

The timariot did not own the land; he collected its taxes in kind and cash, kept the peace locally, and reported for campaign with his own horse, arms and a number of armed retainers proportional to his grant's income. Grants were recorded in detailed cadastral registers (tahrir defterleri) and were in principle revocable and non-hereditary, though sons often succeeded to part of a father's timar. The system let the empire field a large provincial cavalry without a cash treasury, and underpinned Ottoman expansion in the Balkans and Anatolia in the 14th–16th centuries.

Decline

From the late 16th century, inflation, the growing importance of musket infantry paid in cash, and the spread of tax farming (iltizam) eroded the timar system. Sipahis lost ground to kapıkulu standing troops and provincial notables; timars were progressively converted or absorbed, and the system was formally abolished in the Tanzimat era (1830s), though the cavalry had long ceased to be militarily decisive.

Comparisons

The timar resembles the Byzantine pronoia and, more loosely, the European fief, but differed in the absence of vassalic homage and of hereditary ownership of the land itself.
