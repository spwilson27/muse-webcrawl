SN2 reaction

TypeNucleophilic substitution
KineticsBimolecular; second order
StereochemistryInversion of configuration (Walden inversion)

The SN2 reaction is a type of nucleophilic substitution in organic chemistry in which a nucleophile attacks an electrophilic carbon from the side opposite to the leaving group, displacing it in a single concerted step. The name stands for substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular: the rate of reaction depends on the concentration of both the substrate and the nucleophile.

Mechanism

Attack occurs at the σ* antibonding orbital of the carbon–leaving-group bond. As the new bond forms and the old bond breaks, the carbon passes through a planar, sp²-like transition state with the three remaining groups in a plane; the product therefore has the opposite configuration to the starting material — a phenomenon called Walden inversion.

Factors affecting rate

SN2 reactions are fastest with methyl and primary substrates and slow or absent with tertiary substrates, because bulky groups hinder backside attack. Good leaving groups (such as bromide, iodide and tosylate) and strong nucleophiles accelerate the reaction, and polar aprotic solvents (such as DMSO or acetone) are usually preferred because they do not solvate the nucleophile strongly. The reaction competes with the SN1 and elimination pathways depending on conditions.
