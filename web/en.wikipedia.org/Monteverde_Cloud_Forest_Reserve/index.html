The Monteverde Cloud Forest Reserve (Spanish: Reserva Biológica Bosque Nuboso Monteverde) is a Costa Rican reserve located along the Cordillera de Tilarán within the Puntarenas and Alajuela provinces. Named after the nearby town of Monteverde and founded in 1972, the reserve consists of over 10,500 hectares (26,000 acres) of cloud forest, more than 90% of which is virgin forest. It is one of the most famous reserves in the world, visited by roughly 70,000 tourists a year.

Biodiversity

The reserve contains an extraordinary concentration of biodiversity: some 2,500 plant species (including more than 400 orchids), 100 species of mammals, 400 bird species, 120 species of reptiles and amphibians, and tens of thousands of insect species. It is one of the few remaining habitats of all six species of the cat family of the area — jaguars, ocelots, pumas, oncillas, margays, and jaguarundis.

Monteverde is especially celebrated for its birds, including the resplendent quetzal, the three-wattled bellbird, the bare-necked umbrellabird, and the emerald toucanet. It was also the best-known home of the golden toad, an endemic species last seen in 1989 and widely cited as one of the first documented victims of climate-driven amphibian declines.

History

The Monteverde area was settled in the 1950s by Quaker families from the United States who left partly to avoid the draft, and who set aside a portion of their land as a watershed preserve. In 1972, scientist George Powell and his wife, together with naturalist Wilford Guindon, promoted the creation of a protected area in response to threats from homesteading, hunting, and land clearing. The Tropical Science Center assumed administration, purchasing land with support from conservation organizations and donors.

Tourism and research

The reserve offers some 13 km of trails, hanging bridges nearby, and guided tours including night walks. Only a limited number of visitors are allowed inside at any one time to reduce impact. Monteverde hosts several biological research stations and receives hundreds of visiting scientists each year; the surrounding community has become a center of ecotourism in Costa Rica.
