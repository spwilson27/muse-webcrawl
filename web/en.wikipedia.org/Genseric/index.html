Genseric (c. 389 – 25 January 477), also spelled Gaiseric or Geiseric, was king of the Vandals and Alans from 428 to 477. He ruled over a kingdom in late Roman North Africa and is famous for the sack of Rome in 455 and for defeating two major Roman expeditions sent against him. Despite modest beginnings, he built the Vandals into the dominant naval power of the western Mediterranean and was one of the most formidable opponents of the declining Western Roman Empire.

Early life and accession

Genseric was the illegitimate son of King Godigisel and a slave woman. Described by the historian Jordanes as of moderate height and lame from a fall from his horse, he was said to be deep-thinking, sparing of speech, contemptuous of luxury, and furious in anger. He succeeded his half-brother Gunderic in 428, inheriting a people who had crossed the Rhine in 406, fought through Gaul, and settled in Hispania Baetica.

Conquest of North Africa

In May 429 Genseric ferried his entire people — some 80,000 souls by ancient estimates — across the Strait of Gibraltar into Roman Africa, possibly at the invitation of the rebellious Roman governor Boniface, though the story is disputed. Marching east through Mauretania and Numidia, he defeated Boniface near Calama in 430 and besieged him in Hippo Regius, where Saint Augustine died during the siege. A treaty in 435 granted the Vandals territory in Numidia and Mauretania, but in October 439 Genseric seized Carthage itself in a surprise attack, giving his kingdom the richest city and finest harbor of Roman Africa, along with a fleet.

Sack of Rome

In 455, after the murder of Emperor Valentinian III, Genseric sailed for Italy, claiming his daughter-in-law, the empress Licinia Eudoxia, had summoned him. Pope Leo I met him outside Rome and, according to tradition, persuaded him to refrain from massacre and fire. The Vandals nevertheless systematically plundered the city for fourteen days — a more thorough, if less violent, affair than the Visigothic sack of 410 — carrying off the treasures of the Temple of Jerusalem that Titus had brought to Rome, the gilded roof tiles of the Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus, and thousands of captives, including the empress and her daughters. The thoroughness of this sack gave rise to the modern word "vandalism."

Wars with the Eastern Empire

Genseric repeatedly defeated Roman attempts to dislodge him. In 460 he destroyed a Western imperial fleet off Spain before it could sail. In 468 the Eastern emperor Leo I mounted an enormous joint expedition under Basiliscus — over a thousand ships by some accounts — which Genseric destroyed at the Battle of Cape Bon by using fireships against the anchored Roman fleet. The disaster bankrupted the Eastern treasury and ended serious Roman efforts to recover Africa. A lasting peace followed in 476.

Death and legacy

Genseric died at Carthage on 25 January 477, at around 87 years of age, having reigned 49 years. He was succeeded by his son Huneric. An Arian Christian, Genseric persecuted the Nicene clergy of Africa, confiscating churches and exiling bishops, though his rule also brought order and prosperity to his kingdom, which endured until the Byzantine reconquest under Belisarius in 533–534.
