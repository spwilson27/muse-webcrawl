Jamestown was the first permanent English settlement in the Americas. It was established on 14 May 1607 by the Virginia Company of London on the James River in present-day Virginia, and served as the capital of the Colony of Virginia from 1616 until 1699.

Founding and early years

Three ships — the Susan Constant, Godspeed and Discovery — carrying 104 men and boys reached Virginia in April 1607 and chose a defensible site on a peninsula (now an island) in the James River, named for King James I. The settlers suffered disease, brackish water, famine and conflict with the Powhatan confederacy. During the "Starving Time" winter of 1609–10, the population fell from about 500 to 60. The colony was saved by supply ships and by the arrival of Lord De La Warr. John Rolfe's cultivation of tobacco from 1612 gave the colony a profitable export; in 1613 Pocahontas, daughter of Chief Powhatan, was captured, converted and married Rolfe, bringing a period of peace.

Growth and later history

In July 1619 the first representative legislative assembly in the Americas, the House of Burgesses, met at Jamestown. In August 1619 the first enslaved Africans arrived in English America aboard the White Lion — a turning point in American history. The town survived the Powhatan attack of 1622 and Bacon's Rebellion in 1676, during which it was burned. After the statehouse burned again in 1698, the capital moved to Williamsburg in 1699 and Jamestown declined into farmland.

Today

The site is preserved as two attractions: Historic Jamestowne, part of Colonial National Historical Park, where archaeologists since 1994 have excavated the original fort and recovered over a million artefacts; and Jamestown Settlement, a living-history museum with re-created fort, Powhatan village and replica ships. Major commemorations were held in 1957 (350th) and 2007 (400th anniversary, visited by Queen Elizabeth II).
