Alms

Alms are money, food, or other material goods donated to people living in poverty. Providing alms is often considered an act of charity and virtue in many religious traditions, including Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism. The word derives from the Old English ælmesse, from late Latin eleemosyna, itself from Greek eleēmosynē, "pity, alms".

In religion

In Islam, almsgiving (zakat) is one of the Five Pillars: a compulsory levy, traditionally 2.5% of a Muslim's savings and wealth above a threshold, given to the poor; voluntary charity beyond this is called sadaqah. In Judaism, tzedakah — the obligation to give charitably — is a commandment rather than a mere kindness. In Christianity, almsgiving is one of the traditional works of mercy and a customary Lenten practice, and in the Middle Ages monasteries and parishes distributed alms at their gates.

In Buddhism and Hinduism, giving alms (dāna) to monks and the poor is a principal way for laypeople to earn merit. Buddhist monks traditionally make a daily alms round (pindapata), carrying an alms bowl and receiving food offerings in silence, a practice still observed in Theravada countries such as Thailand, Laos and Myanmar.

History

Organised almsgiving produced institutions such as the almshouse — charitable housing for poor elderly people, common in England from the medieval period onward — and shaped poor-relief systems administered by parishes and guilds. Almsgiving remains a practice of individuals, religious bodies, and charities worldwide, and survives in vocabulary such as "alms bowl", "almoner", and in folklore figures such as the alms-gathering friars.
