The Sistine Chapel (Latin: Sacellum Sixtinum; Italian: Cappella Sistina) is a chapel in the Apostolic Palace, in Vatican City, the official residence of the Pope. Originally known as the Cappella Magna, it takes its name from Pope Sixtus IV, who had it built between 1473 and 1481. Since that time it has served as a place of both religious activity and as the site of the papal conclave, the process by which a new pope is selected.

The chapel is famous above all for its frescoes: the walls were decorated by a team of Renaissance painters including Botticelli, Perugino, Pinturicchio and Ghirlandaio; the ceiling (1508–1512) and The Last Judgment on the altar wall (1534–1541) were painted by Michelangelo. The chapel's proportions are traditionally said to follow those of the Temple of Solomon.

History

Sixtus IV rebuilt an older chapel, fortifying it against the threats of the Ottoman Empire and the Medici. The first mass was celebrated on 15 August 1483, the Feast of the Assumption. In 1508 Pope Julius II commissioned Michelangelo to repaint the ceiling, which had been decorated with a starry sky by Pier Matteo d'Amelia; the resulting nine scenes from Genesis, framed by prophets, sibyls and ignudi, were unveiled in 1512.

Paul III later commissioned The Last Judgment, completed in 1541, which covers the entire altar wall with Christ's second coming and the resurrection of the dead. A major restoration of the frescoes was carried out between 1980 and 1994, controversially removing centuries of grime (and some later overpainting) to reveal vivid original colours.

The conclave

The chapel has hosted conclaves since 1492. During a conclave, cardinals are sequestered in the chapel, ballots are burned after each vote, and smoke signals announce the result — black smoke for no decision, white for the election of a new pope.
