Secularism

Term coined1851, by George Jacob Holyoake
Core principleSeparation of government institutions from religious institutions and beliefs
Related conceptsLaïcité, secular state, freedom of religion

Secularism is the principle of seeking to conduct human affairs based on naturalistic considerations, without involvement of religion. In political terms it denotes the separation of the state from religious institutions, the neutrality of the state toward religious beliefs, and the protection of both freedom of religion and freedom from religion.

History

Ideas of separating religious and political authority have roots in antiquity and were developed by Enlightenment thinkers such as John Locke, whose Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) argued that the state should not coerce religious belief. The First Amendment to the United States Constitution (1791) prohibited an establishment of religion, and Thomas Jefferson's metaphor of a "wall of separation between church and state" became influential. In France, the 1905 law on the separation of churches and the state established the assertive model known as laïcité. The British writer George Jacob Holyoake coined the term "secularism" in 1851.

Models

Secular arrangements vary: the United States combines non-establishment with free exercise; France's laïcité excludes religious symbols from many public institutions; countries such as Turkey, India and others have adopted their own variants, India's being a "principled distance" model permitting state engagement with all religions. Some states retain established churches while remaining broadly secular in governance.
