Graphic notation

Graphic notation (or graphic score) is the representation of music through the use of visual symbols outside the realm of traditional musical notation. It evolved in the 1950s among experimental composers who found conventional staff notation inadequate for new sounds, extended techniques, and indeterminate music — works in which performers are invited to interpret visual shapes, drawings, colours, and prose instructions as music. Graphic scores range from precise geometric systems to free-form artworks that leave nearly everything to the performer's imagination.

History

Precursors appear in the puzzle canons and eye music of earlier centuries, but graphic notation flourished with post-war avant-garde music. Morton Feldman's Projection series (1950–51) used grids of boxes indicating register, timing, and articulation without specific pitches. Earle Brown's December 1952 — a field of horizontal and vertical rectangles — became an emblem of the open score. John Cage used transparencies, points, and lines to generate scores such as Fontana Mix (1958), and Cornelius Cardew's Treatise (1963–67), a 193-page score of abstract graphics with no instructions at all, is the genre's monumental work.

Approaches

Graphic notation spans a spectrum: at one end, modified staff notation with new symbols for new sounds; in the middle, "prose scores" and instruction pieces (such as La Monte Young's text scores); at the far end, purely visual artworks intended to provoke an interpretive musical response. Composers have valued graphic scores for freeing performers from habits, democratising music-making (scores readable by non-musicians), and making the score itself an object of visual art — many are exhibited in galleries.

Legacy

Indeterminacy and graphic scores influenced improvisation-based ensembles such as the Scratch Orchestra (co-founded by Cardew), graphic-score practices in jazz and electronic music, and contemporary animated and digital scores in which the notation moves or responds to the performer.
