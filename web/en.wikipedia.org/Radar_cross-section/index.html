Radar cross-section

Radar cross-section (RCS) measures how detectable an object is by radar.

Radar cross-section (RCS), denoted σ, is a measure of how detectable an object is by radar. A larger RCS indicates that an object is more easily detected. Formally, RCS is a measure of the ratio of backscatter power per steradian (in the direction of the radar) to the power density that is intercepted by the object: it is the effective area of a hypothetical perfectly reflecting sphere that would return the same echo strength.

RCS depends on the object's size, shape, material, surface roughness, and on the radar's frequency, polarisation and the viewing angle. A stealth aircraft is designed to have an RCS orders of magnitude smaller than a conventional aircraft of similar size, by shaping surfaces to deflect radar energy away from the source and by using radar-absorbent materials.

Factors affecting RCS

Size alone does not determine RCS. Flat surfaces and corner reflectors return strong echoes; curved and angled surfaces scatter energy in many directions. Edges, cavities such as engine intakes, and rotating parts like fan blades can all produce strong returns. At low frequencies, where the wavelength approaches the size of the object's features, resonance effects can increase the RCS substantially, which is why long-wave radars are of interest for counter-stealth applications.

Measurement

RCS is measured on instrumented radar ranges using scaled or full-size models mounted on pylons, and in anechoic chambers. Results are commonly plotted as a function of azimuth angle, producing a characteristic pattern of peaks where flat surfaces align with the radar.

Reduction

Techniques for reducing RCS include planform alignment (concentrating scattered energy into a few narrow spikes), blended curved surfaces, internal carriage of weapons, serrated edges on doors and panels, and radar-absorbent coatings. The equations used for RCS prediction and reduction were pioneered by the Soviet physicist Pyotr Ufimtsev, whose work underpinned the design of the F-117 Nighthawk and B-2 Spirit.
