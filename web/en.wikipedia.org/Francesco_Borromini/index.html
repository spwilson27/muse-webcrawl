Francesco Borromini

San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane, Borromini's first independent commission

Francesco Borromini (25 September 1599 – 2 August 1667), born Francesco Castelli, was an Italian architect from Ticino who, with his contemporaries Gian Lorenzo Bernini and Pietro da Cortona, was a leading figure in the emergence of Roman Baroque architecture. His buildings are famed for their inventive manipulation of classical forms, complex geometry, and dramatic, undulating plans.

Career

Trained as a stonemason, Borromini came to Rome in 1619 and worked under Carlo Maderno at St Peter's Basilica, later collaborating — and then clashing — with Bernini. His first independent commission, the church of San Carlo alle Quattro Fontane (1634), is a masterpiece of Baroque spatial invention built on an oval-and-lozenge plan. Other major works include the Oratory of Saint Philip Neri, the church of Sant'Ivo alla Sapienza with its spiral lantern, Sant'Agnese in Agone on Piazza Navona, and work at the Lateran basilica and Palazzo Barberini.

Style and legacy

Unlike Bernini's sculptural splendour, Borromini's architecture relies on rigorous geometry, modulated light, and curving walls that seem to ripple. Perpetually overshadowed by his rival and plagued by melancholy, he died by suicide in 1667. His daring handling of space profoundly influenced later Baroque architecture, notably Guarino Guarini and the Central European Baroque.
