Islamic architecture

Islamic architecture comprises the architectural styles of buildings associated with Islam, developing over fourteen centuries across the Middle East, North Africa, Spain, Persia, Central Asia, and South Asia. It encompasses both religious structures — mosques, madrasas, and tombs — and secular ones such as palaces, caravanserais, and fortifications.

Characteristics

Common elements include the minaret (the tower from which the call to prayer is announced), the dome, the iwan (a vaulted hall opening onto a courtyard), the mihrab (a niche indicating the direction of Mecca), and the minbar (pulpit). Decoration favors calligraphy, geometric patterns, and arabesque vegetal ornament over figurative imagery in religious settings, along with muqarnas (stalactite vaulting) and glazed tilework.

Early development

The Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (691–692), the first major monument of Islamic architecture, and the Umayyad Great Mosque of Damascus set early precedents, adapting Byzantine and Sasanian techniques. Under the Abbasids, the vast spiral minaret of Samarra and the hypostyle Great Mosque of Córdoba in al-Andalus developed new forms.

Regional styles

Persian architecture developed the four-iwan plan and elaborate tile mosaics (Isfahan's Shah Mosque); Ottoman architecture culminated in Sinan's domed mosques, above all the Süleymaniye and Selimiye; Moorish architecture produced the Alhambra of Granada; and Mughal architecture in India fused Persian and local elements in monuments such as Humayun's Tomb and the Taj Mahal.
