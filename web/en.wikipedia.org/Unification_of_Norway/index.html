Unification of Norway

The unification of Norway was the process by which the many petty kingdoms and chiefdoms of Norway were gradually brought under a single king during the Viking Age and early Middle Ages. Tradition credits Harald Fairhair (Harald Hårfagre) with uniting Norway after his victory at the Battle of Hafrsfjord, conventionally dated to about 872, though modern historians regard unification as a long process extending from the late ninth century into the eleventh and twelfth centuries.

Background

Early medieval Norway was divided among regional rulers — kings of Vestfold, Hordaland, Rogaland, Trøndelag and others — whose power rested on kinship, assemblies (things) and control of trade along the coastal sea route, the Norðvegr ("north way") that gave Norway its name. The Vestfold dynasty of the Ynglings, based near the Oslofjord, was well placed to extend its power.

Harald Fairhair and Hafrsfjord

According to the kings' sagas, written down centuries later, Harald inherited Vestfold as a youth and vowed — in a story linked to his courtship of Gyda Eiriksdatter — not to cut his hair until he had subdued all Norway. His defeat of a coalition of western kings at Hafrsfjord near Stavanger made him overlord of much of the coast. Harald imposed taxes and royal officials, provoking emigration to Iceland, the Faroes, Orkney and Shetland, whose settlement traditions blamed his tyranny.

Consolidation

Harald's many sons divided the realm after his death (c. 930), and real unification was repeatedly undone by civil war, Danish overlordship and regional earls such as the Earls of Lade in Trøndelag. The Christian kings Olaf Tryggvason (995–1000) and Olaf Haraldsson (1015–1028, later St Olaf) used conversion and law to bind the country, and only with the end of the civil war era in 1240 and the national law code of Magnus the Lawmender (1274) was a unified Norwegian kingdom fully institutionalized.
