Peer pressure


Peers influence behaviour in school, work, and social life

Peer pressure is a direct or indirect influence on peers — members of a social group with similar status, age, or interests — that can affect their thoughts, feelings, and behaviours, typically encouraging conformity to group norms. It is a central topic in developmental and social psychology, studied from early childhood through adulthood, though it is most strongly associated with adolescence.

Forms

Peer pressure may be direct, such as explicit dares, teasing, or offers (for example, being urged to drink alcohol), or indirect, such as modelling and the desire to fit in — changing dress, speech, or opinions to match friends. Psychologists distinguish normative influence, acting to be liked and accepted, from informational influence, acting because peers are assumed to know what is correct. It can be negative, encouraging risky or harmful behaviour, or positive, encouraging studying, exercise, or generosity.

Development and research

Susceptibility to peer influence generally rises in early adolescence, peaks around age 14–15, and declines thereafter as identity and autonomy develop. Classic conformity experiments by Solomon Asch demonstrated powerful group pressure even on unambiguous judgements. Research links peer influence to adolescent risk-taking in the presence of friends — including in driving studies using brain imaging — but also finds that close friendships and parental monitoring buffer harmful effects.

Responding to peer pressure

Prevention programmes teach refusal skills and correct misperceptions of peer norms (most adolescents overestimate how many peers drink or smoke). Positive peer pressure is deliberately harnessed in peer tutoring, mentoring, and anti-bullying initiatives.
