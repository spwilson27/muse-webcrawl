Permaculture

Permaculture is an approach to land management and settlement design that adopts arrangements observed in flourishing natural ecosystems. It includes a set of design principles derived from systems thinking, with central themes of working with, rather than against, nature; protracted and thoughtful observation rather than protracted and thoughtless labour; and seeing systems in all their functions rather than asking only one yield of them.

History

The term was coined in the mid-1970s by the Tasmanian academic Bill Mollison and his student David Holmgren, from "permanent agriculture" and later extended to "permanent culture". Their book Permaculture One was published in 1978. The concept drew on earlier work in organic farming, agroforestry and keyline design by figures such as P. A. Yeomans, as well as on the observation of traditional land-management systems.

Permaculture spread internationally in the 1980s through design courses, and Mollison's 1988 Designers' Manual became its standard reference. Holmgren later systematised twelve design principles in Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability (2002).

Ethics and principles

Permaculture rests on three ethics: care for the earth, care for people, and fair share (return of surplus to the first two). Holmgren's twelve principles include "observe and interact", "catch and store energy", "obtain a yield", "apply self-regulation and accept feedback", "produce no waste", "design from patterns to details", "integrate rather than segregate", "use small and slow solutions", "use and value diversity", "use edges and value the marginal", and "creatively use and respond to change".

Design methods

Common techniques include zoning land by frequency of use (zones 0–5), sector analysis of sun, wind and water flows, stacking functions so that each element serves several purposes, companion planting, forest gardens, swales and keyline ploughing for water harvesting, and composting systems such as worm farms and hot compost.

Applications and criticism

Permaculture is applied in farms, gardens, intentional communities, urban design and disaster-relief contexts. Critics in the scientific literature have noted a lack of rigorous peer-reviewed evidence for some claimed yields, while supporters argue that its whole-systems orientation complements conventional agronomy.
