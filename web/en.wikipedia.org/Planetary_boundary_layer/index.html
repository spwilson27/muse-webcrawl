Planetary boundary layer

In meteorology, the planetary boundary layer (PBL), also known as the atmospheric boundary layer (ABL) or peplosphere, is the lowest part of the atmosphere, the part directly influenced by its contact with a planetary surface. On Earth it usually extends up to about 1 km, though it can range from a few tens of metres to several kilometres depending on conditions. The behaviour of the PBL governs the exchange of heat, moisture, and momentum between the surface and the free atmosphere above.

Characteristics

Within the boundary layer, physical quantities such as flow velocity, temperature, and moisture display rapid fluctuations due to turbulence, and vertical mixing is strong. Above the PBL, in the "free atmosphere", the wind is approximately geostrophic, while in the surface layer (the lowest tenth or so of the PBL) it is greatly affected by friction with the surface and by obstacles.

Diurnal cycle

Over land in high-pressure regions the boundary layer follows a marked daily cycle. During the day, solar heating generates convective turbulence and the layer grows to its maximum depth, with a well-mixed layer of nearly constant potential temperature. At night, radiative cooling of the surface produces a stable nocturnal boundary layer, often capped by a low-level jet, with a residual layer above it retaining the previous day's mixed properties.

Significance

The PBL is where surface fluxes that drive weather and climate occur; it controls fog formation, frost, dispersion of pollutants, and the environment in which humans and ecosystems live. Accurate representation of boundary-layer processes is essential in weather forecasting and climate models.
