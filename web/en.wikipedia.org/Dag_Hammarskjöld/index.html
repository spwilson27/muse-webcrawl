Dag Hjalmar Agne Carl Hammarskjöld (29 July 1905 – 18 September 1961) was a Swedish economist and diplomat who served as the second Secretary-General of the United Nations from April 1953 until his death in a plane crash in September 1961. He remains the youngest person to have held the post, at the age of 47 upon his appointment, and the only Secretary-General to die in office.

Hammarskjöld is considered one of the most effective Secretaries-General, credited with developing the concepts of preventive diplomacy and UN peacekeeping. U.S. President John F. Kennedy called him "the greatest statesman of our century." He was posthumously awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1961.

Early life and career

Hammarskjöld was born in Jönköping into a prominent family; his father, Hjalmar Hammarskjöld, was Prime Minister of Sweden from 1914 to 1917. He studied law and economics at Uppsala and Stockholm, worked in the Swedish finance ministry and central bank, and served as chairman of the board of governors of the Bank of Sweden. He entered the Swedish foreign service, became minister without portfolio, and was a central figure in postwar economic cooperation, helping to establish the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation.

Secretary-General

Chosen in 1953 as a compromise candidate after Trygve Lie's resignation, Hammarskjöld reorganized the UN Secretariat and asserted the independence of the international civil service. His achievements included negotiating the release of American airmen captured by China in 1955, organizing the first UN peacekeeping force (UNEF) during the Suez Crisis of 1956, and intervening in conflicts in Lebanon, Laos, and the Congo. His activist interpretation of the office, crystallized in his 1961 Oxford speech on the international civil servant, provoked the Soviet Union to demand his resignation and propose replacing the Secretary-General with a three-person "troika."

Death

On the night of 17–18 September 1961, Hammarskjöld was flying to Ndola in Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia) to negotiate a ceasefire in the Congo Crisis when his Douglas DC-6, the Albertina, crashed, killing all aboard. The circumstances have remained controversial; successive inquiries, including a UN investigation reopened in the 2010s under Mohamed Chande Othman, found significant new information suggesting the possibility of an external attack or threat, and the matter has never been fully resolved.

Legacy and writings

Hammarskjöld's posthumously published spiritual journal, Markings (Vägmärken, 1963), became a widely read work of modern devotion. The Dag Hammarskjöld Library at UN headquarters in New York is named for him, as is the Dag Hammarskjöld Foundation in Uppsala.
