Pyrimidine metabolism

Pyrimidine metabolism refers to the biochemical pathways by which cells synthesize, salvage, and degrade pyrimidine nucleotides — the building blocks UTP, CTP, and TTP needed for RNA and DNA. Unlike purines, the pyrimidine ring is assembled first and then attached to ribose phosphate.

Pyrimidine metabolism
Processes: De novo synthesis, salvage, catabolism
Key intermediate: Orotidine monophosphate (OMP)
Key enzymes: CAD, DHODH, UMPS
End products of breakdown: β-amino acids

De novo synthesis

The ring is built from bicarbonate, the amino group of glutamine, and aspartate, which supplies the remaining atoms. Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase II (part of the multifunctional CAD enzyme in animals) forms carbamoyl phosphate, which condenses with aspartate to give carbamoyl aspartate; ring closure and oxidation yield orotate. Orotate is attached to phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate (PRPP) and decarboxylated to form UMP. UMP is phosphorylated to UDP and UTP, and UTP is aminated to CTP; thymidylate (TMP) for DNA is made by methylation of dUMP by thymidylate synthase, a target of anticancer drugs such as 5-fluorouracil and methotrexate.

Salvage and degradation

Cells recover free pyrimidine bases and nucleosides through salvage enzymes. Catabolism reduces the ring of uracil and thymine and opens it, producing the β-amino acids β-alanine and β-aminoisobutyrate, which are water-soluble and excreted. Defects in degradation enzymes cause neurological symptoms due to accumulation of intermediates.
