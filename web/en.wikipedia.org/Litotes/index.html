Litotes

In rhetoric, litotes (from Greek λιτότης, "simplicity") is a figure of speech in which understatement is used to emphasise a point by stating a negative to further affirm a positive — often incorporating double negatives. Saying "not bad" to mean "good", or "no small feat" to mean a major achievement, are common modern examples. Litotes functions as a form of deliberate understatement and is related to, but distinct from, meiosis.

Examples

"He is no fool." — meaning he is wise.
"It isn't rocket science." — meaning it is easy.
"Not unattractive." — meaning attractive.
In Beowulf and other Old English poetry, kennings and affirmations like "nor was that a poor man" exemplify litotes: the warrior's quality is affirmed by denying its opposite.

Usage in literature and culture

Litotes is characteristic of Old English and Old Norse literature, where heroic understatement signalled stoicism. It is also proverbial in stereotypically British understatement ("quite pleased" for delighted; "a bit of a bother" for a serious problem) and in classical Latin rhetoric, where Cicero and others used non ignoro ("I am not unaware") constructions. In French, the standard negation ne... pas and expressions like pas mal ("not bad", i.e. quite good) illustrate the device's persistence.

Related figures

Litotes is sometimes confused with meiosis (understatement generally), euphemism, and the double negative of informal speech ("I don't know nothing"), which is a grammatical rather than rhetorical phenomenon. Its opposite is hyperbole, deliberate overstatement.
