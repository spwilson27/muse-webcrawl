Cowboy

Cowboy
Occupation: Animal herder
Region: North American West
Tradition: Vaquero (Spanish/Mexican)
Era: 19th century–present

A cowboy is an animal herder who tends cattle on ranches in North America, traditionally on horseback, and often performs a multitude of other ranch-related tasks. The historic American cowboy of the late 19th century arose from the vaquero traditions of northern Mexico and became a figure of special significance and legend.

History

Cattle ranching in the Americas began with Spanish colonists in the 16th century. Mexican vaqueros developed techniques for herding, roping, and branding cattle in the arid Southwest. After the American Civil War, demand for beef in northern cities drove the great cattle drives from Texas to railheads in Kansas along trails such as the Chisholm Trail. The classic cattle-drive era lasted roughly from 1866 to the mid-1880s, when barbed wire, railroads, and harsh winters ended the open range.

Work and skills

Cowboys drive herds, repair fences, brand calves, administer medicine, and break horses. Signature tools include the lariat (from Spanish la reata), chaps, spurs, and the wide-brimmed hat. Rodeo sports grew out of informal competitions in these working skills.

Culture

The cowboy became a central figure of the Western genre in novels, film, and television, symbolizing independence and the frontier, though the historical workforce was ethnically diverse, including many African American, Mexican, and Native American cowboys. Modern cowboys continue to work on ranches today.
