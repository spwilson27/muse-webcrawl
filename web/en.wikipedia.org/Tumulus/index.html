Tumulus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tumulus (plural tumuli) is a mound of earth and stones raised over a grave or graves. Tumuli are also known as barrows, burial mounds, or (in Siberia and Central Asia) kurgans, and may be found throughout much of the world. A cairn, which is a mound of stones built for various purposes, might also originally have been a tumulus.

Types

Tumuli range from small, low heaps a few metres across to monumental structures such as the Silbury Hill in Wiltshire, England, and the royal tombs of ancient Lydia at Bin Tepe. Common forms include round barrows (bowl, bell, disc and pond barrows), elongated long barrows of the Neolithic, and chambered mounds containing stone or wooden burial chambers and passage graves.

Historical distribution

Burial mounds were constructed across Eurasia from the Neolithic through the Bronze and Iron Ages and into the early Middle Ages. Famous examples include the Scythian and Sarmatian kurgans of the Eurasian steppe, the Etruscan tumuli of Italy, the Sutton Hoo ship-burial mounds in England, the viking-age mounds of Scandinavia, and the keyhole-shaped kofun of Japan. In North America, cultures such as the Adena and Hopewell built extensive mound complexes.

Contents

Excavated tumuli frequently contain grave goods — weapons, jewellery, pottery, horse burials and sacrificed retainers — revealing the status of the dead and the beliefs of the builders. Because mounds were conspicuous, many were robbed in antiquity or reused for secondary burials.
