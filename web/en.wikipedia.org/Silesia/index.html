Silesia (Polish: Śląsk; German: Schlesien; Czech: Slezsko) is a historical region of Central Europe, lying mostly in modern-day Poland with smaller parts in the Czech Republic and Germany. It stretches along the upper and middle Oder (Odra) river and the Sudetes mountains. Its historic capital is Wrocław (German: Breslau). Silesia is divided traditionally into Lower Silesia in the west and Upper Silesia in the southeast, the latter a major coal and steel industrial district.

History

First ruled by the Polish Piast dynasty, Silesia fragmented into numerous small Piast duchies in the Middle Ages and gradually came under the Bohemian crown, and with it the Habsburg monarchy. Frederick the Great of Prussia seized most of Silesia from Austria in the Silesian Wars (1740–1763), a conquest confirmed by the Treaty of Hubertusburg; the small remainder, Austrian Silesia, lay around Troppau (Opava). Under Prussia and then the German Empire, Upper Silesia industrialised massively on its coal seams, while the population remained a mixture of German speakers, Polish speakers (including speakers of the Silesian dialect or language), and Czechs.

After the First World War, the region was contested between Germany and the reborn Poland: the Upper Silesia plebiscite of 1921 and three Silesian Uprisings led the League of Nations to partition Upper Silesia in 1922. After the Second World War, the Potsdam Conference transferred nearly all of Silesia east of the Oder–Neisse line to Poland; its German population — millions of people — was expelled westward, and the region was resettled by Poles, including many displaced from Poland's eastern territories annexed by the USSR. A part of Czech Silesia remains in the Czech Republic around Opava and Český Těšín.

Modern region

Today Polish Silesia comprises the Lower Silesian, Opole, and Silesian voivodeships, with cities including Wrocław, Katowice, Opole, and Gliwice. The Silesian metropolitan area around Katowice is one of Poland's largest urban-industrial conurbations, transitioning from coal and steel toward services and technology. A Silesian regional identity remains strong: in Polish censuses hundreds of thousands declare Silesian nationality, and the status of Silesian as a language is debated in parliament.
