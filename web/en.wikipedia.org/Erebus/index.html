Erebus

For other uses, see Erebus (disambiguation). For the Antarctic volcano, see Mount Erebus.

In Greek mythology, Erebus (Ancient Greek: Ἔρεβος, "darkness") was one of the primordial deities, a personification of darkness and of the deep shadowy region beneath the earth through which the dead passed on their way to Hades. His name also designated that region itself, often used interchangeably in early poetry with Tartarus and the underworld generally.

In Hesiod

In Hesiod's Theogony, Erebus is born from Chaos, alongside Gaia (Earth), Tartarus, Eros, and Nyx (Night). Erebus then mates with his sister Nyx, fathering Aether (the bright upper air) and Hemera (Day) — light being paradoxically born from the union of darkness and night. Other sources give Erebus and Nyx a larger brood of abstract beings, including Moros (Doom), Thanatos (Death), Hypnos (Sleep), the Fates, Nemesis, and Charon, the ferryman of the dead.

The region

As a place, Erebus is the dark region between Earth and Hades proper, through which souls passed immediately after death. It appears in later literature, including Roman retellings, as a part or synonym of the underworld. The word also entered European languages as a byword for profound darkness.

Legacy

The name was given to HMS Erebus, the ship of Sir John Franklin's lost 1845 expedition in search of the Northwest Passage (found wrecked in 2014), and to Mount Erebus in Antarctica, named by James Clark Ross after his own expedition ship. The moth genus Erebus and the butterfly family Erebidae also take their names from the deity.
