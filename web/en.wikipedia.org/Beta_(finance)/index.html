Beta (finance)

In finance, the beta (β) of an investment indicates whether it tends to be more or less volatile than the market as a whole. It is a measure of the investment's systematic risk — the risk that cannot be eliminated by diversification — and is a key input of the capital asset pricing model (CAPM). A beta of 1 indicates that the asset's price moves in step with the market on average; a beta greater than 1 indicates greater volatility than the market; a beta less than 1 indicates less volatility; and a negative beta indicates movement opposite to the market.

Calculation

Beta is calculated as the covariance of the asset's returns with the market's returns, divided by the variance of the market's returns: β = Cov(Ra, Rm) / Var(Rm). In practice it is estimated as the slope of a regression of the asset's historical returns on the returns of a market index such as the S&P 500, typically using several years of monthly or weekly data. Different data providers use different windows and intervals, so published betas for the same stock can differ.

Interpretation and use

Under the CAPM, the expected return of an asset equals the risk-free rate plus its beta multiplied by the market risk premium. Defensive sectors such as utilities and consumer staples typically have betas below 1, while technology and small-cap growth stocks often have betas well above 1. Portfolio beta is the weighted average of the betas of its constituents, and investors use it to gauge and hedge overall market exposure. Levered and unlevered betas adjust for a company's capital structure when valuing firms or projects.

Limitations

Beta assumes normally distributed returns and a stable linear relationship with the chosen index; it is backward-looking and can change substantially over time. Academic work on the Fama–French factor models has shown that beta alone poorly explains the cross-section of stock returns, yet it remains the most widely used single measure of market risk in practice.
