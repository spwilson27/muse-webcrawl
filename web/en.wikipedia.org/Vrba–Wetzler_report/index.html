Vrba–Wetzler report

The Vrba–Wetzler report, also known as the Auschwitz Protocols in part, is a 32-page document written in April 1944 by two Slovak Jewish prisoners, Rudolf Vrba and Alfréd Wetzler, after their escape from the Auschwitz concentration camp. It provided one of the first detailed, eyewitness accounts of the systematic extermination of Jews at Auschwitz-Birkenau to reach the outside world, and it is credited with helping to halt the deportation of Hungarian Jews in July 1944, thereby saving many lives.

The escape

Vrba (born Walter Rosenberg) and Wetzler escaped from Birkenau on 7 April 1944 by hiding for three days in a hollowed-out woodpile beyond the inner perimeter, sprinkling the area with petrol-soaked Russian tobacco to defeat the scent-tracking dogs — a method they had learned from earlier escape attempts. After the search cordon was lifted, they walked south for roughly ten days, reaching Žilina in Slovakia around 21 April 1944.

Contents

With the help of the Bratislava Working Group, an underground Jewish organisation, the two men dictated their account, which was typed up in Slovak and later translated into German and other languages. The report contained precise descriptions of the camp layout, the gas chambers and crematoria, the registration and numbering system, and carefully compiled statistics of transports and deaths. Vrba, who had worked in record-keeping details, was able to reconstruct from memory the approximate number of people murdered at Auschwitz up to that point — around 1.75 million.

Distribution and impact

Copies of the report reached Jewish leaders in Slovakia and Hungary, the Vatican, and Allied diplomats in Switzerland; it was published in the Western press in June and July 1944. Coming as the mass deportation of Hungarian Jews was under way — some 437,000 people deported between May and July 1944 — the publicity, combined with international pressure, is widely believed to have influenced Admiral Miklós Horthy to halt the deportations on 7 July 1944, sparing most of Budapest's remaining Jewish population.

The report later formed part of the 1945 American publication The Auschwitz Protocols and was used as evidence at the Nuremberg trials.
