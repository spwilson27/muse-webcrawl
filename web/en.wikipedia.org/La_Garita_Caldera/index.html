The La Garita Caldera is one of the largest known volcanic calderas on Earth, located in the San Juan Mountains of southwestern Colorado near the town of Creede. It was identified in the 1990s through geological mapping that recognised the extent of its single enormous eruptive deposit, the Fish Canyon Tuff — leading La Garita to be recognised as the source of one of the most voluminous explosive eruptions in the geological record.

The Fish Canyon eruption

About 27.8 million years ago, during the Oligocene ignimbrite flare-up of the San Juan volcanic field, the Fish Canyon eruption discharged an estimated 5,000 cubic kilometres of magma as a vast ignimbrite sheet, the Fish Canyon Tuff, which spread over thousands of square kilometres. The eruption is classed as VEI 8; some researchers have proposed it merits a magnitude of about 9.2, making it larger than the eruptions of Yellowstone or Toba. Collapse of the magma chamber roof formed the elongated caldera, roughly 35 by 75 km.

Geology and later volcanism

The caldera is partly buried by younger volcanic deposits and is cut by later calderas of the San Juan field, including the Bachelor, San Luis and Creede calderas of subsequent eruptions. Mineralisation associated with the region's volcanism produced the Creede mining district's silver ores. The Fish Canyon Tuff is an important dating standard in geochronology because its sanidine crystals give extremely consistent argon-argon ages, used to calibrate the geological timescale.
