Ignition system

An ignition system generates a spark or heats an electrode to a high temperature to ignite a fuel–air mixture in spark-ignition internal combustion engines, oil-fired and gas-fired boilers, and rocket engines. In a petrol engine, the system provides a timed, high-voltage pulse to each spark plug in sequence, synchronized with the rotation of the crankshaft and the position of the pistons.

Types

Early systems used magnetos, which generate their own electricity and remain common in small engines and aircraft. Battery-and-coil (Kettering) systems, introduced in the 1910s, used a contact-breaker (points) and distributor to switch current through an ignition coil. Modern engines employ electronic ignition, in which sensors and an engine control unit time the spark, often with a separate coil for each plug (coil-on-plug), providing more precise timing, stronger sparks, and lower emissions.

Other uses

Beyond engines, ignition systems include igniters for gas appliances, furnaces, and rocket motors, where pyrotechnic or spark igniters initiate combustion in the chamber.
