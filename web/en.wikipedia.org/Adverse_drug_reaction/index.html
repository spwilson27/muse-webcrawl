Adverse drug reaction

SpecialtyPharmacology, clinical medicine
ClassificationType A (augmented, dose-related) and Type B (bizarre, idiosyncratic); extended Rawlins–Thompson scheme A–F
MonitoringPharmacovigilance systems (e.g. Yellow Card, FDA Adverse Event Reporting System)

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful, unintended response to a drug that occurs at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis or therapy. Adverse drug reactions are a significant cause of morbidity and mortality: studies have estimated that they account for a notable percentage of hospital admissions, and classic meta-analyses have ranked fatal ADRs among the leading causes of death in hospitalised patients.

The most widely used classification, proposed by Rawlins and Thompson, divides ADRs into Type A ("augmented") reactions — predictable from the drug's pharmacology, dose-related and common, such as bleeding on anticoagulants or hypoglycaemia on insulin — and Type B ("bizarre") reactions — unpredictable, not dose-related and often immunological or genetic in mechanism, such as anaphylaxis to penicillin or malignant hyperthermia. An extended scheme adds Type C (chronic, dose- and time-related), Type D (delayed), Type E (end-of-use/withdrawal) and Type F (failure of therapy) reactions.

Assessment and prevention

Assessing causality in an individual case uses structured algorithms (such as the Naranjo scale) considering timing, dechallenge and rechallenge. Prevention and detection are the work of pharmacovigilance: spontaneous reporting systems such as the UK's Yellow Card scheme and the US FDA's reporting system gather suspected reactions after marketing, and signals from these data lead to label changes, restrictions or withdrawals — the thalidomide disaster of the early 1960s and the withdrawal of rofecoxib (Vioxx) in 2004 being famous examples. Pharmacogenomics increasingly allows some Type B reactions to be predicted, for example HLA screening before abacavir or carbamazepine.
