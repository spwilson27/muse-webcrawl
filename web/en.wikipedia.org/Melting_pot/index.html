Melting pot

A melting pot is a monocultural metaphor for a heterogeneous society becoming more homogeneous, the different elements "melting together" with a common culture. Historically, it is used to describe the cultural integration of immigrants to the United States.

History of the metaphor

Images of melting metals to describe the fusion of peoples appeared in American writing as early as the 18th century — J. Hector St. John de Crèvecœur wrote of individuals of all nations being "melted into a new race of men" — and Ralph Waldo Emerson used the image of a smelting furnace for America. The term was popularised by Israel Zangwill's 1908 play The Melting Pot, whose hero proclaims America "God's Crucible, the great Melting-Pot where all the races of Europe are melting and re-forming".

Assimilation and debate

The melting pot became the dominant metaphor for assimilation in the mass-immigration era, implying that immigrants would shed their old cultures and merge into a common American identity — in practice often modelled on Anglo-American norms. From the mid-20th century, critics argued the metaphor concealed pressure to conform and the exclusion of non-European groups. Alternative metaphors of cultural pluralism — the "salad bowl", "mosaic" or "kaleidoscope" — propose that ethnic groups retain distinct identities within a shared society.

Use elsewhere

The concept has been applied to other immigrant-receiving countries and, more loosely, to any setting where diverse cultural influences merge, from cities and schools to popular music and cuisine.
