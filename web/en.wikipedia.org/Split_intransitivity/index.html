Split intransitivity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Unaccusative vs. unergative alignment patterns

Split intransitivity (also called split-S alignment) is a linguistic pattern in which the single argument of an intransitive clause is treated grammatically in two different ways, depending on the semantics of the verb. In some languages the subject of an intransitive verb is marked like the agent of a transitive clause for some verbs (the unergative pattern) and like the patient for others (the unaccusative pattern).

Unaccusativity hypothesis

David Perlmutter's unaccusative hypothesis (1978) proposed that intransitive verbs fall into two classes based on whether their surface subject originates as an underlying subject (unergative, e.g. work, laugh) or as an underlying object (unaccusative, e.g. arrive, break). Diagnostics include auxiliary selection in languages such as Italian and Dutch — where unaccusatives select "be" and unergatives select "have" — and the distribution of resultative constructions.

Typology

In languages with fluid or split-S marking, such as many indigenous languages of the Americas and the Caucasus, case marking of the intransitive subject varies by verb class or by properties such as volitionality and telicity. The hierarchy of verb classes involved is often described by semantic determinants including control, causation and affectedness.
