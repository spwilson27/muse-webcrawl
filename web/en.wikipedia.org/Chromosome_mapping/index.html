Chromosome mapping

Chromosome mapping, also called gene mapping or genome mapping, is the process of determining the locations of genes and other markers on a chromosome and the distances between them. Two main kinds of maps exist: genetic (linkage) maps, which order markers according to how often they are inherited together, and physical maps, which give positions in base pairs of DNA. Mapping is a foundational step in identifying genes responsible for inherited diseases and traits.

A genetic map orders loci by recombination frequency, measured in centimorgans.

Genetic linkage mapping

Genes close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together because crossing over between them is rare. By measuring recombination frequencies in crosses, geneticists can arrange genes in linear order and estimate relative distances. In 1913 Alfred Sturtevant, an undergraduate in Thomas Hunt Morgan's fly laboratory, produced the first gene map, of six loci on the Drosophila X chromosome. A recombination frequency of one percent defines one map unit, or centimorgan.

Physical mapping

Physical maps place markers at measured distances in base pairs. Techniques include cytogenetic mapping (locating sequences on banded chromosomes by fluorescence in situ hybridization, FISH), restriction mapping, and the assembly of overlapping cloned fragments (contigs). The Human Genome Project combined genetic and physical maps to organize its sequencing work, culminating in a complete human reference sequence.

Modern practice

Today, whole-genome sequencing and optical mapping have largely replaced classical map construction for model organisms, but linkage mapping remains essential for finding disease genes in families (positional cloning), for quantitative trait locus (QTL) analysis in agriculture, and for validating genome assemblies.
