1972 visit by Richard Nixon to China

The 1972 visit by Richard Nixon to China, formally the visit of President Richard Nixon to the People's Republic of China from 21 to 28 February 1972, was the first visit by a sitting president of the United States to China and a pivotal event in Cold War diplomacy. Nixon met Chairman Mao Zedong and Premier Zhou Enlai, and the visit ended 25 years of no communication or diplomatic ties between the two countries, beginning the rapprochement that led to normalisation of relations in 1979.

Nixon shaking hands with Zhou Enlai on arrival in Beijing, 21 February 1972.

Background

The United States had refused to recognise the People's Republic since 1949, maintaining relations with the Republic of China on Taiwan. Both Washington and Beijing had reasons to draw closer: the Sino-Soviet split had turned violent in the 1969 border clashes, and Nixon and his national security adviser Henry Kissinger sought leverage over the Soviet Union and a path out of the Vietnam War. In April 1971 the American table tennis team was invited to China — "ping-pong diplomacy" — and in July 1971 Kissinger made a secret trip to Beijing via Pakistan, leading Nixon to announce the planned visit on television.

The visit

Nixon arrived in Beijing on 21 February 1972; his handshake with Zhou Enlai at the airport — pointedly reversing John Foster Dulles's refusal to shake Zhou's hand in 1954 — became a famous image. That afternoon Nixon met Mao Zedong for about an hour. In the following days Nixon held long negotiating sessions with Zhou, toured the Great Wall and the Forbidden City, and travelled to Hangzhou and Shanghai. Television coverage brought American audiences their first extensive views of China in decades.

Shanghai Communiqué

The visit concluded with the Shanghai Communiqué (27 February 1972), in which each side stated its positions. The United States acknowledged that "all Chinese on either side of the Taiwan Strait maintain there is but one China and that Taiwan is a part of China", and affirmed the ultimate objective of withdrawing American forces from Taiwan. Both sides pledged to work toward full normalisation and to expand trade and cultural exchanges.

Significance

The opening to China reshaped the Cold War balance, pressuring the Soviet Union toward détente with Washington. Liaison offices were exchanged in 1973, and full diplomatic recognition followed under President Jimmy Carter on 1 January 1979. The phrase "Nixon goes to China" has become a political metaphor for an unexpected, credibility-lending reversal of policy.
