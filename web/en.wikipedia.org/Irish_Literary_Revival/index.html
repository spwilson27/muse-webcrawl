Irish Literary Revival

Periodc. 1885–1939 (also called the Celtic Revival)
Key figuresW. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory, J. M. Synge, Douglas Hyde, George Russell (Æ)
InstitutionsGaelic League (1893), Abbey Theatre (1904), Cuala Press

The Irish Literary Revival, also known as the Celtic Revival, was a flowering of Irish literature, theatre, and cultural nationalism in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Writers sought to create a distinctly Irish literature in English (and to revive the Irish language) drawing on Ireland's mythology, folklore, and peasant life, partly in reaction to centuries of cultural Anglicisation.

Development

Precursors included Standish O'Grady's retellings of heroic Irish legend and the antiquarian scholarship of the 19th century. W. B. Yeats, Lady Gregory, and Edward Martyn founded the Irish Literary Theatre in 1899, which led to the Abbey Theatre (1904), the first national theatre in the English-speaking world. Douglas Hyde's Gaelic League (1893) promoted the Irish language, while Hyde's own work collected and translated Irish folk poetry.

Major works and controversies

J. M. Synge's The Playboy of the Western World (1907) provoked riots at the Abbey for its unsentimental portrayal of rural Ireland. The Revival also encompassed the poetry of Yeats (Nobel Prize, 1923), the plays of Seán O'Casey, and publishers such as the Cuala Press. Younger modernists, notably James Joyce, defined themselves partly against the Revival's idealised Ireland.

Legacy

The Revival is credited with shaping the cultural identity of the Irish Free State and with establishing Irish writing in English as a major world literature.
