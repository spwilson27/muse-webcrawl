Joint enterprise

Joint enterprise
Legal areaCriminal law (England and Wales)
NatureForm of secondary liability for crime
Key caseR v Jogee [2016] UKSC 8

Joint enterprise is a doctrine of English criminal law under which a person who participates in a joint criminal venture can be liable as a secondary party for crimes committed by others in the group. It has been especially controversial in murder cases involving groups, often of young men.

The doctrine

Where two or more people agree to commit a crime (crime A) and, in the course of it, the principal commits a further crime (crime B), a secondary party who intended to assist or encourage crime B — including by conditional intent — can be convicted of it. For about thirty years after Chan Wing-Siu (1985), the law was widely understood to require only that the secondary party foresaw that the principal might commit crime B, a test known as "parasitic accessory liability".

R v Jogee

In 2016 the UK Supreme Court in R v Jogee held that the law had taken a "wrong turn": foresight is evidence from which intent may be inferred, but is not itself the test. The decision led to appeals by some of those convicted under the old understanding, though few convictions were overturned.

Debate

Campaigners argue joint enterprise has disproportionately swept up bystanders and minority-ethnic defendants in gang-related prosecutions; prosecutors respond that it is needed to address group violence where individual roles are hard to prove.
