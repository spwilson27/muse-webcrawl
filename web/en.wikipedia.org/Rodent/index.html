Rodents (from Latin rodere, "to gnaw") are mammals of the order Rodentia, characterised by a single pair of continuously growing incisors in each of the upper and lower jaws. About 40% of all mammal species are rodents — more than 2,200 species — and they are found in vast numbers on all continents except Antarctica, in almost every terrestrial habitat. Familiar rodents include mice, rats, squirrels, prairie dogs, porcupines, beavers, guinea pigs, and hamsters; the largest living rodent is the capybara of South America.

Characteristics

The defining feature of rodents is their dentition: a single pair of prominent, ever-growing incisors in each jaw, separated from the cheek teeth by a gap called the diastema. The incisors have enamel only on the front surface, so the softer dentine behind wears faster, producing a sharp, self-maintaining chisel edge. Because the incisors never stop growing — up to several millimetres a week in some species — rodents must gnaw constantly to keep them worn down. Most rodents are herbivorous, feeding on seeds, stems, and fruit, though many are omnivorous and some specialise on insects or other prey.

Diversity and classification

Rodents range in size from the tiny African pygmy mouse, weighing about 7 grams, to the capybara at up to 65 kg (with extinct relatives such as Josephoartigasia reaching the size of a buffalo). Modern classifications divide the order into suborders including Sciuromorpha (squirrels, marmots, beavers in some schemes), Myomorpha (mice, rats, hamsters, voles, lemmings — the largest group), and Hystricomorpha (porcupines, guinea pigs, capybaras, chinchillas). Rodents occupy niches from burrowing (gophers, mole-rats) and gliding (flying squirrels) to semiaquatic life (beavers, muskrats) and desert specialisation (kangaroo rats, jerboas).

Behaviour and ecology

Most rodents are nocturnal or crepuscular and have keen senses of smell and hearing. Social structures vary from solitary species to the highly organised colonies of naked mole-rats, one of the few eusocial mammals, and the vast prairie dog towns of North America. Rodents are among the most fecund mammals, with short gestations and large, frequent litters — a strategy that supports enormous populations and makes them a crucial food source for predators. They are also important seed dispersers, soil aerators, and ecosystem engineers, most famously in the dam-building of beavers.

Relations with humans

Rodents are simultaneously among humanity's most damaging pests and most useful animals. Rats and mice destroy or contaminate large quantities of stored food, damage buildings, and transmit diseases including plague, leptospirosis, hantavirus, and typhus. Conversely, domesticated rodents serve as pets (guinea pigs, hamsters, fancy rats, gerbils), as food (guinea pigs in the Andes, capybaras), as sources of fur (chinchillas, nutrias), and above all as laboratory animals: the house mouse and brown rat are the principal model organisms of biomedical research, accounting for the great majority of mammals used in science. Invasive rodents, especially rats introduced to islands, have driven many native species to extinction and are targets of large-scale eradication campaigns.
