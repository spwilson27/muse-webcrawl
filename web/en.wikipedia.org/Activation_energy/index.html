Activation energy

Activation energy
SymbolEa
UnitskJ/mol (SI: J/mol)
Introduced bySvante Arrhenius (1889)

In chemistry and physics, activation energy is the minimum amount of energy that must be provided for compounds to result in a chemical reaction. It is the height of the potential energy barrier separating the reactants from the products: only collisions between molecules with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy can lead to reaction.

Arrhenius equation

The concept was introduced by the Swedish scientist Svante Arrhenius in 1889. The dependence of the reaction rate constant k on temperature T and activation energy Ea is given by the Arrhenius equation:

k = A · e−Ea/(RT)

where A is the pre-exponential (frequency) factor and R is the universal gas constant. Because the exponential term rises steeply with temperature, reaction rates typically roughly double for a 10 °C rise in temperature for reactions with typical activation energies near room temperature.

Transition state

At the top of the energy barrier the reacting system passes through an unstable, high-energy configuration called the transition state or activated complex. Transition state theory (the Eyring equation) refines the Arrhenius picture by expressing the barrier in terms of the enthalpy and entropy of activation. The difference in energy between reactants and the transition state determines the rate; the difference between reactants and products determines only the thermodynamics (whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic), not its speed.

Catalysis

A catalyst accelerates a reaction by providing an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, without itself being consumed and without changing the overall energy change of the reaction. Enzymes are biological catalysts that can lower activation energies dramatically, enabling metabolism to proceed at body temperature. Positive catalysts lower the barrier; inhibitors raise it or block active sites.

Negative and barrierless reactions

Some reactions, such as certain radical recombinations and ion–molecule reactions, proceed with effectively zero activation energy, and a few show a negative apparent activation energy because their rates decrease with temperature — usually a sign of a multi-step mechanism with a pre-equilibrium. Fires and explosions illustrate activation energy in everyday life: a fuel–air mixture is stable until a spark supplies the energy needed to surmount the barrier, after which the reaction becomes self-sustaining.
