Appliqué

Appliqué is ornamental needlework in which pieces or patches of fabric, in different shapes and patterns, are sewn or stuck onto a larger piece to form a picture or pattern. It is commonly used as decoration, especially on garments, quilts, and banners. The technique is accomplished either by hand stitching or by machine, and the word derives from the French verb appliquer, "to apply".

Techniques

In needle-turned appliqué, the seam allowance is turned under with the needle as the piece is stitched down, giving a hand-finished look. Raw-edge appliqué leaves the edges unfinished and is often secured by machine satin stitch or fusible webbing. Reverse appliqué — as in the mola panels of the Guna people of Panama and the ralli quilts of Sindh — layers fabrics and cuts away upper layers to reveal colours beneath.

History

Appliqué has been practised since antiquity: ancient Egyptian wall hangings and the leatherwork of Central Asian nomads used applied decoration, and the technique appears in traditions worldwide, from Benin's appliqué banners and the Fon cloths of Abomey to Hawaiian quilts and the album and Baltimore quilts of nineteenth-century America. It remains a popular craft and a feature of contemporary fibre art.
