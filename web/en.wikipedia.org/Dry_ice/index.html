Dry ice
CompositionSolid carbon dioxide (CO2)
Sublimation point−78.5 °C (−109.3 °F) at atmospheric pressure
AppearanceWhite, opaque solid; pellets, blocks or snow

Main usesRefrigeration, shipping of perishables and vaccines, fog effects, blasting

Dry ice is the solid form of carbon dioxide. It is commonly used for temporary refrigeration because at atmospheric pressure it sublimates directly from a solid into a gas at −78.5 °C (−109.3 °F), leaving no liquid residue — hence "dry". It is also used for special effects, industrial cleaning (dry-ice blasting), and the transport of temperature-sensitive goods.

History and manufacture

The French chemist Charles Thilorier first reported solid carbon dioxide in 1835, upon opening a cylinder of the compressed liquid. Commercial production began in the 1920s in the United States, where the name "dry ice" was trademarked in 1925. Industrially, carbon dioxide gas (often recovered from ammonia production or fermentation) is pressurised and cooled into liquid, then expanded: part evaporates, chilling the rest into snow, which is compressed into pellets or blocks.

Uses

Dry ice refrigerates food, biological samples and pharmaceuticals — it was essential to the ultra-cold distribution of some COVID-19 vaccines. In entertainment it generates low-lying fog when placed in warm water. Dry-ice blasting propels pellets to clean machinery without abrasive residue. Other uses include freeze-branding, plumbing repairs, pest control of grain stores and burrowing rodents, and laboratory cold traps.

Safety

Dry ice causes severe frostbite on prolonged skin contact and must be handled with gloves. The subliming gas can displace oxygen and cause asphyxiation in confined spaces, and sealing it in an airtight container can cause the container to burst as pressure builds.
