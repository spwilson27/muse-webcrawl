Stereophonic sound

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A stereo loudspeaker arrangement with a central listening position.

Stereophonic sound, commonly shortened to stereo, is a method of sound reproduction that recreates a multi-directional, three-dimensional audible perspective. This is usually achieved by using two independent audio channels through a configuration of two loudspeakers (or stereo headphones) in such a way as to create the impression of sound heard from various directions, as in natural hearing.

History

The principle was demonstrated by Clément Ader in Paris in 1881, transmitting opera over paired telephone lines. In the 1930s Alan Blumlein at EMI patented the modern techniques of stereo recording, including the coincident microphone pair and stereo disc cutting. Bell Laboratories under Harvey Fletcher gave large-scale demonstrations, and Walt Disney's Fantasia (1940) brought multi-channel sound to cinemas with Fantasound. Commercial stereo LPs arrived in 1957–58, and stereo became the default format for recorded music.

Methods

Recordings may be made with spaced or coincident microphone arrays, by panning monophonic sources between channels in mixing, or binaurally with a dummy head for headphone listening. Because the two channels carry different information, stereo contrasts with monophonic sound, in which all channels are identical.

Balance and playback

Domestic stereo systems place two speakers at roughly 60 degrees to the listener, who sits in the "sweet spot". The balance control adjusts the relative level of the channels. Stereo remains the standard for music distribution, broadcasting and film sound, and is the basis on which surround-sound formats build.
