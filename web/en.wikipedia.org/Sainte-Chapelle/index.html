Sainte-Chapelle

The Sainte-Chapelle (Holy Chapel) is a royal chapel in the Gothic style, within the medieval Palais de la Cité on the Île de la Cité in the centre of Paris, France. It was built in the 1240s by King Louis IX of France to house his collection of Passion relics, including Christ's Crown of Thorns — one of the most important relics in medieval Christendom. The chapel is celebrated for its stained glass: fifteen enormous windows of the upper chapel, among the finest surviving examples of Rayonnant Gothic glass.

History

Louis IX acquired the Crown of Thorns from the Latin Emperor Baldwin II in 1239, purchasing it for 135,000 livres — far more than the cost of building the chapel itself (about 40,000 livres). Other Passion relics followed, including a fragment of the True Cross and the Holy Lance. The chapel was constructed, apparently between 1242 and 1248, possibly under the master mason Pierre de Montreuil, and was consecrated on 26 April 1248. It served as the palace chapel of the kings of France and the shrine of the Crown of Thorns until the relics were transferred to Notre-Dame Cathedral during the French Revolution.

Architecture

The chapel has two levels of equal footprint: a low, crypt-like lower chapel for palace staff, and the taller upper chapel for the king and his court, where the relics were displayed in a great gilded shrine (the Grande Châsse, melted down during the Revolution). The upper chapel's structure is reduced to slender piers between windows, so that the walls appear to be made almost entirely of glass — the defining achievement of the Rayonnant Gothic style.

Stained glass

The fifteen windows of the upper chapel, each about 15 metres high, contain 1,113 scenes from the Old and New Testaments arranged in lancets, roundels, and quatrefoils, telling the story of humanity from Creation to the arrival of the relics in Paris. Two-thirds of the glass is original thirteenth-century work. The rose window of the west front, added in the later fifteenth century, depicts the Apocalypse.

Later history and restoration

The chapel was badly damaged during the Revolution and used as an administrative office and archive store. A celebrated restoration in the nineteenth century, led by Félix Duban, Jean-Baptiste Lassus, and the young Eugène Viollet-le-Duc, returned it close to its thirteenth-century appearance. A further campaign of conservation, including cleaning and protecting the glass, was completed in time for the 850th anniversary of its consecration period commemorations in 2015.
