Rebirth (Buddhism)

Rebirth in Buddhism refers to the teaching that the actions of a person lead to a new existence after death, in an endless cycle called saṃsāra. This cycle is driven by ignorance (avidyā), craving, and aversion, and by karma, the moral consequences of intentional action. Liberation from the cycle — nirvana — is the goal of Buddhist practice.

Distinction from reincarnation

Buddhist rebirth differs from reincarnation in Hindu traditions because Buddhism denies the existence of a permanent, unchanging self or soul (ātman) that transmigrates. The doctrine of anattā (non-self) teaches that what continues from one life to the next is not a soul but a causal continuum of consciousness and karmic tendencies. A classic analogy, used in the Milinda Pañha, compares the process to lighting one candle from another: the flame of the second is neither the same as nor wholly different from the first.

Realms of rebirth

Traditional cosmology describes six realms of rebirth — gods (deva), demigods (asura), humans, animals, hungry ghosts (preta), and hell beings — arranged on the wheel of life. The realm and circumstances of one's rebirth are determined by one's karma. Rebirth as a human is considered especially fortunate because it offers the best opportunity to hear the dharma and attain awakening. Dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) explains the mechanism by which rebirth occurs without any underlying self.
