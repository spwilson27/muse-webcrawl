Cloning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cloning is the process of producing individual organisms with identical genomes, either by natural or artificial means. In nature, some organisms produce clones through asexual reproduction; in biotechnology, molecular cloning refers to making copies of DNA fragments, while cellular cloning produces populations of identical cells. The term usually refers to artificial cloning of whole organisms.

Types

Molecular cloning copies DNA fragments, often by inserting them into plasmid vectors propagated in bacteria. Reproductive cloning creates a multicellular organism genetically identical to a donor, most famously by somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT), in which the nucleus of a body cell is transferred into an enucleated egg cell. Therapeutic cloning aims to produce embryonic stem cells matched to a patient for research and medicine rather than to create a new organism.

History

Plant cloning by cuttings and grafting is ancient. In animals, Hans Spemann proposed nuclear transfer in 1928; frogs were cloned from embryonic cells in 1952 and from more differentiated cells by John Gurdon in 1962. In 1996, researchers at the Roslin Institute in Scotland produced Dolly the sheep, the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell, proving that a differentiated cell's nucleus retains the full genetic information needed to direct development. Since then, many species — including cattle, pigs, cats, dogs, and horses — have been cloned, and some companies offer commercial pet cloning. In 2018, Chinese scientists reported the first cloned primates, the macaques Zhong Zhong and Hua Hua, using fetal cells.

Ethics and law

Cloning raises ethical issues concerning animal welfare (cloning remains inefficient, with many failed pregnancies and abnormalities), conservation (possible use for endangered or extinct species, "de-extinction"), and human cloning. Most countries and international bodies prohibit reproductive cloning of humans, while therapeutic cloning research is regulated to varying degrees.
