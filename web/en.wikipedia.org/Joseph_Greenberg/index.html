Joseph Greenberg

Joseph Harold Greenberg (May 28, 1915 – May 7, 2001) was an American linguist, known mainly for his work in language typology and the genetic classification of languages. He taught for many years at Stanford University.

Work

Greenberg's best-known contribution is his classification of the languages of Africa in The Languages of Africa (1963), in which he proposed four major families: Afroasiatic, Niger–Congo, Nilo-Saharan, and Khoisan. Although achieved by the method of "mass comparison" rather than the traditional comparative method, the classification was broadly accepted for Africa and reshaped the field.

He later applied mass comparison to the indigenous languages of the Americas in Language in the Americas (1987), proposing that nearly all belonged to a single "Amerind" family alongside Na-Dene and Eskimo–Aleut — a claim most specialists rejected. His late work on an "Eurasiatic" macrofamily was similarly controversial.

Typology and universals

Greenberg was also a founder of modern linguistic typology. His 1963 paper on word-order universals identified implicational universals — statements of the form "if a language has property X, then it has property Y" — that influenced generations of typologists. His work on numerical systems and on the markedness concept further shaped the discipline. He served as president of the Linguistic Society of America and was a member of the National Academy of Sciences.
