Revenge tragedy (sometimes referred to as revenge drama, revenge play, or tragedy of blood) is a theatrical genre in which the principal theme is revenge and the protagonist's quest for vengeance, typically for a murder, often leading to the deaths of most of the central characters including the revenger. The genre flourished in early modern English theatre, drawing on the tragedies of the Roman playwright Seneca, with its ghosts, madness, soliloquies and scenes of carnage.

Hamlet, the most famous revenge tragedy, deliberates over avenging his father's murder.

Conventions

  • A secret murder, usually of a ruler or kinsman, revealed to the revenger — often by a ghost demanding vengeance.
  • The revenger's hesitation and deliberation, expressed in soliloquies, and real or feigned madness.
  • A play-within-the-play, disguise, intrigue and counter-plotting by the villain.
  • Escalating violence and a finale strewn with corpses, in which the revenger also dies.

Major works

Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) established the genre in England, featuring the ghost of Don Andrea and the revenger Hieronimo. William Shakespeare's Hamlet (c. 1600) is its most celebrated example; his earlier Titus Andronicus is a blood-soaked Senecan exercise. Jacobean successors include John Webster's The Duchess of Malfi, Thomas Middleton's (or Cyril Tourneur's) The Revenger's Tragedy, and plays by John Marston, John Ford and Philip Massinger.

Themes

Revenge tragedies explore the conflict between the private duty of vengeance and public justice, the corruption of courts where law fails, providence, madness, and the moral cost of revenge — the revenger typically becomes as tainted as the villain he destroys. Modern criticism, from Fredson Bowers' foundational study to feminist and new-historicist readings, continues to debate whether the plays endorse or condemn their revengers.

Legacy

The genre's devices — the ghost, the feigned madness, the catastrophic finale — passed into later drama, Gothic fiction, and modern revenge films.
