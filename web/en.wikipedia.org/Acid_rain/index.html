Acid rain

Acid rain is rain or any other form of precipitation that is unusually acidic, meaning that it has elevated levels of hydrogen ions (low pH). Unpolluted rain is slightly acidic (pH ≈ 5.6) because of dissolved carbon dioxide, but acid rain typically has a pH of 4.2–4.4, driven by sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) emitted by burning fossil fuels, which react in the atmosphere to form sulfuric and nitric acids.

Effects

Acid deposition acidifies lakes and streams, harming fish and aquatic life, especially in regions with thin, poorly buffered soils such as Scandinavia, Canada and the Adirondacks. It damages forests by leaching nutrients and releasing toxic aluminium, corrodes limestone and marble buildings and monuments, and contributes to respiratory illness as a component of particulate air pollution.

History and policy

The term was coined in 1872 by Scottish chemist Robert Angus Smith, who studied precipitation around Manchester. The issue became prominent in the 1970s–80s, leading to the 1979 Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution in Europe and the 1990 Amendments to the U.S. Clean Air Act, whose cap-and-trade programme for SO₂ is considered a landmark success in environmental economics. Emissions of SO₂ have since fallen sharply in North America and Europe, though acid deposition remains a problem in parts of Asia.
