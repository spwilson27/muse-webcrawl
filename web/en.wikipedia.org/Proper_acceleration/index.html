Proper acceleration

[Placeholder image: Spacetime diagram showing a worldline of constant proper acceleration]

In relativity theory, proper acceleration is the physical acceleration (i.e., measurable acceleration as by an accelerometer) experienced by an object. It is thus acceleration relative to a free-fall, or inertial, observer who is momentarily at rest relative to the object being measured. An accelerometer at rest in a gravitational field measures proper acceleration because the surface on which it rests prevents it from following a free-fall (geodesic) path.

Proper acceleration contrasts with coordinate acceleration, which is the rate of change of velocity relative to a chosen coordinate system and depends on that choice. At low speeds and in a single spatial dimension, proper acceleration α and coordinate acceleration a are related by α = γ³a, where γ is the Lorentz factor. A classic result is that an object undergoing constant proper acceleration follows a hyperbolic worldline in spacetime, x(τ) = (c²/α)(cosh(ατ/c) − 1), a motion known as hyperbolic motion.

Definition

Proper acceleration is the spatial part of the four-acceleration, the derivative of four-velocity with respect to proper time. Its magnitude is invariant: all observers agree on its value. For motion in a straight line, the proper acceleration equals the rate of change of rapidity multiplied by c. In general relativity, an object in free fall has zero proper acceleration even though its coordinate acceleration (for example, a falling stone accelerating toward the ground in Earth-fixed coordinates) may be non-zero; this is the essence of the equivalence principle, by which gravity is not a force.

Examples

A person standing on Earth's surface experiences an upward proper acceleration of about 9.8 m/s² (1 g), supplied by the normal force of the ground. Astronauts in orbit, by contrast, are in free fall and measure near-zero proper acceleration (microgravity), despite their large coordinate acceleration toward Earth. Travellers in a hypothetical rocket accelerating at a constant 1 g would, according to special relativity, experience ever-increasing relativistic effects, including time dilation relative to inertial observers, and would approach but never reach the speed of light.

Relation to force

In the instantaneous rest frame of an accelerating object, Newton's second law holds in the form F = mα, where F is the net (proper) force and α the proper acceleration. For a charged particle in electromagnetic fields and for rockets, proper acceleration is the quantity directly linked to thrust and to the g-forces felt by passengers.
