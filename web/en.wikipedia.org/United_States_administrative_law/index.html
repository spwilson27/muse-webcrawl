United States administrative law is the body of law governing the procedures, powers, and judicial review of the administrative agencies of the United States federal government. It encompasses executive orders, administrative rules and regulations, and case law on the legality of agency action. The foundational statute is the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) of 1946.

Sources of administrative law

Administrative law arises from the Constitution, statutes enacted by Congress (including the "organic" or enabling statutes that create agencies and delegate authority), the APA, executive orders, and the regulations agencies issue. Two volumes record agency output: the Federal Register, published daily with proposed and final rules, and the Code of Federal Regulations, the codified body of general and permanent rules.

Rulemaking

Most substantive rules are made through informal "notice-and-comment" rulemaking under APA § 553: the agency publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register, accepts public comments for a set period, and then publishes a final rule with a statement of basis and purpose responding to significant comments. Formal rulemaking — requiring trial-type hearings — is rare. Agencies also issue interpretive rules and policy statements exempt from notice-and-comment, though the line between legislative and non-legislative rules is heavily litigated.

Adjudication

Agencies adjudicate disputes such as benefit claims, immigration cases, and enforcement actions. Formal adjudications are heard by administrative law judges (ALJs), whose independence the APA protects. Decisions are typically reviewable within the agency and then by federal courts.

Judicial review

Under APA § 706, courts may set aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, contrary to constitutional right, or in excess of statutory authority. The Supreme Court's 1984 decision in Chevron U.S.A. v. Natural Resources Defense Council long required courts to defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes; in 2024, Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo overruled Chevron, holding that courts must exercise independent judgment on questions of statutory interpretation. Courts apply the "hard look" review of Motor Vehicle Manufacturers Association v. State Farm to agency reasoning, and the major questions doctrine — articulated in West Virginia v. EPA (2022) — requires clear congressional authorization for decisions of vast economic and political significance.

Constitutional debates

Administrative law is the focus of continuing debates over the separation of powers: the nondelegation doctrine, the constitutionality of independent agencies insulated from presidential removal (upheld in Humphrey's Executor, 1935, but narrowed in Seila Law v. CFPB, 2020), and the status of ALJs under the Appointments Clause (Lucia v. SEC, 2018).
