Pseudonymity is the state of using a pseudonym — a fictitious or assumed name — in place of one's real name. A pseudonymous identity differs from a fully anonymous one: actions performed under a pseudonym can be linked together and attributed to the same persistent persona, building a reputation, even though the persona is not (or not openly) connected to a legal identity.

In literature and the arts

Writers have long published under pen names: Samuel Clemens wrote as Mark Twain, Mary Ann Evans as George Eliot, and the Brontë sisters initially published as Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell. Pseudonyms have been adopted to conceal gender, to evade censorship or persecution, to separate genres, or simply for artistic effect. In music and theatre, equivalent stage names are common.

On the Internet

Online communities are typically organised around persistent pseudonyms: usernames, handles and avatars. Cryptographer David Chaum and later researchers formalised the concept — a pseudonym can be a public key, whose holder proves authorship of messages by signing them without revealing a legal identity. Satoshi Nakamoto, the pseudonymous creator of Bitcoin, is a prominent example. Pseudonymity underlies the reputation systems of forums, wikis and open-source projects.

Pseudonymity and anonymity

Anonymity means actions cannot be linked either to a legal identity or to each other; pseudonymity relaxes the second property, allowing linkability to a stable persona. This makes accountability and reputation possible while still protecting the underlying identity. However, pseudonyms can sometimes be de-anonymised ("doxed") by correlating writing style, metadata or behaviour across contexts.

Legal and social aspects

The right to publish pseudonymously has been defended as an aspect of freedom of expression — the United States Supreme Court has recognised a right to anonymous speech, historically exercised in works such as The Federalist Papers, published under the name "Publius". Conversely, some platforms and jurisdictions impose real-name policies, arguing they improve civility or traceability.
