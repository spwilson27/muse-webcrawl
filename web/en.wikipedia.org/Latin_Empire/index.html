The Latin Empire, known to its rulers as the Empire of Romania (Latin: Imperium Romaniae), was a feudal Crusader state founded by leaders of the Fourth Crusade on lands captured from the Byzantine Empire. Established after the sack of Constantinople in April 1204, it was intended to replace the Byzantine Empire as the Roman Empire in the east, with a Latin (Catholic) emperor and patriarch. It lasted until 1261, when the Byzantine Greek Empire of Nicaea retook the capital.

History

By the Partitio terrarum imperii Romaniae, the conquerors divided the Byzantine lands between the emperor, the Venetians — who took three-eighths, including Crete and key ports — and the crusader barons, who carved out vassal states such as the Kingdom of Thessalonica, the Principality of Achaea and the Duchy of Athens. Baldwin of Flanders was crowned first emperor but was captured by the Bulgarians at Adrianople in 1205 and never seen again. The empire, perpetually short of money and men, shrank in the face of the Byzantine successor states of Nicaea and Epirus and of the Second Bulgarian Empire; its emperors were reduced to pledging relics — most famously the Crown of Thorns, sold to Louis IX of France — and even stripping lead from palace roofs.

Fall

On 25 July 1261 a small Nicaean force under Alexios Strategopoulos slipped into Constantinople while the Latin garrison and Venetian fleet were away, and Michael VIII Palaiologos was crowned in the restored Byzantine Empire. The last Latin emperor, Baldwin II, fled; his descendants and the titular emperors of the Angevin and Courtenay lines pressed claims in the west for generations, while several Latin states in Greece — the Principality of Achaea, the Duchy of Athens and the Venetian colonies — survived for centuries longer.

Legacy

The Latin Empire deepened the schism between the Greek East and Latin West: the memory of 1204 remains a byword in Orthodox–Catholic relations, and Pope John Paul II formally apologised for the sack in 2001. It also shifted Mediterranean trade decisively towards Venice.
