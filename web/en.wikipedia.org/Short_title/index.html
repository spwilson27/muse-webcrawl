Short title

A short title is the brief formal name by which a statute is cited.

In certain jurisdictions, a short title is the formal short name by which a piece of primary legislation may be cited. Many statutes are also given a long title: a fuller statement, conventionally beginning "An Act to ...", which describes the legislation's purposes and scope. The short title, by contrast, is a concise name — typically a few words followed by the year of enactment — that serves purely as a label for reference.

Practice by jurisdiction

In the United Kingdom, Acts of Parliament were historically cited by regnal year and chapter number; from the nineteenth century they acquired official short titles, and the Short Titles Act 1896 retrospectively assigned short titles to many earlier statutes. A British short title usually takes the form "X Act 2025"; a provision known as the citation section typically declares that "This Act may be cited as the X Act 2025". In the United States, federal statutes commonly declare a short title in their first section (for example, the "Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act"), though they are also cited by their location in the Statutes at Large or the United States Code. Similar practices exist in Canada, Australia, Ireland and other common-law legislatures.

Usage

The short title does not limit the meaning of the statute; courts treat it as a label rather than an interpretive aid of any great weight. Short titles sometimes become far better known than the legislation's content — and political nicknames and abbreviations (such as "Obamacare") are not official short titles at all.
