Breakwater
TypeCoastal defence structure
PurposeWave energy reduction, harbour shelter
Common formsRubble mound, caisson, floating

A breakwater is a permanent structure constructed at a coastal area to protect against tides, currents, waves, and storm surges. Breakwaters create calmer water in harbours so that ships can load and berth safely, and they reduce beach erosion by weakening wave energy before it reaches the shore.


A breakwater armoured with concrete units.

Types

Rubble mound breakwaters are heaps of rock or concrete armour units — such as tetrapods, accropodes, or dolosse — with voids that dissipate wave energy. Caisson breakwaters consist of large concrete boxes sunk into place. Floating breakwaters are anchored buoyant structures suitable for moderate wave climates, and submerged reefs can perform a similar function with less visual impact.

Effects

By interrupting longshore drift, breakwaters can cause sand to accumulate on one side (accretion) and erosion on the other. Detached breakwaters are often built parallel to a beach to form a sheltered zone in which salients or tombolos of sand build up. They also create habitats for marine organisms, though they can alter local ecosystems.

Notable examples

Among the largest are the breakwaters at Plymouth in England, begun in 1812, and the harbour works at Cherbourg and Dover. Ancient harbour moles, such as those of the Phoenicians and Romans, are early examples of the same principle.
