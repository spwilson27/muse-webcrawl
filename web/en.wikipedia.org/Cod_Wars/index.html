Cod Wars

The Cod Wars (Icelandic: Þorskastríðin) were a series of confrontations between the United Kingdom and Iceland between the late 1940s and 1976 over fishing rights in the North Atlantic. Each of the disputes ended in Iceland's favour, as the Icelandic government successively extended its exclusive fishing zone from 4 to 12, then 50, and finally 200 nautical miles (370 km), forcing British trawlers out of the rich fishing grounds around Iceland.

Background

Fish, above all cod, was the foundation of Iceland's economy, accounting for the great majority of its exports. As foreign trawlers — especially from Britain and West Germany — fished ever closer to Iceland's coasts with increasingly efficient vessels, Iceland sought to protect its stocks and its livelihood by unilaterally extending its territorial waters and fishing limits, steps most other maritime states initially refused to recognize.

The three Cod Wars

The First Cod War (1958–1961) followed Iceland's extension of its fishing limit to 12 nautical miles. Royal Navy warships escorted British trawlers inside the zone, and incidents occurred, but a settlement in 1961 saw Britain accept the 12-mile limit. The Second Cod War (1972–1973) began when Iceland extended the limit to 50 miles; Icelandic coast guard vessels cut the trawl wires of British trawlers, and an agreement allowed a limited British catch. The Third Cod War (1975–1976) followed the move to 200 miles and was the most serious: there were dozens of ramming incidents, Iceland broke off diplomatic relations with Britain — the first such rupture between NATO allies — and threatened to leave NATO and close the strategically vital base at Keflavík. Under American and NATO pressure, Britain conceded in June 1976, accepting the 200-mile limit.

Aftermath

The Cod Wars were a striking success for a small state: Iceland, with no army and only a handful of patrol vessels, prevailed over a major naval power by leveraging its strategic importance during the Cold War. The 200-mile exclusive economic zone Iceland fought for soon became the international standard under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. The loss of the grounds devastated British distant-water fishing ports such as Hull and Grimsby.
