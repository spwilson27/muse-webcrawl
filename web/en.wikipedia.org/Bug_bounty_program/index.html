Bug bounty program

A bug bounty program is a deal offered by many websites, organizations, and software developers by which individuals can receive recognition and compensation for reporting software bugs, especially those pertaining to security exploits and vulnerabilities. These programs allow developers to discover and resolve bugs before the general public is aware of them, preventing incidents of widespread abuse.

History

The first well-known bug bounty was offered by Netscape in 1995, which paid cash rewards to users who found bugs in its Navigator browser. The practice spread slowly until the 2010s, when major technology companies including Google, Microsoft, Facebook, and Apple launched large programs, and intermediary platforms such as HackerOne and Bugcrowd made managed programs widely available.

Operation

Programs typically publish a scope defining which systems may be tested and which findings qualify, along with rules of engagement and a reward table based on severity. Responsible disclosure terms give researchers safe harbor from legal action for good-faith testing. Payments range from small sums to more than a million dollars for critical vulnerabilities in widely used products.

Criticism

Critics note that bounty amounts can be small relative to the value of vulnerabilities on illicit markets, that triage can be slow or inconsistent, and that programs may be used to obtain security research cheaply. Supporters argue the programs crowdsource testing at scale and build trust with the research community.
