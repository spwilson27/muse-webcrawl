The Rock Garden of Chandigarh, also known as Nek Chand's Rock Garden, is a sculpture garden in Chandigarh, India, created by Nek Chand Saini (1924–2015), a government road inspector who began building the garden secretly in 1957 on a forested gorge near Sukhna Lake. Constructed almost entirely from industrial and household waste — broken ceramics, glass bangles, tiles, electrical fittings, and discarded pottery — it has grown into one of India's most celebrated works of outsider art.

History

Nek Chand, who had been displaced from his home village by the Partition of India, worked as a road inspector for the Public Works Department during the construction of Chandigarh, the new modernist capital designed under Le Corbusier. In his spare time he began clearing a small patch of jungle in a gorge and arranging stones and discarded materials into sculptures and winding chambers, working in secret for some eighteen years because the land was a protected forest buffer where construction was forbidden.

When the authorities discovered the garden in 1975, it had already grown to about 13 acres with hundreds of sculptures. Although technically illegal, public support mobilised behind it, and instead of demolishing the garden the administration officially inaugurated it in 1976 and gave Nek Chand a salary and a workforce of labourers to expand it.

Description

The garden is laid out as a labyrinth of interconnected chambers, narrow passageways, arches, waterfalls, and open-air courtyards populated by thousands of figures — humans, dancers, musicians, animals, and gods — fashioned from recycled materials. Broken bathroom fittings become bodies; glass bangles form colourful bands; slag and rubble are worked into cliffs and grottoes. Later phases added large mosaic-covered structures, an amphitheatre, and elaborate water features fed by recycled water channels.

A distinctive feature is the use of "raw" and waste material to evoke the lost village of Nek Chand's childhood, and the garden's informal layout contrasts sharply with the rationalist grid of Chandigarh around it.

Threats and preservation

In 1996, while Nek Chand was abroad, the city administration withdrew funding and workmen briefly faced the prospect of the garden's demolition to make way for a road; a public campaign and international attention saved it. Smuggling of sculptures by collectors has also been a recurring problem. In 1997 the garden appeared on an Indian postage stamp. Nek Chand received the Padma Shri in 1984, and his foundation continues to maintain the site, which remains one of Chandigarh's most visited attractions.
