Gundestrup cauldron

The Gundestrup cauldron is a richly decorated silver vessel, dating to the late La Tène period (roughly the 2nd or 1st century BC), found in a peat bog near Gundestrup in Jutland, Denmark, in 1891. It is the largest known example of European Iron Age silverwork and is now in the National Museum of Denmark in Copenhagen.

Description

The cauldron consists of thirteen plates — a round base plate, five interior plates and seven (originally eight) exterior plates — of almost pure silver, partly gilded, measuring about 42 cm high and 69 cm in diameter. The plates are covered with repoussé images of gods, goddesses, warriors, animals and mythological scenes. One famous interior plate shows a large figure dipping a warrior into a vat, often interpreted as a scene of sacrifice or rebirth; another shows a horned figure, usually identified as the god Cernunnos, seated cross-legged among animals.

Origin and interpretation

Where and by whom the cauldron was made is debated: the workmanship suggests Thracian silversmiths of south-eastern Europe, while much of the imagery is Celtic, including torcs, carnyx war trumpets and Celtic deities. It may have been captured as booty or traded north, then ritually deposited in the bog as an offering. The cauldron remains a key source for Celtic religion and iconography.
