Thatching

Thatching is the craft of building a roof with dry vegetation such as straw, water reed, sedge, rushes, heather, or palm branches, layering the vegetation so as to shed water away from the inner roof. It is a very old roofing method and has been used in both tropical and temperate climates. Thatch is still employed by rural communities in many parts of the world, and in some countries, notably England, it survives as a valued heritage feature of vernacular architecture.

Materials and method

The principal materials in Britain are long straw (from specially grown wheat), combed wheat reed, and water reed, mainly from the Norfolk Broads and imported from eastern Europe. The thatcher fixes bundles of the material (yealms) to the roof battens with spars of twisted hazel, working in courses from the eaves upward; the outer surface weathers to a hard, water-shedding crust. A good water-reed roof may last 50–60 years, with the ridge — often finished in a decorative flush or block pattern — renewed every 10–15 years. Because the material insulates well, thatched roofs are warm in winter and cool in summer.

History

Thatch was the standard roofing of rural Europe until cheap Welsh slate and tile spread with industrial transport in the nineteenth century. Fire risk, once a serious objection, is mitigated by modern fire barriers and retardants, and most thatch fires start in the chimney rather than the roof itself. Roughly 60,000 thatched buildings survive in the United Kingdom, and a revival of the craft has made thatching a recognised heritage trade.
