Jaynes–Cummings model

The Jaynes–Cummings model (sometimes abbreviated JCM) is a theoretical model in quantum optics. It describes the system of a two-level atom interacting with a quantized mode of an optical cavity (or a bosonic field), with or without the presence of light (in the form of a bath of electromagnetic radiation that can cause spontaneous emission and absorption). It was originally developed to study the interaction of atoms with the quantized electromagnetic field in order to investigate the phenomena of spontaneous emission and absorption of photons in a cavity.

History

The model was first proposed in 1963 by Edwin Jaynes and Fred Cummings in their paper "Comparison of quantum and semiclassical radiation theories with application to the beam maser". The model was developed with the intention of giving a detailed account of the effects of natural experiments, such as those performed by George E. Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit in the 1920s on the behaviour of atoms in magnetic fields.

Formulation

The Hamiltonian describing the Jaynes–Cummings model consists of a free field part, an atomic excitation part and an interaction part. In the rotating wave approximation, the interaction term takes a simple form in which the atom exchanges energy with the field mode by absorbing or emitting a single photon at a time. The model is exactly solvable, which makes it one of the most important and widely used models in quantum optics.

A hallmark prediction of the model is the phenomenon of collapse and revival of Rabi oscillations: when an atom interacts with a coherent field, the probability of finding the atom in its excited state oscillates with an amplitude that first decays (collapse) and later reappears (revival), a purely quantum-mechanical effect with no classical analogue.

Applications

The Jaynes–Cummings model underlies much of cavity quantum electrodynamics and circuit quantum electrodynamics, and its generalizations (such as the Tavis–Cummings model for many atoms) are central to the study of quantum information processing with superconducting qubits and trapped ions.
