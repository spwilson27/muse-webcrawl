Dowry


A dowry transfers wealth from the bride's family at marriage.

A dowry is a transfer of parental property, gifts, or money at the marriage of a daughter, given by the bride's family to the groom or his family. It is the counterpart of bride price, paid by the groom's side, and of dower, property settled on the bride herself.

History

Dowries are documented from ancient Babylon, Greece, and Rome through medieval and early modern Europe, where they helped establish a new household and provided a daughter's inheritance in advance. In England, expectations of dowry shaped aristocratic marriage markets for centuries; the practice declined with changes in women's property rights in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Modern controversies

Dowry remains common in parts of South Asia despite legal prohibitions — India banned the practice with the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961. Dowry demands are associated with harassment, domestic violence, and "dowry deaths," prompting sustained activism and further legislation. In other regions the practice has transformed into voluntary wedding gifts or contributions to the couple.
