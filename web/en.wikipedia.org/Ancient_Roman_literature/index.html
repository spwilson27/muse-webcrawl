Ancient Roman literature

Ancient Roman literature, written in Latin, developed from the 3rd century BC under strong Greek influence and flourished through the Republic and Empire. Roman authors adapted Greek genres — epic, tragedy, comedy, history, oratory — to Roman themes and created new ones, notably satire and the novel.

Early and Republican literature

Livius Andronicus translated Homer's Odyssey into Latin; Plautus and Terence wrote comedies adapted from Greek New Comedy; Ennius composed the epic Annales. The late Republic produced Lucretius' philosophical poem De Rerum Natura, Catullus' lyric poetry, Caesar's war commentaries, Sallust's histories, and the speeches and treatises of Cicero, the master of Latin prose.

The Augustan Age

Under Augustus, literature reached a "Golden Age": Virgil's Aeneid, Eclogues, and Georgics; Horace's odes, satires, and Ars Poetica; Ovid's Metamorphoses; and the elegies of Tibullus and Propertius. Livy wrote his monumental history of Rome.

Imperial literature

The "Silver Age" included Seneca's philosophy and tragedies, Lucan's epic, Pliny's letters and Natural History, Tacitus' histories, Juvenal's satires, Martial's epigrams, Suetonius' biographies, and Apuleius' novel The Golden Ass.
