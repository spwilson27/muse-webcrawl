Alzheimer's disease is a neurodegenerative disease that usually starts slowly and progressively worsens. It is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for an estimated 60–70% of cases worldwide. The disease is named after the German psychiatrist Alois Alzheimer, who described the case of Auguste Deter in 1906.

Signs and symptoms

The earliest symptom is often difficulty remembering recent events. As the disease advances, symptoms can include problems with language, disorientation, mood swings, loss of motivation, self-neglect, and behavioural issues. Gradually, bodily functions are lost, ultimately leading to death; life expectancy after diagnosis is typically three to nine years.

Causes and pathology

The cause of most cases remains poorly understood, though genetic factors — notably the APOE ε4 allele — increase risk, and rare early-onset familial forms are caused by mutations in the APP, PSEN1, and PSEN2 genes. In the brain, Alzheimer's is characterised by extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of tau protein, accompanied by loss of neurons and synapses, particularly in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on medical history, cognitive testing, brain imaging, and increasingly biomarker tests of cerebrospinal fluid or blood. There is no cure. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine offer modest symptomatic benefit, and monoclonal antibodies such as lecanemab, approved in 2023, can modestly slow decline in early disease. Prevention research points to cardiovascular health, physical exercise, and cognitive engagement as protective factors.
