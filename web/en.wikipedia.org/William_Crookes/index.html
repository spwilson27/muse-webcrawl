William Crookes

Sir William Crookes
Born: 17 June 1832, London
Died: 4 April 1919, London
Known for: Discovery of thallium; Crookes tube; radiometer

Sir William Crookes (17 June 1832 – 4 April 1919) was a British chemist and physicist who attended the Royal College of Chemistry in London and worked on spectroscopy. He is noted for discovering the element thallium, for his vacuum tubes (Crookes tubes), and for inventing the Crookes radiometer. He was a pioneer of vacuum tubes, inventing the Crookes tube in 1875, and was one of the first scientists to investigate plasma, which he called "radiant matter."

Thallium and spectroscopy

In 1861 Crookes announced the discovery of a new element with a bright green emission line in its spectrum, which he named thallium, from the Greek thallos for a green shoot. His work on thallium's atomic weight, conducted in a vacuum balance to eliminate buoyancy errors, occupied him for a decade and earned him election to the Royal Society in 1863.

Vacuum tubes and radiant matter

Crookes's researches into electrical discharges in rarefied gases produced the Crookes tube, a partially evacuated glass tube with electrodes at either end. His experiments with cathode rays — showing that they travel in straight lines, cast shadows, and can turn a small paddle wheel — suggested they were streams of particles, paving the way for J. J. Thomson's identification of the electron. In 1879 he described the plasma state as a "fourth state of matter."

His radiometer (1873), whose vanes rotate when exposed to light, arose from anomalies in his precision weighings; its motion is driven by thermal effects in the residual gas, not by radiation pressure itself.

Psychical research

Crookes also investigated spiritualist mediums, controversially endorsing the phenomena of Florence Cook and D. D. Home; these investigations damaged his scientific reputation among sceptical colleagues, though he maintained they were conducted impartially.

Honours

Crookes was knighted in 1897, served as president of the Royal Society from 1913 to 1915, and received the Royal Medal, the Davy Medal, and the Copley Medal.
