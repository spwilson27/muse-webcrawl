Raisin

A raisin is a dried grape. Raisins are produced in many regions of the world and may be eaten raw or used in cooking, baking, and brewing. In the United Kingdom, Ireland, New Zealand, and Australia, the word raisin is reserved for the dark-colored dried large grape, with sultana being a golden-colored dried grape, and currant a dried small Black Corinth grape.

Raisins produced from Thompson Seedless grapes, the most common variety in California.

Production

Raisins are produced by drying grape berries, traditionally in the sun on paper trays laid between vineyard rows, or by mechanical dehydration after a brief dip in hot water or alkaline solution that cracks the skin and speeds drying. Most of the world's raisins are made from the Thompson Seedless grape (known as the sultana in many countries). Major producing regions include California's San Joaquin Valley, Turkey, Iran, Greece, Chile, and South Africa.

Nutrition and uses

Raisins are about 60% sugars (mainly fructose and glucose) and provide dietary fiber, potassium, iron, and boron. They are used in cereals, trail mixes, baked goods such as raisin bread and fruitcake, and dishes such as couscous and curries. The word "raisin" comes from the Latin racemus, "a cluster of berries". Dogs and cats can suffer kidney failure from eating raisins and grapes, though the toxic agent has only recently been identified as tartaric acid.

History

Grape drying was practiced in ancient Persia and Egypt, and raisins were prized in Roman times as sweeteners, snacks, and even currency. Spanish missionaries introduced raisin grapes to Mexico and California, where a heat wave in 1873 that dried grapes on the vine helped launch the Californian industry, organized later by the Sun-Maid cooperative (founded 1912).
