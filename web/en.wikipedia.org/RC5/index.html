In cryptography, RC5 is a symmetric-key block cipher notable for its simplicity. Designed by Ronald Rivest of RSA Security in 1994, "RC" stands for "Rivest Cipher", or alternatively "Ron's Code". Unlike many designs, RC5 has variable block size, key size and number of rounds; the common parameterisation RC5-32/12/16 uses 32-bit words, 12 rounds and a 16-byte key.

Algorithm

RC5 features a Feistel-like network operating on two word-sized blocks. Encryption uses expanded round keys generated by a key schedule seeded with the constants derived from e and the golden ratio φ ("nothing up my sleeve" numbers). Each round applies XOR, modular addition and — distinctively — data-dependent rotations, in which the rotation amount is determined by bits of the data itself, frustrating differential and linear cryptanalysis. Decryption is straightforward and nearly identical in structure.

Security and use

RC5 was patented by RSA Security (patent expired 2015) and used in products including early TLS/SSL suites and the SET protocol. Twelve-round RC5 has been subject to differential attacks with large amounts of chosen plaintext, but recommended versions with 18–20 rounds have resisted practical attack; distributed.net famously brute-forced short-key RC5 challenges (breaking 56- and 64-bit keys after years of computation) to demonstrate the inadequacy of short keys. Its successor is RC6, an AES finalist.
