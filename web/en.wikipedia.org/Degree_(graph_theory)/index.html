Degree (graph theory)

In graph theory, the degree (or valency) of a vertex of a graph is the number of edges incident to the vertex. In a graph with loops, each loop contributes 2 to the degree of its vertex. The degree of a vertex v is commonly denoted deg(v) or degG(v).

Basic properties

The handshaking lemma, proved by Euler in 1736 in connection with the Seven Bridges of Königsberg, states that the sum of the degrees of all vertices of a finite undirected graph equals twice the number of edges, because each edge contributes exactly 2 to the total. An immediate corollary is that every graph has an even number of vertices of odd degree. A vertex of degree 0 is isolated; a vertex of degree 1 is a leaf. The maximum degree of a graph G is denoted Δ(G) and the minimum degree δ(G).

Degree sequences

The degree sequence of a graph is the list of its vertex degrees in non-increasing order. Not every sequence of non-negative integers is realisable by a graph: the Erdős–Gallai theorem characterises the "graphic" sequences, and the Havel–Hakimi algorithm constructs a realisation when one exists.

Directed graphs

In a directed graph, the degree splits into the in-degree (number of edges directed into the vertex) and the out-degree (number directed out of it). Summed over all vertices, total in-degree equals total out-degree, both equalling the number of edges.

Special graphs

A graph in which every vertex has the same degree k is called k-regular; cubic graphs (3-regular) are a major object of study. In network science, the degree distribution of a network — the fraction of vertices of each degree — distinguishes random graphs (Poisson-like) from scale-free networks (power-law).
