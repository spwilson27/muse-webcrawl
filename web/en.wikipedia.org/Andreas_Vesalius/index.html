Andreas Vesalius (31 December 1514 – 15 October 1564) was a Flemish anatomist and physician, widely regarded as the founder of modern human anatomy. His great work, De humani corporis fabrica libri septem ("On the fabric of the human body, in seven books", 1543), revolutionised the study of anatomy by insisting on direct observation and dissection of human cadavers rather than reliance on ancient authority.

Life

Vesalius was born in Brussels into a family of physicians and apothecaries serving the Habsburg court. He studied at Leuven and Paris, where he trained under Jacobus Sylvius in the Galenic tradition, then took his doctorate at Padua in 1537 and was immediately appointed professor of surgery and anatomy. At Padua he performed public dissections himself — breaking with the custom by which professors lectured from a raised chair while barber-surgeons cut — and drew on executed criminals' bodies for systematic study.

The Fabrica

Published in Basel in 1543, when Vesalius was 28, the Fabrica corrected more than 200 errors in Galen, whose anatomy had been based on animal dissection: it showed, for example, that the human jaw is a single bone and that the breastbone has three segments, not seven. Its woodcut illustrations, produced by artists from Titian's circle, set new standards for anatomical art. Vesalius later served as physician to Emperor Charles V and to Philip II of Spain. He died in 1564 on the island of Zakynthos, returning from a pilgrimage to the Holy Land.

Legacy

The Fabrica marks a turning point in the history of science: observation over dogma. Vesalius's students at Padua, including Falloppio, carried his method forward, and his work stands alongside Copernicus's De revolutionibus, published the same year, as an emblem of the Scientific Revolution.
