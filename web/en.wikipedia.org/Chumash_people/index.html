The Chumash people are a Native American people of the central and southern coastal regions of present-day California, in portions of what are now Morro Bay in the north to Malibu in the south, and including the northern Channel Islands. Their territory centered on the modern counties of Santa Barbara, Ventura, and San Luis Obispo.

History

Archaeological evidence indicates the Chumash and their ancestors occupied the region for thousands of years, developing one of the most complex hunter-gatherer societies in North America. They built plank canoes called tomol, which allowed trade and travel between the mainland and the Channel Islands.

Spanish contact began with the expedition of Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo in 1542. During the mission era, beginning in 1769, many Chumash were brought into the Franciscan missions, where disease and forced labor drastically reduced their population. In 1824, Chumash people at several missions staged a significant revolt against Spanish and Mexican authorities.

Culture

The Chumash are renowned for their intricate basketry, rock art (including polychrome pictographs), and shell bead money, which circulated widely in trade networks across California. Their society was organized into chiefdoms with hereditary chiefs, specialized craftspeople, and a religious system centered on celestial beings.

Languages

The Chumashan languages formed a small family of closely related languages, including Barbareño, Ventureño, Obispeño, Purisimeño, Ineseño, and Island Chumash. The last native speakers died in the twentieth century, but language revitalization efforts are underway, drawing on the field notes of linguist John Peabody Harrington.

Modern era

The Santa Ynez Band of Chumash Indians is the only federally recognized Chumash tribe, based in Santa Barbara County. Other Chumash groups continue to seek federal recognition and work to preserve their cultural heritage.
