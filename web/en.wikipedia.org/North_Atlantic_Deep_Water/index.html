North Atlantic Deep Water

North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) is a deep water mass formed in the North Atlantic Ocean. It is a key component of the thermohaline circulation, which is itself a component of the global ocean conveyor belt. NADW forms through the cooling and sinking of surface waters in the Labrador Sea and the Nordic Seas (Greenland, Iceland, and Norwegian Seas), where overflow waters spill over the Greenland–Scotland ridge through the Denmark Strait and the Faroe Bank Channel.

Formation and properties

NADW is characterised by relatively high salinity and temperature compared with other deep water masses, and by high oxygen and low nutrient concentrations reflecting its recent contact with the atmosphere. Its formation involves deep convection in the Labrador and Irminger Seas and the entrainment of overflow waters from the Nordic Seas. The resulting water mass occupies depths of roughly 1,500 to 4,000 metres in the Atlantic.

Circulation

From its formation regions, NADW flows southward through the Atlantic basin at depth, much of it carried by the Deep Western Boundary Current along the continental slope of North America. It crosses the equator, continues into the South Atlantic, and eventually joins the Antarctic Circumpolar Current system, from where it spreads into the Indian and Pacific Oceans. The formation of NADW is balanced by the northward flow of warm surface waters such as the Gulf Stream and North Atlantic Current, forming the upper limb of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC).

Climate significance

Changes in the rate of NADW formation are implicated in past abrupt climate events, including the Younger Dryas and Dansgaard–Oeschger events recorded in ice cores. Because deep-water formation transports heat, carbon, and oxygen into the ocean interior, its possible weakening under global warming is an active area of climate research, with potential consequences for regional climate, sea level, and marine ecosystems.
