Coal liquefaction

Coal liquefaction is a family of processes for converting coal into liquid hydrocarbons resembling crude oil and refined fuels. Because coal is hydrogen-poor and carbon-rich compared with petroleum, liquefaction either adds hydrogen and breaks down coal's molecular structure (direct liquefaction) or gasifies the coal completely and rebuilds liquid fuels from the resulting synthesis gas (indirect liquefaction). The resulting products are sometimes called coal-to-liquids (CTL) fuels.

Direct liquefaction

The Bergius process, patented by Friedrich Bergius in 1913, reacts coal slurry with hydrogen at high temperature (400–500 °C) and pressure (up to about 70 MPa) in the presence of catalysts such as iron compounds, yielding gasoline, middle distillates, and heavy oils. IG Farben industrialised the process at Leuna in Germany, and by 1944 German hydrogenation plants supplied a large share of the Luftwaffe's aviation fuel. Modern direct liquefaction is practised commercially at the China Shenhua plant at Erdos in Inner Mongolia, commissioned in 2008 — the only large direct liquefaction facility in operation.

Indirect liquefaction

Indirect liquefaction gasifies coal with steam and oxygen to produce synthesis gas (carbon monoxide and hydrogen), cleans the gas, and converts it to liquid hydrocarbons via Fischer–Tropsch synthesis. The South African company Sasol built large plants at Sasolburg and Secunda — the latter the world's largest single-point source of industrial carbon dioxide emissions — which supplied a significant fraction of South Africa's transport fuel, a strategic investment begun under apartheid-era oil sanctions.

Economics and environment

CTL plants are capital-intensive and generally competitive only at sustained high oil prices or where energy security is prioritised, as in wartime Germany and sanctions-era South Africa. Without carbon capture, the full life-cycle carbon dioxide emissions of CTL fuels are roughly double those of conventional petroleum fuels, because carbon is rejected both during conversion and when the fuel is burned. Proposed projects in the United States, Australia, and elsewhere have mostly stalled on cost and emissions grounds, although interest persists in China.
