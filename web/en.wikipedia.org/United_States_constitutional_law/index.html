United States constitutional law

United States constitutional law is the body of law governing the interpretation and implementation of the United States Constitution, the supreme law of the land. It encompasses the structure of the federal government and its relations with the states, the powers of Congress, the President and the judiciary, and the civil rights and liberties of individuals. Because the Constitution is brief and much of it is written in general terms, most constitutional law is the product of judicial review — the courts' power, established in Marbury v. Madison (1803), to strike down laws and executive actions that conflict with the Constitution.

Structure and federalism

Articles I, II and III vest legislative, executive and judicial power in Congress, the President and the federal courts — a separation of powers tempered by checks and balances (the presidential veto, Senate confirmation, impeachment, and judicial review). Federalism divides sovereignty between the federal government, whose powers are enumerated, and the states, whose general police power is preserved by the Tenth Amendment. Key interpretive engines of federal power include the Necessary and Proper Clause (read broadly in McCulloch v. Maryland, 1819), the Commerce Clause (expanded during the New Deal, as in Wickard v. Filburn, 1942, and somewhat limited since United States v. Lopez, 1995), and the Supremacy Clause, under which valid federal law preempts state law.

Rights and liberties

The first ten amendments — the Bill of Rights (1791) — originally restricted only the federal government (Barron v. Baltimore, 1833), but after the Civil War amendments, most of its guarantees were "incorporated" against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause (selective incorporation), a process completed only piecemeal into the 21st century. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) guarantees citizenship, due process and equal protection, and is the source of much modern rights jurisprudence — from Brown v. Board of Education (1954), ending school segregation, to cases on privacy, marriage and abortion (Roe v. Wade, 1973, overruled by Dobbs, 2022). The First Amendment protects speech, press, religion (free exercise and non-establishment), assembly and petition; the Fourth through Eighth Amendments govern criminal procedure, including search and seizure, self-incrimination, jury trial and cruel and unusual punishment.

Interpretation

Major interpretive schools include originalism (meaning fixed at ratification, associated with Justice Antonin Scalia), textualism, living constitutionalism, pragmatism and common-law constitutionalism. Recurring controversies include the countermajoritarian difficulty (unelected judges overruling legislatures), executive power in war and emergencies, and the scope of unenumerated rights. Each state also has its own constitution and constitutional law, which may grant greater rights than the federal floor.
