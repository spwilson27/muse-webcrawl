Hierarchy

A hierarchy (from Greek: ἱεραρχία, hierarkhia, 'rule of a high priest', from hierarkhes, 'president of sacred rites') is an arrangement of items (objects, names, values, categories, etc.) that are represented as being "above", "below", or "at the same level as" one another. In abstract terms, a hierarchy is a partially ordered set or an ordered group.

Etymology and history

The word derives from the Greek terms hieros (ἱερός, 'sacred') and arkho (ἄρχω, 'rule' or 'lead'). It was first used for the graded orders of angels described by Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite around the 5th century CE, and later applied to church government and, by extension, to any system of ranked persons or things.

Formal structure

Hierarchies are often represented as trees or pyramids, in which each element except the root has one parent and zero or more children. A hierarchy can link entities either directly or indirectly, and can link entities either vertically or diagonally. The relationships in a hierarchy may be transitive: if A outranks B and B outranks C, then A outranks C.

Applications

Hierarchical structures appear across many fields: organizational management (corporate hierarchies), biology (taxonomic ranks and dominance hierarchies among animals), computer science (file systems, class hierarchies, tree data structures), linguistics (Chomsky hierarchy), geography, and library classification. The Catholic Church and other religious bodies retain explicitly hierarchical governance.

Criticism

Hierarchies have been criticized for concentrating power and information, and contrasted with heterarchies, networks, and flat organizational structures. Nevertheless, hierarchical organization remains a widespread strategy for managing complexity, because it allows a complex system to be understood and controlled level by level.
