The Avro Shackleton was a British long-range maritime patrol aircraft (MPA) used by the Royal Air Force (RAF) and the South African Air Force (SAAF). It was developed by Avro from the Avro Lincoln bomber, itself a development of the famous wartime Lancaster. It was named after the polar explorer Sir Ernest Shackleton.

Development and design

The Shackleton was designed to meet a requirement for an aircraft to counter the growing Soviet submarine threat. It was powered by four Rolls-Royce Griffon piston engines driving contra-rotating propellers, and carried a large unpressurised bomb bay for depth charges, torpedoes and sonobuoys. Early marks carried nose-mounted 20 mm cannon. The type was noted for its endurance — flights of 12 hours or more were routine — and for its noise, earning the affectionate description "a hundred thousand rivets flying in close formation".

Operational history

The Shackleton entered RAF service in 1951 and served with Coastal Command in the maritime reconnaissance and anti-submarine roles throughout the Cold War. South Africa operated the MR.3 variant from 1957 to 1984. After replacement in the patrol role by the jet-powered Nimrod, a number of Shackletons were converted to the airborne early warning role as the AEW.2, fitted with the AN/APS-20 radar in a distinctive nose radome; these served with No. 8 Squadron RAF until 1991, when they were replaced by the Boeing E-3 Sentry.

Variants

  • MR.1 / MR.1A – initial maritime reconnaissance versions.
  • MR.2 – improved version with revised nose and retractable radome.
  • MR.3 – major redesign with tricycle undercarriage and, in SAAF service, auxiliary Viper jet engines.
  • T.4 – navigation trainer conversion.
  • AEW.2 – airborne early warning conversion of the MR.2.
