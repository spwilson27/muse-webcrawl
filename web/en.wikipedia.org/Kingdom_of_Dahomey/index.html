Kingdom of Dahomey

The Kingdom of Dahomey was a West African kingdom located within present-day Benin that existed from approximately 1600 until 1904. Rising from the Fon people of the Abomey plateau, it became a highly centralised regional power, trading slaves and later palm oil with Europeans at the port of Whydah (Ouidah), and famous for its women soldiers, the Agojie — dubbed "Dahomey Amazons" by Europeans.

History

Founded around 1600–1620 by the Fon, Dahomey expanded under Wegbaja and his successors. King Agaja (r. 1718–1740) conquered the coastal kingdoms of Allada (1724) and Whydah (1727), giving Dahomey direct access to the Atlantic slave trade, though in 1730 it became a tributary of the Oyo Empire until the 1820s. King Ghezo (r. 1818–1858) threw off Oyo, professionalised the army — including its celebrated women regiments — and shifted commerce toward palm oil as the slave trade was suppressed. Annual Customs at Abomey honoured royal ancestors with lavish ceremonies that included human sacrifice of war captives, a practice Europeans seized upon in judging the kingdom.

Fall

King Béhanzin resisted French encroachment in the Franco-Dahomean Wars (1890 and 1892–94); defeated, he burned Abomey's palaces and was exiled to Martinique and Algeria. France made Dahomey a protectorate (1894), deposed his successor Agoli-agbo in 1900, and annexed the kingdom in 1904 into French West Africa.

Legacy

The Royal Palaces of Abomey are a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1985). Dahomey gave its name to the modern republic of Benin between 1975 and 1990 ("People's Republic of Benin" replaced the Republic of Dahomey). The Agojie have become global cultural icons, depicted in the 2022 film The Woman King. Twenty-six royal treasures looted in 1892 were returned by France to Benin in 2021.
