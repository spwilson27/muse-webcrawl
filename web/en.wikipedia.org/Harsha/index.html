Harsha (c. 590 – 647 CE), also known as Harshavardhana, was an Indian emperor who ruled northern India from 606 to 647 CE. A member of the Vardhana dynasty (Pushyabhuti dynasty), he ruled first from Thanesar and later from Kanyakubja (modern Kannauj). At its height his empire extended across the Indo-Gangetic Plain from Punjab to Bengal, though his attempt to expand south of the Narmada River was checked by the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II. Harsha's reign is documented by his court poet Banabhatta's Harshacharita and by the travel account of the Chinese Buddhist monk Xuanzang, who visited his court.

Early life and accession

Harsha was the younger son of Prabhakaravardhana, king of Thanesar. After his father's death, his elder brother Rajyavardhana campaigned against the Malava king Devagupta and the Gauda king Shashanka, but was treacherously killed. Harsha succeeded to the throne in 606 CE at about sixteen years of age, vowing to avenge his brother. He rescued his widowed sister Rajyashri, who had been imprisoned at Kannauj, and united Thanesar with the Maukhari kingdom of Kannauj, which became his capital.

Empire and administration

Harsha campaigned for years across northern India, bringing most of the region between the Himalayas and the Narmada under his control, though some conquests attributed to him may have been temporary overlordships. His southward advance was defeated by Pulakeshin II on the banks of the Narmada around 618–619 CE, an event recorded in Chalukya inscriptions and by Xuanzang. Administration was relatively decentralized, relying on feudatory chiefs (samantas); land grants to Brahmins and officials were common. Harsha maintained diplomatic relations with Tang China, exchanging embassies with Emperor Taizong.

Religion and culture

Though born into a family devoted to Shiva and Surya, Harsha became an eminent patron of Mahayana Buddhism in his later reign, building monasteries and stupas and supporting the university of Nalanda. Xuanzang described his great quinquennial festival at Prayaga, where the emperor distributed accumulated wealth in alms to Buddhists, Brahmins and the poor over 75 days. Harsha is traditionally credited with three Sanskrit plays: Ratnavali, Priyadarsika, and the Buddhist-themed Nagananda, though some scholars attribute them to a court poet writing in his name. He died in 647 CE without an heir, and his empire fragmented soon after.
