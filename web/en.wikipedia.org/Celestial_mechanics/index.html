Celestial mechanics is the branch of astronomy that deals with the motions of objects in outer space. Historically, it applied the principles of physics (classical mechanics) to astronomical objects, such as stars and planets, to produce ephemeris data. Today it underlies spacecraft navigation, satellite orbit determination, and the study of the long-term dynamics and stability of planetary systems.

History

Celestial mechanics grew out of efforts to model planetary motion. Johannes Kepler derived his three laws of planetary motion (1609–1619) from Tycho Brahe's observations. Isaac Newton's Principia (1687) explained Kepler's laws from a single law of universal gravitation and founded the discipline. In the 18th and 19th centuries, Euler, Lagrange, and Laplace developed perturbation theory; discrepancies in Uranus's orbit led Adams and Le Verrier to predict Neptune (discovered 1846), a famous triumph of the field. Poincaré's work on the three-body problem in the 1890s laid foundations for chaos theory.

Core concepts

  • Two-body problem — two masses under mutual gravity move on conic-section orbits (ellipse, parabola, hyperbola); fully solvable.
  • Orbital elements — six quantities (semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, longitude of ascending node, argument of perihelion, mean anomaly) specify an orbit.
  • N-body problem — no general closed-form solution for three or more bodies; handled numerically and with perturbation methods.
  • Perturbations and resonances — gravitational tugs between planets cause slow changes in orbits; mean-motion resonances structure the asteroid belt and ring systems.
  • Relativistic effects — general relativity explains, e.g., the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion.

Modern applications

Celestial mechanics is applied in computing ephemerides, planning interplanetary trajectories and gravity-assist manoeuvres, maintaining satellite constellations (including GPS), tracking near-Earth asteroids, and investigating whether the Solar System is stable over billions of years (numerical studies show it is chaotic on ~5-million-year Lyapunov timescales, though macroscopically stable for the Sun's lifetime).
