Language policy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Bilingual road signs illustrating official language policy]

Language policy comprises the laws, regulations, and practices by which societies govern the use of languages — which languages are official, which are taught in schools, used in government and courts, and supported or discouraged in public life. Language policies may be explicit, set out in constitutions and legislation, or implicit in administrative practice.

Language planning

Language policy is closely linked with language planning, classically divided into status planning (deciding the functions of languages in society), corpus planning (developing the language itself — its script, spelling, and terminology), and acquisition planning (organizing language teaching and learning). Bodies such as the Académie Française, the Hebrew Academy, and various national language councils carry out aspects of this work.

Examples

Many states adopt one or more official languages: Canada's federal bilingualism in English and French, South Africa's eleven official languages, and India's complex schedule of recognized languages illustrate contrasting approaches. Language policy has been a potent political issue — examples include Quebec's Charter of the French Language, Catalonia's linguistic normalization, the promotion of Hebrew in Israel, and debates over English-only policies in the United States. Colonial and post-colonial states frequently retain a former colonial language as a neutral official medium.

Minority and endangered languages

Since the late twentieth century, international attention has turned to the rights of linguistic minorities and the revitalization of endangered languages. Instruments such as the Council of Europe's European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages (1992) and successful revitalization programmes for Welsh, Māori, and Hawaiian reflect this trend, while UNESCO tracks the world's languages in danger.
