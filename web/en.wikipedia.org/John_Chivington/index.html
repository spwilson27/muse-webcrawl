John Chivington

John Milton Chivington (27 January 1821 – 4 October 1894) was a Methodist minister and US Army officer who commanded the Colorado volunteers responsible for the Sand Creek massacre of 29 November 1864, in which his troops killed well over a hundred Cheyenne and Arapaho, mostly women, children and the elderly, in a village that believed itself under US protection.

Early life and ministry

Born in Ohio, Chivington became a Methodist minister in 1844 and served missions on the frontier, including in Missouri — where pro-slavery congregants once threatened him at gunpoint — and later in Nebraska and Colorado. A physically imposing man, he was known as the "Fighting Parson".

Civil War

Appointed a major in the 1st Colorado Infantry in 1861, Chivington played a decisive role in the Union victory at Glorieta Pass in New Mexico in March 1862, leading the flanking column that destroyed the Confederate supply train. Promoted to colonel and placed in command of the District of Colorado, he aspired to political office and championed aggressive action against the Plains tribes amid the Colorado War of 1864.

Sand Creek and aftermath

On 29 November 1864 Chivington led about 700 men of the 1st and 3rd Colorado Cavalry in a dawn attack on the Sand Creek encampment of the Cheyenne chief Black Kettle, who had negotiated peace with the Army and flew both a US flag and a white flag. Between roughly 70 and 600 Native people were killed — modern estimates commonly range from 130 to 230 — with widespread mutilation of the dead. Congressional and military investigations condemned the attack as a massacre. Chivington, whose hundred-day volunteers had mustered out, resigned his commission and escaped court-martial, though his political ambitions were destroyed. He later worked as a freight hauler, deputy sheriff and journalist in Nebraska and Colorado, defending his actions until his death in Denver in 1894.
