Organizational behavior

Organizational behavior (OB) is the study of how individuals and groups act within organizations, and how organizational systems and structures affect behaviour. It draws on psychology, sociology, anthropology, economics and management science to explain and predict workplace behaviour, with the practical aim of improving organizational effectiveness.

Levels of analysis

The field is conventionally studied at three levels. At the individual level, topics include personality, perception, attitudes, motivation and learning. At the group level, researchers examine team dynamics, communication, leadership, power and conflict. At the organizational level, they study structure, culture, change and human resource policies.

History

Early antecedents include Frederick Taylor's scientific management (1911), which sought efficiency through task analysis, and the Hawthorne studies of the 1920s–30s at Western Electric, associated with Elton Mayo, which suggested that social factors and attention affect productivity. The human relations movement and later work by Douglas McGregor (Theory X and Theory Y), Abraham Maslow, Frederick Herzberg and others brought motivation and job design to the fore. From the 1960s, contingency approaches held that effective management depends on situational factors.

Key topics

  • Motivation theories, including expectancy theory and goal-setting theory
  • Leadership styles and theories
  • Organizational culture and climate
  • Decision-making and its biases
  • Organizational change and development
  • Job satisfaction, stress and well-being
