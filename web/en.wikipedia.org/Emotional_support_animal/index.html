Emotional support animal

An emotional support animal (ESA) is an animal that provides therapeutic benefit to a person with a mental health or psychiatric disability. The animal does not require any specific task training, unlike a service animal, which is trained to perform particular tasks for a person with a disability. Most commonly, emotional support animals are dogs and cats, but the term has at times been applied to many other species.

Legal status

In the United States, the Fair Housing Act has been interpreted to require landlords to make reasonable accommodations for assistance animals, including emotional support animals, when a licensed mental health professional documents a disability-related need. However, ESAs do not have the public-access rights of service animals under the Americans with Disabilities Act. In 2021, the U.S. Department of Transportation revised its rules so that airlines are no longer required to treat emotional support animals as service animals, following controversies over unusual animals brought into cabins, including peacocks, ducks and pigs.

Evidence and criticism

Research on the mental-health benefits of ESAs is limited and mixed; some studies suggest pet companionship can reduce anxiety and loneliness, but critics argue the ESA designation is frequently obtained through online letter mills without genuine clinical evaluation. Mental-health organisations differ on how strongly to endorse ESAs as a treatment.

Distinction from service animals

Service animals, in U.S. law generally dogs individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability, are protected in public places; psychiatric service animals, trained to perform tasks related to a psychiatric disability, are distinct from emotional support animals, whose role is simply their presence.
