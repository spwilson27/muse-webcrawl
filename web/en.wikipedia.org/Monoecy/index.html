Monoecy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Monoecy (from Greek mono-, "single", and oikia, "house") is a sexual system in plants in which separate male and female reproductive structures occur on the same individual plant. A monoecious species bears both staminate (male) and pistillate (female) flowers on one plant, in contrast to dioecious species, which bear male and female flowers on different plants, and to species with bisexual (hermaphroditic) flowers.

Occurrence and examples

About 5–6% of flowering plant species are monoecious. Familiar examples include maize (corn), in which the tassel is the male inflorescence and the ears develop from female flowers; cucurbits such as squash, cucumber, and melon; and many wind-pollinated trees, including oaks, birches, alders, hazel, and most conifers, which produce separate male and female cones.

Significance

Because the sexes are spatially separated within the plant, monoecy can reduce self-pollination compared with bisexual flowers — especially when male and female flowers also mature at different times — while still allowing a single colonising individual to reproduce. The arrangement of male and female flowers can be plastic: in some species, sex expression responds to plant size, age, light, and nutrient status, allowing resources to be allocated flexibly between male and female function. The evolutionary pathways between monoecy, dioecy, and hermaphroditism are a central topic in plant reproductive biology.
