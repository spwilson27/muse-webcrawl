Pediatrics


A pediatrician examining an infant

Pediatrics (also spelled paediatrics in Commonwealth English) is the branch of medicine that involves the medical care of infants, children, and adolescents. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends pediatric care through the age of 21 — in some contexts up to 26. The word derives from Greek pais ("child") and iatros ("doctor"). A medical doctor who specialises in this area is known as a pediatrician.

Scope

Pediatrics differs from adult medicine in many respects: children's bodies are physically and physiologically immature and change rapidly, congenital defects, developmental issues, and genetic conditions figure much more prominently, and drug dosing and normal vital-sign ranges vary with age and weight. Preventive care is central — well-child visits track growth and developmental milestones and deliver immunisations — alongside the treatment of common childhood illnesses, injuries, and chronic conditions such as asthma, diabetes, and epilepsy.

Subspecialties

Pediatric subspecialties include neonatology (care of newborns, especially premature infants), pediatric cardiology, oncology, neurology, critical care, emergency medicine, gastroenterology, endocrinology, and adolescent medicine. Children's hospitals concentrate specialised expertise and child-sized equipment.

History

Although physicians such as Hippocrates, Soranus, and the Persian physician Rhazes wrote about children's diseases, pediatrics emerged as a distinct specialty in the 19th century: the first children's hospitals opened in Paris (1802) and London (Great Ormond Street, 1852), and Abraham Jacobi, a founder of the field in the United States, established the first American pediatric clinic in New York in 1860. Vaccination, antibiotics, and neonatal intensive care transformed child survival in the 20th century.
