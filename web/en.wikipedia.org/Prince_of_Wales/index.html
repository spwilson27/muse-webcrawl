Prince of Wales

Prince of Wales
Style: Royal Highness
Type: Title granted by the sovereign to the heir apparent
First English holder: Edward of Caernarfon (1301)

Prince of Wales (Welsh: Tywysog Cymru) is a title traditionally granted to the heir apparent to the English and later British throne. Since 1301, when Edward I of England gave the title to his son Edward of Caernarfon, it has usually — though not automatically — been conferred on the sovereign's eldest son or heir.

History

Before the English conquest of Wales, native Welsh rulers such as Llywelyn ap Gruffudd used the title Prince of Wales. After the conquest, Edward I presented his infant son — born at Caernarfon Castle — as Prince of Wales. The title is created by the monarch for each holder individually and does not pass automatically on the death of the previous holder or the accession of a new sovereign. Holders are usually invested in a formal ceremony; the investitures of the future Edward VIII (1911) and of Charles (1969) took place at Caernarfon Castle.

Role

The Prince of Wales is heir apparent to the throne and usually also holds the titles Duke of Cornwall (automatically, as the sovereign's eldest son) and, in Scotland, Duke of Rothesay. The prince's feathers — a plume of three ostrich feathers — is the badge of the Prince of Wales, and the motto Ich dien ("I serve") accompanies it.

Recent holders

Charles, Prince of Wales (created 1958), was the longest-serving holder of the title before succeeding as Charles III in September 2022. His elder son William was created Prince of Wales on 9 September 2022, the day after his father's accession.
