New Zealand land confiscations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The confiscation districts

The New Zealand land confiscations took place during the 1860s to punish the Kingitanga movement for attempting to set up a separate Māori nation, also called a nation within a nation, under a separate Māori King. The punitive confiscation, known in te reo Māori as raupatu, confiscated land for "rebellion against the authority of the Queen" that was actually used as an opportunity for settlement and to open the lands for cultivation.

Legislation and extent

The legal basis was the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863, which allowed the Governor to proclaim districts in which Māori were believed to have been "engaged in rebellion" and to confiscate land within them. In total, about 3.4 million acres (1.4 million hectares) were confiscated in Waikato, Taranaki, the Bay of Plenty, and parts of Hawke's Bay and other districts — much of it from iwi who had not fought, or who had fought on the Crown's side. Some land was later returned or compensated, but often the poorest land, in fragmented parcels, and not to the original owners.

Legacy and redress

The confiscations left affected iwi largely landless and impoverished for generations, and raupatu remains one of the most grievous breaches of the Treaty of Waitangi. From the 1970s the Waitangi Tribunal investigated the confiscations, and in the 1990s the Crown formally apologised and negotiated settlements — including the Waikato Raupatu Claims Settlement Act 1995, the first of the modern historical Treaty settlements.
