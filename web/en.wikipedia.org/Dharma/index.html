Dharma (Sanskrit: धर्म, romanised: dharma; Pali: धम्म, dhamma) is a key concept in Indian religions — Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism — with multiple meanings. There is no single English word that captures its range: it can denote duty, righteousness, moral law, virtue, cosmic order, the right way of living, religion itself, and (in Buddhist philosophy) the fundamental phenomena that constitute reality. The word derives from the Sanskrit root dhṛ, "to hold, maintain, keep," and originally signified that which upholds or sustains the order of things.

In Hinduism

In Hindu thought, dharma is the moral and cosmic order that makes life and the universe possible, and the duties and conduct that keep a person in harmony with that order. It is one of the four aims of human life (puruṣārtha), alongside artha (prosperity), kāma (pleasure) and mokṣa (liberation). A person's dharma varies by caste (varṇa), stage of life (āśrama), and circumstance; alongside this particular duty (svadharma) stands a universal dharma of virtues such as ahiṁsā (non-violence), truthfulness, patience and self-control. Classical legal and ethical treatises — the Dharmaśāstras, including the Manusmṛti — codify these norms. In the Mahābhārata, Krishna counsels the warrior Arjuna in the Bhagavad Gītā to perform his svadharma without attachment to results.

In Buddhism

In Buddhism, dhamma (Pali) has two principal senses. First, it is the teaching of the Buddha — the truth he discovered and proclaimed — and one of the Three Refuges (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) in which Buddhists take shelter; practitioners "practise the Dhamma" by following the Noble Eightfold Path. Second, in the analytical philosophy of the Abhidhamma, dhammas are the irreducible phenomena or elements into which experience can be analysed, each bearing its own characteristic. In East Asian Buddhism, Chinese translations (法, fǎ) and related usage extend the term to all phenomena whatsoever.

In Jainism and Sikhism

In Jainism, dharma is one of the fundamental substances (dravya) — the medium of motion — and, ethically, the supreme teaching of non-violence and the code of conduct for monks and laypeople alike. In Sikhism, dharam denotes righteousness, religious duty and the path of living truthfully before God, as taught in the Guru Granth Sahib.

Symbols

The most widespread symbol of dharma is the dharmachakra, the "wheel of dharma," whose turning signifies the Buddha setting his teaching in motion; a stylised 24-spoke version, the Ashoka Chakra, appears at the centre of the national flag of India, where it also represents the eternal law of dharma and motion in life.
