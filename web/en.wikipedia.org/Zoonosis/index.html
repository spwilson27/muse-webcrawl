Zoonosis

Zoonotic pathogens can jump from animal hosts to humans through direct contact, vectors, food, or the environment.

A zoonosis (plural zoonoses) is an infectious disease of humans caused by a pathogen — such as a bacterium, virus, parasite, or prion — that has jumped from a non-human animal to a human. The word derives from the Greek zōon (animal) and nosos (disease). Zoonotic diseases are a major category of newly emerging infectious diseases and a significant share of all known human infectious diseases; roughly 60% of known human pathogens are zoonotic, and about 75% of emerging infectious diseases originate in animals.

Transmission

Zoonoses can be transmitted in several ways. Direct contact with infected animals, their blood, saliva, urine, or faeces can spread diseases such as rabies and leptospirosis. Vector-borne zoonoses are carried by arthropods such as mosquitoes and ticks — examples include plague (fleas), Lyme disease (ticks), and West Nile virus (mosquitoes). Foodborne transmission occurs through consumption of contaminated meat, eggs, or unpasteurised dairy products, as with salmonellosis and E. coli infections. Waterborne and environmental routes, and occupational exposure among farmers, veterinarians, and abattoir workers, are also important.

Examples

Well-known zoonotic diseases include rabies, avian influenza, Ebola virus disease, HIV/AIDS (which originated from simian immunodeficiency viruses in primates), SARS, MERS, and COVID-19, which is believed to have originated in bats or an intermediate animal host. Historical pandemics such as the Black Death (plague, caused by Yersinia pestis circulating in rodents) were zoonotic in origin.

Reverse zoonosis

Transmission can also run in the opposite direction: humans can infect animals, a phenomenon called reverse zoonosis or anthroponosis. Documented cases include human influenza and tuberculosis spreading to captive and wild animals, and SARS-CoV-2 spreading to mink, deer, and zoo animals.

Prevention and control

Control strategies include vaccination of animals (such as mass dog vaccination against rabies), vector control, food safety regulation, surveillance of wildlife and livestock, and the One Health approach, which integrates human, animal, and environmental health. Deforestation, wildlife trade, intensive farming, and climate change are recognised drivers that increase the frequency of zoonotic spillover events.
