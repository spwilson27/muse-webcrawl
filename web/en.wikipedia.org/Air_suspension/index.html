Air suspension

Air suspension is a type of vehicle suspension powered by an electric or engine-driven air pump or compressor. Flexible bellows filled with compressed air — usually textile-reinforced rubber — take the place of steel coil or leaf springs. Air suspension is standard on most modern buses and heavy trucks and is offered on many luxury cars and SUVs; it is also widely used on trains and in aftermarket custom car culture.

Operation

A compressor supplies air to a reservoir; valves direct air into or out of each air spring to maintain a set ride height whatever the load. Height sensors and an electronic control unit typically manage levelling automatically. Because the air springs' effective rate changes with height and pressure, the system can keep the vehicle level when loaded, lower it at highway speed for better aerodynamics, raise it for rough roads, or "kneel" buses to kerb height for boarding.

History

Pneumatic springs were patented in the nineteenth century (an early design by John Lewis in 1847) and tried on bicycles and early motor cars. Practical automotive systems arrived in the 1950s: General Motors introduced air suspension on the 1957 Cadillac Eldorado Brougham and on transit buses, and air suspension later became near-universal in intercity coaches and heavy trucks, where it protects cargo and road surfaces. Citroën pioneered the related hydropneumatic suspension on the 1955 DS. Lowrider culture adopted hydraulic and later air systems to raise and lower cars dramatically.

Advantages and drawbacks

Advantages include a smooth ride, constant ride height, reduced road wear, and adjustable handling. Drawbacks include complexity and cost: compressors, valves and rubber bellows age and can leak, and repairs are typically more expensive than for steel springs.
