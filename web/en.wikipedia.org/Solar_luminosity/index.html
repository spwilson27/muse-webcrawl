Solar luminosity

The solar luminosity, denoted L☉, is a unit of radiant flux (power emitted as light) conventionally used by astronomers to express the luminosities of stars and galaxies relative to the output of the Sun. One solar luminosity equals the Sun's total power output, approximately 3.828 × 1026 watts.

Determination

The solar luminosity is calculated from the solar irradiance measured at Earth's orbit (the solar constant, about 1,361 W/m²) multiplied by the surface area of a sphere with radius one astronomical unit: L = 4π(1 AU)² × TSI. The International Astronomical Union in 2015 adopted a nominal solar luminosity of exactly 3.828 × 1026 W for use in unit conversions.

Variability

The Sun's luminosity is not perfectly constant. It fluctuates by about 0.1% over the 11-year solar cycle, and it has increased steadily over the Sun's main-sequence lifetime — the young Sun was only about 70% as luminous as today, a fact central to the "faint young Sun paradox" concerning liquid water on early Earth and Mars. Standard solar models predict the luminosity will continue to rise by roughly 10% every billion years.

Use in astronomy

Stellar luminosities span an enormous range: red dwarfs may emit less than 0.001 L☉, while the most massive stars exceed a million solar luminosities. Luminosity, together with temperature, determines a star's position on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram and relates to mass through the mass–luminosity relation for main-sequence stars. Luminosities of galaxies are also quoted in solar units — the Milky Way radiates roughly 5 × 1010 L☉.

Energy source

The luminosity is powered by nuclear fusion in the Sun's core, where hydrogen is converted to helium mainly through the proton–proton chain. The fusion rate inferred from the luminosity was historically compared with the observed flux of solar neutrinos, giving rise to the solar neutrino problem.
