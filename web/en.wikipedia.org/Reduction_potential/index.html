Reduction potential

Cell potentials measured against a reference electrode give the reduction potentials of half-reactions.

The reduction potential (also known as the redox potential) is a measure of the tendency of a chemical species to acquire electrons from or lose electrons to an electrode, and thereby be reduced or oxidised respectively. Reduction potentials are measured in volts (V) relative to a standard reference electrode, conventionally the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), whose potential is defined as 0.00 V under standard conditions.

Standard electrode potential

The standard reduction potential (E°) of a half-reaction is measured under standard conditions: 25 °C, 1 M concentrations for solutes, and 1 atm pressure for gases. Species with a more positive reduction potential are stronger oxidising agents — they are more readily reduced — while species with a more negative potential are stronger reducing agents. The standard potential of an electrochemical cell is the difference between the reduction potentials of its two half-cells, and a positive value indicates a spontaneous (galvanic) reaction.

Factors affecting potential

Actual potentials depend on concentrations, temperature and pH, as described by the Nernst equation. In biochemistry, potentials are often quoted at pH 7 (E°′) because many redox reactions in cells involve protons; the electron transport chain of respiration is organised as a cascade of carriers with increasingly positive reduction potentials, from NADH (−0.32 V) to oxygen (+0.82 V).

Applications

Reduction potentials are used to predict the direction of redox reactions, the feasibility of extracting metals from their ores, corrosion behaviour, battery voltages, and the sequence of electron flow in photosynthesis and cellular respiration.
