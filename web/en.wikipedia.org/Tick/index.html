Ticks are parasitic arachnids of the order Ixodida. Together with mites they form the subclass Acari. All ticks are obligate blood-feeding ectoparasites of vertebrates during at least part of their life cycle, and many are important vectors of disease.

Biology

Ticks have a fused body (idiosoma) and a specialised piercing capitulum armed with a barbed hypostome that anchors in the host's skin; they lack antennae and have eight legs as nymphs and adults, six as larvae. "Hard ticks" (Ixodidae) possess a dorsal scutum and feed for days at a time, often on three different hosts across their life stages; "soft ticks" (Argasidae) feed briefly and repeatedly, mostly in nests and burrows. Many hard ticks find hosts by "questing" — climbing vegetation and waiting with forelegs extended, detecting carbon dioxide, heat and odour via Haller's organ.

Diseases

Ticks transmit more kinds of pathogens than any other arthropod group except mosquitoes. Important tick-borne diseases include Lyme disease, tick-borne encephalitis, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, anaplasmosis, babesiosis, ehrlichiosis, Crimean–Congo haemorrhagic fever and the red-meat allergy (alpha-gal syndrome) associated with lone star tick bites. Prevention relies on protective clothing, repellents, prompt removal and, in some regions, vaccination (against tick-borne encephalitis).

Ecology

Ticks occur on every continent, including Antarctic coasts where they parasitise seabirds. Populations are expanding in many temperate regions as climates warm and deer and rodent hosts increase, raising the incidence of tick-borne illness.
