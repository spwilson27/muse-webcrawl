Deaf culture

Deaf culture is the set of social beliefs, behaviours, art, literary traditions, history, values and shared institutions of communities of deaf people who use sign languages as their main means of communication. Members of these communities often regard deafness not as a disability to be cured but as a cultural and linguistic identity — a distinction reflected in the convention of writing "Deaf" with a capital D for the cultural community and "deaf" for the audiological condition.

Language and identity

A shared sign language — such as American Sign Language, British Sign Language or French Sign Language — is the core of Deaf culture in each country. Because roughly 90 percent of deaf children are born to hearing parents, the culture is usually acquired at schools for the deaf and through Deaf clubs, sports and organisations rather than within the family. Cultural norms include direct eye contact, visual attention-getting strategies such as tapping or light-flashing, name signs, and storytelling traditions in sign.

History and debates

Deaf communities took shape around the first schools for the deaf in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The 1880 Milan Conference, which declared oral education superior and drove sign language out of classrooms, is remembered as a catastrophe for Deaf culture. Modern controversies include the use of cochlear implants in children, mainstreaming in education, and genetic technologies, which many culturally Deaf people view as threats to their community's survival. Institutions such as Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C., the world's only liberal arts university for deaf students, remain cultural centres; its 1988 "Deaf President Now" protest was a landmark of Deaf civil rights.
