Puget Sound (Lushootseed: x̌ʷəlč) is a complex estuarine system of interconnected marine waterways and basins in the U.S. state of Washington, part of the Salish Sea. It is a sound — a large sea or ocean inlet — lying along the northwestern coast of the state, separated from the Pacific Ocean by the Olympic Peninsula and connected to it through the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Admiralty Inlet.

Geology and geography

The Sound was carved by the Puget Lobe of the Cordilleran ice sheet during the last glacial period, roughly 15,000 years ago. Its shorelines extend for over 2,000 miles and enclose numerous islands, including Whidbey, Bainbridge, Vashon and the San Juan archipelago to the north. Major inlets include Hood Canal, a fjord-like arm on the west side. The largest cities on its shores are Seattle, Tacoma, Everett and Olympia.

History

Indigenous peoples — including the Lushootseed-speaking Coast Salish groups — have lived around the Sound for millennia. In 1792 Captain George Vancouver's expedition charted the waterway; Vancouver named it for his lieutenant Peter Puget. The 1850s treaties, logging, shipbuilding and fishing drove rapid settlement, and the region became a major port complex.

Ecology and transport

Puget Sound supports salmon runs, shellfish beds and a population of orcas, and is the subject of large-scale environmental restoration. The Washington State Ferries system, the largest ferry network in the United States, links the Sound's peninsulas and islands, while its deep-water ports are among the principal gateways for trans-Pacific trade.
