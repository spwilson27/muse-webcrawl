Peace–Athabasca Delta

CoordinatesApprox. 59°N 111°W

DesignationRamsar wetland (1982); part of Wood Buffalo National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site

The Peace–Athabasca Delta in northeastern Alberta, Canada, is one of the world's largest inland freshwater deltas. It is formed where the Peace and Athabasca rivers flow into the western end of Lake Athabasca and the Slave River. Much of the delta lies within Wood Buffalo National Park.

Geography and ecology

The delta is a vast complex of shallow lakes, channels, sedge meadows, and willow shrubland. Because the Peace River flows northward and is dammed by ice in spring, its waters periodically reverse direction and flood the delta, replenishing perched basins that would otherwise dry out. This flooding regime sustains exceptional productivity.

The delta is a critical staging and breeding area for migratory waterfowl on all four North American flyways, and it supports one of the largest free-roaming herds of wood bison. Muskrats, moose, wolves, and whooping cranes—which nest in the wider park region—are among its notable wildlife.

Conservation and threats

The delta was designated a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 1982, and it lies within a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Water levels declined after construction of the W. A. C. Bennett Dam on the Peace River in British Columbia (completed 1968), which dampened the natural flood pulse; subsequent dry years in the 1970s caused serious ecological stress. Concerns continue over upstream hydroelectric development, water withdrawals, oil sands development in the Athabasca watershed, and climate change.

People

The delta region is the traditional territory of Cree and Chipewyan (Dene) peoples, including the Mikisew Cree First Nation and the Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation, whose members continue to hunt, fish, and trap in the delta.
