Lucrezia Borgia (Italian: [luˈkrɛttsja ˈbɔrdʒa]; 18 April 1480 – 24 June 1519) was an Italian noblewoman of the House of Borgia, the illegitimate daughter of Pope Alexander VI and his mistress Vannozza dei Cattanei. Her three marriages — arranged and rearranged to serve her father's and her brother Cesare's political ambitions — made her a central piece in the power politics of Renaissance Italy. Rumours of poisonings and scandal, amplified by her family's enemies, fixed her posthumous image as a femme fatale, though modern historians generally portray her as an accomplished courtier and patron, more pawn than poisoner.

Early life

Lucrezia was born at Subiaco, outside Rome, the only daughter among the children of Cardinal Rodrigo Borgia and Vannozza dei Cattanei; her brothers Cesare, Giovanni (Juan) and Gioffre (Jofré) were raised with her in Rome, largely in the household of her cousin Adriana de Mila. She was educated in Latin, Italian, French and music and was famed for her golden hair and charm. Her father became Pope Alexander VI in 1492, and her marital value as the pope's daughter rose accordingly.

Marriages

Giovanni Sforza (1493–1497)

At thirteen Lucrezia married Giovanni Sforza, lord of Pesaro, sealing an alliance with Milan. When the alliance became useless, Alexander annulled the marriage on grounds of non-consummation — a humiliation Sforza avenged by spreading claims of incest within the Borgia family.

Alfonso of Aragon (1498–1500)

Her second marriage, to Alfonso of Aragon, Duke of Bisceglie, tied the Borgias to Naples. When policy shifted toward France, Alfonso was attacked on the steps of St Peter's in July 1500 and, while recovering, strangled in his room — a murder widely attributed to Cesare's agents. Lucrezia, genuinely attached to Alfonso, withdrew to Nepi in grief.

Alfonso I d'Este (1501–1519)

Her third marriage, to Alfonso d'Este, heir to the Duchy of Ferrara, was negotiated for a dowry of 200,000 ducats. In Ferrara Lucrezia remade herself as a Renaissance duchess: patron of poets including Ludovico Ariosto and Pietro Bembo (with whom she exchanged famous letters), correspondent of scholars, and an effective regent during her husband's absences on campaign. She bore several children, including Ercole II d'Este, and was respected for her charity and religious devotion, becoming a Franciscan tertiary.

Death and reputation

Lucrezia died at Ferrara on 24 June 1519, ten days after giving birth to a stillborn daughter, aged 39; she was buried in the convent of Corpus Domini. Her black legend — poison rings, a hollow ring filled with venom, banquets of murder — owes more to Borgia enemies and later dramatists (Victor Hugo's play Lucrèce Borgia and Donizetti's opera of the same name) than to evidence; historians find no proof she committed any murder. A lock of her golden hair, kept in a reliquary at the Pinacoteca Ambrosiana in Milan, inspired Lord Byron, who visited it and stole a strand.
