Periaqueductal gray

The periaqueductal gray (PAG), also known as the central gray, is a region of gray matter in the midbrain that plays a central role in analgesia (pain suppression), defensive behaviour, and autonomic control. It surrounds the cerebral aqueduct connecting the third and fourth ventricles.

Anatomy

The PAG is organised into distinct longitudinal columns — dorsomedial, dorsolateral, lateral and ventrolateral — each associated with different patterns of response. It has extensive connections with the hypothalamus, amygdala, rostral ventromedial medulla and the frontal cortex.

Function

Electrical stimulation of the PAG produces profound analgesia, an effect mediated in part by endogenous opioids (enkephalins) acting on connections to the medulla, which in turn inhibit pain transmission in the spinal cord. The dorsolateral PAG organises active coping responses such as fight and flight, while the ventrolateral column mediates passive responses such as freezing and recuperative behaviour. The PAG also plays roles in vocalisation, micturition and cardiovascular regulation.

Clinical relevance

Deep brain stimulation of the periaqueductal/periventricular gray has been used since the 1970s to treat chronic neuropathic pain. Abnormal PAG activity is also implicated in panic attacks and migraine.
