The Book of Ezekiel is the third of the Latter Prophets in the Hebrew Bible and a major prophetic book in the Christian Old Testament. It records the visions and symbolic actions of Ezekiel, a priest of Jerusalem who was deported to Babylonia in 597 BC and prophesied among the exiles between about 593 and 571 BC. The book is famous for its vivid and sometimes bizarre imagery, including the opening vision of God's chariot-throne (the merkabah) borne by four living creatures.

Structure

The book falls into three broad sections. Chapters 1–24 contain oracles of judgment against Judah and Jerusalem, culminating in the news of the city's fall (586 BC). Chapters 25–32 are oracles against foreign nations, including Tyre, Egypt, and Ammon. Chapters 33–48 turn to consolation and restoration: the watchman oracle, the vision of the valley of dry bones (chapter 37), the prophecy of Gog of Magog, and an extended vision (chapters 40–48) of a restored temple, a reordered land, and a river flowing from the temple that gives life to the Dead Sea.

Themes

Ezekiel emphasizes the transcendence and holiness of God, the departure and hoped-for return of God's glory (kavod) to the temple, and individual moral responsibility — famously insisting that "the one who sins is the one who will die" rather than children being punished for their parents (chapter 18). Its promise of a new heart and a new spirit (36:26) became influential in later Judaism and Christianity. The merkabah vision helped inspire Jewish Merkabah mysticism, and the book's imagery of Gog and Magog and the new temple strongly influenced the New Testament Book of Revelation.

Authorship

Traditional attribution is to Ezekiel himself, and the book shows unusual unity of style and precisely dated oracles. Modern scholars generally accept a historical Ezekiel as the core author while allowing for later editorial expansion by his disciples.
