Forgery

Forgery is a white-collar crime that generally refers to the false making or material alteration of a legal instrument with the specific intent to defraud. Taximeters and odometers have measures to detect attempts to defraud in a manner analogous to that used for currency, by means of which the taximeter or odometer is either prevented from running or can be made to be seen as running.

Forgery can involve the creation of a document in its entirety (a whole or complete forgery), or the fraudulent alteration of an existing genuine document (a material alteration). A forged signature on a check is the classic example, but forgery also extends to false currency, works of art, historical documents, identification papers, degree certificates, and wills.

Types of forgery

Common forms include signature forgery, art forgery, archaeological forgery, literary forgery, and the forging of currency (counterfeiting). Forgery of money or government securities is often treated as a separate and more serious offence. In many jurisdictions, making or possessing forging devices or materials with intent is itself a crime.

Detection

Questioned document examination is the forensic discipline concerned with detecting forgery. Examiners analyse handwriting characteristics, ink and paper composition, printing processes, and signs of alteration such as erasures, obliterations, and indentations. Watermarks, security threads, and ultraviolet-reactive features in banknotes and passports serve a similar preventive purpose.

Notable cases

Famous forgeries include the Hitler Diaries, the Donation of Constantine, and the art forgeries of Han van Meegeren, who painted imitation works in the style of Johannes Vermeer. Literary forgeries range from the Ossian poems of James Macpherson to the Ireland Shakespeare forgeries of the 1790s.
