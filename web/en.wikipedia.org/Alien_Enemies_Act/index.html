Alien Enemies Act

The Alien Enemies Act (officially the Act Respecting Alien Enemies, 1 Stat. 577) is a United States federal law enacted on July 6, 1798, as part of the Alien and Sedition Acts. It authorizes the president of the United States, whenever a declared war exists between the United States and a foreign nation or any invasion or predatory incursion is perpetrated, attempted, or threatened, to apprehend, restrain, secure, and remove citizens of the hostile nation aged fourteen or older as "alien enemies."

The Alien Enemies Act was signed into law by President John Adams in 1798.

History

The act was passed by the Federalist-controlled Fifth Congress amid the Quasi-War with France, when fears of French invasion and espionage ran high. Unlike its companion measures — the Alien Friends Act, the Naturalization Act of 1798, and the Sedition Act — which expired or were repealed in the early nineteenth century, the Alien Enemies Act contained no sunset clause and remains in force today, codified at 50 U.S.C. §§ 21–24.

Invocations

The statute has been invoked during several major conflicts. President James Madison invoked it during the War of 1812 against British nationals. President Woodrow Wilson invoked it during the First World War, and President Franklin D. Roosevelt invoked it during the Second World War against nationals of Japan, Germany, and Italy, forming part of the legal framework within which the internment and exclusion programs of that era were carried out. It was invoked again in 2025 in connection with a proclamation targeting members of a Venezuelan criminal organization, generating substantial litigation over whether the statutory predicates — a declared war or an invasion or predatory incursion by a foreign nation or government — were satisfied.

Legal challenges

In Ludecke v. Watkins (1948), the Supreme Court upheld a deportation under the act, holding that the question of when an enemy alien's removal is warranted is political and largely beyond judicial review. Courts in modern cases have continued to debate how much process is due to individuals detained under the act, including notice and an opportunity to contest their designation.
