Richard I (8 September 1157 – 6 April 1199), known as Richard the Lionheart, was King of England from 1189 until his death. The third son of Henry II and Eleanor of Aquitaine, he was a celebrated military commander of the Third Crusade, but spent only about six months of his ten-year reign in England.

Early life

Raised largely in Aquitaine, his mother's duchy, Richard became an accomplished knight, troubadour poet and political operator, repeatedly rebelling against his father alongside his brothers. After the death of his elder brother Henry the Young King, Richard became heir to England, Normandy and Anjou, and succeeded on Henry II's death in 1189.

The Third Crusade

Richard took the cross and, with Philip II of France, set out to recover Jerusalem from Saladin. En route he conquered Cyprus, a lasting Byzantine loss and a vital crusader supply base. In the Holy Land he captured Acre (1191) and defeated Saladin at Arsuf and Jaffa, twice advancing within sight of Jerusalem, but judged the city untenable and negotiated the Treaty of Jaffa (1192), which secured coastal cities and pilgrim access.

Captivity and final years

Returning home, Richard was captured near Vienna by Duke Leopold of Austria and handed to Emperor Henry VI, who ransomed him for the enormous sum of 150,000 marks — raised by heavy taxation in England. Freed in 1194, he spent his remaining years fighting Philip II in France, building the great castle of Château Gaillard, and was killed by a crossbow bolt while besieging the castle of Châlus-Chabrol in 1199. He was succeeded by his brother John.

Legacy

Richard's reputation as "the Lionheart" celebrates his martial prowess and chivalric image, perpetuated by chronicles and later by romances and the Robin Hood tradition, in which he figures as the absent good king. Modern historians note his neglect of England's governance, the financial burden of his crusade and ransom, and his significance as a commander and as a symbol in both English and French national memory.
