Great Firewall

The Great Firewall (GFW; simplified Chinese: 防火长城; traditional Chinese: 防火長城; pinyin: Fánghuǒ Chángchéng) is the combination of legislative actions and technologies enforced by the People's Republic of China to regulate the Internet domestically. Its role in internet censorship in China is to block access to selected foreign websites and to slow down cross-border internet traffic. It is a part of the broader Golden Shield Project.

The Great Firewall operates by checking transmission control protocol (TCP) packets for keywords or sensitive words. If the search result contains certain keywords related to state security, the GFW can block the transmission. Blocked websites include major foreign platforms such as Google services, Facebook, Twitter (X), YouTube, Instagram and Wikipedia editions at various times, as well as foreign news outlets.

History

The Golden Shield Project was initiated by the Ministry of Public Security in 1998, and its first phase was completed in 2006. Fang Binxing, former president of Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications, is widely credited as the "Father of the Great Firewall" for his role in designing its core filtering systems. The GFW grew more sophisticated over the 2010s, adding deep packet inspection (DPI), probing of circumvention servers, and — from around 2019 — blocking of fully encrypted traffic such as TLS 1.3 with ESNI and later Encrypted Client Hello (ECH).

Methods

  • IP blocking — access to particular IP addresses is denied, sometimes through null routing.
  • DNS spoofing and filtering — incorrect IP addresses are returned for blocked domains, a phenomenon that also causes collateral "DNS poisoning" outside China.
  • URL and keyword filtering — HTTP requests are scanned for target URLs or blacklisted keywords; a match triggers TCP reset packets to both endpoints.
  • Deep packet inspection — packets are examined for objectionable content even within otherwise permitted connections.
  • SNI filtering — the plaintext Server Name Indication in TLS handshakes is used to block HTTPS sites by domain.
  • Active probing — suspected circumvention servers (e.g., Tor bridges or VPN endpoints) are probed by GFW-operated hosts; if a server responds like a proxy, its IP is blocked.
  • Connection resets and throttling — cross-border traffic to some services is degraded rather than fully blocked.

Circumvention

Users inside China circumvent the GFW with virtual private networks (VPNs), Shadowsocks, V2Ray, Trojan and other proxy protocols designed to disguise traffic. Commercial VPNs that are not government-approved are periodically blocked, especially during politically sensitive periods. Apple removed many VPN apps from its Chinese App Store in 2017 at the government's request.

Effects and criticism

The GFW has fostered a domestic internet ecosystem dominated by Chinese platforms such as Baidu, WeChat, Weibo and Alibaba's services. Critics argue it restricts freedom of expression and access to information, while the Chinese government defends it as an exercise of "cyber sovereignty". The system has also caused collateral effects — because of the way DNS responses propagate, some users outside China have occasionally received poisoned DNS results.
