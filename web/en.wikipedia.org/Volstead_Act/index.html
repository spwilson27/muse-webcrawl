Volstead Act

The National Prohibition Act, known informally as the Volstead Act, was the United States federal legislation that provided for the enforcement of the Eighteenth Amendment, which established the prohibition of alcoholic beverages. Congress passed it on 28 October 1919 over the veto of President Woodrow Wilson; it took effect on 17 January 1920. It is named for Andrew Volstead, the Minnesota congressman who chaired the House Judiciary Committee and sponsored the bill.

Provisions

The Act defined "intoxicating liquor" as any beverage containing more than 0.5% alcohol by volume, and set out procedures for enforcement, including permits for medicinal, sacramental and industrial alcohol. It did not ban the consumption or private possession of alcohol outright, but criminalised its manufacture, sale and transport — gaps that fuelled bootlegging, speakeasies and organised crime during the 1920s.

Repeal

Weak enforcement, widespread evasion and the economic pressures of the Great Depression undermined Prohibition. The Volstead Act was first relaxed by the Cullen–Harrison Act of March 1933, which legalised low-alcohol beer, and then rendered void by the ratification of the Twenty-first Amendment on 5 December 1933, the only amendment to repeal another.
