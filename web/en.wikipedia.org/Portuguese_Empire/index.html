The Portuguese Empire was the first and longest-lived of the modern European colonial empires, spanning nearly six centuries from the capture of Ceuta in 1415 to the handover of Macau to China in 1999. Pioneered by the voyages sponsored by Prince Henry the Navigator, Portugal built a global network of trading posts (feitorias), fortresses and colonies across Africa, Asia and South America.

Expansion

Portuguese sailors rounded the Cape of Good Hope (Dias, 1488), reached India (Vasco da Gama, 1498), Brazil (Cabral, 1500), Malacca (1511) and Japan (1543). The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the non-European world between Portugal and Spain. Goa became the capital of the Estado da Índia; Brazil grew into the empire's largest colony on sugar, gold and coffee, and declared independence in 1822.

Decline and decolonisation

The empire contracted under Dutch, English and Omani competition in the 17th century, but Portugal clung to Angola, Mozambique, Goa, East Timor and Macau into the 20th century. After the Carnation Revolution of 1974, the African colonies rapidly became independent (1975); India had annexed Goa in 1961; East Timor's independence was finally recognised in 2002. Today the legacy persists in the Portuguese language, spoken by some 250 million people, and in the Community of Portuguese Language Countries (CPLP).
