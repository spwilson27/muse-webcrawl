Lacteal

A lacteal lies at the centre of each intestinal villus

A lacteal is a lymphatic capillary that absorbs dietary fats in the villi of the small intestine. Its name derives from the milky appearance of the fat-laden lymph — called chyle — that fills it after a fatty meal.

Structure and function

Each finger-like villus projecting into the lumen of the small intestine contains a single central lacteal, surrounded by a network of blood capillaries. Short-chain and medium-chain fatty acids and other small lipid products can enter the blood capillaries directly, but long-chain fatty acids and monoglycerides are reassembled into triglycerides inside enterocytes and packaged into lipoprotein particles called chylomicrons. Chylomicrons are too large to enter blood capillaries; instead they are exocytosed into the lamina propria and enter the lacteal through its permeable, discontinuous endothelium.

From the lacteals, chyle drains through progressively larger mesenteric lymphatics to the cisterna chyli and then via the thoracic duct into the venous circulation at the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins, bypassing first-pass processing in the liver.

Clinical significance

Damage or obstruction of intestinal lymphatics can cause intestinal lymphangiectasia, in which lacteals dilate and leak lymph into the gut lumen, producing protein-losing enteropathy, fat malabsorption and deficiencies of fat-soluble vitamins.
