The Fizeau experiment was carried out by Hippolyte Fizeau in 1851 to measure the relative speeds of light in moving water. Fizeau used a special interferometer arrangement to measure the effect of movement of a medium upon the speed of light.

[Image placeholder: Diagram of Fizeau's 1851 apparatus with water flowing through paired tubes]

The experiment

A beam of light from a single source was split into two beams that travelled in opposite directions through two tubes of rapidly flowing water and were then recombined to produce interference fringes. Light travelling with the flow was expected to be dragged along by the water, and light travelling against the flow to be slowed. The observed fringe shift was intermediate between the predictions of two rival hypotheses: it ruled out complete dragging of the luminiferous aether by the water, and matched the partial-drag coefficient proposed earlier by Augustin-Jean Fresnel, 1 − 1/n², where n is the refractive index of the medium.

Interpretation

Within nineteenth-century aether theory, Fresnel's partial drag was an awkward postulate. The result was revisited by Michelson and Morley in 1886, who confirmed Fizeau's findings with greater accuracy. In 1905 Einstein showed that special relativity explains the effect naturally: the speed of light in a medium moving at velocity v combines with v according to the relativistic velocity-addition formula, whose first-order expansion gives exactly the Fresnel coefficient. Max von Laue gave this derivation in 1907, and the Fizeau experiment is now regarded as an important precursor and experimental support of special relativity. Hendrik Lorentz had earlier obtained the same coefficient from his electron theory with the additional refinement of accounting for dispersion.
