Fireworks are a class of low-explosive pyrotechnic devices used for aesthetic and entertainment purposes. They are commonly used in celebrations and festivals around the world, including New Year's Eve, Diwali, and Independence Day in the United States. Fireworks take many forms to produce the four primary effects: noise, light, smoke, and floating materials. They may be designed to burn with coloured flames and sparks, and to burst into patterned aerial displays.

History

Fireworks originated in China. The earliest fireworks were firecrackers — bamboo stalks that exploded when thrown into a fire, used to frighten away evil spirits. By the Song dynasty (960–1279), gunpowder-filled paper tubes and aerial rockets were in use. The knowledge of gunpowder spread westward along the Silk Road and through Mongol conquests; by the Renaissance, fireworks were a feature of European court celebrations, and Italy and Germany developed famous schools of pyrotechnics.

Chemistry

An aerial shell contains a bursting charge of black powder and "stars" — small pellets of fuel, oxidiser and metal-salt colourants. Strontium compounds give red, barium green, sodium yellow-gold, copper blue (the most difficult colour), and calcium orange; aluminium and magnesium produce brilliant white sparks. A time fuse ignites the burst at the shell's apogee.

Types and safety

Consumer fireworks include sparklers, fountains, Roman candles, rockets and small shells; professional display fireworks are fired electronically by licensed pyrotechnicians. Fireworks cause thousands of injuries and fires annually, and many jurisdictions restrict their sale and use; they are also criticised for noise, air pollution and distress to animals.
