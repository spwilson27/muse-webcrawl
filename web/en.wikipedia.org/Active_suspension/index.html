Active suspension

An active suspension is a type of automotive suspension that uses an onboard control system to control the vertical movement of the vehicle wheels relative to the chassis or vehicle frame, rather than the purely passive response of springs and dampers. Active suspensions are divided into two classes: real active suspensions, which use actuators to raise and lower the chassis independently at each corner, and adaptive or semi-active suspensions, which vary damper firmness in real time.

Principles

Sensors continuously monitor body motion, wheel position, and road conditions. A control unit processes these signals and commands actuators—hydraulic, pneumatic, electromagnetic, or electromechanical—to counteract body roll, pitch, and dive, and to keep the tire contact patch loaded over bumps.

History

Hydropneumatic systems by Citroën in the 1950s were early forerunners. Fully active systems were famously developed in Formula One by Lotus in the 1980s and early 1990s, powering cars such as the Lotus 99T before the technology was banned from the sport in 1994. Production systems include Mercedes-Benz Active Body Control and electromagnetic designs by Bose.
