Anopheles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anopheles is a genus of mosquito first described by Johann Wilhelm Meigen in 1818. Of its roughly 460 recognised species, about 100 can transmit malaria to humans, and some 30–40 species are important vectors. The most significant is Anopheles gambiae sensu stricto, a principal vector of Plasmodium falciparum malaria in Africa. The name means "useless" or "harmful" in Greek.

Life cycle

Like all mosquitoes, Anopheles passes through egg, larva, pupa and adult stages, the first three in water. Larvae lie parallel to the water surface and lack the breathing siphon of other mosquitoes. Adults rest with the abdomen angled away from the surface — a distinctive head-down posture. Only females feed on blood, which they need for egg development; both sexes feed on nectar.

Disease transmission

When an infected female bites, Plasmodium parasites pass in her saliva into the human bloodstream. Some Anopheles species also transmit filariasis and viruses such as O'nyong'nyong virus. Control measures include insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, larval source management, and new approaches such as sterile insect technique and gene-drive mosquitoes.
