Infertility

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Infertility is the failure to achieve a pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular unprotected sexual intercourse; evaluation is typically begun after 6 months for women over 35. The World Health Organization estimates that around one in six people of reproductive age worldwide experience infertility during their lifetime. It may stem from male factors, female factors, a combination of both, or remain unexplained.

Causes

Female infertility commonly results from ovulatory disorders (including polycystic ovary syndrome), tubal damage (often following infection), endometriosis, and uterine abnormalities; fertility declines markedly with age, especially after 35. Male infertility most often involves impaired sperm production or function — low count, poor motility, or abnormal morphology — caused by genetic conditions, varicocele, infection, hormonal disorders, or environmental and lifestyle factors such as smoking, obesity, and heat exposure.

Diagnosis

Assessment generally covers both partners and includes a medical history, semen analysis, confirmation of ovulation, hormone testing, and imaging or surgical evaluation of the uterus and fallopian tubes (for example hysterosalpingography or laparoscopy).

Treatment

Management depends on the cause and ranges from lifestyle measures and ovulation-inducing medicines, through surgery and intrauterine insemination, to assisted reproductive technologies such as in vitro fertilisation (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). Since the birth of Louise Brown in 1978, more than ten million babies have been born worldwide through IVF. Donor gametes, embryo donation, and surrogacy are further options, though legal frameworks vary widely between countries.

Psychological and social aspects

Infertility frequently causes significant distress, strained relationships, and, in some cultures, social stigma, historically borne disproportionately by women. Support groups and counselling form part of comprehensive care.
