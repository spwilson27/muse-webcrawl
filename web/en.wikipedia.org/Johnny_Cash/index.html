Johnny Cash (born J. R. Cash; February 26, 1932 – September 12, 2003) was an American singer-songwriter. Most of Cash's music contains themes of sorrow, moral tribulation, and redemption, especially songs from the later stages of his career. He was known for his deep, calm, bass-baritone voice, the distinctive sound of his backing band the Tennessee Three, his rebelliousness coupled with an increasingly somber and humble demeanor, free prison concerts, and a trademark all-black stage wardrobe, which earned him the nickname the "Man in Black".

Early life

Cash was born in Kingsland, Arkansas, and raised in the farming community of Dyess, where his family worked cotton fields. His older brother Jack died in a sawmill accident in 1944, an event that affected him deeply. After high school he enlisted in the United States Air Force, serving in Germany, where he formed his first band.

Career

Auditioning for Sam Phillips at Sun Studio in Memphis in 1954, Cash recorded early hits such as "Folsom Prison Blues", "I Walk the Line" (1956), "Ring of Fire" (1963) — co-written by June Carter and Merle Kilgore — and "Get Rhythm". He gave legendary free concerts at Folsom Prison and San Quentin, recorded as live albums. His career dipped amid amphetamine addiction in the 1960s, but with the support of June Carter, whom he married in 1968, he recovered and hosted The Johnny Cash Show on television from 1969 to 1971.

Later years and legacy

In the 1990s producer Rick Rubin revived Cash's career with the American Recordings series, introducing him to a new generation; his spare cover of Nine Inch Nails' "Hurt" (2002) became one of his signature recordings. Cash died in 2003, four months after June Carter. One of the best-selling music artists of all time, he sold more than 90 million records worldwide and was inducted into the Country Music, Rock and Roll, and Gospel Music halls of fame.
