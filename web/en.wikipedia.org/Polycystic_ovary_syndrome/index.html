Polycystic ovary syndrome

Polycystic ovary syndrome
SpecialtyGynaecology, endocrinology
SymptomsIrregular periods, excess androgens, acne, hirsutism, infertility
FrequencyAffects roughly 8–13% of women of reproductive age

Polycystic ovary syndrome, or PCOS, is the most common endocrine disorder among women of reproductive age. The name comes from the many small fluid-filled sacs (follicles) that may collect in the ovaries, though not all women with the condition have them, and such cysts also occur in women without the syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

Features of PCOS include irregular or absent menstrual periods, signs of excess androgen hormones such as acne and hirsutism (male-pattern hair growth), obesity and difficulty conceiving. Under the widely used Rotterdam criteria, diagnosis requires two of three findings: infrequent ovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound, after exclusion of other causes.

Causes and pathophysiology

The cause is incompletely understood but involves genetic susceptibility interacting with environmental factors. Insulin resistance is common and drives increased ovarian androgen production; raised luteinising hormone levels and low-grade inflammation also contribute. Women with PCOS have increased long-term risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, endometrial cancer, sleep apnoea and depression.

Treatment

There is no cure, but symptoms can be managed. Lifestyle modification promoting weight loss is first-line for women who are overweight. Combined oral contraceptives regulate cycles and reduce androgen symptoms; metformin improves insulin sensitivity; anti-androgens and cosmetic measures address hirsutism; and ovulation-induction agents such as letrozole treat infertility.
