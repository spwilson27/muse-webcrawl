The Pullman Company, founded by George Pullman, was a manufacturer of railroad cars in the United States, from the mid-to-late 19th century through the first half of the 20th century. Through the late 19th century it also functioned as a paratransit operator, providing its own sleeping cars — staffed by its own porters — on the trains of many railroads under contract. The name "Pullman" became synonymous with luxury rail travel and with the sleeping car itself.

History

George Pullman's first sleeping car conversions date to 1859; his purpose-built luxury car, the Pioneer of 1865, gained fame when it was used in Abraham Lincoln's funeral train. Pullman's Palace Car Company was incorporated in 1867. In 1880 Pullman built the planned company town of Pullman, Illinois (now part of Chicago), to house its workers — an experiment in paternalistic industrial planning that drew international attention.

Wage cuts during the depression of 1893 without reductions in company-town rents provoked the nationwide Pullman Strike of 1894, led by Eugene V. Debs and the American Railway Union, which was broken by federal injunction and troops. Following George Pullman's death in 1897, Robert Todd Lincoln became company president. The company merged its manufacturing and operating interests as the Pullman Company in 1900, absorbing competitors such as Wagner Palace Car Company.

Pullman porters

Pullman employed a large workforce of African American porters, who provided service aboard the cars. Underpaid and dependent on tips, they organized in 1925 as the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters under A. Philip Randolph — the first African American labour union to win a collective bargaining agreement with a major US corporation (1937). The porters played a significant role in the growth of the Black middle class and the civil rights movement.

Decline and breakup

After a federal antitrust ruling, Pullman Inc. divested its operating company (Pullman Operating Company) in 1947; the railroads purchased the sleeping car fleet. The Pullman-Standard manufacturing division continued building freight and passenger cars until the early 1980s. The Pullman name's railroad car business wound down as passenger rail declined in the United States.
