Convection is single or multiphase fluid flow that occurs spontaneously through the combined effects of material property heterogeneity and body forces on a fluid, most commonly density and gravity. Convection is the transfer of heat through the bulk movement of a fluid: warmer, less dense material rises while cooler, denser material sinks, setting up circulation cells that carry energy far faster than conduction alone.

Types

Natural convection arises from buoyancy forces caused by temperature- or composition-driven density differences, as in a pot of boiling water, thunderstorms, and ocean currents. Forced convection occurs when an external device — a pump, fan, or wind — drives the fluid flow, as in a convection oven or a car's cooling system. Granular and capillary convection also exist in specialized settings.

Convection in nature

Convection drives atmospheric circulation and weather, oceanic currents, plate tectonics through mantle convection, and stellar energy transport in the outer layers of stars like the Sun. In the atmosphere, convective instability produces cumulus clouds and thunderstorms; in the Earth's mantle, slow solid-state convection over geological time moves the tectonic plates.

Mathematics

Convective flow is described by the Navier–Stokes equations coupled to energy and mass transport. The onset of natural convection in a heated layer is governed by the dimensionless Rayleigh number; above a critical value (about 1,708 for a layer with rigid boundaries), uniform conduction gives way to patterned convection cells — the classic Rayleigh–Bénard problem.
