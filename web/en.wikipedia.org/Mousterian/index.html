PeriodMiddle Paleolithic
Datesc. 160,000 – 40,000 years ago

The Mousterian (or Mode III) is an archaeological industry of stone tools, associated primarily with the Neanderthals in Europe and with early anatomically modern humans in the Levant and North Africa. It dates from roughly 160,000 to 40,000 years ago and takes its name from the type site of Le Moustier in the Dordogne region of southwestern France, excavated in the 1860s.

Technology

Mousterian toolkits are dominated by flake tools — side scrapers, points, and hand axes — produced using the Levallois technique, in which a stone core is carefully shaped so that a flake of predetermined size and form can be struck from it. Compared with earlier Acheulean industries, Mousterian assemblages show greater standardisation and a wider range of specialised tool forms, struck from flint and other fine-grained stone.

Variation and debate

In the mid-20th century, François Bordes classified French Mousterian assemblages into several variants (Typical, Denticulate, Ferrassie, Quina, and Mousterian of Acheulean Tradition), which he attributed to distinct cultural traditions of different Neanderthal groups. Lewis Binford argued instead that the variants reflected different activities performed at different sites — a famous debate in Paleolithic archaeology about whether assemblage variation is ethnic, functional, or the result of tool resharpening.

Behaviour

Mousterian sites provide evidence of controlled use of fire, hunting of large game, hafting of stone points onto wooden spears, and — debated — deliberate burial of the dead, as at Shanidar Cave in Iraq. The industry disappears around 40,000 years ago as Neanderthals were replaced by modern humans bearing Upper Paleolithic (Aurignacian) toolkits, though transitional industries such as the Châtelperronian complicate the picture.
