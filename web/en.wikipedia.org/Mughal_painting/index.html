Mughal painting
PeriodLate 16th–19th centuries
RegionMughal Empire, South Asia
PatronsAkbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and successors

Mughal painting is a style of painting on paper confined to miniatures, either as book illustrations or single works, that developed in the court of the Mughal Empire from the late 16th century. It emerged from Persian miniature painting, brought to India by artists who followed the exiled emperor Humayun from Persia, and fused with Indian traditions and, later, European influences to create a distinctive, largely secular court art.

Under Akbar (r. 1556–1605), a huge imperial atelier of over a hundred artists — directed by Persian masters Mir Sayyid Ali and Abd al-Samad, and employing mostly Hindu Indian painters such as Basawan and Daswanth — produced vast illustrated manuscripts, including the Hamzanama, the Tutinama and the Akbarnama. Under Jahangir (r. 1605–1627), painting reached its zenith of naturalism, with superb studies of animals, birds and court life by artists such as Mansur, Abu'l-Hasan and Bichitr; European prints brought by Jesuit missions introduced perspective and modelling. Shah Jahan's reign favoured formal court scenes and exquisite marginal decoration, as in the Padshahnama.

Decline and influence

Imperial patronage waned under Aurangzeb and the empire's 18th-century decline, but Mughal-trained artists dispersed to the Rajput and Deccani courts, profoundly influencing regional schools of Indian miniature painting. The style survived into the 19th century in provincial and Company-school offshoots.
