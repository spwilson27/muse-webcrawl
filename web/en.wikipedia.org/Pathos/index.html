Pathos

Pathos (from Greek πάθος, "suffering" or "experience") is one of the three modes of persuasion identified by Aristotle in his Rhetoric, alongside ethos and logos. It appeals to the audience's emotions.

A speaker or writer using pathos seeks to evoke feelings such as pity, sympathy, anger, fear, or hope in order to persuade. Aristotle regarded emotion as a legitimate part of argument, provided the appeal was appropriate to the subject; later rhetoricians cautioned against manipulating emotion to overwhelm reason.

In rhetoric

Pathos operates through vivid description, personal anecdote, figurative language, and tone. In political speeches, advertising, and courtroom argument, emotional appeals are used to make an audience care about the conclusion being urged.

Related senses

In art and literature, "pathos" also names the quality in a work that arouses feelings of pity and sadness — as in the pathos of a tragic scene. The adjective "pathetic" derives from the same root, though in modern usage it often means inadequacy or contemptibility rather than emotional power.
