Aneuploidy

Aneuploidy is the presence of an abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell — one or more chromosomes extra or missing relative to the normal complement. A cell with the correct number is euploid. In humans the normal number is 46; a cell with 45 or 47 chromosomes is aneuploid. Aneuploidy arises chiefly from nondisjunction, the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during meiosis or mitosis. It is the leading genetic cause of miscarriage and congenital disorders, and aneuploidy is also a hallmark of most cancer cells. (Aneuploidy differs from polyploidy, in which whole extra sets of chromosomes are present — a normal condition in many plants.)

Types and examples

  • Trisomy — three copies of one chromosome. Trisomy 21 causes Down syndrome (~1 in 700 births); trisomies 18 (Edwards syndrome) and 13 (Patau syndrome) are usually lethal in infancy. Most other autosomal trisomies end in miscarriage.
  • Monosomy — one copy only. Complete autosomal monosomy is lethal; monosomy X (45,X, Turner syndrome) is the only viable full monosomy in humans.
  • Sex-chromosome aneuploidies — 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome), 47,XXX, and 47,XYY are relatively common and often mild, because of X-inactivation and the gene-poor Y chromosome.
  • Mosaic aneuploidy — arises from mitotic nondisjunction after fertilization, producing a mixture of euploid and aneuploid cells, often with milder effects.

Causes and risk factors

Meiotic nondisjunction increases markedly with maternal age — the risk of a trisomy 21 conception rises from about 1 in 1,500 at age 20 to about 1 in 100 at 40 — reflecting decades-long arrest of oocytes in meiosis I and gradual loss of chromosome cohesion. Errors in sperm occur too but show little age effect. Aneuploidy in early embryos is extremely common in human reproduction; a large fraction of miscarriages (roughly half of first-trimester losses) are aneuploid conceptions. In cancer, chromosomal instability produces highly aneuploid karyotypes, and whether aneuploidy drives or merely accompanies tumorigenesis remains an active research question.

Detection

Prenatal screening includes first-trimester ultrasound (nuchal translucency) and blood markers, non-invasive prenatal testing of cell-free fetal DNA, and diagnostic karyotyping or microarray on chorionic villus or amniotic fluid samples. Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-A) screens IVF embryos, though its benefit and interpretation for mosaic embryos are debated. In plants, aneuploid variants (such as Blakeslee's Datura trisomics) were historically important tools of genetics, and in agriculture aneuploidy is exploited in breeding and seedless fruit.
