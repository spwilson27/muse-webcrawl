Militia (United States)

A militia in the United States is a body of citizens organised for military service. Under current federal law (10 U.S.C. § 246), the militia consists of the organized militia — the National Guard and the Naval Militia — and the unorganized militia, comprising all able-bodied male citizens (and those who have declared an intention to become citizens) between the ages of 17 and 45 who are not members of the organized militia. The militia has deep roots in English and colonial tradition and is referenced in the Second Amendment to the United States Constitution.

Minutemen of the American Revolution are the iconic image of the citizen militia.

Colonial and revolutionary era

English colonies in North America required military service of able-bodied men, organised in local companies that mustered periodically. During the American Revolutionary War, militia forces served alongside the Continental Army: they turned out in large numbers at Lexington and Concord in April 1775, formed the bulk of American forces at Bunker Hill, and contributed decisively at Saratoga and Cowpens, though their reliability in sustained operations was variable. The "minutemen" of Massachusetts were militia companies pledged to turn out at a minute's notice.

Constitutional framework

The Constitution empowers Congress "to provide for calling forth the Militia to execute the Laws of the Union, suppress Insurrections and repel Invasions" and to organise, arm and discipline it, reserving to the states the appointment of officers and the authority of training. The Second Amendment (1791) declares that "a well regulated Militia, [is] necessary to the security of a free State". Anti-Federalists had feared that Congress could disarm or neglect the militia in favour of a standing army; the amendment addressed those concerns, and its scope was addressed by the Supreme Court in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008).

Early republic and 19th century

The Militia Acts of 1792 required enrolment of able-bodied white male citizens aged 18–45 and prescribed uniform organisation, though enforcement decayed over time. Militia performed poorly in parts of the War of 1812 but remained the nation's main land force until the Civil War, when mass conscript armies largely displaced it. Uniformed volunteer militia companies flourished socially in the late nineteenth century and provided the basis for state forces in the Spanish-American War.

The National Guard and modern militia

The Militia Act of 1903 (the Dick Act) formally divided the militia into the organized militia — renamed the National Guard — and the unorganized (reserve) militia, and set federal standards for Guard training and equipment. The National Defense Act of 1916 completed the Guard's integration as a federal reserve force. Many states also maintain state defense forces, authorised under 32 U.S.C. § 109, which are state-only militia components not subject to federal call-up. Separately, the term "militia movement" refers to private paramilitary groups that arose in the 1990s; these are not part of the statutory militia.
