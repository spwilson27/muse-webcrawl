Histology, also known as microscopic anatomy or microanatomy, is the branch of biology that studies the microscopic structure of biological tissues. It is the microscopic counterpart to gross anatomy, which examines larger structures visible without magnification. Histology is an essential tool of biology and medicine: in clinical practice, the microscopic examination of biopsies and surgical specimens — histopathology — is central to the diagnosis of cancer and many other diseases.

Methods

Tissues are typically fixed (commonly in formalin), dehydrated, embedded in paraffin wax, cut into thin sections (usually 3–5 µm) with a microtome, mounted on slides and stained. The standard stain, haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), colours nuclei blue-purple and cytoplasm and extracellular matrix pink. Special stains (such as PAS, Masson's trichrome, or silver stains), enzyme histochemistry and immunohistochemistry — using labelled antibodies to detect specific proteins — reveal further detail. For ultrastructure, electron microscopy examines resin-embedded sections tens of nanometres thick.

The four basic tissue types

  • Epithelial tissue — sheets of cells covering surfaces and lining cavities, and forming glands.
  • Connective tissue — cells embedded in abundant extracellular matrix: bone, cartilage, tendon, adipose tissue and blood.
  • Muscle tissue — skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle, specialised for contraction.
  • Nervous tissue — neurons and glial cells of the brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerves.

History

Marcello Malpighi (1628–1694) pioneered microscopic anatomy, observing capillaries and the structure of organs. Marie François Xavier Bichat (1771–1802), working without a microscope, introduced the concept of tissues (textus) as the fundamental units of the body. In the 19th century, cell theory (Schleiden, Schwann) and Virchow's cellular pathology grounded histology in the cell, and improved microscopes and stains enabled Cajal and Golgi to resolve the nervous system, work recognised by the 1906 Nobel Prize.
