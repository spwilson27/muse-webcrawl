Epiglottitis

A lateral neck X-ray in epiglottitis may show the "thumbprint sign" of a swollen epiglottis.

Epiglottitis is the inflammation of the epiglottis — the flap at the base of the tongue that prevents food from entering the trachea during swallowing. Because the swelling can rapidly obstruct the airway, epiglottitis is a medical emergency. Symptoms typically come on quickly and include difficulty swallowing, drooling, a sore throat, fever, a muffled or hoarse voice, and stridor; children often sit leaning forward in the "tripod position".

Cause

Epiglottitis is usually caused by bacterial infection. Historically, Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) was the predominant cause in children, but widespread Hib vaccination has sharply reduced paediatric cases in countries with immunisation programmes, shifting the burden toward older children and adults. Other causative organisms include Streptococcus pneumoniae, group A streptococcus, and Staphylococcus aureus. Non-infectious causes include thermal injury ("hot tea" epiglottitis), trauma, and caustic ingestion.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by direct visualisation of a cherry-red, swollen epiglottis, ideally in a controlled setting such as an operating theatre, since instrumentation of the airway may provoke complete obstruction — for the same reason, examination of the throat with a tongue depressor is discouraged in suspected cases. Imaging may show the "thumbprint sign" on a lateral neck radiograph.

Treatment and prognosis

Initial management focuses on securing the airway, typically by endotracheal intubation or, if necessary, tracheostomy, followed by intravenous antibiotics such as a third-generation cephalosporin. With prompt treatment, mortality in children is under 1%; without airway intervention, obstruction can be rapidly fatal. George Washington's death in 1799 has retrospectively been attributed by some medical historians to epiglottitis.
