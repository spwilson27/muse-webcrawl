SCR-300

The SCR-300 was a portable radio transceiver used by United States Signal Corps forces in World War II. Although carried on a soldier's back rather than held in the hand, it popularized the nickname "Walkie Talkie", which had earlier been applied to the handheld SCR-536 "Handie Talkie".

Development

The set was developed in 1940 by a team led by Daniel E. Noble at the Galvin Manufacturing Corporation (later Motorola) in Chicago. The United States Army required a battery-powered FM radio that infantry could carry into combat to communicate over short ranges in all weather. Following field tests, the SCR-300 entered service in 1943 and was first used in combat in Italy.

Technical characteristics

The SCR-300 operated in the 40.0 to 48.0 MHz FM band with 41 channels. It weighed about 38 pounds (17 kg) with batteries and had a typical range of about three miles (5 km) over land, varying with terrain. The unit used 18 vacuum tubes and was housed in a metal backpack case with a telescoping antenna; the handset was carried at the operator's chest.

Service and legacy

Approximately 50,000 units were produced during the war. The SCR-300 proved reliable in the Normandy landings, the Pacific campaigns, and later in the Korean War, and its success established FM backpack radios as standard infantry equipment. It was succeeded by the AN/PRC-10 in the 1950s. The set is remembered as a landmark in mobile radio engineering and a direct ancestor of modern tactical radios.
