Icing conditions

In aviation, icing conditions are atmospheric conditions that can lead to the formation of water ice on an aircraft. Ice accretion can affect the external surfaces of an aircraft — in which case it is called airframe icing — or the engine, causing carburettor icing or inlet icing that can disrupt airflow and power.

Formation and types

Airframe icing occurs when supercooled water droplets in cloud strike the aircraft and freeze. Depending on droplet size and temperature the result is rime ice (opaque and brittle, from small droplets freezing instantly), clear or glaze ice (dense and hard to remove, from larger droplets), or mixed ice. Supercooled large droplets (SLD), including freezing drizzle and rain, can form ice beyond protected surfaces and are especially dangerous.

Effects and protection

Ice accumulation degrades lift, increases drag and weight, alters stall behaviour, and can block control surfaces, sensors and antennas. Aircraft certified for flight into known icing carry protection systems such as pneumatic de-icing boots, heated leading edges, bleed-air anti-icing and weeping-wing fluid systems. Pilots encountering icing are advised to exit the conditions promptly, since forecasts and certifications cover only limited envelopes; icing remains a factor in numerous aviation accidents.
