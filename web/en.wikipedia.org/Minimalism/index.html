Minimalism

Minimalism in the arts began in post–World War II Western art, most strongly with American visual arts in the 1960s and early 1970s. Prominent artists associated with minimalism include Donald Judd, Agnes Martin, Dan Flavin, Carl Andre, Anne Truitt, and Frank Stella. The movement, in painting and sculpture, is often interpreted as a reaction against abstract expressionism and modernism; it anticipated contemporary postminimal art practices, which extend or reflect on minimalism's original objectives.

Origins and characteristics

Minimalist works are stripped of emotion and personal expression, using simple, geometric forms often made from industrial materials. The term was first applied to art by the critic Richard Wollheim in 1965. Minimalist artists rejected the idea that art should "represent" something or be an expression of the artist's biography; instead, they emphasized the work as an object in its own right — in Judd's phrase, "specific objects".

Minimalist music

In music, minimalism developed in the 1960s with composers such as La Monte Young, Terry Riley, Steve Reich, and Philip Glass. Works such as Riley's In C (1964), Reich's Music for 18 Musicians (1976), and Glass's Einstein on the Beach (1976) use repetition, steady pulse, and gradual process to build large-scale structures from limited material.

Architecture and design

Minimalist architecture draws on the modernist maxim "less is more", associated with Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, and on traditional Japanese design and Zen philosophy. Architects such as Tadao Ando and John Pawson employ simple geometry, unadorned materials, and careful use of light and space.

Lifestyle

In the 21st century, minimalism has also described a lifestyle movement advocating living with fewer possessions, decluttering, and prioritizing experiences and essentials over consumption — related to voluntary simplicity and, in popular culture, to the tidying methods of Marie Kondō.
