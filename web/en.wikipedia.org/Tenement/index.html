Tenement

Tenement buildings in a dense urban district

A tenement is a type of building shared by many dwellings, typically with flats or apartments on each floor and shared entrance stairways. The term is often associated with working-class housing erected during rapid urbanization and industrialization in the 19th and early 20th centuries, especially in cities such as New York City, Glasgow, Berlin, and Vienna.

History

Tenements arose as landlords sought to house growing populations of industrial workers and immigrants cheaply on expensive urban land. In New York, early tenements were often converted single-family houses or hastily built structures divided into small apartments, many without indoor plumbing, ventilation, or natural light. New York's Tenement House Acts of 1867, 1879, and 1901 progressively imposed standards for fire escapes, running water, windows, and indoor toilets. The 1879 law produced the characteristic "dumbbell" tenement, whose narrow air shafts gave the floor plan its shape.

In Scotland, tenements built of stone became the dominant urban housing form; Glasgow's Victorian tenements, often of red or blond sandstone, remain a defining feature of the city. In Central Europe, large perimeter-block tenements (German: Mietskaserne, "rental barracks") housed Berlin's working class, while Vienna later replaced speculative tenements with celebrated municipal housing projects such as Karl-Marx-Hof.

Conditions and reform

Overcrowding, poor sanitation, and fire risk made tenements a focus of Progressive Era reform. Jacob Riis's 1890 photographic exposé How the Other Half Lives documented conditions in New York's Lower East Side and helped spur housing legislation. Reformers promoted model tenements, settlement houses, and eventually public housing.
