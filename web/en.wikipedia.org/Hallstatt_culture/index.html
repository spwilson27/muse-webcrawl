The Hallstatt culture was the predominant Western and Central European archaeological culture of the Late Bronze Age and Early Iron Age, from the 12th to the 5th centuries BC. It is named for its type site, the rich cemetery and salt-mining settlement at Hallstatt in the Austrian Salzkammergut, excavated from 1846 by Johann Georg Ramsauer, which produced over a thousand burials with lavish grave goods. The culture is generally associated with the emergence of the Celts.

Chronology and spread

Archaeologists divide the culture into phases Hallstatt A–B (Late Bronze Age, c. 1200–800 BC, continuing the Urnfield tradition of cremation) and Hallstatt C–D (Early Iron Age, c. 800–450 BC). Its core lay north of the Alps from eastern France to Bohemia and Austria, with western and eastern provinces; iron working, horse gear, and four-wheeled wagons in elite burials mark the later phases.

Society and trade

Hallstatt society was hierarchical, dominated by a warrior and "princely" elite buried under large mounds with imported luxuries. Wealth flowed from control of salt (mined at Hallstatt and Hallein) and from trade along routes linking the Danube and Rhine to the Mediterranean: Greek and Etruscan bronzes, wine-drinking equipment, silk, and coral reached hillfort centres such as the Heuneburg on the upper Danube and Mont Lassois in Burgundy, near the famous Vix burial of a "princess" interred with the largest known Greek bronze krater. Fortified hilltop settlements, chariot and wagon graves, and distinctive geometric art characterise the culture.

Transition to La Tène

From about 450 BC the Hallstatt world was succeeded by the La Tène culture, whose curvilinear art and historically attested Celtic peoples spread widely across Europe. Whether the change reflects migration, internal development, or shifting trade networks remains debated, but linguistic and material evidence link the Hallstatt zone with early Celtic languages.
