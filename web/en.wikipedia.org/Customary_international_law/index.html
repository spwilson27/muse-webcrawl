Customary international law

Customary international law is an important source of public international law, consisting of rules that arise from "a general practice accepted as law". It is one of the sources listed in the Statute of the International Court of Justice, alongside treaties and general principles of law. Customary rules bind all states, including those that have never signed a treaty on the subject, unless a state has persistently objected to the rule while it was forming.

Elements

Two elements are required for a customary rule to exist: state practice and opinio juris — the belief that the practice is carried out because it is legally required. State practice must be sufficiently widespread, representative, and consistent, though it need not be universal or of long duration. Diplomatic correspondence, official statements, national legislation, and the practice of international organisations can all serve as evidence.

Examples

Much of the law of diplomatic immunity, the basic rules of international humanitarian law, and the prohibition of genocide and slavery are regarded as customary. Some customary rules have the special status of peremptory norms (jus cogens), from which no derogation is permitted.

Relation to treaties

Customary law and treaty law may overlap, codify one another, or develop in parallel. A treaty may declare existing custom, crystallise an emerging rule, or generate new custom through widespread adoption, as the International Court of Justice recognised in the North Sea Continental Shelf cases.
