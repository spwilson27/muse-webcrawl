Epstein–Barr virus

The Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), formally Human gammaherpesvirus 4, is one of the most common viruses in humans and a member of the herpesvirus family. It is best known as the cause of infectious mononucleosis ("mono", or glandular fever). Most people worldwide are infected during their lifetimes, after which the virus persists latently in B lymphocytes for life.

Transmission and disease

EBV spreads mainly through saliva. Infection in childhood is usually asymptomatic, while infection in adolescence often causes mononucleosis with fever, sore throat, swollen lymph nodes, and fatigue. The virus was discovered in 1964 by Anthony Epstein, Yvonne Barr, and Bert Achong in cells from Burkitt lymphoma tumors. EBV is associated with several cancers, including Burkitt lymphoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and some gastric cancers, as well as lymphoproliferative disorders in immunocompromised people. Epidemiological evidence also links EBV infection strongly to later development of multiple sclerosis.

Biology

EBV is a double-stranded DNA virus that infects B cells and epithelial cells. During latency it expresses a limited set of genes, including the EBV nuclear antigens (EBNAs) and latent membrane proteins (LMPs), some of which can drive cell proliferation — a property that made EBV the first virus shown to transform human cells and a foundational model in tumor virology.
