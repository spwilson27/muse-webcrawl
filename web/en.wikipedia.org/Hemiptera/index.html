Hemiptera

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassInsecta
OrderHemiptera (Linnaeus, 1758)
SubordersAuchenorrhyncha, Coleorrhyncha, Heteroptera, Sternorrhyncha

Hemiptera is an order of insects, commonly called true bugs, comprising over 80,000 species within groups such as the cicadas, aphids, planthoppers, leafhoppers, assassin bugs, bed bugs, and shield bugs. They range in size from 1 mm (0.04 in) to around 15 cm (6 in), and share a common arrangement of piercing-sucking mouthparts. The name "true bugs" is sometimes limited to the suborder Heteroptera.

Entomologists reserve the term "bug" for Hemiptera, in contrast to the loose vernacular use of "bug" for any small invertebrate. Most hemipterans feed on plants, using their sucking and piercing mouthparts to extract plant sap; others are predators of insects or, like bed bugs and kissing bugs, feed on the blood of animals including humans.

Anatomy

The defining feature of hemipterans is the rostrum, a beak-like structure in which the mandibles and maxillae are modified into needle-like stylets sheathed within a segmented labium. The stylets pierce tissues and suck liquids, typically plant sap, while the labium braces the structure from outside. In the Heteroptera the forewings are thickened and leathery at the base and membranous at the tip — the "hemelytra" that give the order its name (from Greek hemi-, half, and pteron, wing) — whereas in other suborders the wings are held rooflike over the body.

Life cycle

Most hemipterans are hemimetabolous: the young nymphs resemble the adults but lack fully developed wings and reproductive organs, maturing through a series of moults. Many aphids have complex life cycles involving parthenogenesis and alternation between winged and wingless generations. Some groups, such as cicadas, have exceptionally long developmental periods underground.

Ecology and economic importance

Many species are major agricultural pests: aphids, whiteflies, and scale insects damage crops directly and transmit plant viruses. Conversely, predatory bugs such as assassin bugs and minute pirate bugs are valuable biological control agents. A few species transmit human disease, most notably the triatomine "kissing bugs" that spread Chagas disease, and bed bugs are persistent household pests.
