Walter Alvarez (born 1940) is an American geologist, professor at the University of California, Berkeley, best known for proposing — with his father, the Nobel Prize-winning physicist Luis Alvarez, and chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Michel — that the mass extinction at the end of the Cretaceous was caused by an asteroid impact.

The impact hypothesis

Studying the limestone sequence at Gubbio, Italy, in the 1970s, Alvarez noticed a thin clay layer marking the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) boundary. In 1980 the Alvarez team reported in Science that the layer worldwide contained anomalously high concentrations of iridium, a metal rare in crustal rock but common in asteroids, and argued that a roughly 10-km asteroid impact had caused the extinction that ended the dinosaurs. Initially controversial, the hypothesis gained support with the discovery of the Chicxulub crater in Mexico around 1990 and is now the scientific consensus.

Other work

Alvarez's research spans tectonics of the Mediterranean, magnetic stratigraphy, and the interactions of geology with history. His books include T. rex and the Crater of Doom (1997) and The Mountains of Saint Francis (2009) on the geology and history of Italy. Among his awards are the Penrose Medal of the Geological Society of America.
