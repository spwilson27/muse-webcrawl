Alaska Natives

Total population: approx. 120,000 (Alaska, 2020)
Regions: State of Alaska
Languages: English; Yup'ik, Inupiaq, Aleut, Tlingit, Haida, Athabaskan languages and others

Alaska Natives (also known as Alaskan Natives) are the Indigenous peoples of Alaska. They include the Iñupiat, Yup'ik, Aleut (Unangax), Eyak, Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshian, and a number of Northern Athabaskan cultures, among others. Ancestors of Alaska Natives entered the region thousands of years ago, in at least two different waves of migration.

Groups and cultures

Alaska Native cultures are conventionally grouped by region: the Iñupiat of the Arctic north; the Yup'ik and Cup'ik of the Yukon–Kuskokwim Delta; the Unangax (Aleut) of the Aleutian Islands; the Alutiiq (Sugpiaq) of the Alaska Peninsula and Kodiak; the Athabaskan peoples of the Interior; and the Tlingit, Haida, and Tsimshian of the Southeast panhandle. Subsistence hunting, fishing, and gathering remain central to many communities' way of life and food security.

History

Russian colonization from the 18th century brought the fur trade, forced labour, and disease that devastated Aleutian and coastal populations. Alaska passed to the United States in 1867. Mission schools and federal policies long suppressed Native languages and customs.

ANCSA and modern era

The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (ANCSA) of 1971 settled aboriginal land claims, conveying about 44 million acres and nearly one billion dollars, and establishing thirteen regional Native corporations and more than 200 village corporations. Today Alaska Natives are a major political and economic force in the state, with ongoing efforts to protect subsistence rights, revitalize Indigenous languages, and address the effects of climate change on coastal villages.
