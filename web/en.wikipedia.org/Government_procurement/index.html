Government procurement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Government procurement or public procurement is the purchase of goods, services, and works by governments and state-owned entities. Because it is paid for with public money, public procurement is normally regulated by law to prevent fraud, waste, corruption, and favouritism, and to obtain value for money through competition.

Scale and process

Public procurement typically accounts for around 10–15% of GDP in developed countries. A typical process involves identifying a need, advertising a tender (often above monetary thresholds), evaluating bids against published criteria, awarding a contract, and managing delivery. Unsuccessful bidders can usually challenge awards through review procedures or courts.

Rules and agreements

National regimes include the Federal Acquisition Regulation in the United States and procurement directives in the European Union, which require open competition across member states for larger contracts. Internationally, the World Trade Organization's Agreement on Government Procurement (GPA) commits its parties to non-discriminatory treatment of suppliers.

Policy uses

Governments also use procurement to pursue wider goals — supporting small businesses and disadvantaged groups, promoting innovation, and encouraging green and sustainable purchasing.
