Risotto

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Risotto is an Italian rice dish cooked with broth until it reaches a creamy consistency. It originated in northern Italy, particularly in the rice-growing regions of Piedmont, Lombardy, and the Veneto, and is one of the most common ways of cooking rice in Italian cuisine.

Preparation

Risotto is made with short-grain, high-starch rice varieties such as Arborio, Carnaroli, and Vialone Nano. The rice is first toasted in butter or olive oil with finely chopped onion — the tostatura — then deglazed with wine. Hot broth is added a ladleful at a time, with frequent stirring, allowing the grains to release their starch and form a smooth, flowing sauce while remaining firm to the bite (al dente). The dish is finished off the heat with cold butter and grated Parmigiano-Reggiano, a step called mantecatura.

Varieties

The best-known version is risotto alla milanese, flavoured and coloured with saffron and traditionally served with ossobuco. Other regional versions include risotto al nero di seppia (with cuttlefish ink) in Venice, mushroom risotto (risotto ai funghi), seafood risotto, and risi e bisi, a soupy rice-and-pea dish of the Veneto. The leftover risotto of Milan is sometimes fried as cakes or formed into the Sicilian-style arancini.

History

Rice was introduced to Italy by the Arabs during the Middle Ages, and the humid Po Valley proved ideal for cultivation. Slow-cooked rice dishes in broth developed over centuries; the addition of saffron to the Milanese version is traditionally dated to the sixteenth century, and the modern stirred method was established by the nineteenth century.
