Manusmriti

Manuscript of the Manusmriti

The Manusmriti (Sanskrit: मनुस्मृति), also known as the Laws of Manu or Mānava-Dharmaśāstra, is one of the best-known and most authoritative of the Dharmaśāstra texts of Hinduism — Sanskrit works on law, duty, and conduct. Composed in verse, probably between the 2nd century BCE and the 2nd or 3rd century CE, it presents itself as a discourse given by Manu, the legendary first man and lawgiver, recorded by the sage Bhrigu.

Contents

The text contains about 2,700 verses in twelve chapters. It covers cosmology and creation, the sources of dharma, the duties of the four varnas (classes) and the four ashramas (stages of life), marriage, diet, ritual purity, the law of kingship, civil and criminal law, penances, karma and rebirth, and funeral rites. Its treatment of the caste system and of women has been extensively debated in modern times.

History and influence

The Manusmriti became the most cited Dharmaśāstra in later Indian legal literature and generated numerous commentaries, most famously Medhātithi's in the 9th–10th century. During the British colonial period, administrators seeking to codify "Hindu law" gave it exceptional prominence: William Jones published the first English translation, Institutes of Hindu Law, in 1794. Scholars now regard the text as one prescriptive voice among many in ancient India, whose actual influence on everyday practice was mediated by custom and regional variation.

Criticism and modern reception

The text's injunctions on caste hierarchy and the subordination of women have drawn sustained criticism, notably from B. R. Ambedkar, who publicly burned a copy of the Manusmriti in 1927 as a symbol of protest against caste oppression. It remains a reference point in debates about Hindu law, social reform, and the Indian constitution.
