Pluripotency

A pluripotent cell can form derivatives of all three germ layers.

In cell biology, pluripotency (from Latin plurimus, "very many", and potens, "having power") is the potential of a cell to differentiate into any of the three germ layers: endoderm (such as the gut lining), mesoderm (such as muscle, bone, and blood), or ectoderm (such as skin and nervous tissue). Pluripotent cells can give rise to any fetal or adult cell type of the body proper, though under normal conditions they cannot by themselves form a complete organism because they do not produce the extra-embryonic tissues such as the placenta — a capacity reserved for totipotent cells such as the zygote.

Pluripotent stem cells

The best-known pluripotent cells are embryonic stem cells, derived from the inner cell mass of the blastocyst, first isolated from mouse embryos in 1981 and from human embryos in 1998 by James Thomson's group. In 2006, Shinya Yamanaka showed that ordinary adult cells could be reprogrammed into induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) by introducing four transcription factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc), work that shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Pluripotency is maintained by a core regulatory network centred on the transcription factors Oct4, Sox2, and Nanog.

Testing pluripotency

Pluripotency is demonstrated functionally by showing that cells can differentiate into all three germ layers — in vitro through embryoid body formation or directed differentiation, and in vivo, for animal cells, by formation of a teratoma containing tissues of all three layers after injection into immunodeficient mice. The most stringent test, available only for mouse cells, is contribution to a developing embryo after blastocyst injection, producing a chimera.

States of pluripotency

Researchers distinguish "naïve" pluripotency, resembling the pre-implantation epiblast (as in standard mouse ES cells), from "primed" pluripotency, resembling the post-implantation epiblast (as in conventional human ES and iPS cells), with differences in signalling dependence, metabolism, and X-chromosome state. Capturing stable naïve human pluripotent cells is an active research area with implications for regenerative medicine and disease modelling.
