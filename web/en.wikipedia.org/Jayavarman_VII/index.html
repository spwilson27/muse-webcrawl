Jayavarman VII (Khmer: ជ័យវរ្ម័នទី៧; c. 1125 – c. 1218) is generally considered the greatest king of the Khmer Empire of Cambodia. Reigning from about 1181 to 1218, he expelled the invading Chams who had sacked the capital, reunified the empire, and launched an unprecedented programme of construction — temples, hospitals, rest houses, roads, and reservoirs — while establishing Mahayana Buddhism as the state religion. His capital, Angkor Thom, and its central temple, the Bayon with its serene giant face towers, are among the most famous monuments of Southeast Asia.

Accession

Jayavarman was the son of King Dharanindravarman II and a grandnephew of Suryavarman II, builder of Angkor Wat. He spent years in exile or semi-exile in Champa. In 1177 Cham forces under Jaya Indravarman IV sailed up the Mekong and Tonlé Sap and sacked the Khmer capital Yasodharapura, killing the king. Jayavarman rallied Khmer forces, defeated the Chams in battles on land and on the Tonlé Sap lake — naval engagements later carved in relief at the Bayon and Banteay Chhmar — and was crowned in about 1181. In 1190 he conquered the Cham capital Vijaya, annexing Champa for a time.

Building programme

Jayavarman's construction works are unmatched in Khmer history. He built the walled city of Angkor Thom (about 9 km²) with its five great gates and the Bayon temple at its centre, whose towers bear huge carved faces identified with the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara and the king himself. Other foundations include Ta Prohm (1186), dedicated to his mother and identified with the goddess Prajnaparamita; Preah Khan (1191), dedicated to his father; Neak Pean; Ta Som; Banteay Kdei; and the vast baray of Jayatataka. His inscriptions record a welfare network of 102 hospitals (arogyasala) and 121 rest houses (dharmasala) along imperial roads, together with hundreds of temples and hydraulic works. The strain of this building programme on the empire's resources has often been noted by historians.

Religion and ideology

A devout Mahayana Buddhist — in a line of predominantly Shaivite predecessors — Jayavarman presented himself as a bodhisattva-king whose merit-making and public works relieved the suffering of his subjects; an inscription famously records that "he suffered from the maladies of his subjects more than his own; for it is the public grief which makes the grief of kings, not their own grief." His queens Jayarajadevi and, after her death, her sister Indradevi were influential patrons of Buddhism.

Death and aftermath

Jayavarman VII died around 1218 and received the posthumous name Mahaparamasaugata. Under his successors the empire gradually declined, Theravada Buddhism supplanted Mahayana, and some of his Buddhist imagery was later defaced during a Shaivite reaction under Jayavarman VIII. Nonetheless, Jayavarman VII remains a national hero in Cambodia, remembered as the empire's greatest builder-king.
