Bodhicitta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Mahayana Buddhism, bodhicitta (Sanskrit: बोधिचित्त; "mind of awakening" or "enlightenment-mind") is the mind that strives toward awakening, empathy, and compassion for the benefit of all sentient beings. It is the defining motivation of the bodhisattva path: the resolve to attain full Buddhahood not for oneself alone but in order to liberate all beings from suffering. The 8th-century Indian master Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra is the classic poetic exposition of bodhicitta.

Types

Teachers commonly distinguish:

  • Relative (conventional) bodhicitta — the compassionate aspiration and active engagement in practice for others' welfare, cultivated through methods such as the exchange of self and other (tonglen) and the six perfections (pāramitās).
  • Ultimate bodhicitta — the direct realisation of emptiness (śūnyatā), the wisdom that perceives the true nature of reality, inseparable from compassion.

Śāntideva further distinguishes "aspiring bodhicitta," the intention to free all beings, from "engaged bodhicitta," actually embarking on the practices that fulfil that vow, using the analogy of wishing to go versus setting out on the journey.

Generation and practice

Bodhicitta is formally generated in ceremonies of taking the bodhisattva vow, often before a teacher, and is renewed daily in Tibetan and East Asian practice. The "sevenfold cause and effect" instruction and Atīśa's mind-training (lojong) teachings provide step-by-step methods, grounded in recognising all beings as having been one's mother in past lives, cultivating love and great compassion, and taking personal responsibility for their liberation. In Vajrayana, bodhicitta is also an important term in tantric physiology and ritual symbolism.
