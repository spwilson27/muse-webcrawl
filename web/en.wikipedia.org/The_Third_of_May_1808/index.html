The Third of May 1808 (Spanish: El tres de mayo de 1808 en Madrid) is a painting completed in 1814 by the Spanish painter Francisco Goya, now in the Museo del Prado, Madrid. Together with its companion piece, The Second of May 1808, it commemorates Spanish resistance to Napoleon's armies during the Peninsular War.

Subject

After the uprising of Madrid's citizens against the French occupation on 2 May 1808, French forces under Marshal Murat carried out mass reprisals. In the early hours of 3 May, hundreds of Spaniards — arrested indiscriminately, many innocent of any fighting — were executed at sites around the city, including the hill of Príncipe Pío depicted here.

Composition

A lantern on the ground throws harsh light on a group of prisoners confronting a firing squad. At the centre, a man in a white shirt and yellow trousers flings his arms wide in a cruciform gesture, stigmata visible on his palm, staring at his executioners with an expression of mingled terror and defiance. Behind him, earlier victims lie in pools of blood; before him the squad stands with backs to the viewer — faceless, aligned, mechanical. Nothing in the picture offers consolation or heroism of the traditional kind.

Reception and influence

Goya painted the work six years after the events, apparently at his own request to "perpetuate with the brush the most notable and heroic actions of our glorious insurrection against the Tyrant of Europe." Largely neglected for decades, the painting is now considered a founding image of modern war art: its anonymous victims and dehumanised killers anticipate Picasso's Guernica, Manet's Execution of Emperor Maximilian (which directly borrowed its composition), and modern photojournalism.
