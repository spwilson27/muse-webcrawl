Antinous

Bornc. 111 AD, Claudiopolis, Bithynia
DiedOctober 130 AD (aged about 19), Nile River, Egypt
Known forBeloved companion of Emperor Hadrian; deified after death

Antinous (Greek: Ἀντίνοος; c. 111 – October 130 AD) was a young man of Bithynian Greek origin and the beloved companion of the Roman emperor Hadrian. After Antinous drowned in the Nile in 130 AD, the grief-stricken Hadrian deified him, and a cult devoted to Antinous spread throughout the Roman Empire. He is famous for the extraordinary number of surviving portraits of him — more than of almost any other figure of antiquity except emperors themselves.

Early life

Antinous was born in Claudiopolis (modern Bolu, Turkey) in the Roman province of Bithynia, probably around 111 AD. Little is known of his family, which appears to have been of modest Greek stock. He probably came to Hadrian's attention during one of the emperor's tours of the eastern provinces, possibly around 123 AD, and joined the imperial entourage, perhaps as a page in the paedagogium on the Caelian Hill in Rome before accompanying Hadrian on his travels.

Relationship with Hadrian

Hadrian was known to have taken a deep personal and romantic interest in Antinous. Ancient sources such as Cassius Dio and the Historia Augusta describe the emperor's intense attachment. Antinous accompanied Hadrian on his great tour of the empire, including visits to Greece, Asia Minor, Syria, Judea, and Egypt. In Libya, according to a poem by Pancrates preserved on papyrus, Antinous participated in a lion hunt with the emperor, an episode Hadrian commemorated on medallions and in verse.

Death

In October 130 AD, while the imperial party was sailing on the Nile during the festival of Osiris, Antinous drowned under circumstances that remain unclear. Cassius Dio reported that Antinous had either fallen in accidentally or, as a sensational alternative tradition held, offered himself as a ritual sacrifice to prolong Hadrian's life. Hadrian publicly insisted it was an accident. The emperor wept openly and, according to Dio, "like a woman."

Deification and cult

Hadrian founded the city of Antinoöpolis on the east bank of the Nile near the place of Antinous's death and declared him a god. The cult of Antinous spread rapidly, especially in the Greek East, where he was assimilated to Osiris, Dionysus, Apollo, and Adonis. Temples, games, and priesthoods were established in his honour; the Antinoeia games were held at Athens and Mantineia. Oracles were issued in his name, and a star was said to have appeared marking his apotheosis — Hadrian pointed to a new star (associated with the constellation Aquila) as Antinous's soul.

Art and legacy

Portraits of Antinous — brooding, melancholic youths with thick, tousled hair and downcast eyes — became canonical images of classical beauty. Famous examples include the Mondragone Antinous, the Capitoline Antinous, and the Antinous Braschi type. Some 115 statues, busts, and reliefs are known, along with many coins and gems struck by eastern cities. In the modern era Antinous became an icon of homosexual love; Marguerite Yourcenar's novel Memoirs of Hadrian (1951) gave his story its best-known literary treatment.
