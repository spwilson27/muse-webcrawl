Tetanus

Tetanus, also known as lockjaw, is a bacterial infection caused by Clostridium tetani and characterized by prolonged muscle spasms. The spasms generally begin in the jaw (hence "lockjaw") and then progress to other parts of the body over succeeding days or weeks. The spasms can be so powerful that they cause bone fractures. Other symptoms may include fever, headache, restlessness, and difficulty swallowing.

Cause and mechanism

Clostridium tetani is an anaerobic bacterium whose spores are widely distributed in soil, dust, and animal feces. Infection occurs when spores enter the body through a break in the skin, typically a puncture wound from a contaminated object such as a nail. In the low-oxygen environment of damaged tissue the spores germinate and the bacteria release the powerful neurotoxin tetanospasmin, which travels along peripheral nerves to the central nervous system. There it blocks the release of the inhibitory neurotransmitters GABA and glycine, producing unopposed muscle contraction and the characteristic rigid paralysis.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically appear from three to twenty-one days after infection. The most common presenting sign is trismus (lockjaw), followed by stiffness of the neck and back; severe generalized spasms may arch the body backward in a posture called opisthotonos. A characteristic facial expression, risus sardonicus, results from spasm of the facial muscles. Neonatal tetanus, acquired through infection of the umbilical stump, was historically a major cause of infant death.

Prevention and treatment

Tetanus is preventable by immunization with the tetanus vaccine, usually given in childhood as part of the DPT/DTaP combination, with booster doses recommended every ten years. Because the disease is caused by a toxin rather than by person-to-person spread, herd immunity does not protect the unvaccinated. Treatment of established tetanus involves wound care, human tetanus immunoglobulin to neutralize circulating toxin, antibiotics such as metronidazole, and supportive care — often including sedation, muscle relaxants, and mechanical ventilation — until the toxin bound to nerve endings is cleared.

Epidemiology

Widespread vaccination has made tetanus rare in high-income countries, but it still causes tens of thousands of deaths annually worldwide, mostly among newborns and older people in low-income regions. Global campaigns have eliminated maternal and neonatal tetanus in most countries.
