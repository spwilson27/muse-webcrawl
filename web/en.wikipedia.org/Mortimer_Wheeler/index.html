Sir Robert Eric Mortimer Wheeler CH CIE MC FRS FBA FSA (10 September 1890 – 22 July 1976) was a British archaeologist and army officer. He was one of the best-known archaeologists of the twentieth century, famous for his meticulous excavation technique, his excavations of Roman and Iron Age sites in Britain and of the Indus Valley Civilisation, and his skill at popularising archaeology through books, radio and television.

Career

After service as an artillery officer in the First World War, Wheeler became director of the National Museum of Wales and then Keeper of the London Museum. With his wife Tessa, he excavated the Roman city of Verulamium and the great Iron Age hillfort of Maiden Castle in Dorset, developing the grid-square (box-grid) method of excavation, with its emphasis on stratigraphy and total recording, later refined by Kathleen Kenyon.

Serving again in the Second World War — he rose to brigadier — Wheeler was appointed Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India (1944–1948). There he excavated at Taxila, Harappa, Arikamedu and Mohenjo-daro, founded a training school whose alumni shaped South Asian archaeology, and controversially interpreted a heap of skeletons at Mohenjo-daro as evidence of an "Aryan invasion" massacre, a view later rejected by most scholars.

Legacy

Wheeler founded the Institute of Archaeology in London (now part of UCL) and was a pioneering television presenter on programmes such as the BBC's Animal, Vegetable, Mineral?, becoming one of the first archaeology celebrities. His books include Archaeology from the Earth (1954) and The Indus Civilization.
