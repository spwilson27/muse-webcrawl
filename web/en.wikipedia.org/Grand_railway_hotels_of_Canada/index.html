The grand railway hotels of Canada are a chain of landmark hotels built by Canada's railway companies in the late 19th and early 20th centuries to encourage transcontinental rail travel and settlement of the west. Visionary Canadian Pacific Railway general manager William Cornelius Van Horne argued that "if we can't export the scenery, we'll import the tourists", and raised a chain of palatial hotels at scenic stops and city terminals from the Atlantic to the Pacific.

Canadian Pacific hotels

The CPR's flagship properties include the Banff Springs Hotel (1888, rebuilt 1928) in the Rockies, Château Lake Louise (1890), the Château Frontenac in Quebec City (1893) — designed by Bruce Price and among the most photographed hotels in the world — the Empress Hotel in Victoria (1908), the Royal York in Toronto (1929) and Hotel Vancouver (1939). Most were built in the Châteauesque style, with steep copper roofs, turrets and gables intended to evoke Loire Valley châteaux and to give the young dominion a romantic, distinctively Canadian architectural identity.

Rival chains

The Grand Trunk Railway built competing hotels such as the Château Laurier in Ottawa (1912) and the Fort Garry Hotel in Winnipeg (1913); its successor, Canadian National, added the Hotel Macdonald in Edmonton, the Bessborough in Saskatoon, the Queen Elizabeth in Montreal (1958) and Jasper Park Lodge in the Rockies.

Legacy

Many of the hotels remain the social and architectural centrepieces of their cities, designated National Historic Sites, and are operated today largely under the Fairmont banner (Fairmont itself descends from Canadian Pacific Hotels). Several hosted historic events: the Château Frontenac and the Citadelle hosted the Quebec Conferences of 1943 and 1944, and John Lennon and Yoko Ono's 1969 "bed-in for peace" was held at Montreal's Queen Elizabeth.
