Algebraic integer

AreaAlgebraic number theory
Defined byRoots of monic integer polynomials
ExamplesGaussian integers, Eisenstein integers, golden ratio

In algebraic number theory, an algebraic integer is a complex number which is a root of some monic polynomial (a polynomial whose leading coefficient is 1) with coefficients in the integers. The set of all algebraic integers is closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication, and therefore forms a commutative ring, which is the integral closure of the integers in the field of algebraic numbers.

The concept generalizes the ordinary integers, which are exactly the rational numbers that are algebraic integers. It was developed in the 19th century by mathematicians such as Ernst Kummer and Richard Dedekind in the study of equations like Fermat's Last Theorem.

Definitions

Let K be a number field (a finite extension of the rationals). An element α of K is an algebraic integer if there exists a monic polynomial with integer coefficients of which α is a root. Equivalently, the ring of integers of K, denoted OK, is the set of all algebraic integers in K; it is a free abelian group of rank equal to the degree of K over the rationals, and it is a Dedekind domain.

Examples

  • The rational numbers that are algebraic integers are exactly the ordinary integers.
  • The Gaussian integers a + bi with a, b integers are the algebraic integers in the field of Gaussian rationals.
  • The golden ratio (1 + √5)/2 is an algebraic integer, being a root of x² − x − 1 = 0, whereas 1/2 and √5/2 are not both integers of the same field in the same way.
  • Roots of unity are algebraic integers, as are all roots of monic integer polynomials such as x² − 2 = 0 giving √2.

Non-unique factorization

Unlike the ordinary integers, rings of algebraic integers need not have unique factorization into irreducible elements. Already in the ring Z[√−5], one has 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5), two genuinely different factorizations. Dedekind restored unique factorization at the level of ideals: in the ring of integers of a number field, every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals. The class group measures the failure of unique factorization of elements.
