Loading gauge

A loading gauge is a diagram or physical profile that defines the maximum height and width dimensions of railway vehicles and their loads, ensuring that trains can pass safely through tunnels, under bridges and past platforms and trackside structures without striking them. It is distinct from the track gauge, which is the distance between the rails, and from the structure gauge, which defines the clearance envelope that structures must not intrude into.

Variation by country

Loading gauges vary widely around the world. Britain's loading gauge is famously restrictive — a legacy of early railway construction — which limits train height and width and complicates the introduction of double-decker trains and standard European freight wagons. The continental European "Berne gauge" and the standard UIC gauges are more generous, and North American freight gauges taller still, permitting double-stack container trains. Japan's conventional lines have a narrow, small gauge, while Shinkansen trains are built to a much larger profile on dedicated tracks.

Implications

Because vehicles must fit the smallest gauge on their route, loading gauge constrains rolling-stock design, freight economics and interoperability between networks. Enhancing a route's gauge — for example to carry taller intermodal containers — can require expensive work to enlarge tunnels and lower tracks under bridges. Modern practice distinguishes the static gauge from the larger kinematic and dynamic envelopes that account for vehicle sway and suspension movement at speed.
