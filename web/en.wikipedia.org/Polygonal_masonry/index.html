Polygonal masonry

TechniqueDry stone construction
RegionsAncient Greece, Italy, Peru, Japan
Notable sitesDelphi, Alatri, Sacsayhuamán, Mycenae

Polygonal masonry is a technique of stone construction in which large, many-sided blocks are carefully cut and fitted together without mortar, so that their irregular polygonal faces meet tightly along joints. It is found in ancient cultures on several continents and is admired for both its durability and its resistance to earthquakes.

Characteristics

Unlike ashlar masonry of rectangular blocks, polygonal masonry exploits the natural shapes of stones, dressing each block so its edges match its neighbours. The resulting walls — such as the retaining wall of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, covered with manumission inscriptions — distribute loads evenly and interlock under stress.

Examples

The technique appears in Mycenaean Greece (where Roman-era writers called it "Cyclopean"), in the massive circuit walls of Italian hill towns such as Alatri, Norba, and Segni, in Inca architecture at Sacsayhuamán and Ollantaytambo in Peru, and in the castle walls of Edo-period Japan. The precision of some examples has attracted pseudoarchaeological speculation, which archaeologists attribute instead to skilled craftsmanship and abundant labour.

Technique

Builders are thought to have shaped blocks by hammering, scribing the face of each new stone against the finished work, and checking the fit repeatedly; modern experimental studies show that skilled masons using stone and bronze or iron tools can reproduce the joints.
