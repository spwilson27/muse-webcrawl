Monstrous moonshine

In mathematics, monstrous moonshine, or moonshine theory, is the unexpected connection between the monster group M and modular functions, in particular, the j function. The term was coined by John Conway and Simon Norton in their 1979 paper surveying the area, in which they called the connection "moonshine" because it appeared so far-fetched. Monstrous moonshine is now known to be underlain by a vertex operator algebra called the moonshine module (or monster vertex operator algebra) constructed by Igor Frenkel, James Lepowsky, and Arne Meurman. It was proved by Richard Borcherds in 1992, work for which he was awarded the Fields Medal in 1998.

The observation

In 1978, John McKay observed that the coefficients of the Fourier expansion of the j-invariant,

j(τ) = 1/q + 744 + 196884q + 21493760q2 + …

could be expressed in terms of the dimensions of irreducible representations of the monster, the largest sporadic simple group. In particular, 196884 = 196883 + 1, where 196883 is the dimension of the smallest nontrivial irreducible representation of the monster, and 21493760 = 21296876 + 196883 + 1. Andrew Ogg had earlier noticed a related coincidence concerning the primes dividing the order of the monster.

The conjectures

Conway and Norton conjectured that there exists an infinite-dimensional graded algebra (the moonshine module) acted on by the monster, whose graded dimensions are given by the coefficients of the j function, and that the analogues of the j function for individual elements of the monster (the McKay–Thompson series) are Hauptmoduln — generating functions for certain genus-zero groups of modular functions.

Proof

Richard Borcherds proved the Conway–Norton conjectures in 1992 using the moonshine module constructed by Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman, together with the no-ghost theorem from string theory and a new class of algebraic structures he developed, including generalized Kac–Moody (Borcherds) algebras. The proof famously borrowed ideas from theoretical physics, particularly string theory.

Related developments

Moonshine turned out to be part of a broader landscape: connections of a similar kind were later found for other groups and functions, including Mathieu moonshine (linking the M24 group to the elliptic genus of K3 surfaces) and umbral moonshine. The subject has deep ties to conformal field theory and string theory.
