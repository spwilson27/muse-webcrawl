Aldehyde dehydrogenase

Aldehyde dehydrogenases (EC 1.2.1.3) are a group of enzymes that catalyse the oxidation of aldehydes to carboxylic acids, using NAD+ or NADP+ as a cofactor. They are found in nearly all organisms and play essential roles in detoxifying aldehydes generated by metabolism and by environmental exposure.

Function

The best-known member is acetaldehyde dehydrogenase, which performs the second step of ethanol metabolism: alcohol dehydrogenase converts ethanol to toxic acetaldehyde, and aldehyde dehydrogenases — principally mitochondrial ALDH2 — convert it to harmless acetate. The enzymes also process retinaldehyde in vitamin A metabolism, neurotransmitter-derived aldehydes, and lipid-peroxidation products.

Genetics and health

Humans have 19 functional ALDH genes. A common variant, ALDH2*2, widespread in East Asian populations, greatly reduces enzyme activity; carriers accumulate acetaldehyde when drinking, causing the "alcohol flush reaction" with facial flushing, nausea, and tachycardia, and an increased risk of oesophageal cancer if they drink heavily. The drug disulfiram (Antabuse) inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase and is used to deter alcohol consumption. ALDH activity is also a marker of certain stem cells and cancer stem cells.
