Metaphor

A metaphor describes one thing in terms of another.

A metaphor is a figure of speech that, for rhetorical effect, directly refers to one thing by mentioning another. It may provide clarity or identify hidden similarities between two different ideas. Metaphors are commonly contrasted with similes, which make explicit comparisons using "like" or "as."

Structure

In the terminology of I. A. Richards, a metaphor consists of a tenor (the subject) and a vehicle (the term used metaphorically); in "time is a thief," time is the tenor and thief the vehicle. Cognitive linguists George Lakoff and Mark Johnson argued in Metaphors We Live By (1980) that metaphor is fundamental to thought, structured by conceptual mappings such as ARGUMENT IS WAR and TIME IS MONEY.

Types and uses

Metaphors range from simple comparisons to extended metaphors sustained across a text, allegories, and "dead metaphors" whose figurative origin has been forgotten (such as the "leg" of a table). They are central to poetry and literature but also pervade everyday language, science, law, and politics.
