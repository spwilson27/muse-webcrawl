Human branding

Human branding
Definition: Burning skin with a hot iron or device to leave a permanent scar
Contexts: Punishment, slavery, livestock identification, body modification
Risks: Infection, severe scarring, third-degree burns
Related: Scarification, tattooing

Human branding is the process by which a mark, usually a symbol or ornamental pattern, is burned into the skin of a living person, with the intention that the resulting scar will be permanent. It is related to the branding of livestock and was historically used on enslaved people and criminals as a mark of ownership or punishment; in modern times it is also practised, voluntarily, as a form of body modification.

History

Branding was widespread in the ancient world: Babylonian, Egyptian, Greek and Roman law prescribed brands for runaway slaves and certain offenders, and the practice continued in early modern Europe — France, for example, branded convicts with the fleur-de-lis. In the Atlantic slave trade, enslaved Africans were commonly branded with the marks of trading companies or owners before embarkation and again on sale. Britain branded army deserters (with a "D") and "bad characters" until the 19th century, and branding as a judicial punishment was abolished in Britain in 1829 for most offences, though it persisted elsewhere longer.

Modern practice

Since the late 20th century, branding has been adopted by some body-modification communities and fraternities, using heated metal, cautery pens, lasers or electrocautery ("strike branding" and "multi-strike" techniques). Because branding creates third-degree burns, it carries significant risks of infection, unintended scarring and keloid formation, and healing is slower and less predictable than tattooing. Voluntary branding also raises legal and ethical questions in some jurisdictions, particularly in cases of coercion or hazing.
