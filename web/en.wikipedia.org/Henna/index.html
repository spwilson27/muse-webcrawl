Henna

Source plantLawsonia inermis (henna tree)
Active dyeLawsone (hennotannic acid)
UsesBody art (mehndi), hair dye, textiles

Henna is a dye prepared from the plant Lawsonia inermis, also known as the henna tree, the mignonette tree, and the Egyptian privet, the sole species of the genus Lawsonia. Henna can also refer to the temporary body art resulting from staining the skin with the dye. Henna has been used since antiquity in ancient Egypt and the Indian subcontinent to dye skin, hair, and fingernails, as well as fabrics including silk, wool, and leather.

The name "henna" is used in other skin and hair dyes, such as "black henna" and "neutral henna", which are neither derived from the henna plant nor necessarily safe substitutes; so-called black henna may contain para-phenylenediamine (PPD), which can cause serious allergic reactions.

Preparation and application

Henna powder is produced by drying and milling the leaves of the plant. The dye molecule, lawsone, is released when the powder is mixed into a mildly acidic paste (often with lemon juice) and allowed to rest, a process called dye release. For body art, the paste is applied to the skin through a cone or applicator bottle, left in place for several hours, and then removed; the resulting orange stain oxidises to a reddish-brown over one to three days and fades as the skin exfoliates over one to three weeks. Stains are darkest on thick skin such as the palms and soles.

Cultural traditions

Henna body art, known as mehndi in South Asia, is central to wedding celebrations and festivals such as Eid and Diwali across India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, North Africa, the Horn of Africa, and the Arabian Peninsula. Bridal henna parties, in which intricate designs are applied to the bride's hands and feet, have been practised for centuries; henna nights are likewise a feature of Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, and other regional wedding customs.

Other uses

Henna is used as a natural hair dye and conditioner, imparting a reddish tint, especially valued in regions from North Africa to South Asia. Historically it was also used to dye the manes and hooves of horses, animal skins, and textiles. The plant itself is grown as a hedge in hot climates.

Health issues

Natural henna paste has a long record of safe use, though rare allergies occur. "Black henna" pastes adulterated with PPD can cause contact dermatitis and permanent scarring, and products containing henna should be used with caution in people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency.
