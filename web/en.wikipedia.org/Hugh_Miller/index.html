Hugh Miller (10 October 1802 – 24 December 1856) was a Scottish geologist, writer and folklorist, self-taught while working as a stonemason and quarryman. His vivid popular books on geology — above all The Old Red Sandstone (1841) — made him one of the most widely read science writers of the Victorian era, while his fossil fish discoveries from the Devonian rocks of Cromarty earned the respect of professional geologists.

Life

Miller was born in Cromarty on the Black Isle; his father, a ship's captain, died at sea in 1807. Apprenticed as a stonemason, he educated himself in the quarry and library, published poems and local history, and after a spell as a bank clerk became editor of The Witness, the Edinburgh newspaper of the evangelical party of the Church of Scotland. He was a leading lay figure in the Disruption of 1843 that created the Free Church of Scotland.

Geology and writings

Miller's fossil fishes — including Pterichthyodes ("Miller's winged fish") — illuminated the Old Red Sandstone of Scotland. He defended geology against religious critics while himself opposing Darwinian evolution, arguing in Footprints of the Creator (1849) and the posthumous The Testimony of the Rocks (1857) for a progressive creation. Suffering from illness and depression, he shot himself on Christmas Eve 1856. His autobiographical My Schools and Schoolmasters (1854) remains a classic of Scottish working-class life-writing.
