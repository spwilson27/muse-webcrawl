Indentation hardness

Indentation hardness
Indentation of a material by a hard indenter
Property measuredResistance to localised plastic deformation
Main scalesRockwell, Brinell, Vickers, Knoop, Shore
ApplicationsMetals quality control, materials science, geology

Indentation hardness tests are used in mechanical engineering to determine the hardness of a material against deformation. In such a test, a specially designed indenter of a harder material is pressed into the surface of the test piece under a controlled load, and the size or depth of the resulting impression is related to the material's resistance to permanent (plastic) deformation.

Principles

Hardness in indentation testing is not a fundamental material property but an empirical measure combining yield strength, work hardening, and elastic modulus. For metals, indentation hardness correlates approximately with tensile strength, which is why these quick, nearly non-destructive tests are ubiquitous in quality control. The measured value depends on indenter geometry, load, dwell time, and the surface condition of the specimen, so results are only comparable within a given scale and standard.

Major tests

  • Brinell — a hard ball (usually 10 mm) is pressed into the surface; hardness is computed from the impression diameter. Best for coarse-grained materials and large parts.
  • Rockwell — hardness is read from the depth difference between a minor and major load using a diamond cone or ball. Fast and direct-reading; the most common industrial test.
  • Vickers — a square-based diamond pyramid; used for very hard materials, thin layers, and microhardness.
  • Knoop — an elongated diamond pyramid for brittle materials and thin coatings.
  • Shore — rebound or durometer methods for elastomers, plastics, and (in the scleroscope form) metals.

Nanoindentation

Instrumented indentation at millinewton loads and nanometre depths — nanoindentation — continuously records load and displacement, allowing measurement of hardness and elastic modulus of thin films, coatings, and small volumes of material. Analysis commonly follows the Oliver–Pharr method.
