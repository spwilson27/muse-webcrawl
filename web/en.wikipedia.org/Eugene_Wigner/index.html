Eugene Paul Wigner (1902–1995) was a Hungarian-American theoretical physicist who made foundational contributions to quantum mechanics, nuclear physics, and the theory of symmetries. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1963 "for his contributions to the theory of the atomic nucleus and the elementary particles, particularly through the discovery and application of fundamental symmetry principles."

Work

Wigner introduced group theory into quantum mechanics, formulated Wigner's theorem on symmetry transformations, and developed the Wigner–Eckart theorem. His name attaches to the Wigner quasi-probability distribution, Wigner crystals, the Wigner effect in reactor graphite, and the Wigner surmise in random matrix theory. He also proposed the thought experiment known as Wigner's friend and wrote the famous essay "The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences" (1960).

Manhattan Project and later life

One of the Hungarian émigré scientists known as "the Martians," Wigner worked on the Manhattan Project at the Metallurgical Laboratory in Chicago, designing plutonium production reactors built at Hanford. A lifelong advocate of civil defence, he taught at Princeton University for most of his career.
