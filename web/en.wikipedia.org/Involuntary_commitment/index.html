Involuntary commitment

Involuntary commitment, also known as civil commitment or sectioning (in England and Wales), is a legal process through which an individual who is deemed by a qualified person to have symptoms of severe mental disorder is detained in a psychiatric hospital (involuntarily) for treatment. Criteria and procedures vary widely by jurisdiction.

Grounds and process

Most modern statutes require that the person, because of mental illness, poses a danger to themselves or others, or is gravely disabled and unable to meet basic needs. An emergency hold — for example a 72-hour hold under California's Lanterman–Petris–Short Act, or detention under Sections 2 and 3 of the Mental Health Act 1983 in England and Wales — typically begins the process, followed by a court hearing or tribunal review if longer detention is sought. US Supreme Court decisions such as O'Connor v. Donaldson (1975) and Addington v. Texas (1979) established that non-dangerous individuals capable of surviving safely in the community cannot be confined, and set a "clear and convincing evidence" standard for commitment.

Related arrangements

Related mechanisms include involuntary treatment orders (which may authorize medication against the person's will, sometimes under separate procedures), assisted outpatient treatment or community treatment orders (such as Kendra's Law in New York), and guardianship or conservatorship regimes.

Debate

Involuntary commitment is contested. Supporters argue it prevents harm and provides care to people whose illness impairs their insight. Critics — including many disability-rights advocates and the World Health Organization — argue that coercive detention is discriminatory and should be replaced with supported decision-making, citing the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006). Thomas Szasz famously attacked commitment as a violation of liberty, while psychiatrists such as E. Fuller Torrey have argued that restrictive standards leave seriously ill people untreated. Practices also carry a history of abuse, from Soviet punitive psychiatry to the institutionalization of inconvenient family members, which modern procedural safeguards are designed to prevent.
