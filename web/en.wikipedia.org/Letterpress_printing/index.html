Letterpress printing is a technique of relief printing in which ink is applied to the raised surface of movable type or printing plates and transferred to paper under pressure. For roughly five hundred years it was the principal method of printing text in the West, and it survives today as a craft technique prized for the crisp, slightly impressed character it leaves in the paper.

Process

A compositor sets individual pieces of type — usually cast in a lead alloy — by hand or machine into lines held in a composing stick, then locks the assembled pages into an iron frame called a chase, using wedges ("quoins") and spacing blocks ("furniture"). The form is placed on the bed of a press; rollers pass over it, inking only the raised surfaces; paper is then pressed against it, either on a hinged platen or around a rotating cylinder. Distinctive features of the craft include the paired type cases that gave us the words "uppercase" and "lowercase," and the deep impression ("bite") that traditional printers considered a fault but modern artisans cultivate.

History

Movable type was devised in China by Bi Sheng around 1040 and developed in metal in Korea by the thirteenth century. Independently, Johannes Gutenberg's system of hand mould, alloy type, oil-based ink, and screw press — developed in Mainz around 1440 — made letterpress the engine of the European printing revolution. Steam-driven cylinder presses (from 1814), the Linotype and Monotype composing machines of the 1880s, and photopolymer plates successively industrialised the process before offset lithography displaced it for commercial work in the mid-twentieth century.

Revival

Since the 1990s letterpress has enjoyed a revival among designers and small studios for wedding stationery, posters, and fine editions, often using vintage presses with modern photopolymer plates; the tactile impression of the process is now marketed as a mark of craft quality.
