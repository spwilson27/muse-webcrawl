The Marriage of Figaro

ComposerWolfgang Amadeus Mozart
LibrettistLorenzo Da Ponte
Premiere1 May 1786, Burgtheater, Vienna
Based onBeaumarchais's play La folle journée, ou le Mariage de Figaro (1784)

The Marriage of Figaro (Italian: Le nozze di Figaro), K. 492, is a comic opera (opera buffa) in four acts composed in 1786 by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, with an Italian libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte. It is one of the most performed and beloved operas in the repertory.

Synopsis

The action takes place at the palace of Count Almaviva near Seville. Figaro, the Count's valet, is to marry Susanna, the Countess's maid — but the Count, a serial philanderer, intends to revive the feudal droit du seigneur over Susanna, while the housekeeper Marcellina holds a contract promising Figaro's hand in marriage if he defaults on a loan. Over one "day of madness," Figaro, Susanna and the Countess conspire through disguises, forged letters and a garden assignation in the dark to expose the Count's intentions. Marcellina is revealed to be Figaro's long-lost mother; the Count, caught attempting to seduce his own disguised wife, begs forgiveness — the opera's one moment of genuine pathos — and the marriages proceed.

History

Beaumarchais's play had been banned in Vienna for its satire of the aristocracy; Da Ponte secured permission from Emperor Joseph II by toning down its most subversive material while retaining its dramatic structure. The opera premiered on 1 May 1786 at the Burgtheater and was received warmly, if without the triumph it later enjoyed in Prague — a success that led directly to the commission of Don Giovanni.

Music

The overture, Figaro's "Non più andrai," Susanna and the Countess's letter duet "Sull'aria," and the Countess's "Dove sono" are among the most famous numbers in opera. The Act II finale, an expanding chain of ensembles, is often cited as the greatest feat of comic construction in the genre. Mozart's music gives even the opera's slightest characters psychological depth, and the class comedy of the source is recast in humane, often wistful terms.
