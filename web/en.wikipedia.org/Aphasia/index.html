Aphasia

Aphasia is an acquired language disorder caused by damage to the parts of the brain that process language. It impairs a person's ability to speak, understand speech, read, or write, while leaving general intelligence largely intact. Aphasia most commonly results from a stroke, but it can also follow head injury, brain tumours, infection, or neurodegenerative disease.

Types

Aphasias are usually classified by fluency and by which language abilities are affected. Broca's aphasia (expressive aphasia) follows damage to Broca's area in the frontal lobe; speech is effortful and telegraphic, though comprehension is relatively preserved. Wernicke's aphasia (receptive aphasia) follows damage to Wernicke's area in the temporal lobe; speech is fluent but often meaningless, and comprehension is poor. Global aphasia involves extensive damage and severely impairs all language functions. Other types include conduction aphasia, anomic aphasia, and the primary progressive aphasias associated with dementia.

Causes

The most common cause is stroke in the left cerebral hemisphere, which is dominant for language in most people. Other causes include traumatic brain injury, brain surgery, encephalitis, and degenerative conditions such as Alzheimer's disease.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis involves neurological examination and standardised language testing. Treatment centres on speech and language therapy, which helps patients recover or compensate for lost abilities; recovery is often greatest in the first months after injury. Support groups and communication strategies for families also play an important role.
