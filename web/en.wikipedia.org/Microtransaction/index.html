Microtransaction

Microtransactions are a business model where users can purchase virtual goods with micropayments. Microtransactions are often used in free-to-play games, usually mobile games and online services, allowing developers to monetise content without charging an upfront price. Items sold include cosmetic skins, virtual currency, extra lives, loot boxes, and season passes.

History

The model became prominent in the mid-2000s. Bethesda's 2006 "horse armour" downloadable content for The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion, sold for US$2.50, is frequently cited as an early and controversial example. The model expanded rapidly with smartphone app stores and free-to-play games such as FarmVille and Candy Crush Saga. By the 2010s, microtransactions appeared in full-price console and PC games as well.

Types

  • Virtual currency — intermediate currencies (gems, coins, points) purchased with real money, often sold in bundles that obscure the real price of items.
  • Loot boxes — randomised bundles of virtual items, which have drawn comparisons to gambling.
  • Cosmetic items — skins and outfits that do not affect gameplay.
  • Battle passes — tiered reward tracks unlocked through play and payment.
  • Pay-to-progress items — boosts, extra lives, and unlocks that speed advancement.

Criticism and regulation

Microtransactions have been criticised for exploiting psychological triggers, targeting children, and using "whales" — a small fraction of players who account for a majority of revenue. Loot boxes in particular have prompted regulatory action: Belgium and the Netherlands restricted them as gambling in 2018, China required disclosure of drop probabilities, and various legislatures have debated broader rules. Platform holders have also been required to disclose loot box odds in some jurisdictions.
