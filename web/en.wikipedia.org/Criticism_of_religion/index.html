Criticism of religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. For Kant's philosophical work, see Critique of Pure Reason.


Philosophical criticism of religion spans antiquity to the present

Criticism of religion involves criticism of the validity, concept, or ideas of religion. Historical records of criticism of religion go back to at least 5th-century BCE Greece, and religious criticism continues today, ranging from gentle interfaith dialogue to violent conflict.

Forms of criticism

  • Epistemic criticism questions the truth claims of religions — for example, arguments that the existence of evil undermines belief in an omnipotent, benevolent God (the problem of evil), or that religious claims lack empirical evidence.
  • Moral criticism argues that particular doctrines or institutions have harmful effects — the focus of writers from Voltaire to Karl Marx, who called religion "the opium of the people", and Sigmund Freud, who described it as an illusion in The Future of an Illusion (1927).
  • Internal criticism comes from within traditions, as believers challenge corruption or reinterpret doctrine.
  • New Atheism: in the 2000s authors such as Richard Dawkins (The God Delusion, 2006), Christopher Hitchens and Sam Harris popularised arguments that religion is false and socially harmful.

Responses

Defences of religion include theodicies responding to the problem of evil, arguments from religious experience and natural theology, and sociological accounts (Durkheim, William James) stressing religion's role in meaning-making and social cohesion.
