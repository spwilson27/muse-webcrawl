Marginal propensity to consume

In economics, the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is the proportion of an additional unit of income that a household or consumer spends on consumption rather than saving. It is a central concept of Keynesian economics, introduced by John Maynard Keynes in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), and it determines the size of the fiscal multiplier.

Definition

The MPC is defined as the change in consumption (ΔC) divided by the change in disposable income (ΔY) that produced it:

MPC = ΔC / ΔY

For example, if a household receives an extra $100 of income and spends $80 of it, the MPC is 0.8. The remainder is captured by the marginal propensity to save (MPS); for a closed private economy, MPC + MPS = 1.

In the standard Keynesian consumption function, C = a + bY, consumption consists of an autonomous component (a) and an induced component whose slope (b) is the MPC, normally assumed to lie between 0 and 1.

Role in the multiplier

Because one person's spending becomes another's income, an initial increase in spending generates successive rounds of consumption. The simple spending multiplier equals 1 / (1 − MPC) = 1 / MPS: with an MPC of 0.8, the multiplier is 5, so an extra $1 billion of government spending could raise total output by up to $5 billion in the simplest model (before allowing for taxes, imports, interest rates, and price effects, which reduce it). This is why governments interested in stimulating demand often target transfers at lower-income households, which tend to have higher MPCs.

Determinants and evidence

Keynes proposed a "fundamental psychological law": as income rises, people consume more, but not by as much as the increase in income — so the average MPC tends to fall with income, and poorer households typically spend a larger share of any windfall. Empirical research generally finds:

  • Lower-income and liquidity-constrained households have MPCs close to 1, spending transfers quickly; higher-income households save more of a marginal increase.
  • Transitory income changes (tax rebates, one-off payments) are spent at lower rates than permanent changes, as predicted by the permanent income hypothesis and life-cycle theory, though measured MPCs out of stimulus payments are still substantial — often estimated between about 0.2 and 0.6 within a few months.
  • Expectations, credit availability, and whether purchases are durable also affect measured MPC.
