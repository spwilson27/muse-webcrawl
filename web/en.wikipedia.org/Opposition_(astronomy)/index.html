Opposition (astronomy)

In positional astronomy, two celestial bodies are said to be in opposition when they are on opposite sides of the sky as seen from a particular place — that is, their apparent geocentric celestial longitudes differ by 180°. The term is most often used of a superior planet as seen from Earth, with the Sun on the opposite side.

Longitude difference180°
Applies toSuperior planets, Moon, asteroids
Related termConjunction (0° difference)

Observing at opposition

A planet at opposition rises at about sunset, culminates near midnight and sets at sunrise, so it is visible all night. It is also near its closest approach to Earth for the synodic cycle, appearing largest and brightest, which makes opposition the best time to observe the outer planets. Mars oppositions are especially notable because the planet's eccentric orbit makes some approaches much closer than others.

The Moon

The Moon is in opposition to the Sun at full moon, when its illuminated face is turned fully toward Earth. A lunar eclipse can occur only near opposition, when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow.

Symbol

The astronomical symbol for opposition is ☍. Opposition is one of the aspects of traditional astrology, where it is associated with tension between the bodies involved.
