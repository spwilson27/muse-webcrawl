Heraldry is a discipline relating to the design, display, and study of armorial bearings (known as armory), as well as related disciplines, such as vexillology (the study of flags), together with the study of ceremony, rank, and pedigree. Armory, the best-known branch of heraldry, concerns the design and transmission of the heraldic achievement or coat of arms. The achievement comprises a shield on which the arms are painted, the crest, helm, mantling, and frequently supporters, mottoes, and other elements.

Origins

Heraldry emerged in northwestern Europe in the mid-12th century, around the 1140s, apparently in response to practical needs: knights encased in armour on the battlefield needed bold, recognisable devices on their shields, banners, and surcoats, and the increasingly documentary culture of seals demanded distinctive personal marks. Within a generation, arms became hereditary, passing from father to son with systematic modifications (cadency marks) to distinguish younger branches. By the 13th century, heraldic devices appeared across Europe on seals, tombs, architecture, and manuscripts.

Blazon and tinctures

Arms are described in a specialised formal language called blazon, developed from Norman French, which allows a design to be reconstructed precisely from words alone. The shield's background (the field) may be divided geometrically and charged with stylised objects, animals, and plants called charges. Colours are called tinctures: the metals or (gold/yellow) and argent (silver/white), the colours gules (red), azure (blue), sable (black), vert (green), and purpure (purple), plus patterns called furs such as ermine and vair. A widely observed "rule of tincture" forbids placing metal on metal or colour on colour, ensuring contrast and visibility.

Heralds and authority

Originally attendants and messengers of princes, heralds became experts in armory, organising tournaments, recording pedigrees, and eventually regulating the granting of arms. In England, the College of Arms (founded 1484) and its kings of arms still grant and record arms; in Scotland, the Court of the Lord Lyon exercises legal jurisdiction over heraldry. Other countries maintain heraldic authorities or registers, while in many nations arms are simply assumed without regulation.

Modern use

Heraldry survives in state and municipal coats of arms, military insignia, university and school crests, ecclesiastical arms, and corporate logos that borrow heraldic forms. Families and individuals continue to petition for grants of arms, and civic heraldry flourishes in countries such as South Africa, Canada, and Spain.
