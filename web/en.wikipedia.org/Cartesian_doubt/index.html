Cartesian doubt

René Descartes (1596–1650)

Cartesian doubt is a systematic process of being sceptical about (or doubting) the truth of one's beliefs, which has become a characteristic method in philosophy. It is also sometimes referred to as Cartesian scepticism, methodic doubt, methodological scepticism, universal doubt, systematic doubt, or hyperbolic doubt. The method was developed by the French philosopher René Descartes, most fully in his Meditations on First Philosophy (1641).

Method

Descartes resolved to reject as false anything in which he could find even the slightest ground for doubt, in order to discover whether anything remained that was entirely certain. He argued that sensory experience can deceive — as in dreams or illusions — and so the senses alone cannot ground certainty. Pressing the doubt further, he hypothesised that a malicious demon ("malin génie") of supreme power might deceive him about everything, including mathematics and the existence of the external world.

Cogito ergo sum

Descartes found that one thing could not be doubted: his own existence as a thinking thing. Even if deceived about everything else, the very act of doubting implied a doubter. This insight is expressed in the famous phrase cogito, ergo sum — "I think, therefore I am" — first published in French ("je pense, donc je suis") in the Discourse on the Method (1637). From this foundation Descartes attempted to rebuild knowledge, arguing for the existence of God and, through divine veracity, the reliability of clear and distinct perceptions.

Influence

Cartesian doubt inaugurated the modern philosophical preoccupation with scepticism and epistemology. Critics have questioned whether the doubt can be escaped on Descartes's own terms and whether his appeal to God is circular (the so-called Cartesian circle), but the method remains a standard starting point in the philosophy of knowledge.
