Foreign-exchange reserves

Foreign-exchange reserves (also called forex reserves or FX reserves) are assets held on reserve by a central bank in foreign currencies. These reserves are used to back liabilities and influence monetary policy; they allow a government or central bank to pay its international obligations, defend its exchange rate, and provide confidence to markets. Reserves are typically held in major reserve currencies — principally the US dollar, followed by the euro, Japanese yen, pound sterling and Chinese renminbi — as well as gold and Special Drawing Rights.

Scale

China holds the world's largest reserves, which peaked at around US$4 trillion in 2014, followed by Japan and Switzerland. Many emerging economies accumulated large reserves after the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98 as a form of self-insurance against sudden stops in capital flows, a strategy sometimes called "hoarding".

Purposes and costs

Reserves help countries service foreign debt, import essential goods during crises, and intervene in currency markets. Holding them has costs: reserves are usually invested in low-yielding safe assets such as US Treasury securities, and large accumulations can distort exchange rates and trade. Sanctions have also raised questions about reserve safety: in 2022, roughly US$300 billion of the Russian central bank's reserves held abroad were frozen following the invasion of Ukraine.
