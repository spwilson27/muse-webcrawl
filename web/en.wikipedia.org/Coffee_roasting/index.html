Coffee roasting

Coffee roasting is the process of applying heat to green coffee beans to transform them chemically and physically into the aromatic, flavourful beans used to brew coffee. Roasting brings out the aroma and flavour locked inside the green beans, which in their raw state have none of the characteristics of roasted coffee.

Process

Beans are heated in a roaster, most commonly a rotating drum or a fluid-bed (hot air) machine, to internal temperatures around 196–230 °C (385–446 °F). As they roast, beans lose moisture, expand, turn from green through yellow to shades of brown, and undergo the Maillard reaction and other chemical changes that create hundreds of aromatic compounds. At about 196 °C the beans audibly crack — "first crack" — as steam fractures them; a "second crack" occurs at higher temperatures in darker roasts. Oils migrate to the surface in dark roasts, giving a shiny appearance.

Roast levels

Roast levels range from light (such as cinnamon roast and New England roast) through medium (city, American, and full city roasts) to dark (Vienna, French, and Italian roasts). Light roasts retain more acidity and origin character; dark roasts taste more of the roasting process itself, with bitter, smoky, and caramelised notes, but less caffeine per volume than is often supposed — caffeine content changes little with roasting.

After roasting

Freshly roasted beans degas carbon dioxide and are usually rested before brewing. Roasted coffee stales through oxidation and is best consumed within weeks, stored away from air, light, and moisture.
