Found photography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Found photography is a genre of art and collecting based on the recovery, preservation, and re-presentation of pre-existing photographs — typically anonymous vernacular snapshots, studio portraits, photo-booth strips, and discarded prints or negatives — that were not created by the collector. The finder appropriates the images, and their meaning is transformed by selection, sequencing, and new context.

Practice

Found photographs are sourced from flea markets, estate sales, thrift stores, rubbish, and increasingly from online archives and social media. Collectors and artists value the images for their period detail, accidental compositions, emotional charge, and the mystery of their unknown subjects. The practice is closely related to the readymade, found objects, collage, and appropriation art, and raises questions about authorship, privacy, and the afterlife of personal images.

Notable examples

The collector Thomas Sauvin's project Beijing Silvermine rescued hundreds of thousands of discarded negatives from a recycling plant in Beijing and became a celebrated archive of Chinese vernacular photography. Erik Kessels and others have exhibited vast accumulations of amateur photography, such as installations of every image uploaded to Flickr in 24 hours. Artists including Joachim Schmid and the duo Mishka Henner have made found and networked imagery central to their work. Archives such as the "Found Photo" collections preserve snapshots of everyday life that would otherwise be lost.

Relation to vernacular photography

Found photography overlaps with the scholarly study of vernacular photography — images made for everyday purposes rather than as art. As chemical snapshot photography has been replaced by digital images, the rescuing of physical prints has acquired an elegiac dimension, and found-photo communities thrive online.
