In mathematics, the Hausdorff dimension is a measure of roughness, or more specifically, a fractal dimension, introduced in 1918 by mathematician Felix Hausdorff. For instance, the Hausdorff dimension of a single point is zero, of a line segment is 1, of a square is 2, and of a cube is 3: sets with "ordinary" geometry have integer dimension equal to their topological dimension. Many irregular sets, however, have non-integer Hausdorff dimension, giving rigorous content to the idea of a fractal.

Definition

The dimension is defined through Hausdorff measures: for each candidate dimension s, one measures a set by covering it with small sets of diameter at most δ and summing the s-th powers of the diameters; as δ → 0 this measure jumps from infinity to zero at a critical value, which is the Hausdorff dimension. It is always at least the topological (Lebesgue covering) dimension and at most the ambient dimension.

Examples and properties

The middle-thirds Cantor set has Hausdorff dimension log 2/log 3 ≈ 0.631; the Koch curve has log 4/log 3 ≈ 1.262; the Sierpiński triangle has log 3/log 2 ≈ 1.585. Countable sets have dimension zero, and the Hausdorff dimension of a countable union is the supremum of the dimensions. Computing the exact Hausdorff dimension of natural objects is usually difficult: empirical estimates such as Richardson's for coastlines suggest values around 1.25 for the west coast of Britain.
