AKS primality test

The AKS primality test is a deterministic primality-proving algorithm created by Manindra Agrawal, Neeraj Kayal and Nitin Saxena of IIT Kanpur, published in 2002 in the paper "PRIMES is in P". It was the first primality test to be simultaneously general, polynomial-time, deterministic and unconditional — that is, not dependent on unproven hypotheses such as the generalised Riemann hypothesis.

The algorithm

The test rests on the observation that a number n is prime if and only if the polynomial congruence (x − a)n ≡ xn − a (mod n) holds for suitable a. To make this efficient, AKS performs the congruence modulo a polynomial xr − 1 for a carefully chosen small r, after first checking that n is not a perfect power and has no small prime factors. The original time complexity, Õ((log n)12), was later improved by the authors and others to about Õ((log n)6).

Significance

AKS settled a centuries-old question by proving that the problem of determining whether a number is prime belongs to the complexity class P. In practice, however, probabilistic tests such as Miller–Rabin and the elliptic-curve ECPP method remain much faster, so AKS is chiefly of theoretical importance. Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena received the 2006 Gödel Prize and Fulkerson Prize for the work.
