Diocletianic Persecution

The Diocletianic Persecution (or Great Persecution) was the last and most severe persecution of Christians in the Roman Empire. In 303, the emperors Diocletian, Maximian, Galerius, and Constantius issued a series of edicts rescinding Christians' legal rights and demanding that they comply with traditional Roman religious practices.

The persecution began with the edicts of 303 CE under Diocletian and the Tetrarchy.

The edicts

The first edict ordered the destruction of Christian churches and scriptures, the dismissal of Christians from public office, and the arrest of clergy. Later edicts required all inhabitants to sacrifice to the Roman gods on pain of imprisonment, torture, forced labor in mines, or execution. Enforcement varied widely: it was most severe in the eastern provinces under Galerius and Maximinus Daia, and much lighter in Gaul and Britain under Constantius Chlorus.

End and aftermath

The persecution began to collapse with the abdication of Diocletian in 305 and shifting imperial politics. In 311, the dying Galerius issued the Edict of Serdica, granting toleration, and in 313 Constantine and Licinius issued the Edict of Milan, legalizing Christianity throughout the empire. The persecution left a lasting legacy in the cult of the martyrs, the Donatist schism in North Africa over clergy who had surrendered scriptures (traditores), and the era of the martyrs used in Coptic chronology.
