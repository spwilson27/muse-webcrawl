Hyperbolic geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, hyperbolic geometry is a non-Euclidean geometry. The parallel postulate of Euclidean geometry is replaced with: for any given line R and point P not on R, in the plane containing both line R and point P there are at least two distinct lines through P that do not intersect R. In hyperbolic geometry there are in fact infinitely many such lines.

History

For two thousand years mathematicians attempted to prove Euclid's parallel postulate from his other axioms. In the 1820s and 1830s, Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky and János Bolyai independently published geometries built on its negation, while Carl Friedrich Gauss reached similar conclusions without publishing. Eugenio Beltrami's models of 1868 proved hyperbolic geometry consistent if Euclidean geometry is, ending the search for a contradiction.

Properties

In hyperbolic geometry the angles of a triangle sum to less than 180 degrees, with the deficit proportional to the triangle's area. There are no similar figures of different sizes: the angles of a triangle determine its side lengths completely. The circumference of a circle grows exponentially with radius, and the area of the hyperbolic plane is infinite.

Models

Several models represent hyperbolic geometry within Euclidean settings: the Poincaré disk model (conformal, preserving angles), the Poincaré half-plane model, the Klein disk model (with straight chords as lines but distorted angles), and the hyperboloid model, which realises the hyperbolic plane as a sheet of a two-sheeted hyperboloid in Minkowski space.

Applications

Hyperbolic geometry underlies the geometry of saddle surfaces, appears in special relativity through the hyperboloid model of velocity space, and has found modern uses in network science, where hyperbolic embeddings capture hierarchical structure, and in the geometry of certain materials and tilings popularised by M. C. Escher's Circle Limit prints.
