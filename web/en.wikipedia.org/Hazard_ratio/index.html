Hazard ratio

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In survival analysis, the hazard ratio (HR) is the ratio of the hazard rates corresponding to the conditions described by two levels of an explanatory variable. For example, in a clinical trial comparing a drug with a control, the hazard ratio is the hazard of an event (such as death or disease progression) in the treatment group divided by the hazard in the control group, over the study period.

Definition and interpretation

The hazard rate at time t is the instantaneous rate of the event among those still at risk. Under the Cox proportional hazards model, the hazard ratio for two groups is assumed constant over time. An HR of 1 means equal hazard; an HR of 0.5 means the event rate in the treated group is half that of the control group at any given time; an HR of 2 means it is double. Hazard ratios are usually reported with confidence intervals.

Distinguishing features

Unlike a risk ratio (relative risk), which compares cumulative probabilities at a chosen end point, the hazard ratio reflects the entire survival curve and accounts for censoring. The hazard ratio is not a direct measure of relative probability of survival: a given HR implies different absolute survival differences depending on the baseline hazard. When the proportional hazards assumption fails, a single hazard ratio can be misleading, and analysts may instead report restricted mean survival time or time-varying effects.
