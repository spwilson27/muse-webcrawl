Asylum

The term asylum (from Greek asylon, "inviolable place") has several related meanings. In its oldest sense it denotes a place of refuge or sanctuary, where a person could claim protection from pursuit. In modern international law it refers to the protection granted by a state to a foreign national fleeing persecution. Historically, "asylum" was also the common name for institutions housing people with mental illness.

Right of asylum

The right of asylum is an ancient juridical concept, under which people persecuted by their own rulers may be protected by another authority. It is recognized in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (Article 14) and codified for refugees in the 1951 Refugee Convention. A person who requests this protection is an asylum seeker.

Psychiatric asylums

From the 18th century onward, "lunatic asylums" were built across Europe and North America to house the mentally ill. Reform movements in the 19th century promoted more humane treatment, but many institutions became overcrowded custodial warehouses. From the mid-20th century, deinstitutionalization and new psychiatric treatments led to the closure of most large asylums.

Sanctuary in antiquity

Temples and sacred places in the ancient world offered asylum to fugitives, slaves, and debtors; violating sanctuary was a grave religious offence.
