Taishō democracy

Taishō democracy (大正デモクラシー) is a term for the liberal and democratic movements that flourished in Japan during the Taishō era (1912–1926) and into the early Shōwa period. It encompasses the growth of parliamentary party government, mass movements demanding universal suffrage and social reform, and a vibrant public sphere of newspapers, journals, and debate — developments that unfolded within, and in tension with, the imperial constitutional order established under Meiji.

Origins and ideas

The movement's roots lie in the popular rights movements of Meiji and in protests such as the 1905 Hibiya riots. Political scientist Yoshino Sakuzō gave it a theory with minponshugi — "government by the people" as the goal of politics, crafted to be compatible with imperial sovereignty — while Minobe Tatsukichi's "organ theory" treated the emperor as an organ of the state, a view that became mainstream in the 1920s before being denounced in 1935. The 1912–13 Taishō Political Crisis, in which mass protests forced out a military-backed cabinet, announced the era.

Achievements

From 1918 to 1932 Japan was governed mostly by party cabinets drawn from the majority in the elected lower house — a convention known as the "normal course of constitutional government". The franchise expanded step by step, reaching universal manhood suffrage in 1925 (women waited until 1945). Labour unions, tenant unions, a socialist and communist movement, and a women's movement (including the New Women's Association and suffrage leagues) all organised openly.

Limits and decline

Democratisation coexisted with empire and repression: the Peace Preservation Law of 1925 criminalised advocacy of altering the kokutai or abolishing private property, and colonial rule in Korea and Taiwan was undemocratic throughout. Assassination (Hara Takashi, 1921), economic crisis, and military insubordination eroded party government, which effectively ended with the assassination of Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi in the May 15 Incident of 1932.
