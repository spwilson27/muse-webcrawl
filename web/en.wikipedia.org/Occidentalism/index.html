Occidentalism

Occidentalism

Occidentalism refers to stereotyped, essentialised and often hostile views of the Western world — "the West" or "the Occident" — as held by people outside it, and sometimes by Westerners themselves. The term is commonly used as a counterpart to Orientalism, Edward Said's term for Western stereotypes of the East.

Meanings of the term

Scholars use the word in several distinct senses. In the work of anthropologist James G. Carrier and others, Occidentalism denotes the simplified images of the West that both non-Westerners and Westerners construct, parallel to Orientalist images of the East. Xiaomei Chen used it for the discourse by which Chinese intellectuals constructed images of the West for domestic political purposes. Hasan Hanafi proposed an "science of Occidentalism" ('ilm al-istighrab) as an Arab discipline for studying the West from outside.

Buruma and Margalit

In their 2004 book Occidentalism: The West in the Eyes of Its Enemies, Ian Buruma and Avishai Margalit described Occidentalism as a dehumanising ideology that portrays the West as soulless, materialist, rootless, decadent and machine-like. They traced its roots partly to European counter-Enlightenment thought, German Romantic nationalism, Slavophilism and Japanese nationalist writings of the 1930s–40s, arguing that these ideas were exported and adopted by movements hostile to liberal modernity, including some strands of radical Islamism.

Criticism

Critics have argued that the concept can flatten diverse grievances into a single pathology, that it risks implying a false symmetry with the institutional power behind colonial Orientalism, and that many critiques of Western policy are political arguments rather than essentialist stereotypes. Others value the term for highlighting how images of "the West" are constructed and deployed in global politics.
