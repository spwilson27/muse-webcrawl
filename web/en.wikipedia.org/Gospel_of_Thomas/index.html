The Gospel of Thomas (also known as the Coptic Gospel of Thomas) is an extra-canonical sayings gospel. It was discovered near Nag Hammadi, Egypt, in December 1945 among a group of books known as the Nag Hammadi library. Scholars speculate that the works were buried in response to a letter from Bishop Athanasius declaring a strict canon of Christian scripture.

Unlike the canonical gospels, Thomas contains no narrative of Jesus' life, death or resurrection. Instead it consists of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus, introduced by the formula "Jesus said". The text opens: "These are the secret sayings which the living Jesus spoke and which Didymos Judas Thomas wrote down."

Dating and origins

Scholars debate the date of composition, with proposals ranging from the mid-1st century to the late 2nd century. Three Greek fragments of the work were known earlier from the Oxyrhynchus Papyri. Many sayings parallel those in the Synoptic Gospels, while others appear unique, fuelling debate about whether Thomas depends on the canonical texts or preserves an independent tradition.

Theology

The Gospel of Thomas emphasises self-knowledge and the discovery of the Kingdom of God within and around the believer, rather than an apocalyptic future event. Several sayings have been interpreted as reflecting Gnostic or ascetic tendencies, though scholars disagree on classifying the work as Gnostic.
