Ubiquitin

Ubiquitin is a small regulatory protein found in most tissues of eukaryotic organisms. It consists of 76 amino acids and is almost perfectly conserved across eukaryotes — human and yeast ubiquitin differ by only three residues. Its name reflects this ubiquity. Ubiquitin's best-known role is to tag proteins for destruction by the proteasome, but it also regulates protein trafficking, DNA repair, inflammation, and many other processes.

Ubiquitination

The attachment of ubiquitin to a substrate protein, called ubiquitination, proceeds through a three-enzyme cascade: an activating enzyme (E1), a conjugating enzyme (E2), and a ligase (E3) that confers substrate specificity. Ubiquitin is typically linked through its C-terminal glycine to a lysine on the target. Chains of ubiquitin can be built through any of ubiquitin's seven lysine residues; chains linked through lysine-48 generally target the protein to the 26S proteasome for degradation, whereas lysine-63-linked chains serve signaling roles. Deubiquitinating enzymes can remove the tags, making the system reversible.

Discovery and significance

Ubiquitin was identified in 1975 by Gideon Goldstein and colleagues. Its function in ATP-dependent protein degradation was worked out by Aaron Ciechanover, Avram Hershko, and Irwin Rose, who received the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation. Defects in the ubiquitin–proteasome system are implicated in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's and Alzheimer's, and immune disorders; proteasome inhibitors like bortezomib are used in cancer therapy.
