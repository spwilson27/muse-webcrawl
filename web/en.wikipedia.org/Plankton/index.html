Plankton

GroupsPhytoplankton, zooplankton, bacterioplankton
HabitatOceans, seas, freshwater bodies
Size rangeMicrometres to over a metre (jellyfish)
Term coinedVictor Hensen (1887), from Greek planktos, "drifter"

Plankton are the diverse collection of organisms that drift in water (or air) and are unable to swim against a current. They live in oceans, seas, lakes, and ponds and provide a crucial source of food to fish and other aquatic life. The name comes from the Greek planktos, meaning "drifter" or "wanderer," and was coined by Victor Hensen in 1887.

Categories

Phytoplankton are photosynthetic plankton — cyanobacteria, diatoms, dinoflagellates, and coccolithophores — living in the sunlit surface layer, where they form the base of most aquatic food webs and produce a large share of the world's oxygen. Zooplankton are animals, from single-celled radiolarians and foraminifera to crustaceans such as copepods and krill, and the larvae of fish, crabs, and mollusks; some spend their whole lives as plankton (holoplankton), others only their larval stages (meroplankton). Bacterioplankton and virioplankton carry out much of the ocean's nutrient cycling.

Ecology

Many zooplankton undertake diel vertical migration — rising to the surface to feed at night and sinking by day — the largest daily migration on Earth by biomass. Plankton populations respond rapidly to temperature, light, and nutrients, producing seasonal blooms; harmful algal blooms can generate toxins that close fisheries. Plankton also drive the biological carbon pump, carrying carbon to the deep ocean when they die and sink.

Importance

Because of their sensitivity, plankton are key indicators of climate change; long-running surveys such as the Continuous Plankton Recorder (begun in the 1930s) track shifts in their distributions as oceans warm. Fossilized plankton shells also form important geological deposits and archives of past climates.
