Obiter dictum (Latin for "something said in passing"; plural obiter dicta) is a remark or observation made by a judge in a legal opinion that is not essential to the decision in the case. Unlike the ratio decidendi, obiter dicta do not form part of the binding precedent of a judgment, though they may carry persuasive weight.

Meaning and use

When a court delivers judgment, only the statements of law necessary to decide the case — the ratio decidendi — bind lower courts under the doctrine of stare decisis. Judges frequently go further, commenting on hypothetical situations, alternative arguments, or broader points of law. These incidental remarks are obiter dicta. A classic example is a judge explaining what the outcome would have been had the facts been slightly different.

Persuasive authority

Although not binding, obiter dicta from respected judges or higher courts are often highly influential. Lawyers cite them in argument, and later courts sometimes adopt an earlier obiter statement as the ratio of a new decision — a process by which influential dicta have shaped areas of law such as negligence, where Lord Atkin's "neighbour principle" in Donoghue v Stevenson began as a broad statement beyond the narrow facts of the case. In the United States, such remarks are often simply called dicta.

Identifying dicta

Distinguishing ratio from obiter is a central skill of common-law legal analysis and a frequent subject of academic debate, since a statement's status may depend on how widely or narrowly the facts of the original case are characterised. Courts may also deliberately flag remarks as obiter to signal that they are not part of the holding.
