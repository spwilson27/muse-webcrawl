Virology

Virology is the scientific study of biological viruses, the submicroscopic, parasitic organisms of genetic material contained in a protein coat, and virus-like agents. It focuses on the structure, classification and evolution of viruses, their ways of infecting and exploiting host cells for reproduction, their interaction with host organism physiology and immunity, the diseases they cause, the techniques to isolate and culture them, and their use in research and therapy.

History

The discipline began in the 1890s with the work of Dmitri Ivanovsky and Martinus Beijerinck on tobacco mosaic disease, which showed the causative agent could pass through filters that trapped bacteria. Beijerinck coined the term "contagium vivum fluidum" and, in effect, the modern concept of a virus. The first animal virus, foot-and-mouth disease virus, was identified in 1898 by Friedrich Loeffler and Paul Frosch. Wendell Stanley's crystallization of tobacco mosaic virus in 1935 demonstrated that viruses are not ordinary cells. In the mid-20th century, the work of bacteriophage researchers such as Max Delbrück, Salvador Luria, and Alfred Hershey laid foundations of molecular biology.

Structure and classification

Viruses consist of genetic material (DNA or RNA) enclosed in a protein capsid, sometimes surrounded by a lipid envelope. They are classified by the Baltimore system into seven groups based on genome type and replication strategy. The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses maintains the formal virus taxonomy. Unlike cells, viruses have no independent metabolism and replicate only inside living host cells.

Medical importance

Viruses cause diseases including influenza, the common cold, HIV/AIDS, measles, Ebola, and COVID-19. Control relies on sanitation, vaccination, and antiviral drugs. Viruses also serve as tools: bacteriophages are used in phage therapy, and engineered viruses are vectors in gene therapy.
