Gracchi


The Gracchi brothers, tribunes of the plebs.

The Gracchi were the brothers Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus (c. 163–133 BC) and Gaius Sempronius Gracchus (c. 154–121 BC), Roman tribunes of the plebs whose attempts to reform landholding, the grain supply and the courts in the second century BC introduced political violence into the Roman Republic and are conventionally taken to mark the beginning of its long crisis.

Tiberius Gracchus

Sons of the censor Tiberius Gracchus the Elder and Cornelia, daughter of Scipio Africanus, the brothers were aristocrats who championed the poor. As tribune in 133 BC, Tiberius proposed a land law enforcing long-ignored limits on holdings of public land (ager publicus) and redistributing the surplus to poor citizens. When a fellow tribune vetoed the bill, Tiberius had him deposed — a constitutional scandal — and stood for re-election. In the ensuing riot, senators led by his cousin Scipio Nasica clubbed Tiberius and some 300 supporters to death, the first bloodshed in Roman domestic politics in over three centuries.

Gaius Gracchus

Gaius, tribune in 123 and 122 BC, carried a broader programme: a subsidized grain law, jury reform transferring extortion courts from senators to equestrians, new colonies, roads, and a proposal to extend citizenship to Rome's Italian allies that fatally alienated his support. Declared an enemy of the state under the Senate's first "final decree" (senatus consultum ultimum), he fled and ordered his slave to kill him in 121 BC; some 3,000 of his followers were executed.

Legacy

Ancient sources — Appian and Plutarch, whose parallel Lives of the brothers survive — and modern historians treat the Gracchi as the opening act of the century of revolution that destroyed the Republic, pitting populares against optimates and normalizing violence as a political tool.
