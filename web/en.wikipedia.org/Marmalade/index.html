Marmalade

Marmalade is a fruit preserve made from the juice and peel of citrus fruits boiled with sugar and water. The well-known version is made from bitter Seville oranges. It may also be produced from lemons, limes, grapefruits, mandarins, sweet oranges, bergamots or combinations of these. Under European Union rules, the name "marmalade" is reserved for preserves made from citrus fruit.

History

The word derives from the Portuguese marmelada, a preserve of quince (from marmelo, quince). Citrus marmalades developed in Britain in the 18th century, where the bitter Seville orange — too sour to eat fresh — arrived each winter from Spain. Popular tradition credits Janet Keiller of Dundee, Scotland, with developing the modern peel-containing recipe around 1797, and the firm of James Keiller & Son became a major producer. Marmalade became a staple of the British breakfast, spread on toast.

Production and varieties

To make marmalade, the fruit is simmered, the peel thinly sliced (shred), and the mixture boiled with sugar until the natural pectin sets it to a gel. Varieties include thin-cut and thick-cut peel, dark marmalades cooked longer with brown sugar or molasses, and "jelly" marmalades without peel. Ginger, whisky and other flavourings are sometimes added. The annual World's Original Marmalade Awards, held at Dalemain in Cumbria, celebrate amateur and artisan producers.

In culture

Marmalade is famously the favourite food of Paddington Bear, who keeps a marmalade sandwich under his hat; Ian Fleming also made it a breakfast preference of James Bond.
