Injunction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An injunction is a legal and equitable remedy in the form of a court order that requires a party to do, or refrain from doing, specific acts. A party who disobeys an injunction faces contempt of court penalties, including fines or imprisonment. Injunctions originated in the English Court of Chancery as a remedy where money damages were inadequate.

Types

  • Temporary restraining order (TRO) — an emergency order lasting days, sometimes granted without notice to preserve the status quo.
  • Preliminary (interlocutory) injunction — granted during litigation, after a hearing, to prevent irreparable harm until trial.
  • Permanent injunction — issued as final relief after judgment on the merits.
  • Mandatory vs. prohibitory — mandatory injunctions compel affirmative action; prohibitory injunctions forbid conduct.

Requirements

In United States federal practice, following Winter v. NRDC (2008) and eBay v. MercExchange (2006), an applicant generally must show a likelihood of success on the merits, a likelihood of irreparable harm without relief, that the balance of equities tips in its favour, and that an injunction serves the public interest. Courts historically denied injunctions where damages were an adequate remedy.

Uses

Injunctions are common in intellectual-property disputes, nuisance and environmental cases, employment non-compete litigation, and constitutional challenges to statutes, where a court may enjoin enforcement of a law. In British practice, controversial "super-injunctions" have forbidden even reporting the order's existence, prompting debate over press freedom.
