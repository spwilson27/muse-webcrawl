The cane toad (Rhinella marina), also known as the giant neotropical toad or marine toad, is a large, terrestrial true toad native to South and mainland Central America, but introduced to various islands throughout Oceania and the Caribbean, as well as Northern Australia. It is a member of the genus Rhinella, which includes many true toad species found throughout Central and South America.

The cane toad is an old species; a fossil toad from the La Venta fauna of the late Miocene is indistinguishable from modern cane toads of northern South America. Adults are large, typically 10–15 cm (4–6 in) long, with dry, warty skin. The species is famous — and infamous — for its use as a biological control agent against sugarcane pests, and for becoming a devastating invasive species, most notoriously in Australia.

Toxicity

Cane toads secrete a milky-white toxin called bufotoxin from the prominent parotoid glands behind their eyes, as well as from other glands across their backs. The toxin contains bufotenine and cardiac glycosides and is dangerous to most animals that mouth or eat the toad; documented deaths include dogs, snakes, lizards and crocodiles in invaded regions. The eggs and tadpoles are also toxic. Cane toad poison has been used traditionally as an arrow poison in South America, and dried toad venom has been smoked recreationally for the hallucinogenic effects of its bufotenine content.

Introduction to Australia

In 1935, about 2,400 cane toads were released in the sugarcane-growing districts of Queensland in an attempt to control cane beetles whose larvae damaged cane roots. The program failed — the toads could not jump high enough to reach the adult beetles on the cane stalks, and the larvae lived underground — but the toads themselves thrived in the absence of predators able to tolerate their toxin. From the release sites they spread across tropical and subtropical Australia, advancing at rates reaching 50–60 km per year in some front regions, and by the twenty-first century numbered in the hundreds of millions. Their advance has caused severe declines in native predators such as northern quolls, monitor lizards and freshwater crocodiles.

Ecology and control

Cane toads are prolific breeders: a single female can lay up to 30,000 eggs in a season, spawning in almost any still or slow-moving water. They are opportunistic nocturnal predators of insects and other invertebrates, and will also eat small vertebrates and carrion. Control efforts in Australia include community "toad busting" collection nights, fencing of water points, research into pheromone traps using the toads' own tadpole-attracting chemicals, and studies of genetic and viral biocontrols. Evolutionary research has shown that toads at the invasion front have developed longer legs and greater endurance, while some native species have evolved resistance to the toxin or learned avoidance.
