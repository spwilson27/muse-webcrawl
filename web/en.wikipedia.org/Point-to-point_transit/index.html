Point-to-point transit

Point-to-point transit refers to a transportation system in which passengers travel directly from their origin to their destination without passing through a central hub. In commercial aviation, it contrasts with the hub-and-spoke model, in which flights are funneled through one or more hub airports where passengers connect. The term is also used in telecommunications and logistics, where it likewise denotes direct links between endpoints rather than distribution through a central node.

In aviation

Legacy network carriers traditionally operate hub-and-spoke systems, maximizing connections but adding travel time and congestion at hubs. Low-cost carriers, following the model pioneered by Southwest Airlines, have emphasized point-to-point routes, reducing connection complexity, turning aircraft around quickly, and serving secondary airports. The growth of long-range, fuel-efficient aircraft—such as the Boeing 787 Dreamliner and Airbus A321XLR—has enabled airlines to open direct long-haul routes between city pairs that could not previously sustain nonstop service, shifting the industry partly toward point-to-point operations.

Advantages and disadvantages

Point-to-point service generally offers passengers shorter travel times and fewer misconnections, and spreads traffic across more airports. However, it requires sufficient demand between each pair of cities; hub systems can aggregate thin demand from many origins to fill larger aircraft, achieve higher frequencies, and serve small communities more economically.
