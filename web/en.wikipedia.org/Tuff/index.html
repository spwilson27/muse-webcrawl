Tuff

Tuff, a rock formed from consolidated volcanic ash

Tuff is a type of rock made of volcanic ash ejected from a vent during a volcanic eruption. Following ejection and deposition, the ash is lithified into a solid rock. Rock that contains greater than 75% ash is considered tuff, while rock containing 25% to 75% ash is described as tuffaceous.

Formation

Tuff is a relatively soft rock formed when a volcano erupts ash. The ash settles and is compacted and cemented over time through processes of compaction and cementation. Tuff can vary in thickness from a fraction of a centimetre to many metres, and can extend over large distances. It can be classified according to the nature of the fragments it contains: vitric tuff consists primarily of glass shards, crystal tuff of crystal fragments, and lithic tuff of rock fragments.

Welded tuff and ignimbrite

When volcanic ash is still hot at the time of deposition, the particles may weld together under their own heat and the weight of overlying material, producing welded tuff. Very hot, gas-charged deposits from pyroclastic flows form a rock type known as ignimbrite. The famous Bishop Tuff of California, erupted about 760,000 years ago, is a well-known example.

Use as building stone

Tuff has been used as a building material since antiquity because it is relatively soft and easy to cut when quarried, yet durable. The ancient Romans used tuff extensively; the Servian Wall of Rome and many other structures were built of it. In Armenia, tuff is widely used in architecture, giving buildings in Yerevan and Gyumri their characteristic pink hues. The Easter Island moai statues were carved largely from tuff quarried at Rano Raraku.
