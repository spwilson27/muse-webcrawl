A metalloid is a chemical element whose properties are intermediate between, or a mixture of, those of metals and nonmetals. There is no standard definition of a metalloid, and the term is used loosely in chemistry. The six commonly recognised metalloids are boron, silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony, and tellurium; five less often recognised are carbon, aluminium, selenium, polonium, and astatine. On a standard periodic table, most metalloids lie along a diagonal band running from boron to astatine.

Properties

Metalloids typically look like metals — they are shiny solids — but behave chemically more like nonmetals, forming acidic or amphoteric oxides and covalent rather than ionic compounds. Their electrical conductivity is intermediate: silicon and germanium are semiconductors, the foundation of the electronics industry. Metalloids are usually brittle and have electronegativities and ionisation energies between those of metals and nonmetals.

Applications

Silicon is the most important metalloid technologically, used in computer chips, solar cells, and glass (as silica). Boron compounds appear in detergents, glasses, and semiconductors. Arsenic and antimony have historical uses in alloys, pigments, and poisons, while tellurium is used in solar panels and thermoelectric devices.
