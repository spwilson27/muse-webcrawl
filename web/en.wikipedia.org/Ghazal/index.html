Ghazal

The ghazal is a form of amatory lyric poetry originating in Arabic literature, later adopted and transformed in Persian, Turkish, Urdu, and other literary traditions. It consists of a series of rhyming couplets (sher), each of which is semantically independent — a chain of self-contained "pearls" — unified by a strict rhyme scheme and refrain.

Form

A classical ghazal typically has five to fifteen couplets. The first couplet, the matla, establishes the rhyme (qafia) and refrain (radif), which then recur at the end of the second line of every subsequent couplet. The final couplet, the maqta, traditionally contains the poet's pen name (takhallus). Metre is uniform throughout the poem.

Themes and history

The ghazal developed from the amatory prelude (nasib) of the Arabic qasida and was perfected in Persian by poets such as Saadi and Hafez, whose Divan remains central to Persian culture. Its characteristic themes are earthly and divine love, longing and separation, wine, and the cruelty of the beloved — imagery often deliberately ambiguous between worldly and mystical readings, which made the ghazal a favoured vehicle of Sufi poetry. In Mughal India the form flowered in Urdu in the hands of Mir Taqi Mir, Ghalib, and later Iqbal. In South Asia the ghazal also became a major genre of sung music, popularised in the twentieth century by singers such as Begum Akhtar, Mehdi Hassan, and Jagjit Singh. Goethe's West–Eastern Divan introduced the form to German literature, and poets such as Agha Shahid Ali later established it in English.
