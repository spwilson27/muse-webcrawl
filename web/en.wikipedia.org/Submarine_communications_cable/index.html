Submarine communications cable

A submarine communications cable is a cable laid on the sea bed between land-based stations to carry telecommunication signals across stretches of ocean. Modern cables use optical fiber technology and carry the overwhelming majority of intercontinental data traffic — telephone calls, internet traffic, and private data — having largely displaced communications satellites for high-volume routes because of their far greater capacity and lower latency.

History

The first submarine cables, laid in the 1850s, carried telegraph traffic. The first transatlantic telegraph cable was completed in 1858 but failed within weeks; durable transatlantic service began in 1866 with the SS Great Eastern. Coaxial telephone cables followed in the 20th century (the first transatlantic telephone cable, TAT-1, entered service in 1956). The first transatlantic fiber-optic cable, TAT-8, began service in 1988.

Construction and laying

Modern cables consist of optical fibers protected by layers of gel, copper or aluminium (which also carries power to undersea repeaters), polycarbonate, steel armour wires, and waterproof sheathing. Near shore, cables are heavier-armoured and often buried beneath the seabed by ploughs to protect them from anchors and fishing gear. Specialised cable-laying ships install and repair them; breaks — most often caused by anchors, trawling, earthquakes, and occasionally shark bites — are located electrically and repaired by grappling the cable to the surface and splicing in new sections.

Capacity

Contemporary systems transmit many terabits per second per fiber pair using dense wavelength-division multiplexing; large cables may carry a dozen or more fiber pairs and total design capacities of hundreds of terabits per second.
