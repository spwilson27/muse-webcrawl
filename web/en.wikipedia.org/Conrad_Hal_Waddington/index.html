Conrad Hal Waddington

Conrad Hal Waddington CBE FRS FRSE (8 November 1905 – 26 September 1975) was a British developmental biologist, paleontologist, geneticist and philosopher who founded systems biology and coined fundamental concepts of modern developmental biology, including the epigenetic landscape, canalisation, genetic assimilation and the chreode.

Life and career

Educated at Cambridge, Waddington worked on embryology in the 1930s, collaborating with Joseph Needham and others on the "organiser" phenomenon in development. During the Second World War he worked in operational research for RAF Coastal Command. In 1947 he became professor of animal genetics at the University of Edinburgh, where he remained for the rest of his career, building a leading school of developmental genetics.

Key ideas

Waddington introduced the term epigenetics in 1942 for the study of how genes interact with their environment to produce phenotypes. His epigenetic landscape metaphor pictured a developing cell as a ball rolling down a branching valley system, its path constrained ("canalised") into stable routes he called chreodes. In experiments with Drosophila in the 1950s he demonstrated genetic assimilation: environmentally induced traits, after generations of selection, could appear without the environmental trigger, a finding he used to argue for interactions between evolution, development and environment well beyond a simple gene-centred view.

Legacy

Waddington's books, including Organisers and Genes (1940), The Strategy of the Genes (1957) and The Evolution of an Evolutionist (1975), influenced evolutionary developmental biology and the revival of epigenetics, and his landscape image remains one of the most reproduced diagrams in biology.
