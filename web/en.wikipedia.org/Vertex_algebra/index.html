Vertex operator algebra

In mathematics, a vertex operator algebra (VOA) is an algebraic structure that originated in two-dimensional conformal field theory and string theory. Vertex algebras were introduced by Richard Borcherds in 1986, motivated by the vertex operators of string theory and by the construction of the monster vertex algebra used in his proof of the monstrous moonshine conjectures, work for which he received the Fields Medal in 1998.

Definition

Roughly, a vertex algebra is a vector space V equipped with a state–operator correspondence assigning to each vector v a formal power series Y(v, z) of operators (the vertex operators), together with a vacuum vector and axioms — including a locality (or Jacobi identity) condition — that encode the operator product expansion of quantum field theory. A vertex operator algebra additionally has a conformal vector whose modes generate a representation of the Virasoro algebra with a given central charge, and a grading of V by conformal weight.

Examples and applications

Important families of examples include vertex algebras built from lattices, from affine Lie algebras at fixed level, and from Virasoro minimal models. The moonshine module constructed by Frenkel, Lepowsky and Meurman is a vertex operator algebra with automorphism group the monster group, the largest sporadic finite simple group; its graded dimension is given by the modular j-function, explaining the numerical coincidences of monstrous moonshine. Vertex algebras are now a central tool in representation theory, the geometric Langlands program, and the mathematical foundations of conformal field theory.

History

The axioms distilled ideas circulating in physics during the early 1980s, when vertex operators were used informally in string theory. Borcherds' rigorous definition, and the subsequent work of Frenkel, Lepowsky and Meurman (who coined "vertex operator algebra"), turned the subject into a rich branch of algebra with deep connections to modular forms, infinite-dimensional Lie algebras and sporadic groups.
