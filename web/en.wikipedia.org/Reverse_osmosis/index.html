Reverse osmosis (RO) is a water purification process that uses a partially permeable membrane to separate ions, molecules, and larger particles from water. By applying pressure greater than the natural osmotic pressure, water is forced through the membrane in the opposite direction to osmosis, leaving most dissolved contaminants behind in a concentrated reject stream.

Principle

In ordinary osmosis, solvent moves across a semipermeable membrane toward the side of higher solute concentration until equilibrium. In reverse osmosis, applied pressure (typically 2–17 bar for brackish water and household systems, and 55–80 bar for seawater) exceeds the osmotic pressure — about 27 bar for seawater — so water flows from the concentrated to the dilute side. Modern membranes are thin-film composites, usually a polyamide layer on a polysulfone support, wound into spiral modules; they reject 95–99% or more of dissolved salts plus virtually all bacteria, viruses, and colloids.

History

The phenomenon of osmosis through membranes was studied from the 18th century; the breakthrough for practical separation came in 1959–1960 when Sidney Loeb and Srinivasa Sourirajan at UCLA produced the first practical asymmetric cellulose acetate membrane, making desalination by RO economically feasible. A demonstration plant opened in Coalinga, California, in 1965. Improvements in thin-film composite membranes from the 1970s cut pressures and costs dramatically.

Applications

Seawater reverse osmosis is now the leading desalination technology worldwide, supplying cities in Israel, Australia, Spain, Singapore, and California; the largest plants produce hundreds of thousands of cubic metres per day at energy consumptions around 3 kWh/m³, aided by isobaric energy-recovery devices. RO also provides domestic under-sink drinking water systems, boiler and pharmaceutical ultrapure water, wastewater reuse, and concentration duties in the food industry (e.g., maple sap and fruit juices).

Drawbacks

RO produces a reject (brine) stream that must be disposed of, wastes a fraction of the feed water — old household units could reject several litres per litre produced, though modern systems are much better — and removes beneficial minerals, so remineralisation is common for municipal supplies. Membranes are vulnerable to fouling and chlorine damage and require pretreatment.
