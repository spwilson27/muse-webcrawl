Serotiny

Serotinous cones open to release seed after fire

Serotiny in botany is a form of environmental dormancy in which seed release occurs in response to an environmental trigger rather than at seed maturation. The most common and best studied trigger is fire, in which case the term pyriscence is also used. Other triggers may include death of the parent plant, wetting, warming, or cycles of drying.

Fire-adapted serotiny

Many plants in fire-prone environments, including numerous species of Pinus (such as lodgepole pine and jack pine), Banksia, Hakea and some cypresses, retain seeds for years in closed cones or woody fruits sealed with resin or lignified tissue. The heat of a wildfire melts the resin or cracks the fruit, releasing a large pulse of seed onto a freshly burned, ash-enriched seedbed with reduced competition — conditions highly favourable for germination.

Ecology

Serotiny is most prevalent where fires are frequent but not so frequent as to kill plants before they build up a canopy seed bank. The degree of serotiny within a population can vary with fire intervals: populations subject to longer fire-free intervals tend to be more strongly serotinous. Climate change and altered fire regimes — fires that are too frequent or too rare — can threaten strongly serotinous species whose life histories are tuned to a particular fire cycle.

Other forms

Related strategies include seeds held in the soil seed bank until fire cues such as heat or smoke chemicals (karrikins) trigger germination, and fruits that open only after the parent branch dies (necriscence).
