United States Secretary of the Interior

The United States secretary of the interior is the head of the United States Department of the Interior. The department is responsible for the conservation and management of federal land and natural resources, and for the administration of programs relating to Native Americans, Alaska Natives, Native Hawaiians, and territorial affairs. The secretary is a member of the president's Cabinet.

Duties

The Department of the Interior manages roughly one-fifth of the land in the United States through bureaus such as the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Bureau of Reclamation, and the U.S. Geological Survey. The secretary oversees energy development, grazing, mining, and water projects on federal lands, as well as the government's trust responsibilities to Native American tribes.

History

The department was created in 1849, gathering functions previously scattered across the Treasury, War, and State departments; Thomas Ewing of Ohio was its first secretary. Conservation-era secretaries such as James R. Garfield and later Stewart Udall expanded the national park system. Albert Fall, secretary under Warren G. Harding, resigned in 1923 and was later imprisoned in the Teapot Dome scandal, becoming the first Cabinet member to go to prison for crimes committed in office.
