Introspection

Introspection is the examination of one's own conscious thoughts and feelings. In psychology, the process of introspection relies on the observation of one's mental state, while in a spiritual context it may refer to the examination of one's soul. Introspection is closely related to human self-reflection and self-discovery and is contrasted with external observation.

Philosophical background

Introspection has been a subject of philosophical discussion for thousands of years. Plato asked whether "the soul inquires alone by itself," and the Delphic maxim "know thyself" made self-knowledge a central aim of ancient philosophy. Descartes treated introspective awareness as the foundation of knowledge in his dictum cogito, ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"). Later philosophers debated whether introspection yields self-knowledge of a special, infallible kind, or whether one's awareness of one's own attitudes is interpretive and fallible like knowledge of others.

In psychology

Introspection was the principal method of the first school of experimental psychology. Wilhelm Wundt, founder of the first psychology laboratory in Leipzig in 1879, trained observers to report their conscious experiences under controlled conditions. Edward Titchener developed this approach into "structuralism" in the United States. The method fell out of favor in the early 20th century: behaviorists such as John B. Watson argued that mental states were not suitable subjects for science, and studies showed that introspective reports were often unreliable — people readily confabulate explanations for their own behavior, a finding demonstrated by Nisbett and Wilson in 1977.

Introspection nonetheless remains in use in modern research, for example in experience-sampling methods, think-aloud protocols, and phenomenological psychology, typically with greater caution about its limits.

Related concepts

Introspection is distinguished from reflection, rumination, and metacognition, though these overlap substantially. Excessive self-focused introspection in the form of rumination is associated with depression and anxiety, whereas structured self-reflection can support emotional regulation and learning.
