Ahimsa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ahimsa (Sanskrit: अहिंसा, ahiṃsā) is the ancient Indian principle of non-violence toward all living beings. It is a central virtue in Hinduism, Buddhism and especially Jainism, in which it is the foremost of the five vows (mahavratas). The word derives from the Sanskrit root hiṃs (to cause injury) with the negative prefix a-.

In Jainism

Jainism gives ahimsa its most rigorous interpretation: monks and nuns vow to avoid harming any creature, including micro-organisms, in action, speech and thought. Practices include vegetarianism, filtering water, sweeping the path to avoid stepping on insects, and wearing mouth coverings. Lay Jains observe these vows in a less strict form.

In Hinduism and Buddhism

In Hinduism, ahimsa appears in the Upanishads, the Mahabharata ("Ahimsa is the highest dharma") and Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, where it is the first of the yamas. In Buddhism, non-harming is embodied in the first of the Five Precepts and in the ideal of compassion (karuna) toward all beings.

Modern influence

Mahatma Gandhi made ahimsa the philosophical foundation of his method of nonviolent resistance (satyagraha), which influenced civil-rights movements worldwide, including those led by Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela. The principle also underpins much modern advocacy of vegetarianism and animal welfare.
