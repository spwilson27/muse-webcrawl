Flight control surfaces

Flight control surfaces are aerodynamic devices that allow a pilot to adjust and control the flight attitude of an aircraft. The development of an effective set of control surfaces is a critical aspect of aircraft design. The primary control surfaces — ailerons, elevators and rudder — control the aircraft about its roll, pitch and yaw axes respectively, while secondary surfaces such as flaps, slats, spoilers and trim systems modify lift, drag and control loads.

Primary control surfaces

Ailerons are mounted on the trailing edge of each wing near the tips and move in opposite directions to control roll: raising one and lowering the other increases lift on one wing and decreases it on the other, banking the aircraft. The elevator is mounted on the tailplane (horizontal stabilizer) and controls pitch, raising or lowering the nose. On aircraft without a separate tailplane, combined surfaces called elevons perform both elevator and aileron functions. The rudder, mounted on the fin (vertical stabilizer), controls yaw, moving the nose left or right; it is used particularly to coordinate turns and to counter adverse yaw.

Secondary control surfaces

Flaps extend from the wing trailing edge to increase lift and drag at low speeds, enabling slower take-offs and landings; common types include plain, split, slotted and Fowler flaps. Slats on the leading edge increase the maximum lift coefficient by controlling airflow over the wing. Spoilers disrupt airflow over the wing to reduce lift and increase drag, assisting roll control and acting as airbrakes; when deployed symmetrically on the ground after touchdown they are called lift dumpers. Trim tabs and adjustable stabilizers relieve control forces so the pilot need not hold constant pressure on the controls.

Operation

In light aircraft the surfaces are usually moved by cables and pulleys connected directly to the control column and rudder pedals; larger aircraft use hydraulic power, and many modern airliners employ fly-by-wire systems in which electronic signals command actuators. Control surfaces must be carefully balanced to avoid flutter, a dangerous aeroelastic oscillation.
