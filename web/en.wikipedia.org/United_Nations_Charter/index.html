United Nations Charter

The Charter of the United Nations (UN Charter) is the foundational treaty of the United Nations, an intergovernmental organization. It establishes the purposes, principles, and organs of the UN, and as a treaty under international law, all member states are bound by its articles. The Charter was signed on 26 June 1945 in San Francisco, at the conclusion of the United Nations Conference on International Organization, and came into force on 24 October 1945.

History

The Charter grew out of wartime diplomacy: the Atlantic Charter (1941), the Declaration by United Nations (1942), and the Dumbarton Oaks Conference (1944), where the framework was drafted. At San Francisco in the spring of 1945, delegates from 50 countries revised the Dumbarton Oaks proposals, strengthening the General Assembly's role and adding provisions on human rights promoted by smaller states and non-governmental consultants. Ratification by the five permanent members of the Security Council and a majority of other signatories brought the Charter into force on 24 October 1945, now celebrated as United Nations Day.

Provisions

The preamble famously begins "We the peoples of the United Nations." Chapter I states the UN's purposes — maintaining peace and security, developing friendly relations, and achieving cooperation on economic, social, and humanitarian problems — and principles including the sovereign equality of members and the prohibition, in Article 2(4), of the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state. Subsequent chapters define the six principal organs: the General Assembly, the Security Council, the Economic and Social Council, the Trusteeship Council, the International Court of Justice, and the Secretariat. Chapter VII empowers the Security Council to authorize sanctions and military action, and Chapter XIV incorporates the Statute of the International Court of Justice.

Amendments

The Charter has been amended only a few times, chiefly to enlarge the Security Council (from 11 to 15 members, in 1965) and the Economic and Social Council as UN membership grew from 51 to 193 states. Amendments require ratification by two-thirds of member states including all five permanent Security Council members, which has made structural reform difficult despite decades of debate.
