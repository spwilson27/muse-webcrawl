Carte de visite

A carte de visite portrait, c. 1860s

A carte de visite (French for "visiting card", abbreviated CdV) is a type of small photograph that became enormously popular in the 1860s. It consists of a thin paper albumen print, usually a portrait about 54 × 89 mm, mounted on a slightly larger card. The format was patented in Paris in 1854 by the photographer André-Adolphe-Eugène Disdéri, whose multi-lens camera could produce eight small negatives on a single plate, making portraits cheap enough for mass production.

The "cardomania" craze

The carte de visite became a worldwide fad after Disdéri photographed Napoleon III in 1859 and the resulting cards sold in huge numbers. Studios sprang up in every city; sitters posed in their best clothes against painted backdrops and studio props, and the cards were exchanged among friends, collected in albums, and bought as celebrity portraits — "cardomania" swept Britain, France and the United States. During the American Civil War, soldiers and their families exchanged cartes de visite in vast numbers, and portraits of Lincoln, generals and other public figures sold commercially.

Characteristics

Early cartes de visite have thin mounts and square corners; later examples are thicker with rounded corners, gilt edges and elaborate studio backmarks that are today used by collectors and genealogists to date images. Full-length portraits predominated in the early 1860s, with head-and-shoulders vignettes becoming fashionable later in the decade.

Decline and successors

From the late 1860s the larger cabinet card (about 108 × 165 mm) gradually displaced the carte de visite, offering a grander image, though cartes continued to be produced into the early 1900s. Millions survive, making them a major primary source for costume history, genealogy and the social history of photography. The format is widely credited with democratizing portraiture, bringing likenesses of ordinary people within reach for the first time.
