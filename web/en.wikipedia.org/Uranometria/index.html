Uranometria is a star atlas produced by the German astronomer and lawyer Johann Bayer (1572–1625). It was published in Augsburg in 1603 by Christoph Mang (Christophorus Mangus) under the full title Uranometria: omnium asterismorum continens schemata, nova methodo delineata, aereis laminis expressa ("Uranometria, containing charts of all the constellations, drawn by a new method and engraved on copper plates"). The name derives from Urania, the Greek muse of astronomy, and the Greek word for "measure".

It was the first atlas to cover the entire celestial sphere and introduced the system of designating stars with Greek letters — the Bayer designations — that remains in use today.

Contents

The atlas contains 51 engraved plates. The first 48 charts depict the 48 constellations listed by Ptolemy in the Almagest. The 49th chart presents twelve new southern constellations based on observations made during voyages to the East Indies, principally by the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman. The remaining plates show the two hemispheres in planisphere form.

The engravings were executed by the Augsburg artist Alexander Mair. The charts are notable for their artistry: the constellation figures are drawn in elaborate detail, and stars are plotted with unprecedented positional accuracy for the period, drawing in part on Tycho Brahe's star catalogue.

Bayer designations

In Uranometria, Bayer assigned each bright star a lowercase Greek letter, generally in order of decreasing brightness within its constellation — alpha (α) for the brightest, beta (β) for the second brightest, and so on. When the 24 Greek letters were exhausted, he continued with Latin letters. These designations, such as Alpha Centauri and Beta Pictoris, are still the standard names for the brighter stars.

Influence

Uranometria set the standard for celestial cartography for more than a century and influenced later atlases by John Flamsteed, John Bevis and others. Its constellation figures, modeled in turn on earlier maps, remained canonical until the eighteenth century, and several of the southern constellations introduced in it are among the 88 modern constellations recognized by the International Astronomical Union.
