Artemisinin

Chemical formula: C₁₅H₂₂O₅

Source: Artemisia annua (sweet wormwood)

Class: Antimalarial

Discovered: 1972, by Tu Youyou's team

Artemisinin, also known as qinghaosu, is a drug used in the treatment of malaria. It is a sesquiterpene lactone containing an unusual peroxide (endoperoxide) bridge, which is believed to be responsible for its antimalarial activity. The compound is isolated from the plant Artemisia annua, sweet wormwood, long used in traditional Chinese medicine.

Discovery

Artemisinin was discovered in 1972 by the Chinese scientist Tu Youyou and her team working under Project 523, a secret military programme launched during the Vietnam War to find new antimalarial drugs. Guided by ancient texts describing cold extraction of qinghao, Tu developed a low-temperature ether extraction that preserved the compound's activity. She shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.

Medical use

Artemisinin and its derivatives — including artesunate, artemether and dihydroartemisinin — are the most effective antimalarial drugs available. Because of their short half-lives, they are used in artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) with a longer-acting partner drug, which the World Health Organization recommends as first-line treatment for uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria. Intravenous artesunate is the treatment of choice for severe malaria.

Resistance and production

Partial artemisinin resistance, associated with mutations in the parasite's kelch13 gene and delayed parasite clearance, emerged in Southeast Asia and has since been detected in parts of Africa, prompting surveillance and new drug development. Artemisinin is produced commercially from cultivated A. annua, and semi-synthetic production using engineered yeast has supplemented the agricultural supply.
