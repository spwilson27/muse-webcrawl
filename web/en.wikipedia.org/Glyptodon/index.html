Glyptodon

Glyptodon was a heavily armored relative of modern armadillos.

Glyptodon is a genus of glyptodont, an extinct group of large, heavily armored mammals related to armadillos. It lived from the Pliocene through the early Holocene in South America, going extinct roughly 11,000 years ago at the end of the last Ice Age. The name means "grooved or carved tooth", referring to the shape of its molar teeth.

Description

Glyptodon grew to about 3.3 metres (11 ft) in length and roughly 2 tonnes in weight — comparable in size and shape to a small car. Its back was covered by a rigid carapace up to 2.5 cm thick, made of more than a thousand fused bony plates called osteoderms. Unlike some other glyptodonts, it had a relatively smooth, non-spiked tail armored with rings of bone. It was a grazing herbivore with ever-growing cheek teeth adapted to coarse grasses.

Evolution and extinction

Glyptodonts evolved in South America and spread north during the Great American Interchange after the Isthmus of Panama formed. Charles Darwin collected Glyptodon remains in Uruguay during the voyage of the Beagle, and Richard Owen formally described the genus. Its extinction coincided with both the end-Pleistocene climate warming and the arrival of human hunters in the Americas; evidence suggests glyptodonts were hunted, and their shells may have been used as shelters.
