Hemolytic disease of the newborn

Hemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN), also called hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) or erythroblastosis fetalis, is an alloimmune disorder in which antibodies produced by a mother against blood-group antigens inherited by her fetus from the father cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells. The resulting anaemia and jaundice can range from mild to fatal.

Causes

The classic cause is Rh(D) incompatibility: an Rh-negative mother exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood — usually at delivery, but also through miscarriage, abortion, amniocentesis or abdominal trauma — becomes sensitised and produces IgG anti-D antibodies. In a later pregnancy with an Rh-positive baby, these antibodies cross the placenta and cause haemolysis. ABO incompatibility (typically a group O mother with a group A or B baby) is more common but usually milder, because anti-A and anti-B are partly IgM, which cannot cross the placenta, and fetal red cells carry fewer A/B antigens. Antibodies against other antigens, notably Kell, and also c, E, Duffy and Kidd antigens, can also cause the disease.

Clinical features

Fetal anaemia may lead to high-output heart failure and hydrops fetalis — generalised oedema that is often fatal without treatment. After birth, rapid red-cell breakdown floods the newborn with bilirubin, producing severe jaundice; untreated, bilirubin can cross into the brain and cause kernicterus, permanent neurological damage. Signs range from a mildly jaundiced but otherwise healthy infant to profound anaemia with an enlarged liver and spleen.

Prevention and treatment

Rh(D) disease has been largely prevented in developed countries since the late 1960s by giving Rh-negative mothers anti-D immunoglobulin (Rho(D) immune globulin) after delivery and other sensitising events, and routinely during pregnancy, clearing fetal cells before the mother can become sensitised. Sensitised pregnancies are monitored with antibody titres and ultrasound (middle cerebral artery Doppler) for fetal anaemia; severely affected fetuses can receive intrauterine blood transfusions. After birth, treatment includes intensive phototherapy for jaundice, exchange transfusion in severe cases, and simple transfusions for anaemia.
