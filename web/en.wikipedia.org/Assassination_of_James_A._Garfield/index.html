Assassination of James A. Garfield

James A. Garfield, the 20th president of the United States, was shot on July 2, 1881, at the Baltimore and Potomac Railroad Station in Washington, D.C. He died 79 days later on September 19, 1881, in Elberon, New Jersey, from infections related to his wounds. He was the second of four U.S. presidents to be assassinated.

The shooting

Garfield was shot twice by Charles J. Guiteau, a disgruntled office-seeker and former supporter who believed he was owed a consular post under the spoils system. One bullet grazed the president's arm; the other lodged in his back. Guiteau was arrested immediately and declared he was a "Stalwart of the Stalwarts", referring to a Republican Party faction.

Medical treatment

Historians widely blame Garfield's death on the unsterile probing of his wound by his physicians, led by Doctor Willard Bliss, who repeatedly inserted unwashed fingers and instruments, causing severe infection and abscesses. Alexander Graham Bell devised a metal detector in an attempt to locate the bullet, but it failed, partly because Bliss restricted the search. The president's condition declined through the summer despite periods of optimism.

Aftermath

Vice President Chester A. Arthur succeeded Garfield. Guiteau was tried, convicted, and hanged on June 30, 1882. The assassination spurred civil service reform, culminating in the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883.
