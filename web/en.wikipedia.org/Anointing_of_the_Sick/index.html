Anointing of the Sick

Anointing of the Sick
TypeSacrament (Catholic and Orthodox Christianity); rite in other traditions
MatterOil blessed by a bishop (chrism or oil of the infirm)
MinisterPriest or bishop
Biblical basisJames 5:14–15; Mark 6:13

The Anointing of the Sick is a sacrament of the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church (where it is called Holy Unction), administered to a believer who is seriously ill, frail through old age, or facing major surgery. It involves prayer and anointing with blessed oil by a priest, and is understood to give spiritual strength, peace and courage, forgiveness of sins, union with the suffering of Christ, and — if God wills — physical healing. In the medieval Western church it became known as Extreme Unction, one of the "last rites", because it was commonly administered only in danger of death.

Biblical basis and history

The rite is grounded in the Epistle of James: "Is anyone among you sick? Let them call for the elders of the church to pray over them and anoint them with oil in the name of the Lord" (James 5:14). In the early church anointing was a healing rite for the sick generally; over the medieval centuries it was progressively reserved to the dying and linked with final repentance, and the Council of Trent (1551) defined it as a sacrament instituted by Christ. The Second Vatican Council restored the earlier usage: the 1972 apostolic constitution Sacram Unctionem Infirmorum renamed it the Anointing of the Sick and directed that it be given to those who are seriously ill, not only those at the point of death.

The rite

In the Roman rite the priest lays hands on the sick person, blesses or uses oil previously blessed by the bishop, and anoints the forehead and hands (and, in the Orthodox tradition, other parts of the body), saying: "Through this holy anointing may the Lord in his love and mercy help you with the grace of the Holy Spirit. May the Lord who frees you from sin save you and raise you up." The sacrament is often combined with the sacraments of Penance and the Eucharist (as Viaticum when given to the dying). It may be repeated if the person recovers and falls ill again, or if the illness worsens.

Other traditions

Eastern Orthodox practice ideally gathers seven priests for Holy Unction, and in Greek and Russian practice it is offered communally to all the faithful during Holy Week. Anglican and Lutheran churches retain anointing of the sick as a pastoral rite of healing (found in the Book of Common Prayer tradition), though not numbered among the sacraments; many Pentecostal and evangelical churches also practise anointing with oil for healing prayer.
