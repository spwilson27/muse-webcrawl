Rebellion

A rebellion is an organized, often armed, resistance to an established government or authority.

Rebellion is a violent uprising against an authority, government, or ruler. Rebellions are usually considered to be motivated by a sense of grievance or disenchantment with authority, and range from localized riots that flare up spontaneously to organized and armed resistance movements that aim at seizing control of territory or overthrowing the existing order.

Distinctions

Related terms describe variations in aim and scale. A revolt is often used interchangeably with rebellion, sometimes for smaller or more spontaneous risings. An insurgency is a sustained armed campaign, typically by irregular forces, against a governing authority. A mutiny is rebellion within a military body against its commanders. A coup d'état is a seizure of power by a small group, usually from within the state apparatus, and is therefore not generally classed as a rebellion of the ruled. A revolution aims not merely to resist authority but to replace the political and social order itself — a rebellion that succeeds in such aims is commonly re-labelled a revolution, while failed revolutions may be recorded as mere rebellions.

Causes and study

Historian Charles Tilly and other social scientists have studied rebellion as a form of contentious politics, arising where grievances combine with organization, resources and political opportunity. Relative deprivation theory, associated with Ted Robert Gurr's Why Men Rebel (1970), holds that rebellion becomes likely when the gap between what people expect and what they receive grows intolerable. James C. Scott drew attention to everyday, low-profile forms of resistance among peasants — foot-dragging, evasion and gossip — that fall short of open rebellion but share its motives.

Examples

Well-known rebellions include the Peasants' Revolt in England (1381), the Pugachev Rebellion in Russia (1773–75), the Taiping Rebellion in China (1850–64) — among the deadliest conflicts in history — and the Irish Easter Rising of 1916. In law, organizing or joining a rebellion against the state has historically been prosecuted as treason or sedition.
