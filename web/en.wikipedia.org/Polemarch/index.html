Polemarch

A polemarch (from Ancient Greek: πολέμαρχος, polemarchos) was a senior military title in various ancient Greek city states (poleis). The title derives from the words polemos (war) and archon (ruler, leader) and translates as "warleader" or "warlord". The name indicates that the polemarch's original function was to command the army; presumably the office was created to take over this function from the king. The title subsequently survived even after this function was assumed by other officials.

Athens

In Athens, the polemarch was one of the nine archons, originally elected and later chosen by lot. In the early democracy the polemarch remained the commander-in-chief of the Athenian army, and a polemarch — Callimachus — held the decisive vote before the Battle of Marathon in 490 BC and was killed in the fighting. After 487/6 BC, when the archons came to be chosen by lot, real military command passed to the strategoi (generals), and the polemarch's role became largely judicial: he presided over cases involving metics, foreigners and freedmen, and had responsibility for certain religious duties, including sacrifices to Artemis Agrotera and the funeral games for the war dead.

Other cities

In Sparta, a polemarch (polemarchos) was a senior army officer, commanding a mora, the largest division of the Spartan army, numbering several hundred men; six polemarchs advised the kings on campaign. In Thebes, polemarchs likewise combined military and civic functions, and in many smaller Greek states the polemarch remained a leading magistrate.
