The Rudolphine Tables (Latin: Tabulae Rudolphinae) consist of a star catalogue and planetary tables published by the German astronomer Johannes Kepler in 1627, using observational data collected by Tycho Brahe (1546–1601).

Background

Emperor Rudolf II had commissioned Brahe to produce new planetary tables, and Kepler inherited the task after Brahe's death in 1601. Unlike earlier tables based on circular models, Kepler computed positions using his own laws of planetary motion, with elliptical orbits and the Sun at one focus. The work was delayed for decades by disputes with Brahe's heirs and by the turmoil of the Thirty Years' War; Kepler also had to use logarithms, which he explained and tabulated in the work.

Contents

The tables contain positions for 1,005 stars from Brahe's catalogue and additional stars for a total of 1,440 entries, together with tables for computing the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets far more accurately than the preceding Alfonsine and Prutenic Tables.

Impact

The tables' accuracy provided decisive practical confirmation of the Copernican system as refined by Kepler. Their prediction of the transit of Mercury of 1631, observed by Pierre Gassendi, was a notable early success. They remained in use by astronomers for more than a century.
