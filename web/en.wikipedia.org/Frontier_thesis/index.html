Frontier thesis

The Frontier thesis, or Turner thesis, is the argument advanced by historian Frederick Jackson Turner in his 1893 essay "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" that the existence of a moving frontier of free land shaped American democracy, individualism, and national character. Turner delivered the paper at a meeting of the American Historical Association in Chicago during the World's Columbian Exposition.

Argument

Turner argued that the frontier experience — repeated settlement of the wilderness at the edge of "civilization" — stripped away European customs and generated distinctly American traits: practicality, restlessness, egalitarianism, and democratic institutions. He pointed to the 1890 United States census, which declared that a continuous frontier line could no longer be drawn, as marking the closing of the first period of American history.

Criticism and legacy

The thesis dominated American historiography for decades but has been heavily criticized since the mid-20th century: it largely ignored Native Americans, for whom the "frontier" was an invasion; understated the role of cities, immigration, and regions; and romanticized violence and conquest. "New Western" historians such as Patricia Nelson Limerick recast the West as a place of conquest and continuity rather than a closing frontier. The idea nevertheless remains influential in American culture and political rhetoric, from John F. Kennedy's "New Frontier" onward.
