Independent agencies of the United States government

Independent agencies of the United States federal government are agencies within the executive branch that Congress has structured to be partly insulated from direct presidential control. Unlike executive departments headed by secretaries serving at the President's pleasure, many independent agencies are governed by multimember commissions whose members serve fixed, staggered terms and can be removed only for cause.

Characteristics

Features commonly associated with independence include bipartisan membership requirements, fixed terms spanning presidential administrations, for-cause removal protections, and in some cases independent funding or litigation authority. The constitutional basis for removal protections was upheld in Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935) and litigated in later cases such as Seila Law v. CFPB (2020), which held that a single-director agency's removal protection was unconstitutional.

Examples

Well-known independent agencies include the Federal Reserve Board, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA), the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA), the U.S. Postal Service, Amtrak, and independent regulatory commissions created during the New Deal and Progressive eras. Some bodies, like the EPA and NASA, are "independent" in that they are not part of a cabinet department, though their administrators serve at the President's pleasure.
