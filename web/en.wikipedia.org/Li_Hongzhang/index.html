Li Hongzhang (15 February 1823 – 7 November 1901) was a Chinese statesman, general and diplomat of the late Qing dynasty. He quelled several major rebellions and served in important positions in the Qing imperial court, including the Viceroyalty of Zhili, Huguang and Liangguang, and became the most powerful and influential official in China for the last three decades of the 19th century. Foreign diplomats sometimes compared his influence to that of Otto von Bismarck in Germany.

Rise to power

Born into a scholar-official family in Anhui, Li passed the imperial examinations and entered the Hanlin Academy. During the Taiping Rebellion he served under Zeng Guofan, and in 1862 raised his own force, the Huai Army, which, equipped with Western weapons and assisted at times by foreign-led units such as the Ever Victorious Army, played a decisive role in defeating the Taiping and later the Nian Rebellion. These campaigns made him one of the dynasty's most powerful regional leaders.

Modernisation

As a leading advocate of the Self-Strengthening Movement, Li founded arsenals, shipyards, the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company, telegraph lines, a military academy, and the Beiyang Fleet — at its height the strongest navy in Asia. His projects were long hampered by court politics, corruption and the diversion of naval funds, and the fleet was destroyed by Japan in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), a defeat that badly damaged his reputation.

Diplomacy

Li negotiated many of the era's treaties on China's behalf, including the Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895) with Japan — during whose negotiation he survived an assassination attempt — and the Boxer Protocol of 1901, signed two months before his death. His 1896 world tour, during which he met Bismarck, Queen Victoria and President Cleveland, made him internationally famous; the giant noodle dish "chop suey" was popularly (if apocryphally) associated with his American visit.

Legacy

Assessments of Li remain divided: he has been praised as a pragmatic moderniser working within a decaying system and condemned as corrupt and as the signatory of humiliating "unequal treaties". He remains one of the most studied figures of late Qing history.
