Flight of the Earls

The Flight of the Earls (Irish: Imeacht na nIarlaí) took place on 14 September 1607, when Hugh O'Neill, Earl of Tyrone, and Rory O'Donnell, 1st Earl of Tyrconnell, and about ninety of their followers, set sail from Rathmullan, County Donegal, for Spanish Flanders. Their departure marked the end of the old Gaelic order in Ireland and paved the way for the Plantation of Ulster.

Background

Following the Irish defeat in the Nine Years' War (1594–1603), O'Neill and O'Donnell had been restored to much of their lands and titles under the Treaty of Mellifont (1603), but they chafed under English administration and feared arrest. O'Donnell in particular resented encroachments on his territory by the governor of Derry, Sir George Paulet. Both earls sought Spanish help for a renewed rising, but Spain, now at peace with England, offered none. In 1607 word reached them — possibly through a forged letter — that their arrest was imminent.

The flight

The earls, with their families and leading followers of the O'Neill and O'Donnell clans — about ninety-nine people in total — boarded a ship at Rathmullan on Lough Swilly and sailed for Spain. Bad weather forced them ashore in Normandy, France, whose king refused them passage to Spain; they travelled instead overland to the Spanish Netherlands and then to Italy. They reached Rome in April 1608, where Pope Paul V received them and granted them pensions. O'Donnell died in Rome in July 1608; O'Neill, increasingly blind and disappointed of Spanish aid, died there in 1616. Both are buried in the church of San Pietro in Montorio.

Consequences

The English government declared the earls traitors and confiscated their extensive lands in Ulster — some 500,000 acres across six counties. The forfeited territory was opened to systematic colonisation in the Plantation of Ulster, which from 1609 settled thousands of English and Scottish Protestants and transformed the province's demographics, with consequences that have shaped Irish history ever since.
