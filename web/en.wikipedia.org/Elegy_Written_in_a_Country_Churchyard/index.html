Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard is a poem by Thomas Gray, completed in 1750 and first published in 1751. It is considered one of the most famous poems in the English language. The poem's reflection — set at dusk in a rural churchyard — meditates on death, obscurity and the unfulfilled potential of the poor buried there, and ends with an epitaph for the poet himself.

Background

Gray may have begun the poem around 1742, after the death of his friend Richard West, and completed it at Stoke Poges, Buckinghamshire, in 1750. Learning that William Bentley planned an illustrated edition, Gray hurried it into print anonymously through Robert Dodsley in February 1751 to pre-empt a pirated publication in the Magazine of Magazines. Its immediate success made Gray famous, though he published little else.

Content

The speaker surveys the graves of the "rude forefathers of the hamlet" and contrasts their simple, hidden lives with the ambitions of the great:

The boast of heraldry, the pomp of pow'r,
And all that beauty, all that wealth e'er gave,
Awaits alike th' inevitable hour:
The paths of glory lead but to the grave.

Famous lines include "Far from the madding crowd's ignoble strife" — later the source of Thomas Hardy's novel title Far from the Madding Crowd — and speculation that the churchyard may hold "some heart once pregnant with celestial fire," a mute inglorious Milton or a Cromwell guiltless of his country's blood.

Influence

The Elegy founded the "graveyard school" mood in English poetry and was translated into many languages. Phrases from it entered common usage, and its stanza form — the "elegiac stanza" of iambic pentameter quatrains rhyming ABAB — became standard for reflective verse. Gray himself is buried in the Stoke Poges churchyard.
