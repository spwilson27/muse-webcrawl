Higher criticism

Higher criticism, also known as the historical-critical method, is a branch of literary and biblical criticism that investigates the origins, authorship and historical context of texts. In biblical studies it seeks to determine when and by whom the books of the Bible were written, what sources they used, and what their authors intended, employing historical and literary analysis rather than beginning from assumptions of divine authorship.

Distinctions and methods

Higher criticism is traditionally contrasted with lower criticism (textual criticism), which establishes the wording of the original text from surviving manuscripts. Higher-critical methods include source criticism, which identifies underlying documents such as the proposed sources of the documentary hypothesis for the Pentateuch, form criticism, which studies the genres and oral settings of passages, and redaction criticism, which examines how editors assembled and shaped their materials.

History and controversy

The method developed in German universities in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, associated with figures such as Johann Gottfried Eichhorn and David Friedrich Strauss. Its conclusions — for example that Moses did not write the first five books of the Bible — were controversial with traditional believers and remain debated between scholarly and confessional approaches.
