The mountain gorilla (Gorilla beringei beringei) is one of two subspecies of the eastern gorilla. It lives in two isolated populations: the Virunga Mountains, a chain of volcanoes on the borders of Rwanda, Uganda, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Uganda's Bwindi Impenetrable National Park. Renowned through the work of primatologist Dian Fossey, the mountain gorilla is one of conservation's rare success stories — intensive protection raised its numbers from about 620 in 1989 to roughly 1,063 in 2018, prompting the IUCN to move it from Critically Endangered to Endangered.

Description

Mountain gorillas have thicker, longer fur than other gorillas, an adaptation to cold high-altitude nights. Adult males ("silverbacks", named for the saddle of silver hair on the back) may stand 1.4–1.8 m tall and weigh 140–200 kg; females are roughly half that. They have powerful jaws and long arms, walk mainly on all fours (knuckle-walking), and are distinguished from lowland gorillas by a narrower skull, longer hair, and shorter arms. Almost entirely terrestrial and herbivorous, they feed on leaves, shoots, stems, fruit, and bamboo across more than a hundred plant species.

Social structure

Groups typically number 5–30 individuals, led by a dominant silverback who decides movements, mediates conflicts, and defends the group. Females give birth roughly every four years after an 8.5-month gestation, and infants are dependent for years. Groups make fresh nests of vegetation each night. Though generally gentle and shy, silverbacks will charge and fight to protect their families.

Conservation

Threats include habitat loss, poaching (chiefly snares set for other animals), human disease, and regional instability. Protection combines anti-poaching patrols, veterinary care (the "gorilla doctors"), community development, and tightly regulated tourism — expensive permits for one hour with habituated groups are a major source of revenue in Rwanda and Uganda. Fossey, who studied the Virunga gorillas from 1967 and founded the Karisoke Research Center, was murdered in 1985; her work, popularised by the film Gorillas in the Mist, created the global constituency that funds the gorillas' protection today.
