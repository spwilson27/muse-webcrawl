The Sorbs (Upper Sorbian: Serbja; Lower Sorbian: Serby; German: Sorben) are a West Slavic ethnic group indigenous to Lusatia, a region in eastern Germany spanning southeastern Brandenburg and eastern Saxony. They are Germany's smallest recognised native ethnic minority and speak two closely related languages, Upper Sorbian and Lower Sorbian, which enjoy official protection.

History

Slavic tribes settled the region between the Elbe and Oder during the migration period; in the Middle Ages the Sorbian lands were gradually absorbed into the Holy Roman Empire, and the Sorbs became an island of Slavic speech within a German-speaking sea. They survived centuries of Germanisation pressure as a largely peasant population. A national revival in the 19th century, led by figures such as Jan Arnošt Smoler, created a literary culture; the cultural organisation Domowina was founded in 1912. Under Nazi rule Sorbian organisations were banned and the language suppressed. In East Germany the Sorbs gained formal recognition and institutions, though open-cast lignite mining destroyed many Sorbian villages.

Culture

Sorbian identity is expressed in bilingual place-name signs, folk costumes (tracht), painted Easter eggs, and customs such as the Easter riders (Osterreiten) — processions of men on horseback announcing the Resurrection — the children's wedding (Vogelhochzeit), and Zampern carnival customs. The twin towns of Bautzen (Budyšin) and Cottbus (Chóśebuz) are cultural centres; the Sorbian Institute and the Wendish Museum preserve the heritage.

Language and status

Upper Sorbian is spoken around Bautzen by perhaps 20,000–25,000 people; Lower Sorbian, around Cottbus, is severely endangered with only a few thousand speakers, mostly elderly. Both are protected under the constitutions of Saxony and Brandenburg and the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages, with Sorbian schools, media and a parliamentary council for Sorbian affairs.
