The M1911 (designated Model 1911 before 1940) is a single-action, recoil-operated, semi-automatic pistol chambered for the .45 ACP cartridge. Designed by John Browning, it was the standard-issue sidearm for the United States Armed Forces from 1911 to 1985, and remains in limited service and extremely wide civilian, police, and competitive use.

History

The pistol emerged from US Army trials seeking a self-loading pistol to replace revolvers following experience in the Philippine–American War. Browning's Colt design won the 1911 trials, enduring a 6,000-round endurance test without malfunction. It served through both World Wars, Korea, and Vietnam. In 1924 it was modified as the M1911A1, with a shorter trigger, arched mainspring housing, and grip safety changes. The Beretta M9 replaced it as the standard sidearm in 1985, though special operations units continued using modernised 1911 variants.

Design

The M1911 uses Browning's short-recoil tilting-barrel system, a single-action trigger, both a grip safety and a manual thumb safety, and a seven-round single-column magazine. Its all-steel construction and powerful cartridge earned it a reputation for reliability and stopping power; it is also produced in other calibres, including 9mm and .38 Super, and in countless variants by dozens of manufacturers.

Legacy

More than two million were procured by the US government during its service life, and the design became one of the most copied and customised handguns in history, central to American gun culture and practical shooting sports.
