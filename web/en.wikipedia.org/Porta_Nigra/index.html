Porta Nigra

The Porta Nigra (Latin for "black gate") is a large Roman city gate in Trier, Germany. Built of grey sandstone around AD 170 as the northern gate of the Roman city of Augusta Treverorum, it is the largest Roman city gate north of the Alps and a symbol of Trier. Its name, recorded from the Middle Ages, refers to the darkened colour of its weathered stone. In 1986 it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the Roman Monuments of Trier.

History

The gate, about 36 metres wide and 30 metres high, formed part of a 6.4-kilometre city wall with four gates. Remarkably, its massive blocks — some weighing six tonnes — were held together by iron clamps rather than mortar; most of the clamps were later robbed out, leaving the building pocked with holes. In the eleventh century the Greek monk Simeon of Syracuse lived as a hermit in the eastern tower; after his canonisation the gate was converted into a double church, the Simeonstift, with apses added to both storeys — a transformation that preserved the structure from demolition.

Restoration

During the French occupation, Napoleon ordered the church additions removed in 1804, and nineteenth-century restoration returned the gate largely to its Roman appearance. Archaeological study of the masons' marks on its stones has helped date its construction. Today the Porta Nigra stands at the northern edge of Trier's old town and is one of Germany's best-known Roman monuments.
