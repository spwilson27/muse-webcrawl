General elections were held in Mexico on 2 July 2000. Voters elected a new President of Mexico to serve a single six-year term, all 128 members of the Senate, and all 500 members of the Chamber of Deputies. The elections were a watershed in Mexican history: Vicente Fox of the National Action Party (PAN), standing for the Alliance for Change, won the presidency, ending 71 years of uninterrupted rule by the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI) and its predecessors.

Background

The PRI had governed Mexico since 1929, and presidential successions had customarily been arranged by the outgoing president. A series of reforms in the 1990s, including the creation of the autonomous Federal Electoral Institute (IFE) in 1996, progressively levelled the playing field. President Ernesto Zedillo's PRI chose Francisco Labastida, a former interior minister, through Mexico's first-ever PRI primary. Fox, the charismatic former governor of Guanajuato and Coca-Cola executive, campaigned on the slogan of "change" (el cambio) and a promise to remove the PRI — "¡Ya!" ("Now!") — while Cuauhtémoc Cárdenas stood for a third time for the PRD-led Alliance for Mexico.

Results

Fox won about 42.5 percent of the presidential vote to Labastida's roughly 36.1 percent and Cárdenas's 16.6 percent. On election night Zedillo appeared on television to acknowledge Fox's victory, a gesture widely credited with ensuring a peaceful transfer of power. In Congress the Alliance for Change became the largest bloc in the Chamber of Deputies, though without an outright majority, inaugurating an era of divided government. Fox took office on 1 December 2000.

Significance

The election is generally regarded as the completion of Mexico's long democratic transition and the first fully competitive and transparent federal election of the modern era, administered by the IFE with extensive domestic and international observation.
