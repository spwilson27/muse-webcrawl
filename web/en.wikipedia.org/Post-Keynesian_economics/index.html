Post-Keynesian economics

Post-Keynesians develop what they regard as the core of Keynes's original insights.

Post-Keynesian economics is a school of economic thought with its origins in The General Theory of John Maynard Keynes, with subsequent development influenced by Joan Robinson, Nicholas Kaldor, Michał Kalecki, and Hyman Minsky, among others. Post-Keynesians regard themselves as continuing Keynes's project while rejecting parts of the neoclassical synthesis that they consider distortions of his work.

Main themes

  • Fundamental uncertainty: the future is not calculable as a probability distribution; expectations and conventions drive investment ("animal spirits").
  • Effective demand: output and employment are determined by aggregate demand in both the short and the long run; economies have no automatic tendency to full employment.
  • Endogenous money: loans create deposits; the money supply is driven by the demand for credit rather than controlled directly by the central bank.
  • Income distribution: prices are typically set by mark-ups over costs, and distribution between wages and profits is central to growth and inflation.
  • Historical time: economic processes unfold irreversibly in real time, in contrast to the logical time of equilibrium models.

History

The school emerged in Cambridge, England, in the 1950s around Joan Robinson and the contributors to the Cambridge capital controversies. The Journal of Post Keynesian Economics was founded in 1978. Post-Keynesian ideas influenced Minsky's financial instability hypothesis, which attracted renewed attention after the 2008 financial crisis.
