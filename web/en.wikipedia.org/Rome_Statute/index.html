Rome Statute

Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court
Signed17 July 1998, Rome, Italy
Entered into force1 July 2002
Parties125 states parties (as of 2025)
PurposeTreaty establishing the International Criminal Court (ICC)

The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court is the international treaty that established the International Criminal Court (ICC), the first permanent international court empowered to prosecute individuals for the gravest crimes of international concern. It was adopted at a United Nations diplomatic conference in Rome on 17 July 1998 and entered into force on 1 July 2002 after ratification by 60 states. The Statute takes its name from the city of Rome — not from any ancient Roman institution such as the Roman Forum.

Crimes within the Court's jurisdiction

The Statute gives the Court jurisdiction over four core crimes: genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, and the crime of aggression (added by amendments adopted at Kampala in 2010 and activated in 2018). It defines these crimes in detail, codifying much of customary international humanitarian law, and sets out principles such as individual criminal responsibility, the irrelevance of official position (heads of state enjoy no immunity), command responsibility, and the exclusion of the death penalty — the maximum sentence is life imprisonment.

Complementarity and operation

The ICC works on the principle of complementarity: it may act only when national courts are unwilling or unable genuinely to investigate or prosecute. Cases reach the Court through referral by a state party, referral by the UN Security Council, or the Prosecutor's own initiative authorised by judges. The Court sits in The Hague, Netherlands, and is governed by the Assembly of States Parties. By the mid-2020s it had opened investigations across Africa, Europe, Asia and Latin America and issued arrest warrants against sitting heads of state, including Sudan's Omar al-Bashir (2009) and Russia's Vladimir Putin (2023).

Non-parties and criticism

Several major powers — including the United States, Russia, China and India — are not parties; the United States signed in 2000 but announced in 2002 that it did not intend to ratify. The Court has been criticised for the slowness and cost of its trials, historically Africa-focused docket, and its dependence on states to arrest suspects; Burundi (2017) and the Philippines (2019) withdrew from the Statute.
