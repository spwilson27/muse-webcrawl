Osiris myth
Principal deitiesOsiris, Isis, Set, Horus, Nephthys
Main sourcePlutarch, De Iside et Osiride (c. AD 100), plus Egyptian texts
ThemesKingship, death and resurrection, order vs. chaos

The Osiris myth is the most influential story in ancient Egyptian mythology. It concerns the murder of the god-king Osiris by his brother Set, the efforts of his wife Isis to restore his body, and the posthumous conception and vengeance of their son Horus. The myth expresses Egyptian ideas about kingship, succession, death and the afterlife, and was retold in Egyptian texts for over two thousand years.

The story

Osiris, the eldest son of Geb (earth) and Nut (sky), ruled Egypt justly and taught humankind agriculture and law. His brother Set, jealous of his power, built a magnificent coffin fitted exactly to Osiris's body, tricked him into lying in it at a banquet, then sealed it and threw it into the Nile. The coffin came ashore at Byblos, where a tree grew around it.

Isis and the resurrection

Isis, aided by her sister Nephthys, searched for the body, recovered it, and — after Set found and dismembered it into fourteen pieces — gathered the scattered parts, reassembled them with the help of Anubis, and revived Osiris through her magic and the beating of her wings. Osiris thereby became the first mummy and ruler of the dead in the Duat, rather than returning to earthly life.

Horus and the contending

Isis conceived Horus by the restored Osiris and raised him in hiding in the Nile Delta. Grown, Horus challenged Set for the throne in a long series of contests and legal disputes before the gods — the "Contendings of Horus and Set" — and was finally vindicated as the rightful king. Every living pharaoh was identified with Horus, and every dead pharaoh with Osiris.

Significance

The myth underpinned Egyptian funerary belief: the deceased hoped to be justified and reborn like Osiris. It influenced later Mediterranean religions, and the fullest surviving narrative is the account by the Greek writer Plutarch.
