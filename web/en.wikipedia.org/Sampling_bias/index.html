Sampling bias

FieldStatistics, survey methodology, epidemiology
Also calledSelection bias (closely related)
Famous exampleLiterary Digest poll of 1936
Related conceptsSurvivorship bias, non-response bias, Berkson's paradox

In statistics, sampling bias is a bias in which a sample is collected in such a way that some members of the intended population have a lower or higher probability of being included than others. The result is a sample that does not represent the population, so conclusions drawn from it are systematically distorted. A biased sample may result from non-random selection, from a sampling frame that omits part of the population, or from self-selection by participants.

Types of sampling bias

  • Selection bias — the method of choosing participants favours some groups, as when volunteers differ systematically from non-volunteers.
  • Undercoverage — segments of the population are missing from the sampling frame, for example telephone surveys that reach only landline owners.
  • Non-response bias — those who decline to answer differ from those who respond.
  • Survivorship bias — only subjects that passed a selection process are observed; famously identified by Abraham Wald in his Second World War analysis of returning bomber aircraft, where armour belonged on the parts of planes that did not come back.
  • Volunteer and self-selection bias — in medicine and psychology, people who enrol in studies may be healthier or more motivated than the general population (the "healthy user" effect).
  • Berkson's paradox — sampling from a hospital population can create spurious associations between conditions that are unrelated in the general population.

Famous example: the 1936 Literary Digest poll

The best-known disaster caused by sampling bias is the Literary Digest poll of the 1936 US presidential election. The magazine mailed ten million ballots drawn from telephone directories and automobile registration lists and received some 2.4 million responses predicting a landslide for Alf Landon over Franklin D. Roosevelt. Roosevelt in fact won about 61% of the vote. During the Great Depression, telephone and car owners were wealthier than average and leaned Republican; the poll's frame and its voluntary response mechanism compounded the distortion. In the same election George Gallup, using a much smaller but properly designed sample, predicted the outcome correctly — and even predicted the Digest's error.

Bias in science and medicine

Sampling bias threatens clinical trials, observational epidemiology and genetics studies whenever the studied group differs from the target population — for instance, genome-wide association studies drawn overwhelmingly from people of European ancestry. In machine learning, biased training data can produce models that perform poorly for underrepresented groups. Remedies include probability sampling, stratification, weighting of responses, sensitivity analyses, and careful definition of the population to which results are meant to generalise.
