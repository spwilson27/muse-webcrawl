Neurofibromatosis type I

Neurofibromatosis type I (NF1), also known as von Recklinghausen disease, is a genetic disorder that causes tumours to grow on nerves, skin changes, and bone deformities. It affects about 1 in 3,000 people and is caused by mutations in the NF1 gene on chromosome 17, which encodes the tumour suppressor protein neurofibromin.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic features include multiple café-au-lait spots, freckling in the armpits and groin, benign skin neurofibromas, iris hamartomas called Lisch nodules, and optic pathway gliomas. Learning difficulties are common. Diagnosis is usually made clinically using established criteria, confirmed where needed by genetic testing. NF1 is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, though about half of cases arise from new mutations.

Management

There is no cure; care involves surveillance for complications such as plexiform neurofibromas, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours, and hypertension. The MEK inhibitor selumetinib was approved in 2020 for children with symptomatic, inoperable plexiform neurofibromas, the first drug approved for this condition.
