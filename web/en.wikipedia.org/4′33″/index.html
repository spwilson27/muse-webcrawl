4′33″

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

4′33″ (pronounced "four minutes, thirty-three seconds" or just "four thirty-three") is a three-movement composition by American experimental composer John Cage (1912–1992). Composed in 1952, it instructs performers not to play their instruments for the entire duration of the piece; the "music" consists of the ambient sounds of the environment that the audience hears during the performance. It is often described as four minutes thirty-three seconds of silence, though Cage intended it as a frame for listening rather than literal silence.

Premiere

The work was premiered by pianist David Tudor at the Maverick Concert Hall in Woodstock, New York, on 29 August 1952. Tudor signalled the beginning and end of each movement by closing and opening the piano lid, timing the piece with a stopwatch. The audience, expecting conventional music, grew restless; some walked out. Cage later recalled that listeners heard the wind stirring outside in the first movement, raindrops pattering on the roof in the second, and the sounds of the audience itself in the third.

Conception

Cage's thinking was influenced by his experience in an anechoic chamber at Harvard University, where he heard two sounds—his nervous system and his blood circulation—leading him to conclude that true silence does not exist. He was also inspired by Robert Rauschenberg's White Paintings of 1951, which he described as "airports for lights, shadows and particles." The score, published by Edition Peters in 1960, indicates the three movements last 30″, 2′23″, and 1′40″ respectively, totalling 4′33″.

Reception and legacy

4′33″ became Cage's most famous and controversial work, challenging fundamental assumptions about music and listening. It has been performed by orchestras and soloists worldwide, recorded many times, and in 2004 was broadcast by BBC Radio 3 in a performance by the BBC Symphony Orchestra. In 2010 a campaign took a recording of the piece to number 21 on the UK Singles Chart at Christmas.
