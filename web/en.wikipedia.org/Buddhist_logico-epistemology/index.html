Buddhist logico-epistemology

Buddhist logico-epistemology refers to a tradition of Buddhist scholarship centred on logic (hetuvidyā) and epistemology — the theory of pramāṇa, the valid means of knowledge. Its foundational figures are the Indian masters Dignāga (c. 480–540 CE) and Dharmakīrti (c. 7th century CE).

Foundations

Dignāga's Pramāṇasamuccaya ("Compendium of the Means of Knowledge") argued that there are only two pramāṇas: perception (pratyakṣa), which apprehends the unique, ineffable particular, and inference (anumāna), which operates with concepts and generalities. He defined perception as free from conceptual construction, and formulated the apoha (exclusion) theory of meaning, according to which words refer by excluding what is other.

Dharmakīrti

Dharmakīrti's Pramāṇavārttika and related works systematised the tradition: the three marks of a valid inferential reason (trairūpya), a causal theory of reference grounded in the capacity to fulfil a goal (arthakriyā), and a graded realism about universals. His definition of a pramāṇa as "non-belying cognition" tied knowledge to successful action.

Later developments

Indian successors such as Dharmottara, Jñānaśrīmitra and Ratnakīrti debated the nature of exclusion and momentariness, while the school engaged rival Hindu traditions, especially Nyāya. Transmitted to Tibet, the tradition became the backbone of monastic education as the subject of tshad ma, studied through debate at centres such as Nalanda's heirs in the Gelug curriculum, and in the distinct Sakya tradition of Sa skya Paṇḍita.

Influence

Buddhist logico-epistemology shaped Tibetan and Mongolian scholasticism for a millennium, and modern scholars regard it as one of the most sophisticated pre-modern theories of language, inference and knowledge.
