Fiber to the premises

Fiber to the premises (FTTP) is a form of fiber-optic communication delivery in which optical fiber is run from a central office directly to individual buildings such as residences, apartment buildings, and businesses, providing high-speed Internet access. Related architectures include fiber to the home (FTTH), fiber to the building (FTTB), fiber to the curb (FTTC), and fiber to the node (FTTN), which differ in how close the fiber termination comes to the end user; in the "last mile" the FTTC/FTTN variants complete the connection with copper wiring such as DSL.

Technology

Most FTTP deployments use a passive optical network (PON): a single fiber from an optical line terminal at the provider's facility is split passively among up to 32 or 64 subscribers, each with an optical network terminal at the premises. Standards such as GPON (2.5 Gbit/s downstream) and XGS-PON (symmetric 10 Gbit/s) define the protocols; point-to-point Ethernet over dedicated fiber is an alternative used by some providers. Because light signals attenuate far less than electrical signals in copper, fiber can support much greater bandwidth over longer distances, and can be upgraded by replacing the endpoint electronics alone.

Deployment

Fiber-to-the-home networks are extensively deployed in countries such as South Korea, Japan, Spain, and the United Arab Emirates, and have expanded rapidly in the United States and Europe since the 2010s, driven by providers such as Google Fiber and by municipal and cooperative networks. High installation costs — especially for burying or stringing new cable to every home — remain the principal barrier, and rollout in rural areas often depends on public subsidy programmes.
