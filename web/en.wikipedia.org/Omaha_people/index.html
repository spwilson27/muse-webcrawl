The Omaha are a federally recognized Native American tribe, the Omaha Tribe of Nebraska, residing on the Omaha Reservation in northeastern Nebraska, with land extending into Iowa. Their name in their own language, Umoⁿhoⁿ, means "dwellers on the bluff" or "those going against the wind or current." The Omaha speak a Dhegiha Siouan language closely related to that of the Ponca, from whom they separated in the seventeenth century.

History

The Omaha migrated westward from the Ohio Valley region over several centuries, eventually settling along the Missouri River in present-day Nebraska. By the late eighteenth century they were a powerful trading nation under leaders such as Chief Blackbird. Contact with Europeans brought trade goods but also devastating smallpox epidemics. In 1854 the Omaha ceded most of their land to the United States, retaining a reservation in what is now Thurston County, Nebraska. Unlike many neighboring peoples, they were never removed from their homeland.

Culture

Traditional Omaha society was organized into clans grouped in two moieties representing earth and sky. The tribe combined farming — corn, beans, and squash — with seasonal buffalo hunts. Sacred objects such as the Sacred Pole (Umoⁿ'hoⁿ'ti) were central to tribal ceremony. The annual powwow, held at Macy, Nebraska, remains an important gathering.

Notable people

Notable Omaha include Standing Bear's contemporary leaders Big Elk and Joseph La Flesche, physician Susan La Flesche Picotte — the first Native American woman to earn a medical degree — and her sister, author and activist Susette La Flesche.
