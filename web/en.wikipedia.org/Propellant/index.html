Propellant

A propellant (or propellent) is a mass that is expelled or expanded in such a way as to create a thrust or another motive force in accordance with Newton's third law of motion, and to propel a vehicle or projectile. In vehicles, the engine that expels the propellant is called a reaction engine. Propellants are also used in firearms, aerosol sprays, and airbags.

Rocket propellants

Rocket propellants are classified as liquid, solid, or hybrid. Liquid propellants consist of a fuel and an oxidizer stored separately — common combinations include liquid oxygen with kerosene (RP-1) or liquid hydrogen, and hypergolic pairs such as nitrogen tetroxide and hydrazine derivatives that ignite on contact. Solid propellants combine fuel and oxidizer in a single grain, such as ammonium perchlorate composite propellant (APCP) used in large boosters, or double-base nitrocellulose/nitroglycerin compositions. Performance is measured by specific impulse.

Gun propellants

In firearms and artillery, a propellant is a low explosive — historically black powder (gunpowder), and since the late 19th century smokeless powders based on nitrocellulose, either single-base or double-base with nitroglycerin. The propellant deflagrates rather than detonates, generating gas that pushes the projectile down the barrel.

Other uses

Aerosol cans use liquefied or compressed gas propellants, once commonly chlorofluorocarbons and now typically hydrocarbons or hydrofluoroalkanes. Automotive airbags use gas generants such as sodium azide formulations. Cold gas thrusters use inert compressed gas as propellant for spacecraft attitude control.
