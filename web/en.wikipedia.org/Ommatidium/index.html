An ommatidium (plural: ommatidia) is one of the individual units that make up the compound eyes of arthropods, such as insects, crustaceans, and millipedes. Each ommatidium is a self-contained photoreceptive unit pointing in a slightly different direction, and the brain assembles their signals into a mosaic image. Depending on the species, a compound eye may contain anywhere from a few dozen to tens of thousands of ommatidia — dragonflies have up to about 28,000 per eye.

Structure

Each ommatidium consists of a corneal lens (facet), beneath which lies a crystalline cone that focuses light onto the rhabdom — a rod of light-sensitive microvilli formed by the retinula (photoreceptor) cells, typically seven or eight in insects. Pigment cells surround the unit and absorb stray light, optically isolating it from its neighbors. The axons of the retinula cells carry signals to the optic lobe of the brain.

Types of compound eye

In apposition eyes, typical of day-active insects, each ommatidium is fully isolated and samples a single point of light, yielding sharp images in bright conditions. In superposition eyes, found in many nocturnal insects and deep-water crustaceans, light from many facets is combined onto single rhabdoms, greatly increasing sensitivity at the cost of acuity.

Function

Because each ommatidium samples one point in space, image resolution is limited by facet size, but compound eyes offer a very wide field of view, high temporal resolution (fast flicker-fusion rates useful for detecting motion), and in many species sensitivity to ultraviolet light and to the polarization of light, which aids navigation.
