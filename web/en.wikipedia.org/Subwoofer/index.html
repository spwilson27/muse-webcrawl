Subwoofer

A subwoofer (or sub) is a loudspeaker designed to reproduce low-pitched audio frequencies known as bass and sub-bass, lower in frequency than those which can be (optimally) generated by a woofer. The typical frequency range for a consumer subwoofer is about 20–200 Hz, while professional live-sound subwoofers can go below 100 Hz and THX-certified systems extend to 20 Hz.

Design

Subwoofers use one or more large drivers, commonly 8 to 21 inches in diameter, mounted in enclosures of ported (bass-reflex), sealed, bandpass or horn-loaded design. Most consumer units are active (powered) subwoofers with a built-in amplifier matched to the driver, plus a low-pass filter so only the lowest frequencies are sent to the subwoofer while the main ("satellite") speakers handle the rest.

Applications

Subwoofers are the ".1" low-frequency effects (LFE) channel in Dolby Digital and DTS surround systems and are central to home theater, car audio, cinema sound, and concert sound reinforcement. The first subwoofer was developed in the 1960s by Arnold Nudell and Cary Christie (the Infinity Servo Statik 1); Ken Kreisel's work for film sound in the 1970s helped popularize separate subwoofers in cinemas.

Perception

At very low frequencies sound is felt as much as heard, and localization is weak, which is why a single subwoofer can serve a whole system — though multiple subwoofers can even out room modes.
