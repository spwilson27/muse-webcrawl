Elbridge Gerry

Elbridge Thomas Gerry (July 17, 1744 – November 23, 1814) was an American politician, diplomat, and merchant. He was a signer of the Declaration of Independence and the Articles of Confederation, and served as the fifth vice president of the United States under James Madison from 1813 until his death in 1814.

Gerry served as Governor of Massachusetts from 1810 to 1812. He is best remembered for the redistricting process known as gerrymandering, named after a salamander-shaped district approved during his governorship in 1812, although Gerry himself reportedly disliked the practice.

Early life

Gerry was born in Marblehead, Massachusetts, the third of twelve children. He graduated from Harvard College in 1762 and joined his father's merchant business, trading dried cod to the West Indies and Spain.

Political career

Elected to the provincial assembly in 1772, Gerry became active in the resistance to British taxation. He served in the Continental Congress, where he signed the Declaration of Independence and later the Articles of Confederation. At the Constitutional Convention of 1787 he refused to sign the United States Constitution, citing the lack of a bill of rights.

He served in the U.S. House of Representatives from 1789 to 1793, was a member of the XYZ Affair diplomatic mission to France, and later became vice president.
