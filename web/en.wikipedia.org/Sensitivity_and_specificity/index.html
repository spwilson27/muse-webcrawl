Sensitivity and specificity

Sensitivity and specificity are statistical measures of the performance of a binary classification test, widely used in medicine to characterise diagnostic tests.

Sensitivity (also called the true positive rate) measures the proportion of actual positives correctly identified: sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN). Specificity (the true negative rate) measures the proportion of actual negatives correctly identified: specificity = TN / (TN + FP), where TP, TN, FP and FN are true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives.

Definitions

A test with high sensitivity rarely misses people who have the condition, so it is useful for ruling a condition out when the result is negative. A test with high specificity rarely mislabels people without the condition, so a positive result is strong evidence for ruling the condition in. The mnemonics "SnNout" and "SpPin" summarise these roles.

Trade-offs

For tests based on a continuous measurement, moving the diagnostic threshold trades sensitivity against specificity. The relationship across all thresholds is summarised by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the curve measures overall discriminative ability.

Related measures

Predictive values — the positive and negative predictive values — depend not only on sensitivity and specificity but also on the prevalence of the condition in the tested population. Likelihood ratios combine sensitivity and specificity into a single measure of how much a test result changes the odds of disease.
