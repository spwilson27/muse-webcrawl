Greek tragedy

Greek tragedy is a form of theatre from Ancient Greece and Greek-inhabited Anatolia. It reached its most significant form in Athens in the 5th century BC, the works of which are sometimes called Attic tragedy. Greek tragedy is widely considered an extension of the ancient rites carried out in honour of Dionysus, and it heavily influenced the theatre of ancient Rome and the Renaissance. Tragic plots were most often based upon myths from the oral traditions of archaic epics.

Origins and etymology

The word tragedy derives from the Greek tragōidia — "goat song" — from tragos (goat) and ōidē (song), perhaps referring to a goat sacrificed or awarded as a prize at the Dionysian festivals. Aristotle wrote in his Poetics that tragedy developed from the improvisations of the leaders of the dithyramb, the choral hymn to Dionysus. Thespis is traditionally credited, around 534 BC, with introducing the first actor (hypokrites) distinct from the chorus.

Structure

A tragedy typically began with a prologue spoken before the chorus entered; then the parodos, the chorus's entrance song; a series of episodes of dialogue separated by choral odes (stasima); and the exodos, the concluding scene. At the City Dionysia each tragedian presented a tetralogy — three tragedies plus a satyr play. Aristotle identified six elements of tragedy: plot, character, thought, diction, melody and spectacle, with plot pre-eminent; he described the ideal tragic hero as falling through hamartia (an error or flaw), and the effect of tragedy as catharsis — the purging of pity and fear.

The tragedians

Of the hundreds of tragedies performed, only 32 complete plays survive: seven each by Aeschylus and Sophocles, and eighteen by Euripides (including the disputed Rhesus). Aeschylus added the second actor and reduced the chorus's role; his Oresteia (458 BC) is the only surviving trilogy. Sophocles added the third actor and scene-painting, and wrote Oedipus Rex, Aristotle's model tragedy, and Antigone. Euripides, the most modern-seeming, questioned traditional myths and gods in plays such as Medea and The Bacchae.

Themes and legacy

Tragedies examined fate and free will, hubris, the conflict between family and state, the suffering of war, and the limits of human knowledge. Through Roman adaptations, Seneca, and Renaissance rediscovery, Greek tragedy shaped Shakespearean drama, French classical tragedy (Racine), opera, and modern works from O'Neill to Anouilh.
