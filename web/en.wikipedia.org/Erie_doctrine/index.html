Erie doctrine

The Erie doctrine is a fundamental legal doctrine of civil procedure in the United States that mandates that a federal court applying state law must apply state law as the state courts would apply it. In other words, when deciding a claim under state law, federal judges sitting in diversity jurisdiction must follow state substantive law and the state courts' interpretations of that law, while continuing to apply federal procedural law.

Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins

The doctrine arises from Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 58 (1938). The Court held that the Rules of Decision Act of 1789 requires federal courts to apply state "laws," and overruled Swift v. Tyson (1842), which had allowed federal courts to create a federal "general common law" in diversity cases. Justice Brandeis, writing for the majority, declared that "there is no federal general common law," grounding the holding in constitutional concerns about federalism and in the practical forum-shopping problem Swift had produced.

Substance versus procedure

The boundary between substance (state law) and procedure (federal law) has been refined by later cases. Under Guaranty Trust Co. v. York (1945), the question is whether applying federal rather than state law would significantly affect the outcome ("outcome-determinative test"). Byrd v. Blue Ridge (1958) balanced countervailing federal interests, and Hanna v. Plumer (1965) clarified that the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, valid under the Rules Enabling Act, displace conflicting state rules. The Rules Enabling Act's limitation that federal rules "shall not abridge, enlarge or modify any substantive right" remains the subject of debate.
