Mesoamerica

Region: Central Mexico to Costa Rica
Period: c. 1500 BC – AD 1521
Cultures: Olmec, Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Toltec, Aztec (Mexica)

Mesoamerica is a historical region and cultural area in North America extending from central Mexico through Belize, Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and northern Costa Rica. It was home to some of the most complex societies of the pre-Columbian Americas, united by shared traits including maize agriculture, the Mesoamerican ballgame, stepped pyramids, the 260-day ritual calendar, and vigesimal (base-20) mathematics.

Defining traits

Anthropologist Paul Kirchhoff coined the term in 1943, listing shared features: cultivation of maize, beans, squash, and chili peppers; use of the chinampa and terrace agriculture; markets and long-distance trade in obsidian, jade, cacao, and quetzal feathers; human sacrifice; and a pantheon of deities including a feathered serpent.

Chronology

The Formative (Preclassic) period saw the Olmec, often called the "mother culture," flourish from about 1500 BC. The Classic period (c. AD 250–900) featured Teotihuacan, the Maya city-states, and Monte Albán. The Postclassic (c. 900–1521) saw the Toltecs, the Mixtec kingdoms, and finally the Aztec Empire, which fell to Hernán Cortés and his indigenous allies in 1521.

Languages and writing

Mesoamerica is one of the few regions where writing developed independently. Scripts include Maya hieroglyphs (fully phonetic and logosyllabic), Zapotec, Mixtec pictorial codices, and Aztec glyphs. Major language families include Mayan, Oto-Manguean, Uto-Aztecan, and Mixe-Zoquean.
