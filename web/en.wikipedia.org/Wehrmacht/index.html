The Wehrmacht (German for "defence force") was the unified armed forces of Nazi Germany from 1935 to 1945. It consisted of the Heer (army), the Kriegsmarine (navy) and the Luftwaffe (air force), under the Oberkommando der Wehrmacht (OKW), created in 1938 after Adolf Hitler abolished the War Ministry and assumed direct command. The Wehrmacht replaced the Reichswehr, the smaller force permitted to Germany under the Treaty of Versailles, after Hitler publicly announced rearmament and conscription in March 1935.

Second World War

The Wehrmacht pioneered the combined-arms tactics associated with Blitzkrieg ("lightning war"), achieving rapid victories in Poland (1939), Denmark and Norway, the Low Countries and France (1940), Yugoslavia and Greece (1941). In June 1941 it launched Operation Barbarossa, the invasion of the Soviet Union — the largest military operation in history — which ultimately consumed the bulk of its strength. Defeats at Stalingrad (1942–43) and Kursk (1943) turned the tide in the East, while Allied landings in Normandy (1944) opened the decisive western front. Germany surrendered unconditionally in May 1945, and the Wehrmacht was formally dissolved by the Allies in 1946.

War crimes

Long portrayed in the post-war "myth of the clean Wehrmacht" as apolitical and uninvolved in Nazi crimes, the armed forces are now understood by historians to have been deeply complicit. The Wehrmacht cooperated with the SS Einsatzgruppen in the mass murder of Jews and others behind the Eastern Front, issued and implemented criminal orders such as the Commissar Order and the Barbarossa decree, waged a war of annihilation in the East, and was responsible for the deaths of millions of Soviet prisoners of war through starvation and neglect, as well as reprisal massacres of civilians across occupied Europe. Exhibitions and scholarship since the 1990s have decisively dismantled the exculpatory myth.

Personnel and legacy

More than 18 million men served in the Wehrmacht; roughly 5 million German military personnel died in the war. Senior commanders were tried at Nuremberg, where the OKW and General Staff were not declared criminal organisations as such, though many individuals were convicted. In 1955 West Germany founded a new armed force, the Bundeswehr, deliberately breaking in tradition and name from the Wehrmacht.
