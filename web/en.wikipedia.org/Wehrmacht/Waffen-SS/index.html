Waffen-SS

Active: 17 March 1933 – 8 May 1945
Country: Nazi Germany
Allegiance: Adolf Hitler, Nazi Party
Type: Armoured, cavalry, infantry, artillery
Size: Up to 900,000 men

The Waffen-SS was the armed wing of the Nazi Party's SS organization.

The Waffen-SS (German: [ˈvafn̩ʔɛsˌʔɛs]; lit. 'Armed SS') was the combat branch of the Nazi Party's paramilitary Schutzstaffel (SS) organisation. Its formations included men from Nazi Germany, along with volunteers and conscripts from both German-occupied Europe and unoccupied lands. With the start of the Second World War, tactical control was exercised by the Oberkommando der Wehrmacht (High Command of the Armed Forces), though in reality strategic control was exercised by the SS itself, which answered only to Adolf Hitler.

The Waffen-SS grew from three regiments to over 38 divisions during World War II. Though never fully integrated into the Wehrmacht, its units served alongside the army (Heer), navy (Kriegsmarine), and air force (Luftwaffe) on all fronts. Waffen-SS formations were deeply implicated in war crimes and crimes against humanity, including massacres of prisoners and civilians such as those at Malmedy, Oradour-sur-Glane, and Le Paradis.

Origins

The Waffen-SS originated in 1933 as an armed special unit of the SS, drawing on the Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler, the SS-Verfügungstruppe, and the Totenkopfverbände that guarded the concentration camps. Under Reichsführer-SS Heinrich Himmler it expanded rapidly after 1939, increasingly recruiting ethnic Germans and foreign volunteers and, later in the war, conscripts.

Aftermath

At the Nuremberg trials, the SS — including the Waffen-SS — was declared a criminal organization for its direct involvement in war crimes and in the Holocaust, though conscripts who had no choice and committed no crimes were excepted. Veterans' organizations such as HIAG later campaigned, controversially, to portray the Waffen-SS as ordinary soldiers.
