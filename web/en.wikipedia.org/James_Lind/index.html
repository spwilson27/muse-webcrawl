James Lind (4 October 1716 – 13 July 1794) was a Scottish physician. A pioneer of naval hygiene in the Royal Navy, Lind is best known for conducting one of the first clinical trials, which demonstrated that citrus fruit could cure scurvy, and for arguing for better ventilation, cleanliness, and the hygiene of sailors aboard ships.

Early life and naval career

Lind was born in Edinburgh and educated at the Royal High School. He was apprenticed to a surgeon, George Langlands, before joining the Royal Navy in 1739 as a surgeon's mate. He served in the Mediterranean, off West Africa, and in the West Indies, becoming a full surgeon in 1747.

The scurvy experiment

While serving aboard HMS Salisbury in the Channel Fleet in 1747, Lind conducted his famous experiment. He took twelve sailors suffering from scurvy, divided them into six pairs, and gave each pair a different treatment: cider, diluted sulphuric acid (elixir of vitriol), vinegar, seawater, a paste of garlic and mustard, or two oranges and one lemon. Those on citrus fruit recovered rapidly — one was fit for duty after six days — while the others improved little. Lind published the results in his Treatise of the Scurvy (1753).

Despite the evidence, Lind's conclusions were muddied by his adherence to humoral theory, and the Admiralty did not adopt citrus juice as a standard issue until 1795, after advocacy by Gilbert Blane — after which scurvy was virtually eliminated from the Royal Navy, and British sailors acquired the nickname "limeys" from the lime juice later substituted.

Later work

Lind obtained his MD from the University of Edinburgh in 1748 and was elected a fellow of the Royal Society in 1765. From 1758 until 1783 he was chief physician at the Royal Naval Hospital Haslar at Gosport, then the largest naval hospital in the world. He wrote influential works on diseases of Europeans in hot climates (1768) and on preserving the health of seamen, advocating typhus control by delousing, uniforms, distillation of seawater, and hospital ships.

Legacy

Lind's 1747 trial is often cited as one of the first controlled clinical trials in medicine. The James Lind Library and the James Lind Alliance commemorate his name in modern evidence-based medicine.
