Stellar nucleosynthesis

An "onion-skin" cross-section of an evolved massive star, with successive fusion shells around an iron core.

Stellar nucleosynthesis is the creation of chemical elements by nuclear fusion reactions within stars. It is the process by which the light elements produced in the Big Bang — mainly hydrogen and helium — are transformed into heavier elements, supplying the carbon, oxygen, iron and other nuclei from which planets and life are made.

Hydrogen and helium burning

Main-sequence stars generate energy by fusing hydrogen into helium, either through the proton–proton chain, dominant in stars like the Sun, or the CNO cycle, dominant in hotter, more massive stars. When core hydrogen is exhausted, the core contracts and heats until helium fusion begins: the triple-alpha process converts helium into carbon, and capture of a further alpha particle yields oxygen.

Advanced burning stages

In massive stars, rising core temperatures ignite a sequence of stages — carbon burning, neon burning, oxygen burning and silicon burning — producing successively heavier nuclei in concentric shells. Silicon burning produces iron-group elements; iron is the endpoint of energy-releasing fusion because fusing iron consumes rather than releases energy.

Elements beyond iron

Nuclei heavier than iron are built mainly by neutron capture: the slow s-process in asymptotic giant branch stars, and the rapid r-process in explosive environments such as supernovae and neutron star mergers. When a massive star's iron core collapses, the resulting supernova disperses its enriched layers into the interstellar medium, seeding later generations of stars and planets.

History

The theory was established by Fred Hoyle and by the landmark 1957 paper of Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler and Hoyle (the B²FH paper), which mapped the synthesis of the elements in stars. William Fowler received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1983 for this work.
