Hindu calendar

The Hindu calendar is a collective term for the various lunisolar calendars traditionally used in the Indian subcontinent. Rather than a single system, it is a family of regional calendars that share a common astronomical framework — luni-solar months, intercalary months, and the five-limbed almanac called the panchanga — while differing in the epoch from which years are counted, the month in which the year begins, and whether months begin at the new moon (amanta) or the full moon (purnimanta).

Structure

The calendar tracks both the Moon and the Sun. A lunar month of 29–30 days is divided into tithis (lunar days), grouped into a bright fortnight (shukla paksha) ending at the full moon and a dark fortnight (krishna paksha) ending at the new moon. Twelve lunar months total about 354 days, roughly 11 days short of the solar year, so an intercalary month, adhika masa, is inserted about every 32–33 months to keep festivals aligned with the seasons. Occasionally a month is omitted (kshaya masa), a rare event compensated by two intercalary months nearby.

The panchanga's five elements are the tithi, the vara (weekday), the nakshatra (lunar mansion in which the Moon stands), the yoga (a division based on the combined motion of Sun and Moon) and the karana (half-tithi). Almanacs publish these for every day, guiding the timing of festivals, weddings and religious observances.

Eras and regional variants

Years are commonly counted in the Vikrama Samvat (beginning 57 BCE), dominant in northern India and Nepal, or the Shaka era (beginning 78 CE), used in the Indian national calendar adopted in 1957, which fixed the month lengths to the solar calendar while retaining Shaka years. Regional calendars include the Bengali, Tamil, Malayalam (Kollam era), Telugu, Kannada, Marathi and Odia calendars, each with its own new-year day — variously in mid-April (solar new year) or in the month of Chaitra (lunar new year, as at Ugadi and Gudi Padwa).

Festivals

Major festivals are dated by the lunar calendar: Diwali falls on the new moon of Kartika, Holi on the full moon of Phalguna, and Rama Navami on the ninth tithi of Chaitra, while solar festivals such as Pongal and Makar Sankranti are fixed by the Sun's entry into Capricorn in mid-January.
