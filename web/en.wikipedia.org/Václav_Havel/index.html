Václav Havel (5 October 1936 – 18 December 2011) was a Czech playwright, essayist, and statesman who served as the last president of Czechoslovakia from 1989 to 1992 and the first president of the Czech Republic from 1993 to 2003. A leading figure of the dissident movement under communist rule, he played a central role in the Velvet Revolution that ended one-party rule in Czechoslovakia.

Playwright and dissident

Born into a wealthy Prague family whose property was confiscated after the communist coup of 1948, Havel was barred from formal higher education and worked as a stagehand while writing plays. His absurdist dramas — including The Garden Party (1963) and The Memorandum (1965) — won international acclaim. After the Warsaw Pact invasion crushed the Prague Spring in 1968, his works were banned and he became a prominent dissident.

Havel co-founded the Charter 77 human-rights initiative in 1977 and wrote the influential essay The Power of the Powerless (1978), which analysed life under totalitarianism and urged citizens to "live in truth". He spent roughly five years in prison for his activities.

Presidency

In November 1989, as mass protests swept Prague, Havel co-founded the Civic Forum movement and became the face of the Velvet Revolution. On 29 December 1989 he was elected president of Czechoslovakia. He oversaw the transition to democracy and a market economy, apologised for the post-war expulsion of Sudeten Germans, and presided over the peaceful dissolution of Czechoslovakia in 1993, becoming the first president of the independent Czech Republic. During his tenure the country joined NATO (1999) and moved toward European Union membership.

Later life and legacy

After leaving office in 2003, Havel continued writing — including the play Leaving (2007) and a film adaptation — and campaigned for human rights worldwide. He died at his country home in Hrádeček in December 2011. Prague's airport was renamed Václav Havel Airport in his honour in 2012.
