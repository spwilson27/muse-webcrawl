The Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia, officially known in the Soviet Union as Operation Danube, was a joint invasion of the Czechoslovak Socialist Republic by the Soviet Union and four other Warsaw Pact countries — Poland, Bulgaria, Hungary, and East Germany — on the night of 20–21 August 1968. Approximately 250,000 Warsaw Pact troops and 2,000 tanks entered the country initially, with numbers rising to about 500,000.

Background: the Prague Spring

In January 1968, Alexander Dubček became First Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia and began a programme of liberalization known as the Prague Spring, promising "socialism with a human face": censorship was relaxed, political discussion opened, and economic reforms were planned. The Soviet leadership under Leonid Brezhnev feared the reforms would undermine communist rule across the Eastern Bloc.

The invasion

After negotiations at Čierna nad Tisou and Bratislava failed to satisfy Moscow, Warsaw Pact forces invaded on the night of 20 August 1968. Czechoslovak forces were ordered not to resist. Dubček and other leaders were arrested and taken to Moscow, where they were compelled to sign the Moscow Protocol accepting the reversal of reforms. At least 137 Czechoslovak civilians were killed in the invasion and its immediate aftermath.

Aftermath

In April 1969 Dubček was replaced by Gustáv Husák, who began the policy of "normalization," purging reformists and restoring strict party control. In January 1969 the student Jan Palach set himself on fire in Prague in protest. The invasion was justified by the Brezhnev Doctrine, which asserted the right of the Soviet Union to intervene when socialism was threatened in a socialist country. Soviet troops remained stationed in Czechoslovakia until 1991. The reforms' suppression lasted until the Velvet Revolution of 1989.
