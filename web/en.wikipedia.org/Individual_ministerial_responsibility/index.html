Individual ministerial responsibility

Individual ministerial responsibility is a constitutional convention in governments using the Westminster system, under which a cabinet minister bears personal responsibility for the actions, successes, and failures of their ministry or department, and is accountable to the legislature for them. It is distinct from collective ministerial responsibility, under which the whole cabinet must publicly support government decisions or resign.

The convention

In its classic form, the convention holds that a minister should resign when serious errors occur in their department, even if the minister was not personally at fault. The doctrine expressed in the Crichel Down affair of 1954 — in which the British agriculture minister Sir Thomas Dugdale resigned over maladministration by civil servants — is often cited as its high-water mark. In practice, modern interpretation distinguishes between accountability (the duty to explain and answer to Parliament, which is absolute) and responsibility in the sense of personal blame, which depends on the minister's own conduct, knowledge, and failings.

Grounds for resignation

Ministers have resigned for personal misconduct, misleading Parliament, breaches of the ministerial code, and major policy or administrative failures in their departments. Examples include resignations over personal scandals and security breaches. Scholars note that the convention has weakened: ministers increasingly survive departmental failures by blaming officials, a tension sometimes called the problem of "responsibility without control", since permanent civil servants actually administer departments.

In other systems

Analogous conventions apply in other Westminster-derived systems such as Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, and similar principles of personal political accountability for departments exist in many parliamentary democracies, though the precise triggers for resignation vary by country and era.
