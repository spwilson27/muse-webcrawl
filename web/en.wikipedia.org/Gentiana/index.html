Gentiana is a large genus of flowering plants in the gentian family (Gentianaceae), comprising roughly 300 to 400 species. Gentians are noted for their showy, usually deep-blue trumpet-shaped flowers, and are found in temperate regions of Asia, Europe, and the Americas, often in alpine habitats. The genus is named after Gentius, a king of Illyria in the 2nd century BC, who is said to have discovered the medicinal value of the plants' roots.

Description

Most gentians are herbaceous perennials, ranging from small alpine mats a few centimetres tall to plants over a metre high. The flowers are typically tubular or bell-shaped with five lobes, and blue is the most characteristic colour, though white, yellow, and red species exist. Leaves are usually opposite and untoothed.

Uses

The roots of the great yellow gentian (Gentiana lutea) contain intensely bitter compounds, including amarogentin, and have long been used in herbal medicine and to flavour bitter liqueurs and aperitifs. Many species are prized rock-garden ornamentals, though they are reputedly difficult to cultivate because of their specific soil and moisture requirements.

Symbolism

Gentians are emblematic alpine flowers in Europe and appear in regional symbolism and on coins and stamps; gentian blue is also associated with the Bavarian Alps, where related species flower alongside edelweiss.
