Art Deco is a style of visual arts, architecture, and product design that first appeared in Paris in the 1910s, just before the First World War, and flourished internationally during the 1920s and 1930s. The name derives from the 1925 Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes in Paris. It combined modern styles with fine craftsmanship and rich materials, characterised by bold geometric forms, lavish ornament, stylised sunbursts and chevrons, and the use of materials such as stainless steel, lacquer, inlaid wood, and aluminium.

Origins

Art Deco emerged from a reaction against the organic asymmetry of Art Nouveau and from the influence of Cubism, Fauvism, the Ballets Russes, and the discovery of Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922, which sparked a vogue for Egyptian motifs. Early French designers such as Émile-Jacques Ruhlmann produced luxurious furniture for wealthy clients.

Architecture

Deco architecture is most famous in American skyscrapers: the Chrysler Building (1930) and Empire State Building (1931) in New York, the Guardian Building in Detroit, and the Hoover Building in London. The style also shaped cinemas, hotels, ocean liners such as the SS Normandie, and entire districts, notably the Miami Beach Architectural District with its pastel "tropical Deco" hotels.

Decline and revival

The style's lavishness fell from favour during the austerity of the Second World War, and it was eclipsed by the International Style. Interest revived in the 1960s and 1970s through exhibitions and scholarship — the term "Art Deco" itself was popularised by historian Bevis Hillier in 1968 — and preservation campaigns saved many Deco buildings from demolition.
