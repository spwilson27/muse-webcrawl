Island hopping

Island hopping, also called leapfrogging, was a military strategy employed by the Allies in the Pacific War against Japan during the Second World War. Rather than assaulting every Japanese-held island, Allied forces under commanders including General Douglas MacArthur and Admiral Chester W. Nimitz seized selected islands — chosen for their airfields and ports — while bypassing, blockading and neutralising heavily fortified positions, which were left to "wither on the vine" cut off from supply and reinforcement.

Rationale

Direct assaults on strongpoints such as Rabaul and Truk would have been extremely costly. By hopping from island to island toward Japan, the Allies progressively brought Japanese territory within range of land-based aircraft and eventually of B-29 bombers, while conserving manpower and exploiting Allied superiority in sea power, air power and logistics. Bypassed garrisons, unable to be resupplied because of Allied naval and air blockade, often starved.

Campaigns

The strategy was applied from mid-1943 in two converging drives: MacArthur's Southwest Pacific advance along New Guinea to the Philippines (including the landing at Hollandia, bypassing Wewak), and Nimitz's Central Pacific drive through the Gilberts (Tarawa), the Marshalls (Kwajalein, Eniwetok — bypassing several atolls), the Marianas (Saipan, Guam), and on to Iwo Jima and Okinawa. The great Japanese base at Rabaul was encircled and bypassed rather than stormed, and the carrier raid on Truk in February 1944 destroyed its usefulness as an anchorage.

Legacy

Island hopping is regarded as a classic application of selective attack and manoeuvre warfare, and the term has since entered general usage for strategies that bypass obstacles rather than confronting them directly.
