Prophase

Prophase is the first stage of cell division in both mitosis and meiosis in eukaryotic cells. During prophase, the cell's chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes, the mitotic spindle begins to form, and the nucleolus disappears. It follows the G2 phase of interphase and precedes prometaphase, when the nuclear envelope breaks down. Prophase is the longest stage of mitosis, and its onset is driven by the activation of the cyclin B–CDK1 complex, whose phosphorylation of hundreds of substrates reorganises the entire cell for division.

Events in mitotic prophase

Chromosomes, each already replicated into two sister chromatids joined at the centromere, become visible under the light microscope as they coil and condense — a process mediated by condensin protein complexes. Outside the nucleus, the two centrosomes, duplicated during S phase, begin to separate and move toward opposite poles, nucleating microtubules that will form the spindle. The nucleolus disassembles as ribosomal RNA transcription ceases. Late in prophase, kinetochores assemble on the centromeres in preparation for microtubule attachment.

Prophase I of meiosis

Prophase of the first meiotic division is far longer and more complex, and is divided into five sub-stages: leptotene (chromosomes condense), zygotene (homologous chromosomes pair in synapsis), pachytene (crossing over exchanges genetic material between homologues within the synaptonemal complex), diplotene (homologues begin to separate but remain joined at chiasmata), and diakinesis (final condensation and terminalisation of chiasmata). In human females, oocytes arrest in diplotene from fetal life until ovulation, decades later — a prolonged prophase I during which errors can produce the nondisjunction behind trisomies. Crossing over in prophase I is the principal source of new genetic combinations in sexual reproduction.

Regulation

Entry into prophase is triggered when the phosphatase Cdc25 activates CDK1; the spindle assembly checkpoint and DNA-damage checkpoints can halt progression if chromosomes are unreplicated or unattached. Chromosome condensation and centrosome separation also involve the kinases Aurora A, Aurora B, and Polo-like kinase 1, targets of several anticancer drugs.
