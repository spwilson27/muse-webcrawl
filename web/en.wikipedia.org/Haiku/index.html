Haiku (Japanese: 俳句) is a type of short-form poetry that originated in Japan. Traditional Japanese haiku consist of three phrases composed of 17 phonetic units called on, in a 5–7–5 pattern, and include a kigo, a seasonal reference, and a kireji, a "cutting word" that juxtaposes two images or ideas.

History

Haiku developed from the opening verse (hokku) of the collaborative linked-verse form renga. In the seventeenth century Matsuo Bashō elevated the hokku into a serious art, infusing it with Zen-influenced depth and precise observation of nature. Yosa Buson and Kobayashi Issa were the other great masters of the Edo period. The term "haiku" was coined by Masaoka Shiki in the late nineteenth century, when he advocated treating the verse as an independent poem and modernising its subjects.

Characteristics

A traditional haiku presents two juxtaposed images with a grammatical gap between them, inviting the reader to complete the connection. The kigo anchors the poem in a season — cherry blossoms for spring, frogs for early summer, the harvest moon for autumn. Bashō's most famous haiku reads, in transliteration: furu ike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto — "old pond — a frog jumps in — the sound of water."

Haiku in other languages

Because English syllables are not equivalent to Japanese on, English-language haiku are often shorter than 5–7–5 syllables and prioritise brevity and imagery over the count. Haiku societies and journals exist worldwide, and the form is widely taught in schools. Modern Japanese haiku may also depart from the traditional count and season word (muki haiku).
