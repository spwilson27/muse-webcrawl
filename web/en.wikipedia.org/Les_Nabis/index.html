Les Nabis

Les Nabis (from the Hebrew word nebiim, "prophets") were a group of young French artists active in the 1890s who played a major role in the transition from Impressionism and academic art to abstract art, symbolism and the early avant-garde. Inspired by Paul Gauguin's synthetism, they gathered around Paul Sérusier, whose small landscape The Talisman (1888), painted under Gauguin's direction at Pont-Aven, became the group's founding icon.

Ideas and work

The Nabis met at the Académie Julian and in the Café Voltaire, adopting playful pseudo-Hebrew titles and a semi-secret brotherhood ethos. Maurice Denis gave the movement its manifesto in 1890: "a picture — before being a battle horse, a nude woman, or some anecdote — is essentially a flat surface covered with colours assembled in a certain order." They flattened perspective, used bold unmodulated colour, and worked across media: Bonnard and Vuillard painted intimate domestic scenes ("intimism"), Denis pursued religious subjects, and Vallotton revived the woodcut. The group designed posters, illustrations for La Revue blanche, theatre sets for Lugné-Poe's Théâtre de l'Œuvre, stained glass and decorative panels.

Later years

By about 1900 the Nabis had dispersed as members' styles diverged — Bonnard and Vuillard toward post-Impressionist naturalism, Denis toward classicism, Maillol toward sculpture — but their emphasis on the decorative, flat picture plane paved the way for Fauvism and modernism.
