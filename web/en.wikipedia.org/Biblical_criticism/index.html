Biblical criticism

Biblical criticism is the use of critical analysis to understand and explain the Bible. During the 18th century, when it began as historical-biblical criticism, it was based on two distinguishing characteristics: the scientific concern to avoid dogma and bias by applying a neutral, non-sectarian, reason-based judgment to the study of the Bible, and the belief that the reconstruction of the historical events behind the texts, as well as the history of how the texts themselves developed, would lead to a correct understanding of the Bible.

History

Historical-biblical criticism grew in the 18th and 19th centuries with scholars such as Jean Astruc, Johann Gottfried Eichhorn, and Julius Wellhausen, whose documentary hypothesis proposed that the Pentateuch was compiled from several originally independent sources. In the 20th century the field diversified into a wide range of methods, while a reaction against purely historical approaches, influenced by literary theory, produced new methods such as narrative criticism and canonical criticism.

Methods

Textual criticism

Textual criticism examines the many variant readings of the biblical manuscripts in order to reconstruct, as far as possible, the original wording of the texts.

Source criticism

Source criticism searches for the original sources that lie behind a biblical text, such as the documentary hypothesis for the Torah or the two-source hypothesis for the Synoptic Gospels.

Form criticism and redaction criticism

Form criticism classifies biblical passages by literary genre and reconstructs their setting in the life of the community, while redaction criticism studies how editors shaped and arranged their sources to express theological viewpoints.

Literary and canonical approaches

Literary criticism focuses on the text as a finished work of literature, attending to plot, character, and theme; canonical criticism reads the texts in the form in which they were received by communities of faith.
