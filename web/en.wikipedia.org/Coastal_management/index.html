Coastal management is defence against flooding and erosion, and techniques that stop erosion to protect land and settlements near the coast. Coastal zones occupy less than 15% of the Earth's land area while accommodating more than 40% of its population, and they face growing pressure from sea level rise, storms, and development.

Challenges

Coasts are dynamic systems shaped by waves, tides, currents, and sediment transport. Human activity — dredging, river damming, construction — often disrupts sediment supply and accelerates erosion. Relative sea level rise, driven by climate change and land subsidence, increases flood risk and causes "coastal squeeze" of intertidal habitats against fixed defences.

Hard engineering

Hard engineering uses artificial structures: sea walls reflect wave energy; groynes trap sediment moving alongshore; breakwaters shelter areas of water offshore; revetments armor slopes; and gabions (rock-filled cages) absorb wave energy. Such structures are expensive, can transfer erosion downdrift, and may degrade beach amenity.

Soft engineering

Soft engineering works with natural processes. Beach nourishment adds sand or shingle to widen beaches and dissipate wave energy. Dune regeneration and marsh creation restore natural buffers. Managed retreat (managed realignment) deliberately allows low-value land to flood, creating habitat and reducing defence costs elsewhere.

Integrated coastal zone management

Integrated coastal zone management (ICZM) coordinates policies across sectors and administrative boundaries, weighing economic, social, and environmental objectives. In England and Wales, shoreline management plans divide the coast into sediment cells and assign policies — hold the line, advance the line, managed realignment, or no active intervention — for successive epochs.
