The Bastille (French pronunciation: [bastij]) was a fortress in Paris, known formally as the Bastille Saint-Antoine. Built during the Hundred Years' War to defend the eastern approach to the city, it consisted of eight closely spaced towers linked by thick walls around a rectangular courtyard. It played an important role in the internal conflicts of France and for most of its later history was used as a state prison by the kings of France.

The Bastille was stormed by a revolutionary crowd on 14 July 1789, an event that became the symbol of the French Revolution; the day is celebrated annually as France's national holiday, Bastille Day. At the time of its fall the prison held only seven prisoners, but it had loomed large in the public imagination as a symbol of royal despotism, since prisoners could be held there without trial under lettres de cachet. Famous prisoners had included the Man in the Iron Mask and the writer Voltaire.

The fortress was demolished during the Revolution; stones were reused in building, and many were carved into miniature models of the Bastille and sold as souvenirs. The site is now the Place de la Bastille, marked by the July Column, and the outlines of the fortress are traced in the paving.
