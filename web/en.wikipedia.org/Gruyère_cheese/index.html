Gruyère cheese

Gruyère (French: [ɡʁɥijɛʁ]) is a hard Swiss cheese that originated in the canton of Fribourg, Switzerland. It is named after the town of Gruyères. Gruyère is produced under the Gruyère AOP (appellation d'origine protégée) designation in the cantons of Fribourg, Vaud, Neuchâtel, Jura, and parts of Bern. A similar cheese, Gruyère de France, is made across the border in Franche-Comté and Savoie.

Production and character

Gruyère is made from raw cow's milk. Curds are cut, stirred, and cooked, then pressed into large wheels (typically 25–40 kg) and brined before ageing for at least five months, and often a year or more. Young Gruyère is sweet and slightly salty with a creamy, nutty flavour; with age it becomes more assertive, earthy, and complex, developing small cracks and crunchy protein crystals. Unlike Emmental, Gruyère has few or no holes ("eyes"). The related Alpage variety is made in summer in high Alpine pastures.

Culinary uses

Gruyère is prized as a melting cheese: it is the classic cheese of Swiss fondue (often blended with Vacherin in the moitié-moitié), and it is essential to French dishes such as croque-monsieur, French onion soup, quiche Lorraine, and gratin dishes. It is also eaten on its own as a table cheese.

Name protection

"Gruyère" has been protected in Switzerland and the European Union, and long-running disputes have concerned whether the name is generic elsewhere. In 2023 a United States federal appeals court ruled that "gruyere" is generic in the US market and not protectable as a certification mark there, a decision Swiss and French producer groups opposed.
