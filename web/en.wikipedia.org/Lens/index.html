Lens

A lens is a transmissive optical device that focuses or disperses a light beam by means of refraction. A simple lens consists of a single piece of transparent material, usually glass or plastic, while a compound lens consists of several simple lenses (elements), usually arranged along a common axis. Lenses are used in imaging devices such as cameras, telescopes, microscopes, and binoculars, and in corrective eyewear such as glasses and contact lenses.

Types

Lenses are classified by the curvature of their two optical surfaces. A biconvex (or double convex) lens has two outward-curving surfaces and converges light, forming real images; a biconcave lens diverges light. Other forms include plano-convex, plano-concave, and meniscus (convex-concave) lenses. The power of a lens in air is approximated by the lensmaker's equation, which depends on the refractive index of the material and the radii of curvature of the surfaces.

Focal length and imaging

The focal length of a lens, measured from the lens to the point where collimated rays converge, determines its magnification and field of view. Short focal lengths give strong refraction and wide fields of view; long focal lengths give narrow, magnified views, as in telephoto lenses. The imaging properties of thin lenses are summarised by the Gaussian lens formula, relating object distance, image distance, and focal length.

Aberrations

Real lenses depart from ideal imaging through aberrations. Spherical aberration arises because rays far from the axis focus at different points; chromatic aberration because refractive index varies with wavelength, producing coloured fringes. Compound lenses, aspheric elements, and special low-dispersion glasses are used to correct these defects.

History

Reading stones and burning glasses were known in antiquity; the oldest known lens is the Nimrud lens of Assyria (c. 750–710 BC). Lenses came into wide use with the invention of spectacles in thirteenth-century Italy and of the telescope and compound microscope around 1600.
