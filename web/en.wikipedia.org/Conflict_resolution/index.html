Conflict resolution

Conflict resolution is a range of processes aimed at alleviating or eliminating sources of conflict. Generally, conflict resolution can be used for the resolution of personal issues (intrapersonal conflict) and professional issues (organizational conflict), covering a spectrum that ranges from self-conflict to global political and environmental conflict. Conflict resolution is conceptualized as the methods and processes involved in facilitating the peaceful ending of conflict and retribution.

Methods

Common methods of conflict resolution include negotiation, mediation, arbitration, and litigation. Negotiation is direct discussion between the parties; mediation adds a neutral third party who facilitates agreement without imposing a decision; arbitration produces a binding decision from a third party; litigation resolves disputes in court. Committed group members attempt to resolve group conflicts by actively communicating information about their conflicting motives or ideologies to the rest of the group and by engaging in collective negotiation.

Theory

Conflict resolution draws on psychology, sociology, law, and political science. The dual concern model, based on research by Dean Pruitt and Peter Carnevale, classifies strategies by concern for one's own outcomes versus concern for the other party's outcomes, yielding styles such as competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, and accommodating. Interest-based approaches, popularized by Getting to Yes by Roger Fisher and William Ury, distinguish positions from underlying interests.

Applications

In international relations, conflict resolution encompasses peace processes, diplomacy, and the work of bodies such as the United Nations. In workplaces and schools, mediation and restorative practices are used to address disputes constructively. Dimensions of resolution typically involve the intent or desire to settle, and the parties' ability to follow through on commitments.
