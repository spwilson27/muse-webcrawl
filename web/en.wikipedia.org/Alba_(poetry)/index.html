Alba (poetry)

The alba (Old Occitan: [ˈalba]; "dawn") is a genre of Old Occitan lyric poetry developed by the troubadours. It describes the lovers' parting at dawn after a night spent together, usually lamented by the lady and warned of by a watchman (gaita), who calls out that morning has come and that the lovers must separate before they are discovered.

Form and themes

The alba is typically structured around the opposition between the wished-for night and the dreaded day. A friend or watchman keeps guard and announces the dawn, often with the refrain containing the word alba itself. About eighteen albas survive in the Occitan corpus. The genre has parallels elsewhere in Europe: the German Tagelied, the Old French aube, the Galician-Portuguese alva, and the dawn speeches in medieval narrative and drama, as in the parting of Romeo and Juliet.

Notable examples

One of the best-known albas is the anonymous "En un vergier sotz fuella d'albespi", and Guiraut de Bornelh's "Reis glorios" is a famous example cast as a prayer by the watchman. The genre influenced later dawn-parting scenes throughout European literature.
