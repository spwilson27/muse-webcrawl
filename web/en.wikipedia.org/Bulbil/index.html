Bulbil

A bulbil is a small bulb-like structure that forms on a plant in place of, or in addition to, flowers, and which can drop off and grow into a new plant genetically identical to the parent. Bulbils are a form of asexual or vegetative reproduction and are also termed bulbets, bulblets, or — when formed on underground stems — offsets.

Formation

Bulbils may form in the axils of leaves along a stem, as in the tiger lily (Lilium lancifolium), whose dark purple-black bulbils are conspicuous in summer; in flower heads, as in many wild garlics and onions, where they replace some or all of the flowers; or on the leaves themselves in plants such as Kalanchoe and some ferns.

Examples

Well-known bulbil-bearing plants include garlic, tree onion (Egyptian walking onion), tiger lily, Agave species (which produce bulbils on their flowering stalks), and the fern Woodwardia radicans. In some species, such as pineapple lily relatives and the lesser celandine, root tubers or axillary bulbils are the main means of spread.

Ecology and use

Bulbils allow rapid local spread without the need for pollinators or seed maturation, though they disperse less widely than seeds. Gardeners use bulbils to propagate lilies, garlic, and ornamental plants true to type.
