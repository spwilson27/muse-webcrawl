The Kingis Quair

The Kingis Quair ("The King's Book") is a Scots poem of the early 15th century, traditionally attributed to King James I of Scotland. Written in rhyme royal stanzas in a Chaucerian style, the poem is a semi-autobiographical dream vision describing the king's captivity in England and his love for the woman who became his queen, Joan Beaufort.

Background

James I was captured by the English in 1406, aged eleven, and held for eighteen years, during which he received an excellent courtly education. According to the poem's narrative, from his tower window he saw a beautiful lady walking in the garden below and fell in love. James married Joan Beaufort in 1424, shortly before his release, and the poem is generally dated to around that time.

Content

The poem, of some 1,379 lines, opens with the sleepless narrator reading Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy. Falling asleep, he dreams he is transported to the heavens, visiting the spheres of Venus, Minerva, and Fortune, who counsel him on love and his destiny. He returns to earth hopeful, receives a message of promise from a dove, and awakens to find his fortunes changed and his love reciprocated.

Authorship and significance

The sole surviving manuscript, held by the Bodleian Library, ascribes the poem to James I, and most scholars accept the attribution. The Kingis Quair stands at the head of the tradition of the Scottish Chaucerians and is a landmark of Older Scots literature, blending Boethian philosophy, courtly love conventions, and personal experience. Its authenticity and quality have made it central to debates about early Scottish literature.
