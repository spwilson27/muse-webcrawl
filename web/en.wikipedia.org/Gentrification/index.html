Gentrification


Renovated housing in a formerly working-class district, a common visual marker of gentrification.

Gentrification is the process of changing the character of a neighborhood through the influx of more affluent residents (the "gentry") and investment. There is no agreed-upon definition of gentrification. In the United Kingdom, the term was coined in 1964 by the sociologist Ruth Glass to describe changes she observed in London: "One by one, many of the working class quarters have been invaded by the middle class... until all or most of the working class occupiers are displaced and the whole social character of the district is changed."

Gentrification is a process typically resulting in displacement, as the cost of housing increases and long-term residents and businesses are displaced. These displaced people often have limited options for relocating within their neighborhoods and may be forced to move to other areas. Gentrification is a common and controversial topic in urban politics and planning.

Causes

Theories explaining gentrification include production-side and consumption-side accounts. The economist Neil Smith proposed the "rent gap" theory: gentrification occurs when the gap between the actual ground rent capitalised under current land use and the potential rent under a "higher" use becomes wide enough that developers can purchase, renovate, and resell property at a profit. Consumption-side theories, associated with sociologists such as David Ley, emphasise the changing preferences of a new middle class — drawn to central-city living by cultural tastes, professional employment in city centres, and smaller households — that seeks out older, characterful housing stock.

Other contributing factors include public policy and investment (such as transit improvements, zoning changes, and tax incentives), demographic shifts, rising metropolitan housing demand, and the expansion of the service and knowledge economies that concentrate high-income jobs in a small number of cities.

Effects

Gentrification often shifts a neighborhood's racial and ethnic composition and average household income, as lower-income residents are replaced by higher-income ones or are displaced and unable to return. Supporters point to benefits including reduced vacancy and abandonment, increased property tax revenue, renovation of deteriorated housing, and lower crime in some studies. Critics emphasise displacement — both direct (eviction, unaffordable rent increases) and indirect or cultural (the loss of affordable shops, services, and the character that made a neighbourhood home to its residents) — as well as the segregation that can result when affordable housing concentrates elsewhere.

History and examples

Although the term dates to 1960s London, analogous processes are much older, visible for example in Haussmann's renovation of Paris in the 19th century. Widely discussed contemporary examples in the United States include neighbourhoods in New York City (such as Williamsburg and Harlem), San Francisco's Mission District, and parts of Washington, D.C.; in Europe, districts of Berlin, Amsterdam, and London have been extensively studied. The scale, speed, and state involvement in gentrification vary greatly by city and country.

Responses

Policy responses to displacement associated with gentrification include rent regulation, inclusionary zoning requiring affordable units in new developments, community land trusts, tenant right-to-counsel programmes, property-tax relief for long-term homeowners, and protections against eviction. Debates about gentrification frequently turn on whether the process can be steered to share benefits with existing residents or whether displacement is inherent to it.
