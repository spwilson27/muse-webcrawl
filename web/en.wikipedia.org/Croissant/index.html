Croissant

A croissant is a French pastry made from puff pastry in a crescent shape, named for the French word for "crescent." It is made by layering yeast-leavened dough with butter, then rolling and folding the dough repeatedly — a process called lamination — to produce a flaky, layered texture. Croissants are a staple of French bakeries and breakfast tables and belong to the category of viennoiserie.

History

The croissant descends from the Austrian kipferl, a crescent-shaped roll documented as early as the 13th century. Crescent-shaped pastries were popularised in Paris after the Austrian artillery officer August Zang opened a Viennese bakery there in the late 1830s. French bakers gradually transformed the kipferl using laminated puff pastry, producing the modern croissant by the early 20th century. A popular legend linking the pastry to the 1683 Siege of Vienna is not supported by documentary evidence.

Preparation and varieties

Traditional croissant dough is laminated with butter through a series of folds, cut into triangles, rolled into crescents, proofed, and baked until golden. Variations include the pain au chocolat (with chocolate batons), the almond croissant (filled with frangipane), and croissants filled with ham and cheese. In France, the curved croissant ordinaire may be made with margarine, while the straight croissant au beurre is made with pure butter.
