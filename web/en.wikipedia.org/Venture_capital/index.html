Venture capital

Venture capital (VC) is a form of private equity financing provided by firms or funds to startups and early-stage companies that are deemed to have high growth potential or that have demonstrated high growth. Venture capitalists invest in exchange for equity, accepting high risk for the prospect of outsized returns, and typically provide managerial and technical expertise alongside funding.

Structure

Venture capital funds are usually organized as limited partnerships: limited partners (pension funds, endowments, family offices, and wealthy individuals) supply the capital, while general partners manage the fund, source deals, and sit on portfolio companies' boards. Funds typically run for about ten years and charge a management fee (commonly 2% of committed capital annually) plus carried interest (commonly 20% of profits).

Stages

Financing rounds progress from pre-seed and seed funding, through Series A, B, C and later rounds, as companies grow. Investments are staged so that investors can abandon failing ventures and double down on successes. Returns are realized through exits — initial public offerings or acquisitions. Because most startups fail, returns follow a power law in which a small number of investments generate most of a fund's profit.

History

The modern industry began with the American Research and Development Corporation, founded in 1946 by Georges Doriot, often called the "father of venture capital." Fairchild Semiconductor's financing in 1957 and the subsequent Silicon Valley ecosystem, centred on Sand Hill Road firms such as Kleiner Perkins and Sequoia Capital (both founded 1972), established the model. Venture funding boomed during the dot-com era of the late 1990s and has since expanded globally, including large sovereign and corporate venture funds.
