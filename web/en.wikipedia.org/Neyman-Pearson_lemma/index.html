Neyman–Pearson lemma

In statistics, the Neyman–Pearson lemma describes the existence and uniqueness of the likelihood ratio as a uniformly most powerful criterion for hypothesis testing when testing a simple null hypothesis against a simple alternative. It was introduced by Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson in a paper of 1933 and is a foundational result of statistical hypothesis testing.

Statement

When testing H0: θ = θ0 against H1: θ = θ1, the likelihood-ratio test that rejects H0 when the likelihood ratio Λ(x) = L(θ0 | x) / L(θ1 | x) falls below a constant k (chosen so the test has size α) is the most powerful test of its size: no other test with significance level at most α has greater power against the alternative. Randomisation on the boundary may be needed to achieve exact size α.

Applications

The lemma underlies the design of optimal detectors in signal processing and radar, where the likelihood-ratio test is the ideal observer. Its extensions lead to uniformly most powerful tests for one-sided composite hypotheses in families with monotone likelihood ratio, and it motivates likelihood-ratio procedures throughout statistics.
