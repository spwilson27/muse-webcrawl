Magical thinking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Magical thinking, or superstitious thinking, is the belief that unrelated events are causally connected despite the absence of any plausible link between them — for example, that a person's thoughts, words or ritual actions can directly influence the material world, or that one event causes another although no mechanism connects them. The term is used in anthropology, psychology and psychiatry, and in everyday speech for irrational causal beliefs generally.

Anthropology

Anthropologists such as Edward Tylor and James George Frazer analysed magic as a stage in the development of human thought preceding religion and science. Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890) distinguished sympathetic magic, based on similarity ("like produces like") and contagion ("once in contact, always in contact"). Later anthropologists, including Bronisław Malinowski and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, showed that magical practices coexist with practical knowledge and often serve to manage anxiety and uncertainty rather than reflecting simple error.

Psychology and psychiatry

Developmental psychologists, following Piaget, regard magical thinking as normal in young children, who may believe that their wishes or actions cause external events. In adults, mild magical thinking is widespread — superstitions, lucky rituals, and beliefs in fate — and is associated in moderation with creativity and pattern-seeking cognition. Clinically, excessive magical thinking is a feature of schizotypal personality traits and obsessive–compulsive disorder, in which intrusive thoughts are feared to cause harm unless neutralised by rituals. The concept also overlaps with cognitive biases such as the illusion of control and apophenia.
