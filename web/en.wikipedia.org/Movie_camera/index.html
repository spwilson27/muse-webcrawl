Movie camera

A movie camera (also known as a film camera and cine-camera) is a type of photographic camera that rapidly takes a sequence of photographs on a film stock or image sensor. In contrast to a still camera, which captures a single snapshot at a time, a movie camera takes a series of images; each image constitutes a "frame". This is then accomplished through an intermittent mechanism. The frames are later played back in a movie projector at a specific speed, called the frame rate (number of frames per second). While viewing at a particular frame rate, a person's eyes and brain merge the separate pictures together to create the illusion of motion.

History

The first machine patented in the United States for moving pictures, "to make photographs in succession, to record natural expressions and movements", was designed by Louis Le Prince in 1888. Le Prince used a single lens camera, but it is unclear if any footage from it survives. Thomas Edison and his assistant William Kennedy Dickson developed the Kinetograph, which used an electric motor and a sprocket mechanism to advance the film strip intermittently. The first public exhibition of films made with the Kinetoscope took place in 1893.

In France, the Lumière brothers developed the Cinématographe in 1895, a combination camera, printer and projector that was hand-cranked and much lighter than Edison's device, allowing location filming.

Mechanism

Traditional motion picture cameras use perforated celluloid film stock through which a claw or shuttle mechanism advances the film while the shutter is closed, then opens the shutter to expose one frame. This cycle repeats typically 24 times per second. The film is pulled through the camera by sprocket wheels, and loop-forming guides (the "Latham loop") prevent tension from tearing the perforations.

Types

Movie cameras have been built for film gauges of 8 mm, 16 mm, 35 mm and 65/70 mm. Professional cameras such as those made by Arri, Panavision and Mitchell dominated studio production throughout the 20th century. Since the early 2000s, digital cinema cameras (such as the Arri Alexa and Red cameras) have largely replaced film cameras in professional production.
