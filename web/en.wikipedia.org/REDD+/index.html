REDD+

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

REDD+ (also written REDD-plus) is a framework negotiated under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) to guide activities that reduce emissions from deforestation and forest degradation in developing countries. The original REDD concept — reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation — was expanded by the "+" to include the conservation of forest carbon stocks, the sustainable management of forests, and the enhancement of forest carbon stocks.

Development

Proposals to reward avoided deforestation were first tabled at the 2005 Montreal climate conference by Papua New Guinea and Costa Rica on behalf of the Coalition for Rainforest Nations. The 2007 Bali Action Plan put REDD on the formal agenda, the 2010 Cancún Agreements set out safeguards and phases of implementation, and the 2013 Warsaw Framework for REDD-plus completed the rulebook for results-based payments, including requirements for forest reference emission levels, national forest monitoring systems and measurement, reporting and verification (MRV). Article 5 of the 2015 Paris Agreement reaffirmed the framework.

Implementation and finance

REDD+ proceeds in phases: readiness (national strategies and capacity building), implementation of policies and measures, and fully measured, reported and verified results-based actions. Funding channels include the World Bank's Forest Carbon Partnership Facility, Norway's International Climate and Forest Initiative, the Green Climate Fund and voluntary carbon markets, where jurisdictional REDD+ credits have been debated intensely.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns about carbon leakage and impermanence, the accuracy of baseline deforestation rates, double counting, and the rights and livelihoods of Indigenous peoples and local communities — issues the Cancún safeguards (covering governance, tenure, participation and biodiversity) were designed to address. Supporters argue REDD+ is among the most cost-effective mitigation options, since deforestation and forest degradation contribute a substantial share of global greenhouse-gas emissions.
