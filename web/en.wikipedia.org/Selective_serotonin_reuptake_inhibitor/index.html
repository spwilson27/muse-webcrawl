Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor

SSRIs
Drug classAntidepressants
MechanismBlock the serotonin transporter (SERT), increasing synaptic serotonin
ExamplesFluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram, fluvoxamine
UsesMajor depression, anxiety disorders, OCD, PTSD, panic disorder

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are a class of drugs typically used as antidepressants in the treatment of major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, and some other conditions. They work by blocking the reabsorption (reuptake) of the neurotransmitter serotonin into the presynaptic neuron, increasing the level of serotonin available in the synaptic cleft.

Medical uses

SSRIs are first-line treatments for major depressive disorder and for generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Individual agents carry specific approvals; fluvoxamine is used mainly for OCD, and paroxetine and sertraline are among those approved for PTSD. Antidepressant effects typically take several weeks to emerge.

History

Fluoxetine, developed by Eli Lilly and approved by the US FDA in 1987 under the brand name Prozac, became a cultural phenomenon and one of the most widely prescribed drugs in the world. SSRIs largely displaced older tricyclic antidepressants and monoamine oxidase inhibitors in routine practice because they are safer in overdose and generally better tolerated.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include nausea, insomnia or somnolence, headache, and sexual dysfunction. Abrupt discontinuation can produce a discontinuation syndrome with dizziness and flu-like symptoms. Combining SSRIs with other serotonergic drugs risks serotonin syndrome, a potentially life-threatening condition. Regulatory agencies have issued warnings about a small increase in suicidal thinking in adolescents and young adults early in treatment. See also serotonin and antidepressant.
