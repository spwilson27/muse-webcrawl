Wu wei

Wu wei (simplified Chinese: 无为; traditional Chinese: 無為; pinyin: wúwéi) is a central concept in Daoism meaning "effortless action", "non-action" or "action without striving". It describes acting in effortless alignment with the natural flow of things — the Tao — rather than forcing outcomes through deliberate interference.

Meaning

The Tao Te Ching of Laozi states that "the Tao never acts, yet nothing is left undone" (為無為, "act by non-action"). Wu wei is not mere passivity or laziness: it is the cultivated spontaneity of the sage, who responds to situations without ego-driven striving, like water flowing around obstacles. The Zhuangzi illustrates the principle with the story of Cook Ding, whose blade, moving through natural seams, never dulls despite years of use — skill so attuned to the structure of things that action becomes effortless.

In governance and other schools

Applied to rulership, wu wei counselled the ruler to govern by minimal interference, allowing society to order itself — an ideal later adopted in Han-dynasty statecraft (Huang-Lao) and debated by Legalists, who gave the term their own meaning of the ruler's inscrutable stillness. Confucian texts also use the phrase approvingly of the legendary emperor Shun, who "governed by non-action". The concept has modern resonances in psychology (the state of "flow"), management theory and environmental philosophy.

Related concepts

Wu wei is closely related to ziran (自然, "naturalness" or "self-so") and to the ideal of pu (樸, the "uncarved block"). Its interplay with deliberate effort — effortless action achieved through long practice — is a classic interpretive puzzle of Daoist thought.
