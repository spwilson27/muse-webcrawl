Matched filter

In signal processing, a matched filter is a linear filter designed to detect the presence of a known signal (a template) embedded in noise. It is obtained by correlating the received signal with a time-reversed, conjugated copy of the template, and it maximises the output signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the presence of additive white noise — it is the optimal linear detector in that setting.

Theory

If the received signal is a known waveform s(t) plus noise, the filter that maximises SNR at the sampling instant has impulse response h(t) = s*(T − t): the template flipped in time and conjugated. The output SNR then equals the signal energy divided by the noise power spectral density, regardless of the waveform's shape — the filter "collects" all the signal's energy into one peak. The derivation follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. For coloured (non-white) noise, the signal is first whitened, giving a "generalised" matched filter.

Applications

Matched filtering is fundamental in radar and sonar, where it is used with pulse compression waveforms such as chirps: a long, low-power pulse is transmitted, and filtering on receive compresses its energy into a sharp peak, giving both range resolution and detectability. In digital communications, matched filtering at the receiver maximises SNR for each symbol and underlies the optimal demodulator. In gravitational-wave astronomy, observatories such as LIGO search their data by correlating it with banks of hundreds of thousands of predicted waveform templates (template matching). Cross-correlation in image processing — for example, finding a known pattern in a photograph — is the two-dimensional analogue.

Limitations

The matched filter requires knowing the target waveform in advance; signals deviating from the template are detected less efficiently, and highly manoeuvrable targets, Doppler shifts, and non-Gaussian noise can all degrade performance. In practice, banks of filters covering plausible variations of the signal are used.
