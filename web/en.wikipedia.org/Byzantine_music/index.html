Byzantine music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

Byzantine music (Greek: Βυζαντινή μουσική) is the music of the Byzantine Empire, composed to Greek texts as ceremonial, festival, and liturgical music. It is the chant tradition of the Eastern Orthodox Church, characterized by monophonic vocal singing without instrumental accompaniment, a system of eight modes (the oktoechos), and its own neumatic notation. Byzantine chant continues in use today in Greek Orthodox worship and has been inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

The tradition developed from early Christian chant of the eastern Mediterranean, drawing on Greek musical theory and Syriac and Jewish liturgical influences. Great hymnographers such as Romanos the Melodist (6th century), composer of kontakia, and later John of Damascus and John Koukouzelis shaped the repertory. After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, the tradition was preserved in Ottoman-era monasteries and churches, especially on Mount Athos.

Notation and theory

Byzantine notation evolved from ekphonetic signs to the interval-based neumes of the Middle Byzantine system. In 1814 the "Three Teachers" — Chrysanthos of Madytos, Gregorios the Protopsaltes, and Chourmouzios the Archivist — reformed the notation into the analytic system still used today. Melodies are built from formulaic motifs within the eight modes, and a sustained drone (ison) may accompany the chant.

Genres

Principal liturgical genres include the troparion, kontakion, kanon, sticheron, and the settings of the Divine Liturgy such as the Cherubic Hymn. Secular Byzantine music, including court ceremonial songs and epics like Digenis Akritas, is less completely preserved.
