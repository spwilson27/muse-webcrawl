Prague Spring

The Prague Spring (Czech: Pražské jaro; Slovak: Pražská jar) was a period of political liberalisation and mass protest in the Czechoslovak Socialist Republic that began on 5 January 1968, when reformist Alexander Dubček was elected First Secretary of the Communist Party of Czechoslovakia, and continued until the Soviet Union and other Warsaw Pact members invaded the country on the night of 20–21 August 1968 to suppress the reforms.

Reforms

Dubček's Action Programme promised freedom of speech, press, and travel; a move towards a mixed economy; limits on the secret police; and a possible federalisation of Czechoslovakia into Czech and Slovak republics. Censorship was effectively abolished, and the period saw an explosion of free public debate, literature, and journalism.

Invasion

Fearing the spread of liberalisation, the USSR and four other Warsaw Pact states invaded with hundreds of thousands of troops and thousands of tanks. Dubček and other leaders were arrested and taken to Moscow; most reforms were reversed during the subsequent "normalisation" under Gustáv Husák. The invasion was justified by the Brezhnev Doctrine of limited sovereignty for socialist states.

Legacy

The Prague Spring inspired reform movements across Eastern Europe and is remembered as a symbol of peaceful democratic aspiration; its ideals resurfaced in the Velvet Revolution of 1989. The self-immolation of student Jan Palach in January 1969, protesting the suppression, remains an enduring national memorial.
