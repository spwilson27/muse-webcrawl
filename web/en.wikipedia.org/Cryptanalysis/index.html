Cryptanalysis (from the Greek kryptós, "hidden", and analýein, "to analyse") is the study of analysing information systems in order to understand hidden aspects of the systems — in practice, the art and science of breaking cryptographic security systems, recovering plaintext from ciphertext without knowing the key, or finding weaknesses in protocols and implementations.

History

The earliest known systematic treatment is al-Kindi's 9th-century Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages, which described frequency analysis — exploiting the characteristic letter frequencies of a language to break monoalphabetic substitution ciphers. Polyalphabetic ciphers such as the Vigenère cipher resisted until the methods of Babbage and Kasiski in the 19th century. In the First World War, Britain's Room 40 broke German naval codes and decrypted the Zimmermann Telegram. In the Second World War, Polish and British cryptanalysts at Bletchley Park — including Marian Rejewski and Alan Turing — broke the Enigma machine cipher, producing "Ultra" intelligence, while American cryptanalysts broke Japan's PURPLE machine.

Attack models and methods

Attacks are classified by the attacker's access: ciphertext-only, known-plaintext, chosen-plaintext, and chosen-ciphertext. Modern methods include brute-force key search, differential and linear cryptanalysis of block ciphers (Biham and Shamir; Matsui), algebraic attacks, and — against real systems — side-channel attacks exploiting timing, power consumption, or faulty implementations. Kerckhoffs's principle holds that a system should remain secure even if everything except the key is public, so published cryptanalysis is regarded as essential to trustworthy cipher design, as in the open AES selection process.
