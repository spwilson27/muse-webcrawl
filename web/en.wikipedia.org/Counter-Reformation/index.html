Counter-Reformation

The Counter-Reformation (Latin: Contrareformatio), also sometimes called the Catholic Revival, was the period of Catholic resurgence that was initiated in response to, and as an alternative to, the Protestant Reformations of the 16th century. It is conventionally dated from the Council of Trent (1545) to the end of the European wars of religion in 1648. The effort aimed both to reform the Catholic Church from within and to counter the spread of Protestantism, producing new religious orders, doctrinal clarification, missionary expansion, and the Baroque transformation of Catholic culture.

Council of Trent

Convened by Pope Paul III, the Council of Trent met intermittently at Trento between 1545 and 1563. It rejected Protestant doctrines on justification by faith alone and scripture alone, reaffirming the seven sacraments, transubstantiation, tradition alongside scripture, and the veneration of saints — while also enacting disciplinary reforms: bishops were required to reside in their dioceses, seminaries were established for priestly training, and abuses such as the sale of indulgences were curbed.

Orders and agents

New and reformed orders drove the revival: the Society of Jesus (Jesuits), founded by Ignatius of Loyola and approved in 1540, became pre-eminent in education, confession, and missions from India and Japan to Paraguay; the Theatines, Capuchins, Ursulines, and Discalced Carmelites (associated with Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross) pursued pastoral and spiritual renewal. The Roman Inquisition (1542) and the Index of Prohibited Books (1559) policed heresy and print.

Effects

Combined with Habsburg support, Catholic reform recovered much of Poland, southern Germany, and Austria for Rome, while France remained Catholic after its wars of religion. Baroque art and architecture — Bernini, Rubens, and the Jesuit churches of Rome — gave the movement a triumphant visual programme. Global missions carried Catholicism to the Americas, Africa, and Asia, even as Europe's religious division was permanently confirmed by the Peace of Westphalia in 1648.
