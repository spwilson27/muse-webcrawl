Marburg virus disease

Marburg virus disease is a severe viral haemorrhagic fever caused by Marburg virus, a filovirus closely related to Ebola virus. It was first identified in 1967 during simultaneous outbreaks in Marburg and Frankfurt, Germany, and Belgrade, Serbia, traced to laboratory workers exposed to African green monkeys imported from Uganda.

Symptoms and transmission

Illness begins abruptly with high fever, severe headache and malaise, followed in many patients by vomiting, diarrhoea, rash and haemorrhagic manifestations. Case fatality rates in outbreaks have ranged from about 24% to 88%. The virus's natural reservoir is thought to be the Egyptian fruit bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus); human-to-human transmission occurs through direct contact with blood and body fluids.

Treatment

There is no approved vaccine or specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive, focusing on rehydration and treatment of symptoms. Outbreaks have occurred mainly in sub-Saharan Africa, including Angola (2004–05), Uganda, Ghana, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania and Rwanda.
