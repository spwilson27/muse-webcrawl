Fractional crystallization

Sequential formation and separation of crystals from a cooling magma, progressively changing the composition of the remaining melt.

Fractional crystallization, or crystal fractionation, is one of the most important geochemical and physical processes operating within crust and mantle of a rocky planetary body, such as the Earth. It is important in the formation of igneous rocks because it is one of the main processes of magmatic differentiation. Fractional crystallization is also important in the formation of sedimentary evaporite rocks.

Unlike perfect or equilibrium crystallization, in which the crystals remain in chemical equilibrium with the melt throughout cooling, fractional crystallization removes crystals from the magma soon after they form—for example by settling to the floor of a magma chamber under gravity, or by being left behind as the melt migrates. The removal of early-formed, typically mafic minerals progressively enriches the residual liquid in silica, alkalis and incompatible elements.

Mechanism

As a basaltic magma cools, minerals crystallize in a predictable sequence described by Bowen's reaction series. Olivine and calcium-rich plagioclase crystallize first; if these dense crystals sink and accumulate as cumulates, the remaining melt becomes depleted in magnesium, iron and calcium and enriched in silicon, aluminium, sodium and potassium. Continued fractionation can drive a basaltic melt toward andesitic, dacitic and ultimately rhyolitic compositions.

Geochemical effects

Because elements partition between crystals and melt according to their partition coefficients, fractionation produces characteristic trace-element trends: compatible elements such as nickel and chromium are rapidly depleted, while incompatible elements such as rubidium, barium and the light rare-earth elements are concentrated in the residual melt. These trends are a primary tool for modelling magma evolution and identifying related suites of volcanic rocks.

Examples

Classic natural laboratories include the Skaergaard intrusion of East Greenland, whose layered series records extreme fractionation of a tholeiitic magma, and the Bushveld Igneous Complex of South Africa, where crystal settling concentrated chromite and platinum-group element ores of major economic importance.
