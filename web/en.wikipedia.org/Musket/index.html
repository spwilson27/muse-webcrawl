A musket is a muzzle-loaded long gun that appeared in the early 16th century as a heavier development of the arquebus. Smoothbore muskets were the standard infantry weapon of armies worldwide from the 17th to the mid-19th century, fired in volleys by lines of infantry and typically fitted with a socket bayonet for close combat.

History

The musket first appeared in Europe — the term derives from the Italian moschetto — and spread rapidly. Ignition systems evolved from matchlock to wheellock, snaphaunce, and the true flintlock, which dominated for two centuries; famous models include the British "Brown Bess" Land Pattern Musket and the French Charleville. The percussion cap replaced flint in the early 19th century.

Characteristics and use

Smoothbore muskets were inaccurate beyond roughly 100 metres against individual targets, but devastating in massed volley fire. Trained soldiers could reload and fire two to four rounds per minute using paper cartridges. Tactics revolved around linear formations that maximised firepower.

Replacement

Rifled muskets firing the Minié ball extended effective range dramatically in the mid-19th century, but breech-loading rifles — the Dreyse needle gun, the Chassepot, and later bolt-action magazine rifles — made the muzzle-loading musket obsolete by the 1870s.
