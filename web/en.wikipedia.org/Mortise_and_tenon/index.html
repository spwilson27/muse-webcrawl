Mortise and tenon

Type: Woodworking joint
Components: Mortise (hole/slot), tenon (tongue)
Used in: Furniture, timber framing, shipbuilding, stone construction

A mortise and tenon joint is a classic method of joining two pieces of material—most commonly wood—by fitting a protruding tongue, the tenon, into a matching hole or slot, the mortise. The joint may be glued, pinned, wedged, or drawbored with pegs to lock it in place.

History

The joint is ancient: mortise and tenon joinery was used in Egyptian furniture, in the construction of Stonehenge's trilithons (where tenons on uprights fit into mortises in lintels), in ancient Chinese timber architecture, and in early shipbuilding. Timber-framed buildings across Europe and Asia rely heavily on pegged mortise and tenon joints.

Variants

Common variants include the through tenon, which passes entirely through the mortised piece and may be wedged; the stub tenon, which enters only part-way; the haunched tenon, used at the ends of frames; the bridle joint, an open-ended relative; and double or twin tenons for wide stock. A loose tenon is a separate piece inserted into mortises in both members. Modern woodworking machines, such as mortisers and router jigs, and systems like floating-tenon joinery, have mechanised the joint while retaining its strength.

Properties

The joint's strength comes from its large glue surface and mechanical interlock. It remains a hallmark of quality furniture making and is used for frame-and-panel doors, tables, chairs, and heavy timber frames.
