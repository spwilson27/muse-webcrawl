Czech (Czech: čeština), historically also known as Bohemian, is a West Slavic language of the Czech–Slovak group, written in the Latin script. It is the official language of the Czech Republic and one of the 24 official languages of the European Union. Czech is closely related to Slovak, and the two remain largely mutually intelligible.

History

Czech emerged from Common West Slavic in the 10th–12th centuries. A rich literary language flourished in the Hussite era, when Jan Hus is credited with reforming Czech orthography by introducing diacritics — notably the háček (caron) in letters such as č, š, ž and ř. After the Battle of White Mountain (1620), Germanisation pushed Czech into the countryside; the 19th-century Czech National Revival, led by scholars such as Josef Dobrovský and Josef Jungmann, codified modern literary Czech (spisovná čeština), which still differs noticeably from the common spoken language (obecná čeština).

Grammar and phonology

Czech is a highly inflected fusional language with seven grammatical cases, three genders and a complex system of verbal aspect. Its phonology includes the raised alveolar trill ř, syllabic liquids (as in the tongue-twister Strč prst skrz krk, which contains no vowels), fixed stress on the first syllable and phonemic vowel length. The alphabet uses diacritics rather than digraphs, though the letter combination "ch" counts as a single letter collating after H.
