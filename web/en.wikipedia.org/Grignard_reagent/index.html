Grignard reagent

Grignard reagents are prepared and used in dry ether solvents.

A Grignard reagent or Grignard compound is a chemical compound with the general formula R−Mg−X, where X is a halogen and R is an organic group, normally an alkyl or aryl. They are a class of organomagnesium compounds discovered by the French chemist Victor Grignard in 1900, work for which he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1912.

Preparation

Grignard reagents are formed by the reaction of metallic magnesium with an organo-halide in a dry ether solvent (commonly diethyl ether or tetrahydrofuran). The ether stabilises the reagent through coordination to magnesium. The actual structure in solution is complicated by the Schlenk equilibrium, a mixture of RMgX, R2Mg and MgX2 species.

Reactions

Because the carbon–magnesium bond is strongly polarised, the carbon acts as a nucleophile and a strong base — formally like a carbanion. Grignard reagents react with:

  • Carbonyl compounds — aldehydes, ketones, esters and carbon dioxide — to form new carbon–carbon bonds, giving alcohols (or carboxylic acids from CO2) after work-up; this is the classic Grignard reaction, the most important synthetic use.
  • Protic compounds — water, alcohols, amines and terminal alkynes destroy the reagent, protonating R to RH; hence the need for rigorously dry conditions.
  • Electrophiles generally — epoxides, imines, alkyl halides (coupling), and many others.

Importance

The ability to form carbon–carbon bonds reliably made Grignard reagents foundational to synthetic organic chemistry, and they remain workhorses in research and industry, for example in the industrial synthesis of many pharmaceuticals. Their chemistry led directly to related organometallic reagents, including the more reactive organolithiums and the modern cross-coupling catalysts.
