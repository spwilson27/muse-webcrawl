Carnot cycle

TypeThermodynamic cycle
Proposed byNicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot, 1824
Stages2 isothermal + 2 adiabatic processes

The Carnot cycle is a theoretical ideal thermodynamic cycle describing how a heat engine works. It was introduced by the French engineer Sadi Carnot in his 1824 work Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire. The Carnot cycle is the most efficient cycle operating between two given thermal reservoirs, and its efficiency sets an upper bound that no real engine working between the same temperatures can exceed.


The Carnot cycle drawn on pressure–volume and temperature–entropy diagrams.

The cycle

The cycle operates on a fixed mass of working fluid in four reversible stages:

  • Isothermal expansion — the fluid absorbs heat QH from the hot reservoir at constant temperature TH, expanding and doing work.
  • Adiabatic expansion — insulated from its surroundings, the fluid expands further and cools to TC.
  • Isothermal compression — the fluid rejects heat QC to the cold reservoir at constant temperature TC while being compressed.
  • Adiabatic compression — the fluid is compressed back to its initial state, its temperature returning to TH.

Efficiency and Carnot's theorem

The thermal efficiency depends only on the reservoir temperatures: η = 1 − TC/TH, with temperatures in kelvin. Carnot's theorem states that no engine operating between two heat reservoirs can be more efficient than a Carnot engine between the same reservoirs, and that all reversible engines between the same reservoirs have the same efficiency, regardless of the working fluid.

Significance

Although no real engine can run a truly reversible cycle, the Carnot cycle founded the science of thermodynamics. Analysis of Carnot's reasoning by Clapeyron, Kelvin, and Clausius led to the concepts of absolute temperature and entropy and to the second law of thermodynamics. Run in reverse, the cycle describes the ideal refrigerator or heat pump, whose coefficient of performance is likewise bounded by the reservoir temperatures.
