Muscle spindle

Muscle spindles are stretch receptors within skeletal muscle that detect changes in the length of the muscle and the rate of that change. They are essential for proprioception — the sense of body position — and for the stretch reflex.

Structure

A spindle consists of modified muscle fibres called intrafusal fibres (nuclear bag and nuclear chain fibres) enclosed in a connective tissue capsule, lying parallel to the ordinary extrafusal fibres. Sensory endings — primary (annulospiral) endings served by group Ia afferents and secondary endings served by group II afferents — wrap around the intrafusal fibres.

Function

When a muscle is stretched, spindle afferents increase their firing, exciting alpha motor neurons that make the muscle contract — the basis of the monosynaptic stretch reflex, such as the knee jerk. Gamma motor neurons adjust spindle sensitivity by contracting the intrafusal fibres, keeping the spindle responsive across muscle lengths (alpha–gamma coactivation).
