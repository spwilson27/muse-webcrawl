The Codex Regius (Latin: "Royal Book"; Icelandic: Konungsbók) is an Icelandic codex (shelfmark GKS 2365 4to) in which many of the Old Norse poems of the Poetic Edda are preserved. Written in the 1270s, it is the single most important source for Norse mythology and Germanic heroic legend.

The manuscript consists of 45 vellum leaves containing 29 poems — mythological lays about the gods (including Völuspá, Hávamál, and Vafþrúðnismál) and heroic lays about figures such as Sigurd the dragon-slayer. A gathering of eight leaves is missing between two sections, the so-called "Great Lacuna", in which part of the Sigurd cycle was lost.

History

Nothing is known of the codex's whereabouts until 1643, when it came into the possession of Brynjólfur Sveinsson, Bishop of Skálholt, who attributed its poems to the medieval scholar Sæmundr the Learned. In 1662 Brynjólfur presented it to King Frederick III of Denmark — hence the name Codex Regius — and it was kept in the Royal Library in Copenhagen. In 1971, amid Denmark's return of Icelandic manuscripts, the codex was transported back to Iceland by naval ship, arriving to public celebration on 21 April 1971. It is now held by the Árni Magnússon Institute for Icelandic Studies in Reykjavík.

Contents

The mythological poems include Völuspá (the seeress's prophecy of the creation and doom of the gods), Hávamál (Odin's wisdom sayings), Þrymskviða (the theft of Thor's hammer), and Lokasenna. The heroic section recounts the Völsung and Nibelung legends, the Helgi lays, and the laments of Gudrun.
