Social enterprise

A social enterprise is an organization that applies commercial strategies to maximize improvements in human and environmental well-being rather than maximizing profits for external shareholders. Social enterprises can be structured as for-profit or non-profit entities, and may take forms such as cooperatives, mutual organizations, or trading arms of charities.

Definitions

There is no single agreed definition. The UK government has described a social enterprise as "a business with primarily social objectives whose surpluses are principally reinvested for that purpose." The European Research Network (EMES) identifies economic, social, and participatory governance criteria. In the United States the term is used more loosely, overlapping with social entrepreneurship and benefit corporations.

History

Precursors include 19th-century cooperatives and Muhammad Yunus's Grameen Bank, founded in Bangladesh in 1983 to provide microcredit. The term gained currency in the United Kingdom in the 1990s, and in 2005 the UK created the community interest company (CIC), a legal form with an asset lock for social enterprises.

Legal forms

Depending on jurisdiction, social enterprises may incorporate as CICs, benefit corporations or B Corps (certified by B Lab), low-profit limited liability companies (L3Cs), charities with trading subsidiaries, or worker cooperatives. Funding may come from trading revenue, grants, social investment, and social impact bonds.

Debates

Supporters see social enterprise as combining market discipline with mission. Critics question whether commercial pressures dilute social goals ("mission drift"), whether the label is used for marketing, and how social impact should be measured.
