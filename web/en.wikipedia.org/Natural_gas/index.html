Natural gas

Natural gas (also called fossil gas, methane gas, or simply gas) is a naturally occurring mixture of gaseous hydrocarbons consisting primarily of methane (typically 70–90%), in addition to varying quantities of other higher alkanes (ethane, propane, butane), and sometimes a small percentage of carbon dioxide, nitrogen, hydrogen sulfide, or helium. It is a fossil fuel and non-renewable resource formed when layers of organic matter, mostly marine microorganisms, decompose under anaerobic conditions and are subjected to intense heat and pressure underground over millions of years.

Formation and sources

Natural gas is found in deep underground rock formations, often alongside petroleum deposits (associated gas) or in its own reservoirs (non-associated gas). It also occurs in coal seams (coalbed methane) and in low-permeability shale formations, from which it is extracted by hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") and horizontal drilling — techniques that drove the United States shale gas boom in the 2000s and made the U.S. the world's largest producer. Russia, Iran and Qatar hold the largest proved reserves. Biogas produced by anaerobic digestion of wastes is chemically similar but renewable.

Processing and transport

Raw natural gas is processed to remove water, heavier hydrocarbons (natural gas liquids), hydrogen sulfide ("sour gas" treatment) and other impurities. Pipeline-quality gas is transported through extensive pipeline networks; where pipelines are impractical it is liquefied at about −162 °C to form liquefied natural gas (LNG), which occupies roughly 1/600th the volume and is shipped in specialised tankers. In its pure form the gas is colourless and odourless; odorants such as mercaptan are added to domestic supplies so leaks can be detected by smell.

Uses

Natural gas is used for electricity generation in gas-fired power plants (about a quarter of world electricity), for residential and commercial heating and cooking, as an industrial fuel and feedstock — notably for ammonia and fertiliser production via steam reforming and for hydrogen production — and, in compressed form (CNG), as a vehicle fuel. At the point of use it emits less carbon dioxide per unit of energy than coal or oil.

Environmental impact

Burning natural gas produces roughly half the CO2 emissions of coal per unit of electricity, and gas generation displaced coal in many power systems. However, natural gas is chiefly methane, a greenhouse gas far more potent than CO2 over short timescales, so leakage during extraction, processing and distribution can offset much of its climate advantage. Gas flaring and venting add further emissions. Its role as a "bridge fuel" in energy transitions is debated, as is the long-term risk of stranded gas infrastructure as renewables and storage become cheaper.

Safety

Natural gas is explosive at concentrations of about 5–15% in air, and incomplete combustion can produce carbon monoxide. Distribution leaks and explosions, though rare, periodically cause serious accidents, and ageing pipe networks are a continuing safety concern.
