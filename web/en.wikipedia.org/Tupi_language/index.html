Tupi, more specifically Old Tupi (also called Tupinambá), is an extinct Tupian language that was spoken by the Tupi peoples along the coast of what is now Brazil, from the region of present-day São Paulo to Maranhão, at the time of the Portuguese colonisation of the Americas. It was the first Brazilian language to be described by Europeans and became the basis of the línguas gerais (general languages) used for communication throughout colonial Brazil.

History

Jesuit missionaries, above all José de Anchieta — whose Arte de grammatica da lingoa mais usada na costa do Brasil (1595) was the first grammar of the language — learned, standardised and wrote Tupi, using it in catechesis and administration. Varieties of Tupi mixed with Portuguese formed the Língua Geral Paulista in the south and the Língua Geral Amazônica (Nheengatu) in the north; for much of the colonial period these were spoken by more people in Brazil than Portuguese itself. In the 1750s the Marquis of Pombal's reforms suppressed the mission languages and imposed Portuguese, and Old Tupi declined rapidly.

Legacy

Tupi left a deep mark on Brazilian Portuguese, contributing thousands of words, especially for native animals, plants and place names: e.g. jaguar (from îagûara), piranha, tapioca, capybara (via related Guarani/Tupi forms). Nheengatu, its Amazonian descendant, survives as a co-official language in the municipality of São Gabriel da Cachoeira and is still spoken by thousands in the upper Rio Negro region. Old Tupi has also been studied and partly revived by scholars and enthusiasts in Brazil.
