Intentional fallacy

The intentional fallacy is a term used in literary criticism to describe the fallacy of judging or interpreting a work of literature by reference to the author's intention. The term was coined by W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley in their 1946 essay "The Intentional Fallacy", a foundational text of the New Criticism. They argued that "the design or intention of the author is neither available nor desirable as a standard for judging the success of a work of literary art."

Argument

Wimsatt and Beardsley held that a poem, once published, belongs to the public: its meaning is to be found in the words on the page and the publicly available conventions of language, not in the author's private mental states or later testimony. Evidence about a work is therefore of three kinds: internal evidence (the semantics and syntax of the work itself), external evidence (diaries, letters and statements by the author), and intermediate evidence (about the author's character and habits). Only internal evidence, they argued, is properly part of criticism. Judging a poem by whether it achieves the author's aim confuses the poem with its origins — akin to judging a pudding by the cook's intentions.

Context and debate

The essay, later collected in Wimsatt's The Verbal Icon (1954), paired with Wimsatt and Beardsley's "The Affective Fallacy" (1949) as the twin pillars of New Critical method. The concept anticipates Roland Barthes' "death of the author" (1967) and parallels arguments in analytic aesthetics. Critics such as E. D. Hirsch Jr. defended intentionalism, arguing in Validity in Interpretation (1967) that meaning is what the author meant and that abandoning intention makes interpretation arbitrary. Debate between intentionalists, anti-intentionalists and moderate ("hypothetical") intentionalists continues in aesthetics today.
