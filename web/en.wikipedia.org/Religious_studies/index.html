Religious studies

Religious studies, also known as the study of religion, is the academic field devoted to research into religious beliefs, behaviors, and institutions. It is an interdisciplinary or multidisciplinary field drawing upon methodologies from multiple disciplines, including anthropology, sociology, psychology, philosophy, and history of religion.

Religious studies examines the world's religions from a scholarly, non-confessional standpoint.

Religious studies emerged primarily in English, German, and Dutch academia in the nineteenth century. Scholars who describe their work as religious studies are usually trained in diverse scholarly traditions. The field distinguishes itself from theology in that it approaches religious phenomena from an ostensibly neutral, comparative standpoint rather than from within a particular faith tradition.

History

The comparative study of religion has roots in the work of nineteenth-century scholars such as Friedrich Max Müller, who famously declared that "he who knows one religion knows none." Universities in Germany, the Netherlands, and later Britain and North America established chairs in the history of religions during this period. In the United States, the field expanded rapidly after the 1963 Supreme Court decision in Abington School District v. Schempp, which permitted the academic study of religion in public schools while prohibiting devotional instruction.

Approaches

Major approaches within religious studies include the phenomenology of religion, associated with figures such as Gerardus van der Leeuw and Mircea Eliade; the anthropology of religion, shaped by Clifford Geertz's influential definition of religion as a cultural system; the sociology of religion, descending from Émile Durkheim and Max Weber; and the cognitive science of religion, which investigates the mental mechanisms underlying religious thought and practice.
