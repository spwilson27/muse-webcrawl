Classical Nahuatl

Language family: Uto-Aztecan → Nahuan
Era: c. 1350–1800s
Region: Valley of Mexico and central Mexico
Writing: Aztec pictorial script; Latin alphabet after the conquest

Classical Nahuatl is the variety of the Nahuatl language that was the lingua franca of the Aztec (Mexica) Empire and of central Mexico in the 16th and 17th centuries. It is the best-documented indigenous language of the Americas from the colonial period, preserved in an extensive corpus of alphabetic texts written by Nahua scribes after the Spanish conquest.

Features

Classical Nahuatl is a polysynthetic, agglutinative language with a complex system of honorific speech, absolutive noun suffixes (-tl, -tli, -li), and productive noun incorporation. Its phonology features a glottal stop (the saltillo) and a lateral affricate /tɬ/, written tl. A reverential register, using special prefixes and suffixes, was used when addressing nobles and deities.

Literature and sources

Sources include the Florentine Codex compiled under Bernardino de Sahagún, the annals and primordial titles of Nahua towns, testaments, land records, and the Cantares Mexicanos, a collection of songs and poems. Franciscan friars such as Andrés de Olmos and Horacio Carochi produced influential grammars; Carochi's Arte de la lengua mexicana (1645) is considered a landmark of linguistic description.

Modern Nahuatl variants, spoken today by well over a million people, descend from Classical Nahuatl and its regional contemporaries.
