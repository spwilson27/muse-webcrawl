Arch

An arch is a curved vertical structure that spans an elevated space and may or may not support the weight above it, or in the case of an arch bridge or a dam, the horizontal thrust. Arches appeared as early as the 2nd millennium BC in Mesopotamian brick architecture, and their systematic use started with the ancient Romans, who were the first to apply the technique to a wide range of structures.

How it works

A true arch converts vertical load into compressive stresses carried along the curve to the abutments at either side; the wedge-shaped blocks of a masonry arch are called voussoirs, and the central one is the keystone. Because the structure works mainly in compression, it suits materials such as stone and unreinforced concrete that are strong in compression but weak in tension. The shape may be semicircular (Roman), pointed (Gothic), segmental, parabolic or catenary, each distributing forces differently.

History

Etruscan and then Roman builders used arches in aqueducts, bridges, triumphal monuments and the Colosseum. The arch remained fundamental through Byzantine, Romanesque and Islamic architecture; the pointed arch enabled the soaring vaults of Gothic cathedrals. With industrialisation, arches were built in iron, steel and reinforced concrete, culminating in long-span structures such as the Sydney Harbour Bridge.

Types

Arches are classified by shape (round, pointed, horseshoe, Tudor, and many others) and by structural action (fixed, two-hinged, three-hinged). Related forms include the vault (an extruded arch), the dome (a rotated arch) and the corbel arch, which is not a true arch because it does not convert load into pure compression.
