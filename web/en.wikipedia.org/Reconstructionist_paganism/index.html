Reconstructionist paganism

Reconstructionist pagans rebuild historical polytheistic religions from scholarly and archaeological sources.

Reconstructionist paganism, or simply reconstructionism, is an approach to modern paganism first articulated in the late 1960s and 1970s that emphasizes rebuilding pre-Christian religions as faithfully as possible from historical, archaeological, linguistic and folkloric evidence, as distinct from more eclectic or occult movements such as Wicca, which freely blend sources and modern inventions.

Approach

Reconstructionists seek to reconstruct the beliefs, practices, ethics and worldview of a specific historical culture — for example ancient Germanic, Celtic, Greek, Roman, Egyptian, Slavic or Baltic society — and to adapt them, where necessary, to contemporary life. Adherents typically study primary sources such as the Poetic Edda, classical authors, inscriptions and saga literature, consult academic scholarship, and are expected to be transparent about which elements are historically attested and which are modern innovations filling gaps in the record.

Traditions

Major reconstructionist families include Heathenry, the modern revival of Germanic paganism (subdivided into Ásatrú, Theodism, Forn Sed and others); Hellenism, devoted to the ancient Greek gods; Religio Romana and groups such as Nova Roma, reviving Roman religion; Kemetism, reviving ancient Egyptian religion; and Baltic and Slavic revivals such as Romuva and Rodnovery, some of which in Eastern Europe draw on surviving folk tradition as well as texts.

Relationship to other pagan movements

Reconstructionists frequently distinguish themselves from eclectic neopaganism by their emphasis on cultural specificity, scholarship and devotion to particular gods, and they sometimes criticize invented traditions presented as ancient. Critics within the wider pagan community respond that complete reconstruction is impossible, that sources are fragmentary and biased, and that all living religions necessarily adapt. Reconstructionism overlaps with the broader phenomenon sometimes called ethnic or traditionalist paganism, but politically it spans a wide spectrum, and major reconstructionist organizations have publicly rejected racist appropriations of their traditions.
