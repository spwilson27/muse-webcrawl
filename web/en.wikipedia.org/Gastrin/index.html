Gastrin is a peptide hormone that stimulates the secretion of gastric acid (hydrochloric acid) by the parietal cells of the stomach and aids in gastric motility. It is released by G cells in the pyloric antrum of the stomach, in the duodenum, and in the pancreas, in response to stimuli such as stomach distension, the presence of peptides and amino acids, and vagal stimulation.

Physiology

Gastrin is synthesised as a large precursor (preprogastrin) and processed into several active forms, principally the 34-amino-acid "big gastrin" (G-34) and the 17-amino-acid "little gastrin" (G-17). Acting through the CCK-B receptor, it drives acid secretion both directly and by stimulating histamine release from enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells. Acid in the stomach lumen feeds back to shut gastrin off: low pH triggers somatostatin release from D cells, which suppresses the G cells.

Clinical significance

Excess gastrin (hypergastrinaemia) occurs in Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, where a gastrin-secreting tumour (gastrinoma) causes severe, recurrent peptic ulcers. Gastrin levels also rise appropriately when acid production falls, as in atrophic gastritis, pernicious anaemia, or during treatment with proton pump inhibitors. Fasting serum gastrin is the usual screening test.

History

The existence of a gastric secretagogue was proposed by John Sydney Edkins in 1905, but gastrin was not isolated until 1964, when Roderic Gregory and Hilda Tracy at Liverpool extracted it from pig stomach tissue and determined its structure.
