Fossil fuel

Main typesCoal, petroleum (oil), natural gas
FormationDecomposition of ancient organisms under heat and pressure over millions of years
Share of world energy~80% of primary energy (early 2020s)

A fossil fuel is a flammable carbon compound- or hydrocarbon-containing material formed naturally in the Earth's crust from the buried remains of dead plants and animals that is extracted and burned as a fuel. The main fossil fuels are coal, crude oil and natural gas. Fossil fuels may be burned to provide heat for use directly (such as for cooking and heating), to power engines (such as internal combustion engines in motor vehicles), or to generate electricity.

Formation

Coal formed principally from land plants buried in swampy forests of the Carboniferous period and later, compressed into peat and then progressively into lignite, bituminous coal and anthracite. Oil and gas formed from marine plankton and algae buried in ocean sediments, transformed by heat and pressure into kerogen and then hydrocarbons, which migrated into reservoir rock — the same sedimentary sequences that yield fossils of the source organisms.

Use and importance

Fossil fuels powered the Industrial Revolution and remain the largest source of global energy, as well as feedstocks for plastics, fertilisers and petrochemicals. Their ease of transport and high energy density underpin modern agriculture, aviation and electricity generation.

Environmental impact

Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide — roughly 37 billion tonnes per year in the early 2020s — and is the dominant driver of human-caused climate change. Extraction and combustion also cause air pollution, oil spills and land disturbance. The IPCC and the International Energy Agency project that unabated fossil fuel use is incompatible with limiting warming to 1.5 °C, driving global efforts toward renewable energy and electrification.
