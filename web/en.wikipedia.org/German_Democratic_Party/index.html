The German Democratic Party (German: Deutsche Demokratische Partei, DDP) was a liberal political party in the Weimar Republic. Founded in November 1918 from the merger of the Progressive People's Party and the liberal wing of the National Liberal Party, it was one of the founding parties of the republic and a mainstay of the pro-democracy "Weimar coalition" with the Social Democrats and the Centre Party.

Foundation and early success

The DDP's founders included the sociologist Max Weber, journalist Theodor Wolff, and jurists Hugo Preuß — the principal drafter of the Weimar Constitution — and sociologist Alfred Weber. Its programme combined commitment to the democratic republic, civil rights and a social market economy with nationalist positions on revising the Treaty of Versailles. In the January 1919 election to the National Assembly, the DDP won 18.6 percent of the vote and 75 seats, making it the third-largest party, and DDP politicians served in most Weimar governments of the 1920s. Walther Rathenau, briefly foreign minister before his assassination in 1922, was the party's most prominent figure.

Decline

Through the 1920s the party steadily lost middle-class voters to the more conservative German People's Party and to special-interest parties: its vote share fell to about 8 percent in 1924 and under 5 percent by 1930. In 1930 the rump DDP merged with the nationalist Young German Order to form the German State Party (Deutsche Staatspartei, DStP), an attempted relaunch that failed to reverse the decline.

Dissolution and legacy

After the Nazi seizure of power, the State Party's deputies voted for the Enabling Act of 1933 and the party dissolved itself under pressure in July 1933; leading members such as Theodor Heuss and Reinhold Maier went on to co-found the post-war Free Democratic Party (FDP) and, in Heuss's case, to become the first President of the Federal Republic of Germany. Historians regard the collapse of the DDP as emblematic of the erosion of the liberal democratic centre in Weimar Germany.
