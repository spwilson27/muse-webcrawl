Ptolemaic system

In astronomy, the Ptolemaic system is a geocentric model of the universe developed by the Hellenistic astronomer Claudius Ptolemy in the 2nd century AD, most fully in his treatise the Almagest. It placed the Earth at (or near) the centre of the universe, with the Sun, Moon, planets and stars revolving around it in a series of nested spheres. Refined over many centuries, it remained the authoritative cosmology in the Byzantine, Islamic and European worlds for more than 1,400 years, until displaced by the heliocentric system of Copernicus in the 16th and 17th centuries.

Mechanism

To account for the planets' varying speeds and retrograde motion, Ptolemy combined three devices inherited and developed from earlier Greek astronomy: the deferent, a large circle centred near the Earth; the epicycle, a smaller circle whose centre moved along the deferent while the planet moved around the epicycle; and the equant, an off-centre point about which the epicycle's centre moved with uniform angular speed. The equant improved predictive accuracy considerably but violated the classical ideal of uniform circular motion about the centre — a feature that troubled later astronomers, including Copernicus.

Accuracy and legacy

The Ptolemaic system could predict planetary positions to within a degree or so — good enough for naked-eye astronomy, calendars and astrology — and its mathematical techniques (chord tables, spherical trigonometry) were major achievements. Islamic astronomers such as Ibn al-Shatir developed alternative configurations that removed the equant; strikingly similar geometry later appeared in Copernicus's work. The system's long dominance illustrates both its empirical adequacy and the institutional weight of Aristotelian physics, with which it was integrated in medieval natural philosophy.
