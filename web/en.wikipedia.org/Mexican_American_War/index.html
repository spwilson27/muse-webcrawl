Mexican–American War

The Mexican–American War (1846-1848), known in Mexico as the United States intervention in Mexico, was an armed conflict between the United States and Mexico fought over the US annexation of Texas and a disputed border: the US claimed the Rio Grande as Texas's boundary, Mexico the Nueces River. US President James K. Polk, an expansionist committed to Manifest Destiny, sought also to acquire California and New Mexico.

Course

After a clash in the disputed territory in April 1846, Congress declared war. US forces under Zachary Taylor won battles at Palo Alto, Monterrey and Buena Vista in northern Mexico; Winfield Scott landed at Veracruz and marched inland, defeating Santa Anna's armies and capturing Mexico City in September 1847 after battles including Cerro Gordo, Contreras, Churubusco, Molino del Rey and Chapultepec. Other US forces occupied New Mexico and California, where the Bear Flag Revolt and naval landings had already begun the conquest.

Treaty and consequences

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (2 February 1848) ended the war. Mexico recognised the Rio Grande border and ceded the vast Mexican Cession — present-day California, Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona, and parts of New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming — for $15 million plus assumed claims. The war was controversial in the US: Whigs and abolitionists, including Congressman Abraham Lincoln and later Ulysses S. Grant, who fought in it, condemned it as unjust aggression to expand slave territory; the new lands inflamed the dispute over slavery that led to the Civil War. In Mexico it remains a symbol of national loss and resistance.
