Basic income, in its fullest form called universal basic income (UBI), is a social welfare proposal in which all members of a society regularly receive a sum of money from the government or another public institution, without a means test or work requirement. A basic income system differs from existing welfare systems in being paid to individuals rather than households, paid unconditionally, and paid to everyone regardless of other income.

History

Ideas resembling basic income appear in Thomas More's Utopia (1516) and in Thomas Paine's Agrarian Justice (1797), which proposed payments funded from ground rents to compensate for the enclosure of common land. In the 20th century the idea was advanced from several directions: social credit and social dividend proposals in Britain, Milton Friedman's negative income tax in the United States, and support from Martin Luther King Jr. as a means of abolishing poverty. US President Richard Nixon's Family Assistance Plan, a guaranteed-income scheme, passed the House of Representatives in 1969–70 but failed in the Senate.

Pilots and related programmes

Experiments and quasi-experiments include the Mincome experiment in Manitoba in the 1970s, which found modest reductions in labour supply concentrated among new mothers and students, alongside improved health outcomes; Finland's 2017–18 experiment paying 2,000 unemployed people €560 per month, associated with improved well-being and slightly higher employment; and long-running cash-transfer studies by GiveDirectly in Kenya. The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend, paid annually to all residents since 1982, is often cited as the closest existing analogue of a small, permanent basic income.

Arguments

Supporters argue basic income reduces poverty and its stigma, simplifies welfare administration, strengthens workers' bargaining power, and provides a safety net in economies disrupted by automation. Critics raise the gross cost of a payment large enough to live on, possible labour-supply effects, inflation concerns, and argue that targeted programmes deliver more help per unit of spending. Funding proposals include consolidating existing welfare programmes, new taxes (income, consumption, carbon or resource rents), and sovereign wealth funds.
