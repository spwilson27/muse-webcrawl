Polypropylene

Polypropylene (PP), also known as polypropene, is a thermoplastic polymer used in a wide variety of applications. It is produced via chain-growth polymerization from the monomer propylene. Polypropylene belongs to the group of polyolefins and is partially crystalline and non-polar. Its properties are similar to polyethylene, but it is slightly harder and more heat resistant. It is a white, mechanically rugged material and has a high chemical resistance.

History

Crystalline polypropylene was first polymerized in 1951 by J. Paul Hogan and Robert Banks at Phillips Petroleum, and independently in 1954 by the Italian chemist Giulio Natta, who developed the coordination catalysts (later Ziegler–Natta catalysts) that made highly isotactic polypropylene possible. Commercial production began in 1957, and polypropylene became one of the most important commodity plastics; Natta shared the 1963 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

Properties and forms

Polypropylene is produced as homopolymers and as copolymers with ethylene. Depending on tacticity — the arrangement of methyl groups along the chain — it ranges from soft, atactic material to stiff, highly crystalline isotactic grades. It has excellent fatigue resistance (the "living hinge" of flip-top bottles), low density, good resistance to acids, alkalis and solvents, and a higher working temperature than polyethylene. It is, however, degraded by UV light unless stabilized.

Applications

Polypropylene is used for packaging (films, bottles, food containers, caps and closures), textiles (ropes, thermal underwear, carpets and nonwoven fabrics such as surgical masks), automotive parts, laboratory and medical equipment, banknotes (polymer notes in countries such as Australia), stationery, and countless moulded household goods. It is the second most widely produced commodity plastic after polyethylene.
