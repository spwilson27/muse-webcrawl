Selenocysteine

Selenocysteine differs from cysteine only in that its sulfur atom is replaced by selenium.

Selenocysteine (symbol Sec or U, one-letter code U) is a rare amino acid found in certain enzymes across all three domains of life. It is structurally similar to cysteine, but with the sulfur atom replaced by selenium, forming a selenol group. Selenocysteine is often called the "21st amino acid" because, unlike the standard twenty, it is encoded by the genetic code in a special context-dependent way.

Encoding and synthesis

Selenocysteine is inserted into proteins at the UGA codon, normally a stop codon. Recoding requires a specific messenger-RNA structural element, the SECIS element (selenocysteine insertion sequence), located in the 3′ untranslated region in eukaryotes, together with dedicated factors including a special tRNA (tRNASec) and the elongation factor SelB/EFSec. The amino acid is synthesised on its own tRNA: seryl-tRNA is first charged with serine, which is converted enzymatically to selenocysteine using selenophosphate as the selenium donor.

Selenoproteins

Proteins containing selenocysteine are called selenoproteins. Humans have 25 selenoprotein genes, including the glutathione peroxidases and thioredoxin reductases, which defend against oxidative damage, and the iodothyronine deiodinases, which activate and deactivate thyroid hormones. The selenol group is ionised at physiological pH and is a stronger nucleophile and redox agent than the thiol of cysteine, which is thought to explain its catalytic advantages — and its rarity, given selenium's scarcity and toxicity at high doses.

Discovery

Selenocysteine was identified in the 1970s as the selenium-containing component of clostridial glycine reductase and mammalian glutathione peroxidase, work led by Thressa Stadtman and others. Its unusual encoding mechanism, unravelled in the 1980s, was an early demonstration that the genetic code is not universal in a simple sense.
