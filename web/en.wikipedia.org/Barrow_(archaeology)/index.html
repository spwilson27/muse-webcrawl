Barrow (archaeology)

In archaeology, a barrow is a mound of earth and stones raised over one or more graves. Barrows are found throughout the world in many cultures, under various names — tumulus in the classical tradition and kurgan in the Eurasian steppe among them. In Britain the term is used especially for prehistoric burial mounds of the Neolithic and Bronze Ages.

Types

Long barrows, built in the Early Neolithic (from around 3800 BC in Britain), are elongated mounds, often covering stone or timber burial chambers and forecourts used for ritual activity. Round barrows, characteristic of the Early Bronze Age (roughly 2200–1100 BC), take forms classified as bowl, bell, disc, saucer, and pond barrows, and often contain cremation or inhumation burials accompanied by grave goods such as pottery, weapons, and ornaments. Later peoples also built barrows: Anglo-Saxon and Viking burial mounds such as those at Sutton Hoo and the Heath Wood cemetery continued the tradition into the early medieval period.

Distribution and study

Barrows survive in large numbers in the chalk downlands of southern England — around Stonehenge and Avebury, for example — and across Ireland, northern Europe, and the steppe. Antiquarian excavations of barrows in the 18th and 19th centuries were foundational to the development of archaeology, though many mounds were ploughed out in the 20th century.
