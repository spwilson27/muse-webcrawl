Beltane

Beltane is the Gaelic May Day festival, traditionally held on 1 May or about halfway between the spring equinox and summer solstice. Historically observed across Ireland, Scotland, and the Isle of Man, it marked the beginning of summer, when cattle were driven out to summer pastures. Rituals were performed to protect cattle, crops, and people, and to encourage growth. Central to the festival were bonfires, whose flames, smoke, and ashes were deemed to have protective powers. Special bonfires were kindled on hilltops, and cattle were driven between two fires or around them for protection from disease and supernatural harm.

Historical customs

Early medieval Irish texts describe Beltane as one of the four seasonal festivals, alongside Samhain, Imbolc, and Lughnasa. Yellow flowers such as primrose and marigold were strewed at doorways and windows, and branches of rowan were used as protection. Offerings were made to the aos sí, the spirits or fairies, to ensure their goodwill. A special Beltane bannock (oatcake) was baked, sometimes with a marked piece whose chooser was subjected to playful rituals. A perpetual fire was maintained at the Hill of Uisneach, the mythological centre of Ireland, where a great bonfire was lit.

Survivals and revival

Many Beltane customs persisted into the 19th and 20th centuries in parts of Ireland and the Scottish Highlands, including May bushes, May flowers, and visits to holy wells. In the late 20th century the festival was revived by Celtic neopagans and Wiccans, for whom Beltane is one of the eight sabbats of the Wheel of the Year, associated with fertility and the union of god and goddess. The Beltane Fire Festival, founded in Edinburgh in 1988, draws thousands of spectators each year to Calton Hill.
