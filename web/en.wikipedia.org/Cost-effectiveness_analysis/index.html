Cost-effectiveness analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is a form of economic analysis that compares the relative costs and outcomes (effects) of different courses of action. Unlike cost–benefit analysis, which assigns monetary values to outcomes, CEA measures outcomes in natural or standardised units — such as life-years gained, cases averted, or quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) — and expresses results as a ratio, for example cost per QALY gained.

Method

The central statistic is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER): the difference in cost between two interventions divided by the difference in their effects. Interventions that cost more and produce less benefit are "dominated" and rejected. Decision-makers compare ICERs against a willingness-to-pay threshold; in the United Kingdom, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) generally applies a threshold in the region of £20,000–£30,000 per QALY when appraising new health technologies.

Applications

CEA is most prominent in health economics and public health — evaluating drugs, vaccines, screening programmes and surgical procedures — but it is also used in environmental policy (cost per tonne of emissions avoided), education, transport safety and defence. Sensitivity analysis, including probabilistic modelling, is used to test how results depend on uncertain parameters such as future costs, discount rates and treatment efficacy.

Limitations

Critics note that CEA results depend on the perspective adopted (health system versus societal), the choice of comparator and time horizon, and that QALY-based thresholds raise ethical questions about valuing life extension for different patient groups. Cost-effectiveness also says nothing about affordability or budget impact, which require separate analysis.
