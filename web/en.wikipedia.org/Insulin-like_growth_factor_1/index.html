Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), also called somatomedin C, is a hormone similar in molecular structure to insulin that plays an important role in childhood growth and has anabolic effects in adults. It is produced chiefly by the liver in response to growth hormone (GH) released by the pituitary gland, and it mediates many of GH's growth-promoting actions — the basis of the "somatomedin hypothesis". IGF-1 consists of 70 amino acids in a single chain with three intramolecular disulfide bridges and a molecular weight of about 7,649 daltons.

Synthesis and circulation

The human IGF1 gene lies on chromosome 12. Hepatic IGF-1 production is stimulated by growth hormone and is also strongly influenced by nutritional status and insulin; malnutrition and insulin deficiency reduce IGF-1 despite elevated GH. In the circulation, more than 99% of IGF-1 is bound to IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs), principally IGFBP-3 together with an acid-labile subunit, which prolong its half-life from minutes to many hours and regulate its delivery to tissues. IGF-1 is also produced locally in many tissues — including bone, cartilage and muscle — where it acts in autocrine and paracrine fashion.

Mechanism of action

IGF-1 binds with high affinity to the IGF-1 receptor (IGF1R), a tetrameric receptor tyrosine kinase closely related to the insulin receptor. Activation triggers the PI3K–AKT and MAPK/ERK signalling pathways, promoting cell growth, proliferation, differentiation and survival (inhibition of apoptosis). IGF-1 can also bind the insulin receptor at lower affinity, accounting for its weak insulin-like metabolic effects. Its activity is modulated by the six IGFBPs and by the "mannose-6-phosphate/IGF-2 receptor", which binds the related hormone IGF-2.

Clinical significance

  • Deficiency: Severe primary IGF-1 deficiency (as in Laron syndrome, caused by GH receptor defects) produces severe postnatal growth failure and short stature with normal or high GH levels. Recombinant human IGF-1 (mecasermin) is approved for treating severe primary IGF-1 deficiency.
  • Excess: Overproduction of GH — usually by a pituitary adenoma — raises IGF-1 and causes gigantism in children and acromegaly in adults; measurement of IGF-1 is a key diagnostic and monitoring test for these conditions.
  • Diagnosis: Serum IGF-1, interpreted against age- and sex-matched reference ranges, is used in evaluating short stature and GH disorders because its levels are stable through the day, unlike the pulsatile secretion of GH.
  • Research: The GH/IGF-1 axis is studied in ageing, cancer risk and longevity; reduced IGF-1 signalling extends lifespan in several model organisms, while chronically elevated IGF-1 has been associated epidemiologically with increased risk of some cancers.
