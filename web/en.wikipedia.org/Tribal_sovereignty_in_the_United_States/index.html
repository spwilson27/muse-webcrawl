Tribal sovereignty in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tribal sovereignty in the United States is the legal concept that federally recognised Native American tribes retain inherent powers of self-government. The United States Constitution mentions tribes as distinct political entities (in the Commerce Clause and elsewhere), and the Supreme Court's early "Marshall trilogy" of decisions — Johnson v. McIntosh (1823), Cherokee Nation v. Georgia (1831), and Worcester v. Georgia (1832) — established that tribes are "domestic dependent nations" with rights to their lands and internal self-rule, subject to the overriding authority of the federal government.

Legal foundations

Tribal sovereignty is understood as inherent — predating the United States and not granted by it — though Congress exercises plenary power over Indian affairs and may limit tribal powers. Tribes govern themselves through constitutions, councils, and courts; they determine their own membership, regulate domestic relations, levy taxes, and maintain police and judicial systems on their reservations. Hundreds of treaties signed between the United States and tribal nations between 1778 and 1871 form part of the legal basis, and treaty rights to hunting, fishing, and water continue to be litigated.

Modern era

Federal policy swung between assimilation and self-government: allotment under the Dawes Act (1887) broke up tribal landholdings; the Indian Reorganization Act (1934) encouraged tribal constitutions; the termination policy of the 1950s ended recognition of many tribes; and the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975) inaugurated the modern era of self-determination, under which tribes administer federal programmes themselves. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (1988) recognised tribal gaming rights, reshaping many tribal economies.

Landmark modern cases include United States v. Sioux Nation (1980), California v. Cabazon Band (1987), and McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), in which the Supreme Court held that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation had never been disestablished, with major jurisdictional consequences in eastern Oklahoma. There are 574 federally recognised tribes in the United States today.
