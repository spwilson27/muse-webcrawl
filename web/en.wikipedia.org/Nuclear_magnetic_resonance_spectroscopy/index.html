Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, most commonly known as NMR spectroscopy or magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS), is a spectroscopic technique based on re-orientation of atomic nuclei with non-zero nuclear spins in an external magnetic field. This re-orientation occurs at a characteristic frequency for each nucleus, and energy is absorbed from radio-frequency radiation applied at that frequency. NMR is one of the most powerful techniques available for determining the structure of organic compounds and biomolecules.

Principle

Atomic nuclei with an odd number of protons or neutrons possess an intrinsic angular momentum, or spin, and an associated magnetic moment. In a strong external magnetic field the nuclear spins align in a small number of orientations at different energies; transitions between them are induced by radio-frequency pulses. The exact resonance frequency of a nucleus depends on its local chemical environment — the chemical shift — because surrounding electrons shield the nucleus from the applied field. Interactions between neighbouring spins (spin–spin coupling) split signals into multiplets, giving information about connectivity.

Instrumentation

Modern NMR spectrometers use superconducting magnets operating at fields of roughly 9–28 tesla (400 MHz to over 1.2 GHz for protons). The sample, usually dissolved in a deuterated solvent, is placed in the magnet's bore; a pulsed radio-frequency transmitter excites the nuclei and the resulting free induction decay is recorded and Fourier-transformed to give the spectrum.

Applications

Proton and carbon-13 NMR are routine tools in organic chemistry for verifying structures. Two-dimensional experiments such as COSY, HSQC and NOESY allow the complete assignment of spectra of complex molecules. In structural biology, NMR determines solution structures of proteins and nucleic acids and probes molecular dynamics; in medicine, the same physical principles underpin magnetic resonance imaging.

History

Nuclear magnetic resonance was first measured in molecular beams by Isidor Rabi in 1938 and in bulk matter independently by Felix Bloch and Edward Purcell in 1946; Bloch and Purcell shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics. The discovery of the chemical shift around 1950 turned NMR into a chemical tool.
