Order of succession

An order of succession or right of succession is the line of individuals entitled to hold a high office when it becomes vacated, most famously a monarchy or a headship of state. The order may be specified by a constitution or statute, or determined by tradition and hereditary rules, and it governs the peaceful transfer of power on the death, abdication, or incapacity of the incumbent.


A hereditary line of succession

Forms

  • Primogeniture: succession by the first-born. In male-preference primogeniture sons outrank daughters; in absolute primogeniture the eldest child succeeds regardless of sex — now used by most European monarchies.
  • Salic law: excludes women entirely from succession, historically applied in France and still reflected in Japan's imperial succession law.
  • Seniority and rotation: succession passes among siblings or branches of a family, as in Saudi Arabia's practice among the sons of Ibn Saud.
  • Election or appointment: some offices, including the papacy and certain republican presidencies, have statutory orders of succession for acting officeholders — e.g., the United States presidential line of succession, which runs from the Vice President through the Speaker of the House and cabinet officers.

Stability and dispute

Clear succession rules reduce the risk of civil conflict, historically one of the commonest causes of war — from the Wars of the Roses to the War of the Spanish Succession, both products of contested orders of succession. Modern succession laws often also prescribe rules on religion, marriage, and legitimacy for those in line.
