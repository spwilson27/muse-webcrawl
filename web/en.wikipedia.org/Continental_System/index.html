The Continental System (French: Blocus continental), also known as the Continental Blockade, was a large-scale embargo by Napoleon I against the United Kingdom during the Napoleonic Wars. On 21 November 1806, Napoleon issued the Berlin Decree, which brought into effect a prohibition on all trade between France, its allies and dependencies, and Britain, in response to the British naval blockade of French-controlled coasts and the Orders in Council. The system is unrelated to the Continental Divide Trail in North America despite the similar name.

Implementation and effects

The Milan Decrees (1807) extended the blockade to neutral shipping that complied with British rules. Britain answered with the Orders in Council, and both blockades contributed to the War of 1812 with the United States. The System hurt Britain's exports but also badly damaged French and allied port cities, encouraged smuggling on a massive scale, raised prices, and stimulated import-substituting industries (such as beet sugar and cotton manufacture in the Rhineland). Enforcement required constant policing of Europe's coastline and pushed Napoleon toward further expansion, including the annexation of the Hanseatic cities and Oldenburg.

Collapse

Russia's withdrawal from the System under the strain it placed on its economy was a principal cause of Napoleon's invasion of Russia in 1812; the ensuing disaster, and the defeats of 1813–1814, ended the blockade. Historians judge the Continental System a strategic failure: it could not defeat Britain's maritime trade, and the burden of enforcing it corroded Napoleon's empire.
