Attachment (psycholinguistics)


High vs. low attachment in a parse tree

In psycholinguistics, attachment refers to the way the human sentence parser decides where to attach an ambiguous phrase or clause within the structure of a sentence being read or heard. Attachment preferences are a central topic in the study of sentence processing, because they reveal the strategies and constraints the mind uses to build syntactic structure in real time.

Relative clause attachment

The most studied case involves relative clauses, as in: "Someone shot the servant of the actress who was on the balcony." The clause "who was on the balcony" can attach high (to "servant") or low (to "actress"). Early research by Lyn Frazier and colleagues proposed universal strategies such as Late Closure, favouring low attachment. However, landmark studies by Manuel Carreiras and Charles Clifton showed that Spanish speakers systematically prefer high attachment for such sentences, whereas English speakers prefer low attachment — demonstrating that attachment preferences vary by language and are shaped by factors such as prosody, clause length, and the type of preposition involved.

Other attachment ambiguities

Prepositional-phrase attachment is another classic case: in "The police shot the rioters with guns", the phrase "with guns" can attach to the verb (as an instrument) or to the noun phrase (as a modifier). Experiments using eye-tracking and reading times show that readers resolve such ambiguities using a mix of syntactic principles, lexical information, plausibility and context, findings that have fuelled debate between modular "garden-path" models and constraint-based, interactive models of parsing.

Significance

Attachment research matters beyond theory: it informs models of human language comprehension, cross-linguistic comparison, and the design of natural-language-processing systems that must resolve the same ambiguities.
