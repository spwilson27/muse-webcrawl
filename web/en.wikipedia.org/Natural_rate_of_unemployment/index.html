Natural rate of unemployment

The natural rate of unemployment is the level of unemployment that persists in an economy when the labour market is in equilibrium — the rate arising from frictional and structural factors even when cyclical unemployment is zero. It is closely related to the concept of the NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment).

Origins

The concept was developed independently in 1968 by Milton Friedman, in his presidential address to the American Economic Association, and by Edmund Phelps. Friedman argued that monetary policy could not permanently hold unemployment below its natural rate: attempts to do so would produce ever-accelerating inflation, because workers and firms would adjust their expectations. This expectations-augmented Phillips curve explained the stagflation of the 1970s and undermined the idea of a stable long-run trade-off between inflation and unemployment.

Determinants

The natural rate depends on job-search frictions, the generosity and duration of unemployment benefits, minimum wages, union power, employment protection laws, demographics, and the pace of structural change that mismatches workers' skills with available jobs. It is not directly observable and must be estimated, which makes it a contentious guide for policy.

Criticism

Critics note the imprecision of estimates and the possibility of hysteresis, in which prolonged cyclical unemployment raises the natural rate itself. Despite this, central banks continue to use the concept (often labelled u*) in setting monetary policy.
