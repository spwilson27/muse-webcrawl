Pituitary adenoma

TypeBenign tumour of the pituitary gland
ClassificationMicroadenoma (<10 mm) / macroadenoma (≥10 mm); functioning or non-functioning
Most common typeProlactinoma
Classic visual signBitemporal hemianopsia

A pituitary adenoma is a benign (non-cancerous) tumour arising from the cells of the anterior pituitary gland. Pituitary adenomas are common — small ones are found incidentally in up to about a tenth of adults — and they cause illness either by secreting excess hormone, by compressing nearby structures, or by impairing normal pituitary function. Modern classifications increasingly group them as pituitary neuroendocrine tumours (PitNETs) by cell lineage and hormone production.

Functioning adenomas

Adenomas that secrete hormones produce characteristic syndromes: prolactinomas (the most common type) cause galactorrhoea, amenorrhoea and infertility; growth-hormone-secreting adenomas cause gigantism in children and acromegaly in adults; ACTH-secreting adenomas cause Cushing's disease; TSH- and gonadotropin-secreting adenomas are rarer.

Mass effects

Tumours larger than 10 mm (macroadenomas) can compress the optic chiasm above the gland, classically producing loss of the outer (temporal) halves of the visual fields — bitemporal hemianopsia. Compression of normal pituitary tissue causes hypopituitarism. Sudden haemorrhage or infarction within a tumour, pituitary apoplexy, is a medical emergency presenting with severe headache, visual loss and collapse.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines hormone blood tests, formal visual-field testing and dedicated pituitary MRI. Prolactinomas are usually treated medically with dopamine agonists such as cabergoline, which shrink most tumours. Other adenomas are generally removed by transsphenoidal surgery through the nose and sphenoid sinus; radiotherapy and targeted drugs are used for residual or recurrent disease, with hormone replacement for any resulting pituitary deficits.
