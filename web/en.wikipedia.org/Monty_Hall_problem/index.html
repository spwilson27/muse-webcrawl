Monty Hall problem

The Monty Hall problem is a famous brain teaser in probability, loosely based on the television game show Let's Make a Deal and named after its original host, Monty Hall. It demonstrates how counterintuitive conditional probability can be.

Statement

Suppose you face three doors. Behind one is a car; behind the other two, goats. You pick a door, say door 1. The host, who knows what is behind each door, opens another door, say door 3, which always reveals a goat. He then asks: do you want to switch to door 2? Is switching advantageous?

Solution

Yes — switching wins the car with probability 2/3, while staying wins with probability 1/3. The reasoning: your initial pick had a 1/3 chance of being correct, so the car is behind one of the other two doors with probability 2/3. The host's informed choice of which door to open concentrates that entire 2/3 probability onto the single remaining unopened door. Equivalently, in 2 of the 3 equally likely cases your first pick is a goat, and in exactly those cases switching wins.

History

The problem was posed by Steve Selvin in The American Statistician in 1975. It achieved worldwide fame in 1990 when Marilyn vos Savant's "Ask Marilyn" column in Parade magazine printed the switching answer; nearly a thousand readers with doctorates wrote in to insist she was wrong, before simulations and further analysis confirmed her solution.
