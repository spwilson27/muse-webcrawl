Fixation (visual)

Fixation, in visual perception, is the maintaining of the gaze on a single location. During a fixation the eyes are relatively still, and most visual information intake occurs. Human eyes do not move smoothly across a scene; instead they alternate between rapid jumps called saccades and fixations that typically last 200–300 milliseconds. The study of fixation patterns, usually by eye tracking, is a central method in research on reading, attention and visual search.

Characteristics

Even during fixation the eye is not perfectly still: it exhibits small movements including tremor, drift and microsaccades. These miniature movements prevent the retinal image from fading — a stabilised image disappears from perception within seconds, a phenomenon related to Troxler's fading. Fixations direct the high-resolution fovea to points of interest, so the sequence of fixations reveals where the visual system samples information.

Fixation in reading

In reading, skilled readers fixate most content words, making fixations averaging about 225–250 ms and saccades of roughly 7–9 letter spaces. Regressions — saccades back to earlier text — occur when comprehension falters. Models of eye-movement control in reading, such as E-Z Reader and SWIFT, formalise how lexical processing influences when and where the eyes move.

Clinical relevance

Abnormal fixation behaviour can accompany neurological and psychiatric conditions, and fixation stability is assessed in ophthalmology, for example in patients with macular degeneration who develop a preferred retinal locus outside the damaged fovea.
