Bandsaw

A bandsaw (also written band saw) is a power saw with a long, sharp blade consisting of a continuous band of toothed metal stretched between two or more wheels to cut material. The blade runs in one direction only, which gives bandsaws an even cutting action. They are used principally in woodworking, metalworking, and lumber milling, and are especially valued for cutting irregular or curved shapes, although suitable machines can also make straight cuts and resaw wide boards into thinner stock.

Types

Vertical bandsaws, common in workshops, present a stationary blade at a table through which the workpiece is fed by hand; they excel at freehand curves and intricate shapes. Horizontal bandsaws hold the workpiece stationary while the blade swings down through the cut, and are standard equipment in metal shops for cutting bar stock to length. Portable bandsaws are handheld power tools widely used by plumbers and electricians. Large industrial resaws and sawmill head rigs use wide blades to convert logs and cants into lumber.

Blades

Blades vary in width, tooth pitch, and tooth pattern (regular, skip, and hook being common) to suit the material and type of cut. Carbon steel blades are economical for wood; bi-metal and carbide-tipped blades are used for hard metals and abrasive materials. Because the band is continuous, its ends are welded or brazed to form a loop sized to the machine's wheels.

History

The idea of a saw using an endless band of blade was patented in England by William Newberry in 1809, but practical machines awaited reliable methods of welding blade ends and suitable blade steels later in the nineteenth century. By the early twentieth century bandsaws were standard in sawmills and joinery shops, and smaller bench and floor models became fixtures of home workshops.
