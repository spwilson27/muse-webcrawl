Argument principle

In complex analysis, the argument principle (or Cauchy's argument principle) is a theorem relating the difference between the number of zeros and poles of a meromorphic function inside a closed contour to the winding number of the image of the contour around the origin, or equivalently to the change in the argument of the function along the contour.

Statement

If f(z) is meromorphic inside and on a simple closed contour C, with no zeros or poles on C, then

(1/2πi) ∮C f′(z)/f(z) dz = N − P

where N is the number of zeros of f inside C and P the number of poles, each counted with multiplicity. Equivalently, the change in arg f(z) as z traverses C once counterclockwise is 2π(N − P).

Proof idea

The integrand f′/f has simple poles at the zeros and poles of f, with residues equal to the multiplicity at a zero and minus the multiplicity at a pole; the residue theorem then yields N − P. Geometrically, each zero contributes one counterclockwise turn of f(z) around the origin, and each pole one clockwise turn.

Applications

The argument principle underlies Rouché's theorem, which compares the zero counts of two functions, and counting arguments for the fundamental theorem of algebra. In control engineering it gives the Nyquist stability criterion, in which encirclements of the critical point in the Nyquist plot determine the stability of a feedback system. It also provides a practical method for locating zeros of functions numerically by integrating around candidate regions.

History

The result appeared in Cauchy's work on definite integrals in the nineteenth century and was later generalized, including versions for functions with branch points and for contours passing through zeros or poles.
