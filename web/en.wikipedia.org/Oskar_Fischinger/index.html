Oskar Fischinger


Born: 22 June 1900, Gelnhausen, German Empire
Died: 31 January 1967 (aged 66), Los Angeles, California, US
Occupation: Animator, filmmaker, painter
Known for: Abstract animation, visual music

Oskar Wilhelm Fischinger (22 June 1900 – 31 January 1967) was a German-American abstract animator, filmmaker and painter, notable for creating abstract musical animation many years before the appearance of computer graphics and music videos. He created special effects for Fritz Lang's 1929 film Woman in the Moon, one of the first sci-fi rocket films, and is remembered for his series of Studies synchronising abstract shapes to popular and classical music.

Career in Germany

Fischinger, trained as an organ builder and engineer, turned to filmmaking in the early 1920s, influenced by the abstract films of Walther Ruttmann and Viking Eggeling. He invented a wax-slicing machine that cut thin slices from a block of coloured wax, photographing each slice to produce flowing abstract imagery. His Studies (1929–1934), set to music, were shown in cinemas internationally and made his reputation. His 1935 film Composition in Blue won prizes at Venice and Brussels.

Emigration and Hollywood

When the Nazi regime condemned abstract art as degenerate, Fischinger emigrated to the United States in 1936, working briefly for Paramount, MGM and Disney, where he contributed to the Pinocchio effects and the Toccata and Fugue sequence of Fantasia, though he left uncredited over the figurative changes made to his designs. His later independent masterpiece Motion Painting No. 1 (1947), painted frame by frame to Bach's Third Brandenburg Concerto, won the Grand Prix at the Brussels Experimental Film Competition. He also invented the Lumigraph, a device for performing colour-light shows live.

Legacy

Fischinger's belief in a pure "visual music" influenced generations of animators, including Norman McLaren and John Whitney, and anticipated the modern music video.
