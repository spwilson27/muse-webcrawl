Creation myth

A creation myth or cosmogonic myth is a symbolic narrative of how the world began and how people first came to inhabit it. While in popular usage the term myth often connotes falsehood, academic usage treats creation myths as sacred accounts held to be true within the cultures that tell them. Such myths share a number of features: they are often set in a primordial, undifferentiated state, involve supernatural beings, and explain the origins of the cosmos, the earth, life and human society.

Creation accounts appear in nearly every culture.

Classification

Scholars classify creation myths into recurring types. In ex nihilo ("out of nothing") myths, a god creates the world by thought, word or breath, as in the opening of Genesis. In chaos myths, order emerges from a formless primordial state, such as the Greek Chaos or the Egyptian waters of Nun. World parent myths separate united sky and earth parents — Rangi and Papa in Māori tradition, or Geb and Nut in Egypt. Emergence myths, common in the Indigenous American Southwest, describe humanity ascending through a series of worlds. In earth-diver myths, an animal dives into primordial waters to bring up the mud from which the earth is formed.

Examples

The Babylonian Enūma Eliš recounts creation from the dismembered body of the goddess Tiamat after Marduk's victory. The Egyptian Heliopolitan tradition begins with the self-created god Atum. Hindu cosmology describes cyclical creation and dissolution across immense ages (kalpas). Norse mythology tells of the world fashioned from the body of the giant Ymir, while the Popol Vuh of the K'iche' Maya describes repeated attempts to create humans, succeeding finally with maize dough. Chinese tradition centres on Pangu, who separates heaven and earth and whose body becomes the features of the world.

Functions and study

Creation myths legitimise social order, kingship and ritual, encode ecological knowledge, and answer existential questions about human origins and destiny. Comparative mythology, anthropology and religious studies examine their distribution and symbolism; thinkers such as Mircea Eliade emphasised their role in founding a people's sense of sacred time, while modern readers also study them as literature and as precursors of philosophical cosmogony.
