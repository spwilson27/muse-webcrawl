Stellar parallax

Stellar parallax is the apparent shift in position of a nearby star against the background of distant stars as the Earth orbits the Sun, caused by the change in the observer's viewpoint over a baseline of two astronomical units. It provides the most direct and fundamental method of measuring stellar distances and is the first rung of the cosmic distance ladder. The parsec — the distance at which a star has a parallax of one arcsecond — is defined from this effect and equals about 3.26 light-years.

History

The absence of any observable stellar parallax was long used as an argument against heliocentrism; Tycho Brahe reasoned that if the Earth moved, the stars must be unimaginably far away. James Bradley's discovery of stellar aberration (1728) instead proved Earth's motion while he was searching for parallax. The first successful parallax measurements were published in 1838–1840: Friedrich Bessel measured 61 Cygni (about 0.314 arcseconds), Friedrich Georg Wilhelm von Struve measured Vega, and Thomas Henderson measured Alpha Centauri.

Space astrometry

Ground-based parallax was limited by atmospheric distortion to accuracies of about 0.01 arcsecond. The ESA satellite Hipparcos (1989–1993) measured parallaxes of over 100,000 stars to milliarcsecond precision. Its successor Gaia, launched in 2013, has measured more than a billion stars with microarcsecond precision, revolutionizing the mapping of the Milky Way.
