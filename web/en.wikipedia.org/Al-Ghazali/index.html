Abū Ḥāmid Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad al-Ṭūsiyy al-Ghazālī (c. 1058 – 19 December 1111), known in Persian as Al-Ghazali, was a Persian theologian, philosopher, jurist and mystic, and one of the most influential figures in Islamic intellectual history. He held the prestigious post of professor at the Nizamiyya madrasa in Baghdad before undergoing a spiritual crisis that led him to abandon his career for a decade of Sufi wandering and writing.

Life

Born in Tus in Khorasan, Ghazali studied under the jurist al-Juwayni at Nishapur. In 1091 he was appointed to the Nizamiyya in Baghdad, the most distinguished academic post in the Seljuk world. In 1095, doubting the certainty promised by theology and philosophy, he suffered a breakdown, left Baghdad ostensibly for the Hajj, and spent years in Damascus, Jerusalem and retirement, returning to teach briefly at Nishapur before retiring to Tus, where he died.

Works

His most famous works include Tahāfut al-Falāsifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers), a critique of the metaphysics of Avicenna and al-Farabi, which defended occasionalism and criticised philosophers on twenty problems; Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn (The Revival of the Religious Sciences), his vast synthesis of law, ethics and Sufism; and the autobiographical Deliverance from Error. Averroes later answered him with The Incoherence of the Incoherence.

Legacy

Ghazali reshaped Sunni Islam by integrating Sufism into orthodox theology, and his thought influenced medieval Christian and Jewish thinkers. Historians debate his role in the later trajectory of Islamic science, with some traditional narratives crediting his critique for a decline of philosophy in the Islamic world, a thesis many modern scholars dispute.
