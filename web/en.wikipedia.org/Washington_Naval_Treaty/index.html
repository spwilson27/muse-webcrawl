The Washington Naval Treaty, also known as the Five-Power Treaty, was a treaty signed during 1922 among the major nations that had won World War I, which agreed to prevent an arms race by limiting naval construction. It was negotiated at the Washington Naval Conference in Washington, D.C., from November 1921 to February 1922 and signed by the governments of the United States, United Kingdom, Japan, France, and Italy.

Terms

The treaty set tonnage limits for capital ships (battleships and battlecruisers) in the famous ratio of 5:5:3:1.75:1.75 for the United States, United Kingdom, Japan, France, and Italy respectively — 525,000 tons each for the U.S. and Britain, 315,000 for Japan, and 175,000 each for France and Italy. Individual battleships were capped at 35,000 tons displacement and guns of no more than 16 inches. Aircraft carriers were limited to 135,000 tons total for the U.S. and Britain and 81,000 for Japan, with individual carriers limited to 27,000 tons (33,000 for two conversions from capital-ship hulls, which produced USS Lexington, USS Saratoga, HMS Furious derivatives, and Japan's Akagi and Kaga).

Signatories agreed to a ten-year "holiday" on battleship construction and to scrap numerous existing and planned capital ships; dozens of hulls were cancelled or demolished. The treaty also banned new fortifications and naval bases in specified Pacific island possessions, a non-fortification clause particularly important to Japan's acceptance of the inferior ratio.

Effects and aftermath

The treaty halted the post-war dreadnought race and pushed competition into unrestricted categories — notably heavy cruisers armed with 8-inch guns — prompting the London Naval Treaty of 1930 to extend limits to cruisers, destroyers, and submarines. The Washington system eroded in the 1930s: Japan, aggrieved by its permanent inferiority, gave notice of withdrawal in 1934, and the treaty expired at the end of 1936, freeing all parties for the naval expansion that preceded the Second World War. The treaty system is often cited as the first major modern arms-limitation regime and a landmark of interwar diplomacy.
