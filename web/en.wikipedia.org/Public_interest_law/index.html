Public interest law

Public interest law refers to legal practices and litigation undertaken to advance causes benefiting the public at large or under-represented groups, rather than the commercial interests of individual paying clients. It encompasses work in civil rights, environmental protection, consumer protection, poverty law, disability rights, and government accountability, and is carried out by non-profit legal organizations, government legal aid offices, pro bono programmes of private firms, and law school clinics.

History and development

The modern field crystallized in the United States in the 1960s and 1970s, funded initially by the Ford Foundation and federal programmes, and modelled on the NAACP Legal Defense Fund's campaign against racial segregation. Landmark organizations include the American Civil Liberties Union, the Natural Resources Defense Council, the Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund (now Earthjustice), and the Mexican American Legal Defense and Educational Fund. Fee-shifting statutes, which allow prevailing plaintiffs in civil rights and environmental cases to recover attorneys' fees, provided a durable funding mechanism. Comparable movements developed elsewhere: public interest litigation in India, enabled by liberal standing rules from the late 1970s, allowed courts to entertain petitions on behalf of prisoners, bonded labourers, and polluted communities, and similar mechanisms spread to Pakistan, Bangladesh, and South Africa.

Debates

Supporters describe public interest law as a way of giving legal voice to diffuse or disadvantaged interests that cannot organize or pay for representation. Critics across the political spectrum have charged particular organizations with pursuing ideological agendas through courts rather than legislatures; consequently the term has also been adopted by conservative and libertarian legal foundations whose litigation aims at deregulation and property rights.
