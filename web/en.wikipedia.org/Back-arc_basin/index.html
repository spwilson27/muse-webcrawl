Back-arc basin

A back-arc basin is a geological basin found behind a volcanic island arc, on the overriding plate of a subduction zone. Back-arc basins form where the lithosphere behind the arc is stretched and thinned — a process called back-arc extension — and in mature examples new oceanic crust is generated at a back-arc spreading centre.

Back-arc basin
Type: Submarine geological basin
Setting: Behind island arcs at convergent margins
Examples: Mariana Trough, Sea of Japan, Lau Basin

Formation

As the subducting slab rolls back toward the ocean (slab rollback), the overriding plate is pulled apart behind the volcanic arc. Extension produces rifted basins floored by thinned continental crust or, with continued spreading, by new oceanic crust. Many western Pacific marginal seas — including the Sea of Japan and the South China Sea — originated as back-arc basins, while the Mariana Trough east of the Mariana Islands is an actively spreading example.

Characteristics

Back-arc basins typically show elevated heat flow, thin sediment cover near the spreading axis, and volcanic and hydrothermal activity comparable to mid-ocean ridges. Their closure, by later compression, is a key part of the Wilson cycle and of orogenic (mountain-building) events.
