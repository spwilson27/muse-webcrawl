Stokes mortar

The Stokes mortar was a British trench mortar designed by Sir Wilfred Stokes KBE that was issued to the British Army and the Commonwealth armies, as well as the United States Army in the First World War. The 3-inch trench mortar is a smooth-bore, muzzle-loading weapon for high angles of fire. Although it is called a 3-inch mortar, its bore is actually 3.2 inches (81 mm). It was the first truly modern mortar and became the pattern on which virtually all later mortars were based.

History

Frederick Wilfred Scott Stokes, who later became Sir Wilfred Stokes KBE, designed the mortar in January 1915. At that time the British Army was in urgent need of a weapon to respond to the German Army's Minenwerfers, which were causing heavy casualties in the British trenches on the Western Front. The Stokes mortar was an extremely simple design: a smooth metal tube fixed to a base plate, supported by a bipod. When a bomb was dropped into the tube, an impact-sensitive cartridge at the base of the bomb would contact a firing pin at the base of the tube and ignite the propellant charge in the base, launching the bomb.

The weapon was initially rejected by the War Office, but after the intervention of David Lloyd George, then Minister of Munitions, 1,500 mortars were ordered in August 1915. Manufacture was initially constrained because the Stokes design was not accepted for evaluation until it had been demonstrated that it could fire consistently. The first mortars were delivered to the front in late 1915.

Design

The Stokes mortar consisted of three main parts: the barrel (a smooth-bore steel tube), the base plate, and the bipod mounting with elevation and traverse adjustment screws. The bomb itself was a cast-iron cylinder about 11 pounds (5 kg) in weight in the original design, with a modified percussion fuse and a tail unit carrying the propellant cartridge. Because the bomb was dropped nose-first onto a fixed firing pin, there was no trigger mechanism at all, and a skilled crew could maintain a rate of fire of up to 25 rounds a minute for short periods.

The design was so influential that the United States adopted it as the basis for the M1 81 mm mortar, and Stokes-type mortars remained in service around the world for decades after the First World War.

Combat use

The Stokes mortar was used extensively by British and Empire forces in the First World War for close infantry support, wire-cutting (with high-explosive bombs), and smoke screening. It equipped infantry battalions as part of their light trench mortar batteries. American Expeditionary Forces also used the type in 1918. It remained in British service into the Second World War, although largely superseded by the Ordnance ML 3 inch mortar, which was itself a direct descendant of the Stokes design.
