Isenheim Altarpiece

Isenheim Altarpiece
ArtistMatthias Grünewald (paintings); Niclaus of Haguenau (sculptures)
Yearc. 1512–1516
MediumOil on wood panels; limewood sculpture
LocationUnterlinden Museum, Colmar, France

The Isenheim Altarpiece (German: Isenheimer Altar) is an altarpiece sculpted and painted by, respectively, Niclaus of Haguenau and Matthias Grünewald in c. 1512–1516. It was created for the Monastery of St. Anthony in Issenheim (Isenheim), near Colmar, in Alsace, and is now displayed at the Unterlinden Museum in Colmar. It is Grünewald's largest and most famous work, considered a masterpiece of German Renaissance art.

Context

The Antonite monastery at Issenheim ran a hospital specialising in the treatment of skin diseases, including ergotism — known as "St Anthony's fire" — a condition caused by ingesting ergot fungus in rye bread, which produced convulsions, gangrene, and horrific skin lesions. The altarpiece stood in the choir of the monastery church, where patients were brought before it as part of their spiritual therapy: they could identify with the suffering depicted and find hope in the promise of resurrection.

Structure and iconography

The altarpiece is a polyptych with movable wings, allowing three distinct configurations:

First view (wings closed)

The closed altarpiece shows one of the most harrowing Crucifixions in Western art. Christ's body is twisted and covered with wounds and thorns, his skin taking on a greenish, diseased hue that would have resonated with the hospital's patients. John the Baptist stands to one side, pointing to Christ with an elongated finger beside the Latin inscription Illum oportet crescere, me autem minui ("He must increase, but I must decrease"). Below, the Lamentation shows Christ's body on a white shroud. The outer wings depict St. Sebastian and St. Anthony.

Second view (outer wings open)

Opening the wings reveals joyful scenes: the Annunciation, a concert of angels with the Virgin and Child, and the Resurrection, in which Christ rises in an explosion of radiant light, his body transfigured and free of suffering — a vision of healing offered to the sick who viewed it.

Third view (inner wings open)

The innermost view centres on Niclaus of Haguenau's gilded limewood sculptures of St. Anthony enthroned among the Church Fathers. The painted wings show the Meeting of St. Anthony and St. Paul the Hermit, and the Temptation of St. Anthony, a nightmarish scene of demonic assault in which one bloated, disease-ridden demon directly evokes the symptoms of ergotism.

History

Commissioned under the preceptor Guy Guers (Guido Guersi), the altarpiece survived the dissolution of the monastery during the French Revolution, when it was transferred to Colmar. During the First World War it was taken to Munich, where its exhibition profoundly influenced German modern artists. It has since returned to Colmar, where it remains the centrepiece of the Unterlinden Museum.

Influence

The altarpiece's unflinching imagery has inspired many later works, including Paul Hindemith's opera and symphony Mathis der Maler (1934–1938), based on Grünewald's life. It is frequently cited as a high point of Northern Renaissance painting and of religious art more broadly.
