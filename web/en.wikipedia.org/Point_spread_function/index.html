The PSF describes how an imaging system renders a single point of light.

The point spread function (PSF) describes the response of an imaging system to a point source: the spatial distribution of light in the image formed of an ideal point object. Because no real optical system focuses light to a perfect point, every image can be modelled as the true scene convolved with the PSF; knowing the PSF therefore characterises the system's blur and resolution.

Origin

Even a perfect lens produces a non-point image because of diffraction: the PSF of an ideal circular aperture is the Airy pattern, a central bright disk surrounded by fading rings, whose size sets the diffraction limit of resolution (the Rayleigh criterion). Real systems add aberrations, defocus, scattering and detector pixelation, and in ground-based astronomy atmospheric seeing broadens the PSF to arcsconds — the problem adaptive optics corrects. In microscopy the PSF is elongated along the optical axis, which deconvolution algorithms exploit to reconstruct three-dimensional structure.

Use in image restoration

With a measured or modelled PSF, deconvolution (for example Richardson–Lucy or Wiener filtering) can partially undo blur and sharpen images. This is standard in astronomy and fluorescence microscopy. Space telescopes such as Hubble had their images famously corrected computationally after the discovery of spherical aberration in the primary mirror, before corrective optics were installed.

Related concepts

The Fourier transform of the PSF is the optical transfer function, whose modulus (the modulation transfer function) summarises contrast versus spatial frequency. Analogous impulse-response ideas appear in medical imaging, seismology and signal processing generally.
