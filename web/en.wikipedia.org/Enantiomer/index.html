Enantiomer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemistry, an enantiomer (from Greek enantios, "opposite", plus meros, "part") is one of two stereoisomers of a compound that are non-superposable mirror images of each other. Enantiomers have the same chemical structure and bonds but differ in three-dimensional geometry, much as a left hand differs from a right hand. A molecule that has an enantiomer is said to be chiral.

A pair of enantiomers is often designated as "right-" and "left-handed". Enantiopurity is a measure of the purity of a chiral sample: a sample containing only one enantiomer is enantiopure or enantiomerically pure, while an equal mixture of the two is a racemic mixture.

Properties

Two enantiomers have identical chemical and physical properties in an achiral environment, including melting point, boiling point, density and solubility, and identical spectra in achiral methods. They differ in the direction in which they rotate plane-polarized light — one is dextrorotatory (d- or (+)-) and the other levorotatory (l- or (−)-) — and in how they interact with other chiral substances, a difference of great importance in biochemistry and pharmacology.

Biological significance

Biological systems are built from chiral molecules, principally L-amino acids and D-sugars, so enantiomers of a drug frequently have different physiological effects. The classic cautionary example is thalidomide, whose one enantiomer was an effective sedative while the other caused severe birth defects; the two interconvert in the body. Many modern pharmaceuticals are therefore developed and marketed as single enantiomers.

Nomenclature

Enantiomers of a given stereocentre are labelled (R) and (S) under the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority rules, which rank the substituents around the centre. Older D/L notation, still used for sugars and amino acids, refers to the configuration relative to glyceraldehyde.
