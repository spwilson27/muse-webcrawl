Censorship in the Soviet Union

Censorship in the Soviet Union was the comprehensive control of all printed matter, broadcasting, film, theatre, and other media by the Communist Party and state. It was administered chiefly by Glavlit (the Main Directorate for the Protection of State Secrets in the Press), founded in 1922, whose censors operated in every publishing house, newspaper, and printing plant.

Scope

Glavlit maintained a vast classified index of prohibited information — the "Talmud" — covering state secrets, military and economic statistics, natural disasters, crime, and anything deemed ideologically harmful. Beyond Glavlit, censorship worked through editorial self-censorship, party oversight of media appointments, and control of imports and foreign broadcasts, which were jammed.

Dissidence and samizdat

Forbidden literature circulated as samizdat — hand-typed, self-published copies passed between readers. Works by authors such as Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn and banned poetry, religious texts, and human-rights bulletins like the Chronicle of Current Events spread this way; possession could bring prosecution, exile, or psychiatric confinement.

Glasnost and end

Under Mikhail Gorbachev's glasnost policy after 1986, censorship loosened dramatically: previously banned works were published, media exposed corruption and historical crimes, and the 1990 press law formally abolished censorship. Glavlit was dissolved in 1991 as the USSR collapsed.
