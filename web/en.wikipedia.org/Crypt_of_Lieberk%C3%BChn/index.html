Crypt of Lieberkühn

The crypts of Lieberkühn, also called intestinal glands, are tubular invaginations of the mucosal lining (epithelium) found throughout the small and large intestine, between the villi and extending down to the muscularis mucosae. They are named after the German anatomist Johann Nathanael Lieberkühn, who described them in the 18th century.

Structure and function

The crypts are lined with several cell types: enterocytes, mucus-secreting goblet cells, antimicrobial Paneth cells (in the small intestine), hormone-secreting enteroendocrine cells, and intestinal stem cells at the crypt base. These stem cells continuously divide, renewing the intestinal epithelium every few days — one of the fastest turnover rates in the body.

Clinical significance

Crypt architecture is examined in biopsies: crypt distortion and crypt abscesses (collections of neutrophils) are features of inflammatory bowel disease, while loss of crypts occurs in severe colitis. Mutations in crypt stem cells are an origin of colorectal cancer.
