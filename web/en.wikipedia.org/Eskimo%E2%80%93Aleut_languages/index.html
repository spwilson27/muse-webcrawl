The Eskimo–Aleut languages, increasingly called the Eskaleut languages, are a family of languages spoken across the northernmost coasts of North America and Asia: in Greenland, the Canadian Arctic, Alaska, and the Chukchi Peninsula. The family comprises the Eskimo branch—subdivided into Inuit and Yupik—and the Aleut branch.

History of study

The relationship between Eskimo and Aleut was recognised by Rasmus Rask in the early 19th century and demonstrated systematically in the 20th century. Comparative work by Michael Fortescue and others established a reconstruction of proto-Eskaleut, with the split between Aleut and Eskimo dated to roughly 4,000 years ago.

Structure

The languages are agglutinative and polysynthetic, building large words from roots and hundreds of possible suffixes ("postbases") that modify meaning, followed by inflectional endings marking case, number, and—for verbs—subject and object agreement. Nouns distinguish singular, dual, and plural.

Relations with other families

Proposed links to other families, such as Joseph Greenberg's "Amerind" grouping or Fortescue's Uralo-Siberian hypothesis, remain unproven and are not accepted by most specialists.
