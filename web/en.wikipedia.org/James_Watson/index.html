James Watson

James Dewey Watson (born 6 April 1928) is an American molecular biologist who, with Francis Crick, co-authored in 1953 the academic paper proposing the double-helix structure of DNA. Watson, Crick and Maurice Wilkins shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.

The double helix

Born in Chicago, Watson earned his PhD at Indiana University in 1950 and joined the Cavendish Laboratory at Cambridge, where he worked with Crick. Drawing on X-ray diffraction data — including Rosalind Franklin's "Photo 51" and reports from King's College London — and on model building, Watson and Crick published the double-helix structure in Nature in April 1953, one of the most famous papers in science.

Later career

Watson's bestselling memoir The Double Helix (1968) gave a candid, controversial account of the discovery. He taught at Harvard, directed Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory from 1968 to 1993, transforming it into a leading research centre, and was the first head of the Human Genome Project at the NIH (1988–1992).

Controversy

From 2007 onwards, Watson repeatedly made public remarks on race, intelligence and other subjects that were widely condemned as racist and unsupported by science. Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory stripped him of his honorary titles in 2019, and in 2014 he had auctioned his Nobel medal (later returned to him by the buyer).
