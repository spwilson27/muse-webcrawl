John Harvey Kellogg

Born: 26 February 1852, Tyrone, Michigan, U.S.
Died: 14 December 1943 (aged 91), Battle Creek, Michigan
Occupation: Physician, nutritionist
Known for: Battle Creek Sanitarium; co-inventing corn flakes

John Harvey Kellogg (February 26, 1852 – December 14, 1943) was an American businessman, inventor, physician and advocate of vegetarianism. He is best known for the invention of corn flakes with his brother Will Keith Kellogg and for directing the Battle Creek Sanitarium in Michigan.

Battle Creek Sanitarium

A Seventh-day Adventist, Kellogg took over the church's Western Health Reform Institute in 1876 and transformed it into the Battle Creek Sanitarium, a world-famous resort combining a hospital, hydrotherapy, vegetarian diet and exercise, whose guests included Henry Ford, Amelia Earhart and Thomas Edison.

Food inventions

Seeking bland, easily digested foods for his patients, Kellogg developed breakfast cereals; the accidental flaking of cooked wheat, and then corn, led to corn flakes, patented in 1896. His brother Will commercialised them through the Kellogg Company founded in 1906, and the brothers feuded for decades over the business. Kellogg also promoted peanut butter, meat substitutes and soy milk.

Beliefs

Kellogg advocated "biologic living": vegetarianism, abstinence from alcohol and tobacco, and vigorous exercise. Some of his views, including his campaign against masturbation and later support for eugenics and racial segregation in his Race Betterment Foundation, are controversial. He was disfellowshipped from the Adventist church in 1907 amid disputes over theology and control of the Sanitarium.
