The Arnold Arboretum of Harvard University is a botanical research institution and free public park in Boston, Massachusetts. Founded in 1872, it was designed in collaboration with Frederick Law Olmsted as part of Boston's Emerald Necklace park system and is a National Historic Landmark. Its 281 acres hold one of the world's most significant collections of temperate woody plants.

History

The arboretum was established with a bequest from the will of New Bedford whaling merchant James Arnold, administered by Harvard. Under its first director, Charles Sprague Sargent (director 1873–1927), the collection expanded through plant-collecting expeditions to East Asia by collectors such as Ernest Henry Wilson, who introduced thousands of taxa from China, Japan and Korea. In an unusual arrangement dating to 1882, the City of Boston owns the land and Harvard leases it for one dollar a year for a thousand years, with Harvard maintaining the collections.

Collections and research

Renowned collections include lilacs — celebrated each year on Lilac Sunday in May — as well as maples, crabapples, conifers, rhododendrons and a bonsai and penjing collection. The arboretum's herbarium and library support research in plant systematics, evolution and climate adaptation; all living specimens are documented and tracked by accession, and plants are arranged broadly by family following a modified Bentham and Hooker system.
