Base isolation

Base isolation, also known as seismic base isolation or base isolation system, is one of the most popular means of protecting a structure against earthquake forces. It is a collection of structural elements which should substantially decouple a superstructure from its substructure that is in turn resting on shaking ground, thus protecting a building or non-building structure's integrity during an earthquake.

How it works

A base-isolated structure is supported on flexible bearings — commonly laminated rubber bearings, lead-rubber bearings, friction pendulum bearings or sliding bearings — placed between the building and its foundations. The bearings greatly increase the structure's fundamental period of vibration (typically to two seconds or more) and add damping, so the building moves largely as a rigid body with small inter-storey drifts while the isolators absorb large horizontal displacements. Accelerations transmitted to the superstructure and its contents are greatly reduced.

Applications

Base isolation is widely used in Japan, New Zealand, the western United States, Italy, China and elsewhere, for hospitals, emergency facilities, museums, historic buildings and bridges where continued function or protection of contents justifies the cost. Examples include the Utah State Capitol, Salt Lake City and County Building, Los Angeles City Hall, and Christchurch Women's Hospital. New Zealand engineer Bill Robinson developed the lead-rubber bearing in the 1970s, first used in Wellington's William Clayton Building (1981).

Limitations

Isolation is less effective on soft soil sites with long-period ground motion and requires a seismic gap (moat) around the building, flexible utility connections, and periodic inspection and maintenance of bearings.
