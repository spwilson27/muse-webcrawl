The Satanic panic was a moral panic consisting of over 12,000 unsubstantiated cases of Satanic ritual abuse (SRA) that swept the United States beginning in the early 1980s and spreading to other English-speaking countries. Fuelled by fundamentalist Christian concerns, sensational media, some therapists and law-enforcement seminars, the panic alleged that vast networks of Satanists were ritually abusing children, particularly in daycare centers. Extensive investigations found no evidence for the claims of organized cults.

Origins

The panic is usually traced to the 1980 book Michelle Remembers, in which Michelle Smith and her psychiatrist (later husband) Lawrence Pazder described recovered memories of ritual abuse — accounts later debunked. In 1983, allegations at the McMartin preschool in California launched the longest and most expensive criminal trial in U.S. history to that date; it ended in 1990 with no convictions. Similar daycare cases, such as those involving the Little Rascals center in North Carolina and Fran and Dan Keller in Texas, produced convictions that were later overturned as the suggestive interviewing of children came under scrutiny.

Investigations and decline

A 1992 report by FBI agent Kenneth Lanning concluded that after hundreds of investigations there was no evidence of organized satanic conspiracies abusing children, and a 1994 U.S. government-funded study reached similar conclusions. Academic work on false memory syndrome showed how recovered-memory therapy and leading questions could implant convincing but false recollections. By the mid-1990s the panic had subsided, though wrongful convictions from the era took decades to clear, and scholars study the episode as a classic case of moral panic alongside earlier witch hunts.
