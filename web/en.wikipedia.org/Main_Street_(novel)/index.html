Main Street is a novel by American writer Sinclair Lewis, published in 1920. A satirical portrait of small-town American life, it follows Carol Milford, a librarian from St. Paul, who marries Dr. Will Kennicott and moves with him to his hometown, the fictional Gopher Prairie, Minnesota—modelled on Sauk Centre, Lewis's own hometown. Carol's attempts to reform and beautify the town and to raise its cultural life collide with its complacency, conformity, and gossip.

Plot summary

Carol Kennicott arrives in Gopher Prairie full of plans drawn from her reading and her work in the city. She tries, in turn, to enliven parties, reform architecture, found a drama league, and improve the rest room for farm women, but each scheme is dulled by ridicule, indifference, or the town's absorption of her efforts into routine. Her sympathy for the town's outsiders—the Scandinavian schoolteacher Fern Mullins, the handyman Miles Bjornstam, and the radical tailor Nat Hicks—risks her standing, and her emotional involvement with the young lawyer Erik Valborg deepens her dissatisfaction. During the First World War she leaves for Washington, D.C., works in a government bureau, and gains independence, but ultimately returns to Gopher Prairie and to Will, reconciled to partial victories and to fighting "the village virus" on a smaller scale, while hoping her children may do more.

Reception and legacy

Main Street was a sensation and a bestseller, selling roughly 180,000 copies in its first six months and establishing Lewis's national reputation. It was widely expected to win the 1921 Pulitzer Prize for the Novel; the jury's selection was overruled by Columbia University's trustees in favour of Edith Wharton's The Age of Innocence, an episode said to have embittered Lewis. The novel's central theme—the stifling conformity of the American small town and the "revolt from the village" of the young and educated—gave American English the phrase "Main Street" as a symbol both of hometown normality and of provincial narrowness.
