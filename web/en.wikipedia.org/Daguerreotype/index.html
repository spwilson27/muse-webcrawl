Daguerreotype

The daguerreotype was the first publicly available photographic process, announced in 1839 and widely used during the 1840s and 1850s. Invented by Louis Daguerre in collaboration with Nicéphore Niépce, it produced a unique, highly detailed image on a silver-coated copper plate, with no negative.

Process

A copper plate coated with silver was polished to a mirror finish, sensitised with iodine (later iodine and bromine) vapour to form light-sensitive silver halides, exposed in a camera for seconds to minutes, and developed over heated mercury vapour, which formed an amalgam image in the exposed areas. The image was fixed in sodium thiosulfate ("hypo"), often gilded with gold chloride for stability, and sealed behind glass in a protective case because the surface tarnished easily. The resulting image is laterally reversed and appears positive or negative depending on the viewing angle.

History

Daguerre, a painter and inventor of the diorama, partnered with Niépce in 1829; after Niépce's death in 1833 he developed the process alone. François Arago presented it to the French Académie des Sciences on 7 January 1839, and the French government bought the rights and published the method "free to the world" on 19 August 1839 — except in England, where Daguerre held a patent. Portrait studios proliferated worldwide; in the United States, practitioners such as Southworth and Hawes and Mathew Brady made the daguerreotype the dominant portrait medium. Millions were produced, but the process was displaced in the late 1850s by the cheaper ambrotype and tintype, and by paper prints from glass negatives.

Characteristics

Daguerreotypes are prized for their extraordinary sharpness and detail. Because there is no negative, each image is unique and cannot be exactly copied. Their mirror-like surfaces require viewing at an angle, and long exposure times meant sitters were held still with head braces, producing the era's characteristically solemn portraits.
