Nash equilibrium

Nash equilibrium
Field: Game theory
Introduced by: John Forbes Nash Jr.
Key papers: 1950 (PNAS note); 1951, Annals of Mathematics
Concept: Strategy profile stable against unilateral deviation

A Nash equilibrium is a concept of game theory in which each player's strategy is optimal given the strategies of all other players; no participant can gain by unilaterally changing their own strategy. Named after mathematician John Forbes Nash Jr., who proved in his 1950–51 work that at least one such equilibrium (possibly in mixed strategies) exists in every finite game, it is the most widely used solution concept in non-cooperative game theory.

Definition

Formally, a strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium if each player's strategy is a best response to the others'. In the classic prisoner's dilemma, mutual defection is the unique Nash equilibrium even though both players would do better by cooperating — showing that equilibrium need not be socially optimal. In games like matching pennies no pure-strategy equilibrium exists, but a mixed equilibrium does, in which each player randomizes with specific probabilities.

Applications and extensions

Nash equilibria are used throughout economics (oligopoly, auctions, bargaining), political science, evolutionary biology (where the related concept of an evolutionarily stable strategy appears), and computer science. Refinements such as subgame perfect equilibrium, trembling hand perfect equilibrium, and sequential equilibrium narrow the set of plausible outcomes in dynamic or imperfect-information settings. Nash shared the 1994 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten for this work. See also Game theory and Prisoner's dilemma.
