Nirvana (Sanskrit: निर्वाण, nirvāṇa; Pali: nibbāna) is a concept in Indian religions (Buddhism, Hinduism, Jainism and Sikhism) that represents the ultimate state of soteriological release — freedom from suffering (dukkha) and from the cycle of death and rebirth (samsara).

Etymology and meaning

The word is usually interpreted as "blowing out" or "quenching" — of the three fires of greed (raga), aversion (dvesha) and ignorance/delusion (moha/avidya). When these fires are extinguished, release from the cycle of rebirth is attained. In Buddhism, nirvana is the goal of the path taught by the Buddha: it is not the annihilation of the self (an extreme the Buddha rejected, along with eternalism), but the unconditioned state beyond birth and death, reached when craving and ignorance cease.

In Buddhism

Early texts distinguish nirvana attained in life (the state of an arhat or the Buddha, who at his enlightenment at Bodh Gaya realised it at age 35) from parinirvana, the final passing at death — in the Buddha's case at Kushinagar. The path to it is the Noble Eightfold Path, the fourth of the Four Noble Truths. Mahayana Buddhism developed the ideal of the bodhisattva, who defers final nirvana to liberate all beings, and in some schools (such as Madhyamaka) taught that nirvana and samsara are ultimately not different in nature.

In other religions

In Hinduism, nirvana is closely associated with moksha, the release of the soul (atman) and its realisation of unity with ultimate reality; the Bhagavad Gita speaks of brahma-nirvana. In Jainism, nirvana (also moksha) is the state of the liberated soul, free of karmic matter, attained at the end of an omniscient life — Mahavira's nirvana is commemorated at Diwali. Sikh teaching likewise uses the term for liberation from rebirth through union with God.
