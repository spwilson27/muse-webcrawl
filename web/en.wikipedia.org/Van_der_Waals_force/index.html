Van der Waals force

In molecular physics and chemistry, van der Waals forces are distance-dependent interactions between atoms or molecules that, unlike covalent and ionic bonds, do not result from a chemical electronic bond. They are comparatively weak and are more easily perturbed. The forces are named after Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals.

Components

The term commonly covers three contributions: the Keesom force between two permanent dipoles; the Debye force between a permanent dipole and an induced dipole; and the London dispersion force between two instantaneously induced dipoles, which is present between all atoms and molecules and usually dominates for non-polar species.

Characteristics

Van der Waals forces fall off rapidly with distance, varying roughly with the inverse sixth power of separation for the attractive part, and become strongly repulsive at very short range, where the Lennard-Jones potential is often used as a model. Individually weak, the forces are collectively significant: they explain the condensation of gases, the properties of molecular crystals, and adhesion phenomena such as the ability of geckos to climb smooth surfaces using millions of microscopic hairs (setae) on their feet.

Applications

Understanding van der Waals interactions is important in supramolecular chemistry, materials science, nanotechnology, protein folding and drug design, where the cumulative effect of many weak interactions determines structure and binding.
