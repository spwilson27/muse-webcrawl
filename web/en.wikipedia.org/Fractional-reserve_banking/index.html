Fractional-reserve banking

Under fractional-reserve banking, banks hold only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest.

Fractional-reserve banking is the system of banking that operates in almost all countries worldwide, in which banks required to hold liquid reserves equal to only a portion (a fraction) of their deposit liabilities may lend out the remainder. Because depositors rarely withdraw all their funds at once, the bank can function with reserves well below total deposits while still honouring routine withdrawals.

How it works

When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account, creating a new deposit; in this way the banking system as a whole expands the money supply beyond the stock of central-bank money (base money). The theoretical limit of this expansion is described by the money multiplier, though in practice lending is constrained by capital requirements, liquidity rules, credit demand and central-bank policy rather than by reserve ratios alone. Some jurisdictions, such as the United States since 2020, set required reserve ratios at zero, relying instead on other prudential tools.

History

The practice grew out of early modern goldsmith banking: receipts for gold deposited with goldsmiths circulated as money, and goldsmiths learned they could lend out part of the metal. Fractional-reserve practices were central to recurring banking crises and bank runs in the 19th and early 20th centuries, prompting the creation of central banks as lenders of last resort and, later, government deposit insurance.

Criticism and alternatives

Critics argue the system is inherently fragile and gives private banks an unfair power to create money; supporters respond that it efficiently channels savings into productive credit and that instability is managed through regulation, deposit insurance and central banking. The principal alternative proposed is full-reserve banking (or narrow banking), in which demand deposits must be backed entirely by reserves or safe assets — an idea embodied in the 1930s Chicago Plan and in later sovereign-money proposals.
