A scanning tunneling microscope (STM) is an instrument for imaging surfaces at the atomic level. It was invented in 1981 by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM Zürich, work for which they shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics. The STM can achieve lateral resolution of about 0.1 nm and depth resolution of 0.01 nm, allowing individual atoms to be imaged and manipulated.

Principle of operation

The STM is based on quantum tunneling. A sharp conducting tip is brought very close to the surface to be examined so that a bias voltage produces a tunneling current between them. The current depends exponentially on the tip–sample distance, making it extremely sensitive to height variations. The tip is scanned across the surface using piezoelectric actuators, and feedback electronics either hold the current constant while recording the tip height (constant-current mode) or hold the height constant and record the current (constant-height mode).

Applications

STM is used to study surface structure, electronic states and defects in materials, and to position individual atoms, famously demonstrated when IBM researchers spelled out the company logo with xenon atoms in 1989. It requires electrically conductive samples and is often operated in ultra-high vacuum, though instruments also work in air and liquid. The technique led to the broader family of scanning probe microscopes, including the atomic force microscope.
