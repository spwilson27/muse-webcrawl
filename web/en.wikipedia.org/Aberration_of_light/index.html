Aberration of light

The aberration of light (also referred to as astronomical aberration, stellar aberration, or velocity aberration) is a phenomenon which causes celestial objects to appear to move about their true positions depending on the velocity of the observer. It causes objects to appear to be displaced towards the direction of motion of the observer compared to when the observer is stationary. The effect is significant — the maximum displacement is about 20.5 arcseconds, known as the constant of aberration.

Discovery

Stellar aberration was discovered by the English astronomer James Bradley in 1727 while he was searching for stellar parallax using observations of the star Gamma Draconis made with Samuel Molyneux. Bradley found a regular annual displacement that could not be parallax, and in 1729 explained it as the combination of the finite speed of light with the Earth's orbital motion — the telescope must be tilted slightly forward, just as one tilts an umbrella when walking through rain. The discovery provided the first direct observational proof that the Earth moves and that the speed of light is finite.

Explanation

Classically, if light from a star falls vertically while the observer moves horizontally at speed v, the apparent direction is tilted by an angle whose tangent is v/c. The full modern treatment is relativistic: aberration follows from the transformation of light directions between reference frames in special relativity. Aberration must be corrected in precision astrometry, and is distinct from parallax, since it depends on the observer's velocity rather than position.

Types

Annual aberration arises from the Earth's orbital velocity (~30 km/s). Diurnal aberration, from the Earth's rotation, is much smaller (about 0.3 arcseconds at the equator). Secular aberration results from the motion of the Solar System through the Galaxy.
