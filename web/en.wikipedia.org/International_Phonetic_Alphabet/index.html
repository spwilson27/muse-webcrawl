The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) is an alphabetic system of phonetic notation based primarily on the Latin script. It was devised by the International Phonetic Association in the late 19th century as a standardised representation of the sounds of spoken language. The IPA is used by lexicographers, foreign language learners, linguists, speech-language pathologists, singers, actors, constructed language creators, and translators.

History

The Association, founded in Paris in 1886 by language teachers including Paul Passy, published the first version of the alphabet in 1888, building on Henry Sweet's Romic alphabet and Otto Jespersen's proposals. Its founding principle is that there should be a separate letter for each distinctive sound of a language. The alphabet has been revised repeatedly — major revisions in 1900, 1932, 1989, 1993 and 2005 — and is supplemented by the Extensions to the IPA (extIPA) for disordered speech.

Structure

The IPA provides symbols for consonants, arranged on a chart by place and manner of articulation (pulmonic consonants, plus non-pulmonic clicks, implosives and ejectives), and for vowels, plotted on a quadrilateral according to tongue height and backness. Diacritics modify symbols for finer distinctions, and suprasegmentals mark stress, length and tone. Broad transcription is written between slashes / / and narrow transcription between square brackets [ ]. For example, English pin is broadly /pɪn/ and narrowly [pʰɪn].

Use

Dictionaries use the IPA to specify pronunciation; Wikipedia uses it in pronunciation guides for names and foreign words. Because it assigns one symbol per sound regardless of language, the IPA allows the speech of any language — and disordered speech — to be recorded and compared systematically.
