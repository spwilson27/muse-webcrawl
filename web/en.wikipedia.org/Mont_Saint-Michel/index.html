Mont Saint-Michel is a tidal island and mainland commune in Normandy, France. The island lies about one kilometre off the country's north-western coast, at the mouth of the Couesnon River near Avranches, and is famous for the Gothic abbey dedicated to the Archangel Michael that crowns its rocky summit.

Mont Saint-Michel at high tide.

History

According to tradition, the Archangel Michael appeared to St Aubert, bishop of Avranches, in 708 and instructed him to build a church on the rocky islet. A Benedictine abbey was founded in 966, and the mount became a major centre of pilgrimage and learning. Its natural defences made it a stronghold: it withstood English sieges during the Hundred Years' War and was never taken. During the French Revolution the abbey was used as a prison, an ignominy ended by a campaign led by figures such as Victor Hugo; it closed as a prison in 1863 and the mount was declared a historic monument in 1874.

The abbey

The abbey church, begun in the 11th century, combines Romanesque and Gothic elements. Below it, the monastic buildings known as La Merveille ("The Marvel"), built in the 13th century, stack a refectory, cloister and knights' hall on the steep slope in an extraordinary feat of medieval engineering. A gilded statue of St Michael surmounts the spire.

Tides and access

The bay experiences some of Europe's largest tidal ranges — up to about 14 metres — and the mount is cut off at high tide; at low water it is surrounded by vast sand flats. A causeway long linked it to the mainland; in 2014 a new bridge (pont-passerelle) replaced the old road, allowing water to circulate again and reducing silting. Mont Saint-Michel is one of France's most visited sites, receiving several million visitors a year.
