Philosophical realism

Philosophical realism is the view that some entities — the external world, universals, mathematical objects, moral values, or the entities posited by science — exist independently of our minds, concepts, and perceptions. Realism about a domain asserts that its entities are what they are regardless of whether anyone believes in or perceives them. It stands opposed to various anti-realisms, including idealism, nominalism, and constructivism.

Forms of realism

Metaphysical realism holds that the world has a definite, mind-independent structure. Platonic realism affirms the independent existence of universals and abstract objects — the medieval realism of thinkers who held that universals exist ante rem, before things. Scientific realism holds that mature scientific theories are approximately true and that the unobservable entities they posit, such as electrons, really exist. Moral realism holds that ethical statements express objective facts. In aesthetics and philosophy of mathematics, parallel debates concern aesthetic value and numbers respectively.

History

Realism about universals was central to medieval scholastic debate, opposed by nominalists such as William of Ockham. In the early twentieth century, G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell championed a realist revolt against British idealism ("A Defence of Common Sense"), while later debates — over scientific realism between Bas van Fraassen's constructive empiricism and its realist opponents, and over Dummett's semantic challenges — refined the issues into questions about truth and meaning.
