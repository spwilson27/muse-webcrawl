Repurchase agreement

A repurchase agreement, also known as a repo, RP, or sale and repurchase agreement, is a form of short-term borrowing, mainly in government securities. The dealer sells the underlying security to investors and, by agreement, repurchases it shortly afterwards, usually the following day, at a slightly higher price. The difference between the sale and repurchase prices implies the interest rate, called the repo rate.

In a repo, securities are exchanged for cash with an agreement to reverse the trade.

Mechanics and market role

Economically, a repo is a collateralized loan: the cash investor lends against securities, protected by a "haircut" — the excess of collateral value over cash lent. Repos are usually overnight, though term repos run longer. The repo market is one of the largest and most liquid money markets in the world, used by dealers to finance securities inventories, by central banks to implement monetary policy (the Federal Reserve's open market operations and its Standing Repo Facility operate through repo), and by investors seeking a secured place to park cash. A reverse repo is the same transaction viewed from the counterparty's perspective.

Risks

Repos played a central role in the 2007–2008 financial crisis: declining collateral values and rising haircuts produced a "run on repo," as described by economists Gary Gorton and Andrew Metrick, in which lenders suddenly refused to roll over funding to firms such as Bear Stearns and Lehman Brothers. Stress reappeared in September 2019, when overnight repo rates spiked sharply and the Federal Reserve intervened to add reserves. Regulatory reforms since the crisis have sought to reduce the system's reliance on runnable short-term funding, including the growth of central clearing through the Fixed Income Clearing Corporation.
