Kievan Rus' was a loose federation of East Slavic and Finnic peoples in Eastern and Northern Europe from the late 9th to the mid-13th century, ruled by the Rurik dynasty. Centred on Kiev, at its height it stretched from the Baltic Sea to the Black Sea steppe and was one of the largest states in medieval Europe; modern Belarus, Russia, and Ukraine all trace their statehood to it.

Origins

According to the Primary Chronicle, in 862 the Slavic and Finnic tribes invited the Varangian (Scandinavian) prince Rurik to rule them. Rurik's successor Oleg seized Kiev in 882 and united the northern and southern Rus' lands along the trade route "from the Varangians to the Greeks" — the Dnieper route between Scandinavia and Constantinople, which carried furs, wax, honey, and slaves south and silver and luxuries north.

Christianisation and golden age

In 988 Vladimir the Great adopted Orthodox Christianity from Byzantium, a turning point that brought Byzantine art, architecture, and literacy. His son Yaroslav the Wise (r. 1019–1054) built St Sophia Cathedral in Kiev, promulgated the law code Russkaya Pravda, and married his children into the royal houses of Europe. Kiev's population and wealth rivalled the great cities of the continent.

Decline and Mongol invasion

After Yaroslav, succession disputes and princely fragmentation weakened the federation; power shifted northeast toward Vladimir-Suzdal, and trade declined as routes changed. In 1240 the Mongols of Batu Khan sacked Kiev, an catastrophe from which the city did not recover for centuries. The southern Rus' lands later passed to Lithuania and Poland, while the northeast developed into Muscovy.
