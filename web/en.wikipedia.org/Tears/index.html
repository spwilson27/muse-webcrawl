Tears

Tears are a clear liquid secreted by the lacrimal glands (tear glands) found in the eyes of all land mammals. Tears are made up of water, salts, antibodies, and lysozymes. The functions of tears include lubricating the eyes (basal tears), removing irritants (reflex tears), and aiding the immune system. Tears also occur as a part of the body's natural pain response. Emotional secretion of tears may serve a biological function by excreting stress-inducing hormones built up through times of emotional distress. Tears have symbolic significance among humans, and the act of shedding them is known as crying.

Types

Three types of tears are distinguished. Basal tears are continuously produced in healthy mammalian eyes to keep the cornea wet and nourished. Reflex tears are formed in response to irritants such as dust, onion fumes, or tear gas. Emotional tears (also called psychic tears) are shed in response to strong emotions, including joy, sadness, awe, and physical pain.

Composition

Tear fluid forms three layers in the tear film: an outer lipid layer that slows evaporation, a middle aqueous layer, and an inner mucin layer that helps the film adhere to the cornea. Tears contain electrolytes similar in concentration to blood plasma, as well as enzymes such as lysozyme, which has antibacterial properties.

Lacrimal apparatus

Tears are produced by the lacrimal glands situated above the outer corner of each eye. Blinking spreads the tear film across the surface of the eye. Excess fluid drains through the lacrimal puncta into the tear ducts and ultimately into the nasal cavity, which is why crying is often accompanied by a runny nose.

Crying

Humans are the only animals widely believed to shed emotional tears, although evidence of similar behavior has been discussed in other species. Crying typically begins in infancy; babies cry to communicate needs, and the character of crying changes with development.
