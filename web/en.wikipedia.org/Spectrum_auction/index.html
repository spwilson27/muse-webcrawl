Spectrum auction

A spectrum auction is a process whereby a government uses an auction system to sell the rights to transmit signals over specific bands of the electromagnetic spectrum and to assign scarce spectrum resources. Depending on the specific auction format used, a spectrum auction can last from a single day to several months from the opening bid to the final winning bid.

History

New Zealand was among the first countries to auction spectrum, in 1989–1990. The United States Federal Communications Commission (FCC) began conducting spectrum auctions in 1994, using the simultaneous multiple-round auction designed with input from economists including Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson, who later received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences partly for this work.

Formats

Common formats include the simultaneous multiple-round auction (SMRA), in which many licences are sold at once over successive rounds, and combinatorial clock auctions, which allow package bids. Set-asides and spectrum caps are often used to promote competition.

Notable auctions

The UK 3G auction in 2000 raised £22.5 billion. The US C-band auction (2020–2021) grossed over $80 billion. Auctions for 5G bands have been held worldwide since the late 2010s.

Criticism

Critics argue that high auction prices can burden operators and slow network rollout, while supporters credit auctions with assigning spectrum to its highest-valued uses transparently.
