The esophagus (American English) or oesophagus (British English) is an organ in vertebrates through which food passes, aided by peristaltic contractions, from the pharynx to the stomach. In humans it is a fibromuscular tube about 25 cm (10 in) long that runs behind the trachea and heart, passing through the diaphragm before emptying into the stomach.

Structure

The esophageal wall is made up of mucosa, submucosa, a muscular layer, and an outer adventitia. The upper third contains skeletal muscle under voluntary control; the lower third is smooth muscle. Two sphincters—the upper esophageal sphincter and the lower esophageal sphincter—regulate the passage of food and prevent reflux of gastric contents.

Function

During swallowing, the epiglottis folds over the larynx to prevent food entering the airway, and a coordinated wave of peristalsis propels the bolus down the esophagus within a few seconds. Liquids are moved largely by gravity when upright.

Clinical significance

Disorders include gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), Barrett's esophagus, esophagitis, achalasia, and esophageal cancer. Difficulty swallowing is termed dysphagia.
