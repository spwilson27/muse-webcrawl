Commonplace book

Commonplace books (or commonplaces) are a way to compile knowledge, usually by writing information into books. They have been kept from antiquity, and were kept particularly during the Renaissance and in the 19th century. Such books are similar to scrapbooks filled with items of many kinds: notes, proverbs, adages, aphorisms, maxims, quotes, letters, poems, tables of weights and measures, prayers, legal formulas, and recipes.

History

The practice descends from the ancient and medieval use of loci communes ("common places"), headings under which striking passages were stored for later use in rhetoric. Renaissance humanists such as Erasmus systematised the method in works like De copia, and John Locke published an influential essay on an indexing method for commonplace books in 1686. Early modern students and writers — among them Francis Bacon, John Milton, and later Marcus Aurelius's imitators, and in the 19th century many diarists — used commonplace books as personal knowledge stores.

Unlike a diary or journal, a commonplace book is organised around gathered material rather than personal narrative, and is often indexed by subject headings for reuse in writing and argument. Modern note-taking systems and digital "second brain" tools are frequently compared to the practice.
