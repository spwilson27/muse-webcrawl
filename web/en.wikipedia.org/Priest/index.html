Priest

A priest is a religious leader authorized to perform the sacred rituals of a religion, especially as a mediatory agent between humans and one or more deities. They also have the authority or power to administer religious rites; in particular, rites of sacrifice to, and propitiation of, a deity or deities. Their office or position is the "priesthood", a term which also may apply to such persons collectively. The word derives, via Latin presbyter, from the Greek presbyteros, "elder".

A priest celebrating the Eucharist.

Historical overview

Priesthoods existed in the religions of ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome, where priests maintained temples, offered sacrifices, interpreted omens, and kept sacred calendars. In ancient Israel, the priesthood was hereditary, descending from Aaron, and centred on the Temple in Jerusalem. In Christianity, the priesthood developed from the presbyters of the early church; in the Catholic, Orthodox, and Anglican traditions priests are ordained to celebrate the sacraments, preach, and pastor congregations. Hindu priests (pujari, purohit) conduct worship (puja) in temples and homes; Buddhist, Shinto, and Zoroastrian traditions have their own priestly offices.

Duties and training

Duties vary widely by tradition but commonly include leading worship, administering sacraments or sacrifices, teaching doctrine, blessing people and objects, and providing pastoral care, including ministry to the sick and dying. Training may involve seminary study, apprenticeship, or hereditary succession. Many traditions require celibacy of priests (mandatory in the Latin Catholic Church), while others permit married clergy.

Women priests

Many religions, including most Protestant churches, Reform Judaism, and some Buddhist traditions, ordain women. The Roman Catholic Church and the Orthodox churches do not, a position that remains a subject of debate and reform movements.
