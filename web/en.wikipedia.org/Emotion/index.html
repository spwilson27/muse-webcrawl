Emotion

Emotions are mental states brought on by neurophysiological changes, variously associated with thoughts, feelings, behavioural responses, and a degree of pleasure or displeasure. There is no scientific consensus on a single definition. Emotions are often intertwined with mood, temperament, personality, and motivation, and they play a central role in human decision-making, social interaction, and survival.

Facial expressions are a universal channel of emotional communication

Components

Emotional episodes typically involve several coordinated components: a subjective experience (the feeling itself), physiological changes (such as altered heart rate and hormone release), expressive behaviour (facial expressions, posture, vocal tone), and action tendencies (such as approaching or fleeing). The brain structures most associated with emotion include the amygdala, the insula, and regions of the prefrontal cortex.

Theories

The James–Lange theory holds that emotion is the perception of bodily changes; the Cannon–Bard theory proposes that feelings and bodily responses occur simultaneously; and appraisal theories argue that emotions arise from evaluations of events in relation to one's goals. Paul Ekman's research identified a set of "basic emotions" — including happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, and disgust — whose facial expressions appear recognisable across cultures, while constructionist accounts, such as Lisa Feldman Barrett's, hold that emotions are constructed from core affect and learned concepts.

Classification

Emotions are often modelled along dimensions of valence (positive to negative) and arousal (calm to excited), as in Russell's circumplex model. Robert Plutchik proposed a "wheel of emotions" with eight primary emotions arranged in opposing pairs. Longer-lasting states such as moods are distinguished from brief emotional episodes, and sentiments denote enduring dispositions toward particular objects or people.

Function

Emotions are widely understood as adaptive responses: fear mobilises escape from danger, disgust avoids contamination, anger responds to obstruction or injustice, and joy reinforces beneficial activities. Social emotions such as guilt, shame, pride, and jealousy regulate relationships and group life. Charles Darwin's The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872) founded the evolutionary study of emotion.

Regulation

Emotion regulation refers to the processes by which people influence which emotions they have and how they experience and express them — through strategies such as reappraisal, suppression, and situation selection. Difficulties in regulation are implicated in many psychological disorders, while effective regulation supports wellbeing and social functioning.
