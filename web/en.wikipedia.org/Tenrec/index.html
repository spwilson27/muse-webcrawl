Tenrec

A tenrec is a mammal belonging to the family Tenrecidae, found almost exclusively on the island of Madagascar, with three species of otter shrews in mainland Africa sometimes included. The roughly 31 living species show one of the most striking cases of convergent evolution among mammals, resembling hedgehogs, shrews, moles, otters, and mice. Tenrecs range from the tiny shrew tenrecs of a few grams to the common tenrec, which can exceed 2 kilograms (4.4 lb).

Evolution and diversity

Tenrecs belong to the Afrotheria, a clade of mammals of African origin. Molecular evidence suggests a single colonisation of Madagascar, probably by rafting, tens of millions of years ago, followed by an adaptive radiation into the island's vacant niches. Subfamilies include the spiny tenrecs (such as the hedgehog-like greater and lesser hedgehog tenrecs and the streaked tenrecs), the soft-furred rice tenrecs and shrew tenrecs, and the semi-aquatic web-footed tenrec.

Characteristics

Tenrecs are generally nocturnal, with poor eyesight compensated by keen smell and hearing. Many have cloacas, a single opening for urinary, digestive, and reproductive tracts, unusual among placental mammals, and relatively low and variable body temperatures. The common tenrec produces litters of up to 32 young — among the largest of any mammal — while the streaked tenrec can communicate by stridulation, rubbing specialised quills together. Several species undergo torpor during the dry season, and the tailless tenrec may aestivate for months.

Ecology and conservation

Tenrecs occupy habitats from rainforest to dry spiny scrub and fill roles from burrowing insectivore to aquatic predator. The common tenrec is hunted for food and widely kept as an exotic pet, while many forest-dependent species are threatened by habitat loss from logging and slash-and-burn agriculture; several are listed as vulnerable or endangered by the IUCN.
