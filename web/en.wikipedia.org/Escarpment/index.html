Escarpment

An escarpment is a long, steep slope, particularly one at the edge of a plateau or separating areas of land at different heights. The term is used to describe the steep slope face of a ridge or long cliff (line of cliffs) formed by faulting or erosion and separating two relatively level areas having different elevations.

Escarpments are usually formed through one of two processes: either the differential erosion of sedimentary rocks, or the movement of the Earth's crust at a geologic fault. One example of faulting creates a scarp known as a fault scarp.

Formation and characteristics

The most typical escarpments result from the erosion of horizontal layers of sedimentary rock where some layers are harder and more resistant than others. The underlying weaker strata are worn away, leaving the resistant cap rock standing as a steep face. The gentler slope behind the escarpment is called the dip slope, and the two together form a landform known as a cuesta.

Notable escarpments include the Niagara Escarpment in North America, the Great Escarpment of southern Africa, and the escarpments of the Great Rift Valley in East Africa. The Niagara Escarpment runs more than 1,000 km from New York State through Ontario to Wisconsin.

Etymology

The word derives from the French escarpement, from escarper "to cut steep". In military usage, an escarp was the inner side of the ditch below a rampart; the term later passed into geology and geography.
