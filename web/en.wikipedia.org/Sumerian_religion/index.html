Sumerian religion

Sumerian religion was the religion practised and adhered to by the people of Sumer, the first literate civilization of ancient Mesopotamia. The Sumerians regarded their divinities as responsible for all matters pertaining to the natural and social orders.

Pantheon

The Sumerians worshipped a large pantheon of anthropomorphic gods. At its head stood Anu, god of the sky, though practical authority over the gods rested with Enlil, god of wind and storms, whose temple at Nippur was a major religious centre. Enki (later Ea), god of fresh water, wisdom and creation, was associated with the city of Eridu and the subterranean freshwater ocean, the Abzu. Ninhursag was the great mother goddess, while Inanna (later Ishtar), goddess of love and war, was patron of Uruk and one of the most widely venerated deities in Mesopotamia. The sun god Utu administered justice, and the moon god Nanna was worshipped at Ur.

Temples and cult

Each city-state had a patron deity whose temple, often raised on a stepped platform known as a ziggurat, dominated the urban landscape. The temple was conceived as the god's literal household, staffed by priests who performed daily rituals of washing, clothing and feeding the divine statue. Festivals, above all the New Year (Akitu) celebrations, punctuated the religious calendar and reaffirmed the bond between the king, the gods and the city.

Mythology

Sumerian myths explained the origin and ordering of the world. The creation was often framed as the separation of heaven (An) and earth (Ki). Humans, according to myths such as Enki and Ninmah, were fashioned from clay to relieve the gods of labour. The Epic of Gilgamesh, which incorporated earlier Sumerian poems about the king of Uruk, includes a flood narrative closely paralleled by the separate myth of Ziusudra. Inanna's descent to the underworld (Kur), a realm of dust ruled by Ereshkigal, is among the best-preserved Sumerian mythological compositions.

Afterlife and death

The Sumerians envisioned a bleak afterlife in the Kur, where the dead ate dust and clay regardless of their conduct in life. Funerary offerings by surviving relatives could improve a shade's condition. There was no notion of posthumous moral judgement comparable to that of ancient Egypt.

Legacy

Sumerian religion profoundly influenced the later religious traditions of Mesopotamia, including those of the Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians, who adopted most of the pantheon under Semitic names. Through these cultures, elements of Sumerian mythology, including the flood story, passed into wider Near Eastern tradition.
