Low-power broadcasting is a form of broadcasting in which a radio or television station operates at a significantly lower transmitter power than full-power stations, serving a small local area. Such services are intended to foster localism, diversity of ownership and community media, and are licensed in many countries under special secondary classes of licence.

Low-power FM in the United States

The best-known form in the United States is LPFM (low-power FM), a noncommercial educational service created by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) in January 2000. LPFM stations operate with between 1 and 100 watts, giving a service radius of roughly 3.5 miles (5.6 km). Licences are reserved for nonprofit organisations, schools, churches, and local government and public-safety agencies.

The service faced opposition from commercial broadcasters and National Public Radio over interference concerns, and Congress restricted it in the Radio Broadcasting Preservation Act of 2000. The Local Community Radio Act of 2010 removed most of those restrictions, and a major FCC filing window in 2013 led to well over a thousand new LPFM construction permits. A further window opened in 2023.

Low-power television

The FCC established the low-power television (LPTV) service in 1982 to provide locally oriented programming to rural and underserved urban areas. LPTV stations, and their even smaller Class A and translator cousins, operate on a secondary basis and must not interfere with full-power stations. Many carried niche, religious, foreign-language or home-shopping programming. The transition to digital television and the 2016–17 spectrum incentive auction displaced many such stations.

Around the world

Comparable services exist internationally: community radio in the United Kingdom licensed by Ofcom, "restricted service licences" for short-term broadcasts, campus radio in Canada, and narrowcast licences in Australia. Unlicensed very-low-power transmissions, such as short-range FM transmitters for personal use, are permitted in many jurisdictions under strict emission limits, while unlicensed broadcasting beyond those limits constitutes pirate radio.
