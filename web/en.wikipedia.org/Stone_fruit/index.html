Stone fruit

A peach, showing flesh and central stone

In botany, a stone fruit (or drupe) is a fruit in which an outer fleshy part (exocarp, or skin, and mesocarp, or flesh) surrounds a single shell (the pit, stone, or pyrena) of hardened endocarp with a seed (kernel) inside. These fruits usually develop from a single carpel and mostly from flowers with superior ovaries.

Examples

Familiar stone fruits include members of the genus Prunus — peach, plum, cherry, apricot, and almond — as well as the mango, olive, coconut, and coffee cherry. The family Rosaceae produces many commercially important drupes.

Structure

The hardened endocarp protects the seed during passage through an animal's digestive tract, aiding dispersal. In some fruits, such as the coconut, the mesocarp is fibrous rather than fleshy; in aggregate fruits like the raspberry, each tiny unit is itself a small drupe (drupelet).

Culinary use

Stone fruits are eaten fresh, dried, and cooked, and are central to many cuisines. The seeds of some, such as apricot kernels, contain amygdalin and must be eaten only in small quantities.
