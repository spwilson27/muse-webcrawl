Charge-coupled device

A CCD image sensor.
Invented1969, Bell Labs
InventorsWillard Boyle and George E. Smith
RecognitionNobel Prize in Physics, 2009

A charge-coupled device (CCD) is an electronic device for the movement of electrical charge, usually within a semiconductor, from one stage to the next. In its best-known application, the CCD is an image sensor: an integrated circuit containing an array of linked light-sensitive capacitors that convert photons into electrical charge, which is then shifted row by row to a readout register and converted to a voltage.

History

The CCD was invented in 1969 at Bell Telephone Laboratories by Willard Boyle and George E. Smith, who were working on semiconductor bubble memory when they realised the same structure could act as a shift register for charge and could capture images via the photoelectric effect in silicon. Originally developed with imaging in mind for picture telephones, the device was quickly adopted by astronomers in the 1970s for its high quantum efficiency and linearity compared with photographic plates and vidicon tubes. Boyle and Smith shared half of the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for the invention.

Operation

In an imaging CCD, light falls on a grid of photosites. During exposure, each photosite accumulates charge proportional to the light it receives. After exposure, clock voltages on the gate electrodes shift the charge packets in lockstep toward one edge of the array (parallel register) and then along a serial register to an output amplifier, which converts each packet to a voltage that is digitised. Colour images are commonly produced using a Bayer filter mosaic of red, green, and blue filters over the photosites, with three-chip cameras using dichroic prisms as an alternative in professional equipment.

Properties

CCDs are valued for high fill factor, excellent uniformity, low read noise (especially in cooled scientific cameras), and very high quantum efficiency — back-illuminated scientific devices can exceed 90%. Drawbacks include power consumption, the tendency to "bloom" (charge spilling into neighbouring pixels when a photosite saturates, causing vertical streaks), and the sequential readout that limits frame rates.

Applications

CCDs transformed observational astronomy (including the Hubble Space Telescope's cameras), enabled digital photography from the 1980s onward, and remain standard in photometry, spectroscopy, microscopy, and machine vision. In consumer cameras and phones, CCDs have been largely displaced by CMOS active-pixel sensors, which integrate amplification and readout electronics per pixel, consume less power, and are cheaper to fabricate, though CCDs persist in scientific and industrial niches.
