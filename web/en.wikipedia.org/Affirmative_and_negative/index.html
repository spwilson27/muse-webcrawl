Affirmative and negative

In policy debate, the affirmative (Aff) and the negative (Neg) are the two sides of a formal argument. The affirmative side advocates for a resolution ("The United States federal government should substantially increase..."), typically by presenting a plan, while the negative side argues against the affirmative's case or against the resolution itself.

Roles in a debate round

The affirmative speaks first and last. Its first constructive speech presents the case: typically harms in the status quo, inherency (why the status quo cannot solve them), a plan text, and solvency (why the plan works), often framed as advantages. The negative responds with refutation of the case and with off-case positions such as disadvantages, counterplans, topicality arguments, and critiques (kritiks).

Burden of proof

The affirmative bears the burden of proving the resolution true; the negative generally enjoys presumption — if arguments are tied, the negative wins, because the status quo is presumed preferable to an unproven change. Affirmative teams must win that their plan is desirable and workable; negative teams need only defeat that claim or show the plan's costs outweigh its benefits.

Speeches

A standard policy debate round has eight speeches: two constructive speeches per side, in which new arguments may be introduced, interleaved with cross-examination, followed by rebuttals, in which debaters extend and crystallise arguments rather than introducing new ones. The order is fixed: affirmative constructive, negative constructive, and so on, ending with the affirmative rebuttal.
