Rio Grande rift

The Rio Grande rift is a north-trending continental rift zone in the southwestern United States, extending from central Colorado in the north to the state of Chihuahua, Mexico, in the south. The rift separates the Colorado Plateau in the west from the interior of the North American craton in the east, and the Rio Grande river follows its course for much of its length.

Geology

Rifting began in the mid-Cenozoic, roughly 35 to 29 million years ago, as the crust stretched and thinned in response to changing plate boundary conditions and the buoyancy of the elevated western United States. The rift is not a single continuous fault system but an en echelon series of deep sedimentary basins — including the San Luis, Española, Albuquerque and Socorro basins — bounded by normal faults and filled with volcanic rocks and sediment of the Santa Fe Group. Extension was accompanied by extensive volcanism, producing features such as the Jemez volcanic field and the Taos Plateau volcanic field.

Significance

The rift remains tectonically and geothermally active, with ongoing seismicity, uplift measured around Socorro, and hot springs along fault zones. Major cities including Albuquerque and Santa Fe in New Mexico and El Paso in Texas, together with Ciudad Juárez in Mexico, lie within the rift valley.
