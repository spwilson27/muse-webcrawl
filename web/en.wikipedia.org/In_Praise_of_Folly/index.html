In Praise of Folly

AuthorDesiderius Erasmus
Original titleMoriae Encomium
LanguageLatin
GenreSatire
Published1511

In Praise of Folly (Latin: Moriae Encomium) is an essay written in Latin in 1509 by Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam and first printed in Paris in June 1511. It was inspired by De Triumpho Stultitiae, written by the Italian humanist Faustino Perisauli. In the essay, Folly, personified as a goddess and the daughter of Plutus, praises herself in a satirical oration that lampoons the superstitions, pedantry, and corruption of European society, including theologians, monks, and the Roman Catholic Church of Erasmus's day.

Early printed edition of Erasmus's satire.

Content

The essay is a rhetorical tour de force in which Folly argues that folly, self-deception, and madness are essential to human happiness: children, lovers, hunters, gamblers, and scholars all depend on her gifts. The work grows progressively more pointed, mocking scholastic theologians, monastic orders, indulgences, and the veneration of saints, before turning serious in its closing pages, where Erasmus contrasts worldly folly with the "folly of the Cross" and Christian piety. Hans Holbein the Younger illustrated a copy with famous marginal drawings.

Influence

The essay was an international bestseller and influenced Renaissance humanism and, indirectly, the Protestant Reformation. Martin Luther admired Erasmus's wit, though the two later fell out over free will. The work remains a classic of satirical literature and a key document of Northern Renaissance humanism.
