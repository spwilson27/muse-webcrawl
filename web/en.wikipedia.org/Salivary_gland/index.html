Salivary gland

The three pairs of major salivary glands: parotid, submandibular, and sublingual.

The salivary glands in mammals are exocrine glands that produce saliva through a system of ducts. Humans have three paired major salivary glands — the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands — as well as hundreds of minor salivary glands distributed through the mouth and throat. The glands together produce roughly 0.5 to 1.5 litres of saliva per day.

Major glands

The parotid glands, located in front of and below each ear, are the largest salivary glands; their ducts (Stensen's ducts) open into the cheek opposite the upper second molars. The submandibular glands lie beneath the floor of the mouth and drain through Wharton's ducts beside the tongue's frenulum. The sublingual glands, the smallest of the major glands, sit under the tongue and drain through numerous small ducts.

Saliva

Saliva is mostly water, with electrolytes, mucus, enzymes such as salivary amylase (ptyalin) and lingual lipase, and antimicrobial compounds including lysozyme and secretory IgA. It lubricates food for swallowing, begins starch digestion, protects the teeth by buffering acids, and aids taste and speech. Secretion is controlled mainly by the parasympathetic nervous system, stimulated by the sight, smell, and taste of food.

Clinical significance

Blocked ducts can form salivary stones (sialolithiasis), causing painful swelling at mealtimes. Viral infection of the parotid gland causes mumps. Autoimmune disease, notably Sjögren syndrome, can destroy gland tissue and produce a chronically dry mouth (xerostomia). Tumours of the salivary glands are uncommon and most often arise in the parotid gland.
