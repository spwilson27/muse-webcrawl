Minicomputer

A minicomputer, or colloquially a mini, is a class of smaller general-purpose computers that developed in the mid-1960s, positioned between mainframes and the later microcomputers. Minicomputers were far cheaper than mainframes — often under US$25,000 — allowing departments, laboratories and smaller companies to own dedicated computing power rather than sharing a central machine.

History

Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) is credited with defining the class with the PDP-8 (1965), which sold for about $18,500 and was small enough to sit on a bench. DEC's PDP-11 (1970) became one of the most influential computer designs ever, shaping the C programming language and Unix, both developed on PDP machines at Bell Labs. The 32-bit VAX-11/780 (1977) extended the line into "superminicomputers."

Competitors included Data General (Nova and Eclipse), Hewlett-Packard (HP 2100 and HP 3000), Prime Computer, Wang Laboratories and the IBM System/34 and AS/400 lines. Minicomputers powered process control, CAD, word processing (Wang systems were ubiquitous in offices) and multi-user timesharing for businesses.

Decline

By the late 1980s and 1990s, microprocessor-based workstations and servers running Unix or Windows NT delivered minicomputer performance at a fraction of the cost, and the class effectively disappeared, absorbed into the midrange server market. Their legacy endures in operating system design, interactive computing and the architecture of modern processors.
