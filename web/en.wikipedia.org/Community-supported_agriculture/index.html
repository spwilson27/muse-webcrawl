Community-supported agriculture

Community-supported agriculture (CSA) is a system that connects producers and consumers within the food system more closely by allowing consumers to subscribe to the harvest of a certain farm or group of farms. Subscribers (or "shareholders") pay in advance for a share of the anticipated harvest and receive regular boxes of seasonal produce — and sometimes eggs, meat, flowers, or other farm products — throughout the growing season.

History

The model has roots in several traditions: the teikei ("partnership") movement begun in Japan in the 1960s and 1970s by housewives concerned about food safety and pesticide use; biodynamic farming initiatives in Switzerland and Germany; and the first American CSA farms established in the mid-1980s, including Indian Line Farm in Massachusetts and Temple-Wilton Community Farm in New Hampshire.

How it works

In the classic model, members buy a share before the season begins, giving the farmer working capital and sharing both the risks (poor weather, crop failure) and the bounty (bumper harvests) of farming. Shares are typically distributed weekly at pickup points or delivered. Many CSAs invite members to work on the farm, vote on crops, or attend harvest festivals, strengthening the relationship between growers and eaters.

Growth and variations

CSA programs spread widely in North America, Europe, and East Asia, numbering in the thousands in the United States alone. Variations include multi-farm CSAs, workplace CSAs, meat and seafood shares, and subscription models offered by aggregators. Critics note that the model can be inflexible for consumers and administratively demanding for farmers, but supporters emphasize its benefits for local economies, farm viability, and food literacy.
