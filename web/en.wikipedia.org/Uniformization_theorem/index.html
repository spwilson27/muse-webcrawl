Uniformization theorem

In mathematics, the uniformization theorem states that every simply connected Riemann surface is conformally equivalent to exactly one of three geometries: the Riemann sphere, the complex plane, or the open unit disc (equivalently, the hyperbolic plane). Consequently every Riemann surface admits a complete Riemannian metric of constant curvature, and its universal cover is one of these three model spaces. The theorem is a far-reaching generalization of the Riemann mapping theorem.

Statement and classification

It follows that every Riemann surface falls into one of three types according to its universal cover: spherical (only the Riemann sphere itself), Euclidean/flat (the plane — including tori and cylinders), or hyperbolic (the disc — the generic case, covering all surfaces of genus at least two and most others). A compact Riemann surface of genus g is spherical if g = 0, flat if g = 1, and hyperbolic if g ≥ 2, matching the sign of the Euler characteristic.

History

The theorem grew out of nineteenth-century work by Riemann and Klein on covering surfaces and Fuchsian groups. Felix Klein and Henri Poincaré announced versions of the result in the 1880s; complete proofs were given in 1907 by Poincaré and Paul Koebe, using continuity and potential-theoretic methods. The name "uniformization" reflects the idea of representing a multivalued analytic function by a single-valued ("uniform") function on the covering surface.

Significance

Uniformization underlies the theory of Riemann surfaces, Teichmüller theory and the classification of complex manifolds in dimension one. Its conjectural analogue for three-manifolds, pursued through Thurston's geometrization conjecture and proved by Perelman, showed that similar constant-curvature decomposition governs far more of geometry and topology.
