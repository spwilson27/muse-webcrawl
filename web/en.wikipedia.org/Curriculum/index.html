Curriculum

In education, a curriculum (plural: curricula or curriculums) is broadly defined as the totality of student experiences that occur in the educational process. The term often refers specifically to a planned sequence of instruction, or to a view of the student's experiences in terms of the educator's or school's instructional goals. A curriculum may include the interactive goals, methods, materials, and assessments used to teach a course or program.

The word derives from the Latin curriculum, a race or race course, and was first used in an educational sense in the records of the University of Glasgow in 1633.

Types

  • The explicit (written) curriculum consists of officially planned subjects, syllabi, and learning objectives.
  • The hidden curriculum covers the unwritten lessons students learn from the structure and culture of schooling — norms, values, and attitudes conveyed implicitly.
  • The null curriculum refers to what schools choose not to teach, which itself signals what a society values.
  • The extracurricular dimension includes sports, clubs, and other activities outside formal instruction.

Development and theory

Ralph Tyler's 1949 rationale — organized around purposes, experiences, organization, and evaluation — became a foundational model of curriculum development. Later theorists, including Jerome Bruner with the "spiral curriculum," Joseph Schwab, and Lawrence Stenhouse, emphasized disciplinary structure, deliberation, and the teacher as researcher. Modern curriculum frameworks range from national curricula set by central governments to standards-based approaches such as the Common Core in the United States.

Core curriculum

Many systems mandate a core curriculum of compulsory subjects — typically language, mathematics, science, history, and physical education — intended to guarantee a common foundation for all students. Debates over the curriculum commonly concern the balance between knowledge and skills, the selection of content (including whose history and literature are taught), and the degree of teacher autonomy.
