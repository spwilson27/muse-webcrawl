Armagnac

Armagnac is a distinctive type of brandy produced in the Armagnac region of Gascony in southwest France. It is distilled from wine — traditionally from a blend of grape varieties including Ugni blanc, Folle blanche, Colombard and Baco — using a single continuous column still, the alambic armagnacais, and aged in oak barrels. Distillation in the region is documented as early as the 14th century, making Armagnac one of France's oldest eau-de-vie traditions.

Region

The appellation, defined in 1909 and granted AOC status in 1936, covers parts of the Gers, Landes and Lot-et-Garonne departments and is divided into three sub-regions: Bas-Armagnac in the west, whose sandy soils produce the most prized spirits; Armagnac-Ténarèze around Condom; and Haut-Armagnac to the east and south.

Production

Unlike Cognac, which is double-distilled in pot stills, Armagnac is usually distilled once in a continuous still at a lower strength, retaining more flavour compounds. The young spirit ages in barrels of local black oak or Limousin oak. Armagnacs are sold as blends (labelled VS, VSOP, XO by minimum age) or as vintages from a single harvest year, a practice much more common than in Cognac.

History

Armagnac merchants traded through the port of Bordeaux, and the spirit developed a devoted export market, particularly in Britain and northern Europe. The region shares its name with the historic County of Armagnac and the Armagnac party of the Armagnac–Burgundian Civil War during the Hundred Years' War.
