Quaker Act 1662

Quakers imprisoned in the 1660s

The Quaker Act 1662 (14 Cha. 2. c. 1), formally "An Act for preventing the Mischiefs and Dangers that may arise by certain Persons called Quakers and others refusing to take lawful Oaths," was an Act of the Parliament of England. It was the first of the penal laws aimed at the Religious Society of Friends after the Restoration of Charles II.

Provisions and enforcement

The Act declared it high treason to deny that the king was supreme head of the Church, and made it an offence for Quakers and others to refuse the oath of allegiance — which Quakers refused on principle, citing the biblical injunction against swearing — or to assemble for worship in groups of five or more beyond a single household. Penalties escalated from fines for a first offence, to imprisonment for a second, and to transportation (and the penalties of praemunire) for a third. Large numbers of Friends were prosecuted under the Act, and persecution intensified with the Conventicle Act 1664 and the Five Mile Act 1665.

Repeal and legacy

The provisions were effectively suspended by James II's declarations of indulgence and were repealed by the Toleration Act 1689, although affirmation in place of oath was only fully regularised for Quakers by later legislation. The Act is remembered as a landmark of the Restoration's religious coercion and of the Quakers' early sufferings, carefully documented by the Friends themselves.
