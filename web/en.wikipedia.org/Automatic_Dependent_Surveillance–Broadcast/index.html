Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast

ADS-B lets aircraft broadcast their GPS-derived position to ground stations and other aircraft.

Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B) is an aircraft surveillance technology in which an aircraft periodically broadcasts its identity, position, altitude, velocity and other data, determined from its onboard navigation systems (usually GPS). The name summarises the technology: it is automatic (no pilot input required), dependent (on the aircraft's own navigation data), surveillance information, broadcast to any receiver in range.

How it works

Aircraft equipped with ADS-B Out transmit messages once or twice per second, typically either on the 1090 MHz Mode S Extended Squitter link used by transponders, or, in the United States for general aviation below 18,000 feet, on the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT). Ground stations and other aircraft ("ADS-B In") receive the broadcasts. Because the data does not depend on radar interrogation, ADS-B provides accurate, frequently updated surveillance in remote and oceanic airspace — increasingly supplemented by space-based receivers on the Iridium satellite constellation, which made global coverage possible from 2019.

Mandates and adoption

The United States required ADS-B Out in most controlled airspace from 1 January 2020 under the FAA's NextGen programme; the European Union imposed a similar mandate for larger aircraft from 2020. Australia required ADS-B for instrument flight rules operations from 2017. Benefits include reduced separation standards, better search-and-rescue data — ADS-B tracks were central to reconstruction efforts after the disappearance of Malaysia Airlines Flight 370 — and public flight-tracking services built on networks of volunteer receivers.

Limitations

ADS-B broadcasts are unencrypted and unauthenticated, making them vulnerable to spoofing, and the system depends on the integrity of GNSS signals, which can be jammed or spoofed. Military aircraft may switch off broadcasts, and coverage and equipage remain uneven for light aircraft in some regions.
