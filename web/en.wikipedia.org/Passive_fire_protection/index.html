Passive fire protection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Firestopping around a service penetration in a fire-rated wall

Passive fire protection (PFP) is the set of components and systems of a building or structure that slow or prevent the spread of fire and its effects without needing activation, movement or external power. Unlike active fire protection — sprinklers, alarms and extinguishers, which respond to a fire — passive measures are built into the fabric of the structure and work by compartmentation: dividing the building into fire-rated cells that contain a fire for a specified period, protecting escape routes and structural stability.

Components

The principal elements of passive fire protection include:

  • Fire-resistance rated walls, floors and ceilings, tested to maintain integrity and insulation for a stated period (such as 30, 60 or 120 minutes).
  • Firestopping of penetrations where pipes, cables and ducts pass through rated assemblies, using sealants, collars, wraps and mortars.
  • Fire doors and shutters, which close openings in compartments while allowing normal circulation.
  • Structural fire protection, such as intumescent coatings, sprayed fire-resistive materials, board encasement and concrete cover, which insulate steel and other structural elements from heat.
  • Fire and smoke dampers in ventilation ductwork, and fire-resistant glazing and cavity barriers.

Testing and regulation

Passive systems are qualified by standardised furnace tests — for example under ASTM E119, UL 263, EN 1363 and related standards — which expose assemblies to a standard time–temperature curve and rate them by the duration for which they resist fire penetration and limit temperature rise. Because passive protection is concealed once construction is complete, building codes and fire-safety guidance increasingly emphasise inspection, documentation and maintenance: a single unsealed penetration can defeat an entire compartment.

Relationship to active systems

Modern fire engineering treats passive and active measures together as a balanced package; sprinklers may justify reductions in passive ratings in some codes, while passive compartmentation protects occupants and firefighters even if active systems fail or are overwhelmed.
