The Ives–Stilwell experiment was a landmark test of special relativity performed by Herbert E. Ives and G. R. Stilwell, first reported in 1938. It measured the transverse (second-order) Doppler effect — the redshift of light emitted by a moving source that arises purely from time dilation — and provided one of the most direct early confirmations that moving clocks run slow.

Principle

Classical physics predicts a first-order Doppler shift for light from an approaching or receding source, but no shift at right angles and no net second-order effect. Relativity predicts an additional shift proportional to v²/c²: light from a rapidly moving atom should be slightly redshifted even after the ordinary Doppler effect is removed, because the moving atom's internal "clock" ticks more slowly. Ives and Stilwell measured the wavelengths of hydrogen Balmer lines (Hβ) emitted by a beam of fast hydrogen ions (canal rays), observing the beam both head-on in the forward and backward directions and averaging the two: the first-order shifts cancel in the mean, leaving the tiny second-order displacement of the line's centre of gravity — exactly the offset relativity predicts.

Results and later tests

The 1938 measurements, and a refined 1941 version with higher beam velocities, matched the relativistic prediction within experimental error. Ives himself — a committed opponent of Einstein's interpretation — presented the result as confirming the Lorentz–Larmor theory of length contraction and clock retardation in the ether, an explanation empirically equivalent in this context; the experiment is nonetheless standardly cited as evidence for relativistic time dilation. The Ives–Stilwell design has been repeated with far greater precision using Mössbauer rotors, fast ion beams in storage rings, and laser spectroscopy of lithium ions, which by the 2000s–2010s had verified the time-dilation formula to parts in a billion or better.
