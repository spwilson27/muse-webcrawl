Aquaponics

In aquaponics, fish waste fertilises plants, and the plants clean the water for the fish.

Aquaponics is a food production system that couples aquaculture (raising aquatic animals such as fish, crayfish, or prawns in tanks) with hydroponics (cultivating plants in water) in a symbiotic, recirculating environment. Water laden with fish waste is pumped to the plant beds, where bacteria convert ammonia into nitrates that plants absorb as fertiliser; the cleaned water then returns to the fish tank.

How it works

The system's engine is the nitrogen cycle: fish excrete ammonia, nitrifying bacteria (Nitrosomonas) oxidise it to nitrite, and others (Nitrobacter/Nitrospira) convert nitrite to nitrate. Common designs include media-bed systems (gravel or clay pebbles), nutrient film technique (NFT) channels, and deep water culture rafts. Because the fish and plants share one body of water, aquaponics uses far less water than soil agriculture—often cited as up to 90% less—and needs little or no synthetic fertiliser, since fish feed supplies most nutrients (though iron, potassium, and calcium are commonly supplemented).

History and scale

Antecedents include the Aztec chinampas, Chinese rice-fish farming, and floating gardens of South Asia. Modern aquaponics was developed from the 1970s–90s, notably by researchers at the University of the Virgin Islands (James Rakocy) and through the work of Mark McMurtry and others. Most aquaponic operations are small-scale—backyard, community, and educational systems—though commercial farms growing tilapia and greens operate in several countries.

Advantages and challenges

Advantages include water efficiency, reduced fertiliser and pesticide use, and dual harvests of fish and produce. Challenges include high start-up and energy costs (pumps, aeration, heating), the need to balance the differing pH and temperature optima of fish, plants, and bacteria, and dependence on commercial fish feed.
