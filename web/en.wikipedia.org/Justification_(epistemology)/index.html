Justification (epistemology)

FieldEpistemology
ConcernsBelief, evidence, knowledge
Key problemGettier problem
Main theoriesFoundationalism, coherentism, reliabilism

In epistemology, justification is the property of a belief that makes it rational or warranted for a person to hold it. On the traditional tripartite analysis, knowledge requires belief that is both true and justified. What counts as sufficient justification, and how it is structured, is one of the central disputes in the theory of knowledge.

Theories of justification

Foundationalism holds that justification rests on basic beliefs that are self-evident, incorrigible, or otherwise justified without support from other beliefs; all other justified beliefs derive from these foundations. Coherentism denies that foundations are needed, holding instead that a belief is justified by its coherence with the believer's overall system of beliefs. Infinitism accepts an infinite, non-repeating chain of reasons. The regress problem — that justifying reasons themselves seem to need justification — motivates all three responses.

Internalism and externalism

Internalists argue that the factors justifying a belief must be accessible to the believer's reflection. Externalists disagree: on reliabilism, associated with Alvin Goldman, a belief is justified if it is produced by a reliable cognitive process, whether or not the subject knows the process is reliable. The debate shapes discussions of perception, testimony, and memory as sources of justification.

The Gettier problem

In a famous 1963 paper, Edmund Gettier presented cases in which a belief is true and justified yet seems to fall short of knowledge because its truth is a matter of luck. The counterexamples overturned the classical analysis of knowledge and generated decades of proposed fourth conditions, including no-false-lemma requirements and defeasibility conditions.

Degrees and defeat

Justification typically comes in degrees and can be undermined by defeaters: new evidence may rebut a belief by supporting its negation, or undercut it by attacking the connection between the evidence and the belief. Epistemologists distinguish propositional justification (there is justification for believing something) from doxastic justification (the belief is held in the right way, on the basis of that justification).
