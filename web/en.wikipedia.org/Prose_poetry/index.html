Prose poetry

Prose poetry is poetry written in prose form instead of verse, while otherwise retaining poetic qualities such as heightened imagery, figurative language, and emotional effects. It appears as prose — without the line breaks associated with poetry — though it may make internal use of poetic devices such as repetition, fragmentation, rhyme, and rhythm.

History

The prose poem emerged in France in the early 19th century as a reaction against the rigid metrical rules of French verse, especially the alexandrine. Aloysius Bertrand's Gaspard de la nuit (1842) is an early landmark, and Charles Baudelaire's posthumous Le Spleen de Paris (also called Petits poèmes en prose, 1869) established the form. Baudelaire wrote of his dream of "a poetic prose, musical without rhythm and without rhyme, supple enough and rugged enough to adapt itself to the lyrical impulses of the soul". Arthur Rimbaud's Illuminations and Stéphane Mallarmé further developed the form.

Characteristics

Prose poems typically range from a sentence or two to several pages. They rely on compression, vivid imagery, and tonal intensity rather than lineation, and their prose appearance often makes them hard to distinguish from flash fiction or vignettes — a boundary that remains contested among writers and critics.

Later developments

In the 20th century the form spread widely: writers such as Gertrude Stein, Francis Ponge, and Zbigniew Herbert used it, and it flourished in postwar American poetry through figures including Robert Bly, Russell Edson, and Charles Simic. It remains a popular contemporary form and a staple of literary journals.
