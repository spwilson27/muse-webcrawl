Gilsonite

Gilsonite is the registered trademark name of a form of natural asphaltite known generically as uintahite (or uintaite). It is a naturally occurring solid hydrocarbon resin, a black, brittle, lustrous mineraloid with a high melting point, found principally in the Uinta Basin of northeastern Utah and western Colorado in the United States.

Gilsonite was discovered in the early 1860s and was named after Samuel H. Gilson, who promoted its commercial development in the 1880s. It occurs in vertical veins or dikes up to several metres wide that fill fractures in the sedimentary rocks of the Green River Formation region; these veins were formed when ancient petroleum migrated into cracks and solidified over geological time.

Properties and uses

Gilsonite is soluble in aromatic and aliphatic petroleum solvents and has a softening point that varies by grade, roughly between 160 °C and 220 °C. Because of its high nitrogen content and compatibility with petroleum products, it is used commercially in more than 160 applications, including:

  • Oil well drilling fluids and oil well cementing, where it controls fluid loss and stabilises shale;
  • Printing inks and paints, as a resin and carbon black dispersant;
  • Asphalt modification and paving;
  • Foundry sand additives and chemical products.

Mining

The mineral is extracted by underground mining in the Uinta Basin, originally hauled by wagon and narrow-gauge railway from mines near Dragon and Watson, Utah. "Gilsonite" remains a trademark of the American Gilsonite Company, the principal producer.
