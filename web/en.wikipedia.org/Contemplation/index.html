Contemplation

Contemplation
TypeSpiritual and philosophical practice
FromLatin contemplatio; Greek theōria
Found inChristianity, Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam (Sufism), Greek philosophy

Contemplation is the practice of sustained, attentive reflection or receptive awareness directed toward an object, idea, or the divine. In religious contexts it usually denotes a form of prayer or meditation in which the mind rests in quiet attention rather than analysing or petitioning; in philosophy it can mean deep theoretical reflection on truth.

Etymology and ancient philosophy

The English word derives from Latin contemplatio, translating the Greek theōria — beholding or spectating. Plato and Aristotle regarded contemplation of eternal truths as the highest human activity; Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics praises the contemplative life (bios theōrētikos) as the happiest and most divine. Plotinus and the Neoplatonists later made contemplation the soul's path of return to the One.

In Christianity

Early monastic writers such as Evagrius Ponticus and John Cassian systematised contemplative prayer, and the practice was cultivated by medieval mystics including Bernard of Clairvaux, Julian of Norwich, Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross. In the classic threefold way, contemplation follows purgation and illumination as the stage of loving union with God. Modern revivals include Thomas Merton's writings and the Centering Prayer movement.

In other traditions

Analogous practices appear in Buddhist meditation (such as śamatha and insight meditation), Hindu dhyāna, Sufi dhikr and murāqaba, and Jewish hitbodedut. Contemporary psychology and the mindfulness movement have adapted contemplative practices for secular settings, where they are studied for effects on attention, stress and well-being.
