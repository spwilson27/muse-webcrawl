USS Essex (CV/CVA/CVS-9) was an aircraft carrier and the lead ship of the 24-vessel Essex class, the most numerous class of capital ships built in the 20th century. Built by Newport News Shipbuilding and commissioned on the last day of 1942, she was the fourth US Navy ship to bear the name Essex.

Second World War

After shakedown, Essex joined the Pacific Fleet in May 1943 and became a core unit of the fast carrier task forces. Her air groups attacked Wake Island, raided Rabaul and the Gilbert Islands, supported the landings at Tarawa and Makin, and struck Kwajalein, Truk, Saipan, Tinian and Guam. In 1944 she took part in the Battle of the Philippine Sea, the raids on the Philippines and Formosa, and the Battle of Leyte Gulf.

On 25 November 1944, during operations off the Philippines, a Japanese kamikaze crashed into her flight deck, killing 15 men and wounding 44, but she was repaired and returned to service. In 1945 her aircraft supported the invasions of Iwo Jima and Okinawa and struck the Japanese home islands, including attacks on the battleship Yamato's final sortie. She received the Presidential Unit Citation and 13 battle stars for her wartime service.

Post-war service

Decommissioned in 1947, Essex was modernised under the SCB-27A programme, receiving a strengthened flight deck, new island and hydraulic catapults, and recommissioned in 1951. She served three tours in the Korean War, where her Banshee jets flew some of the Navy's first jet combat sorties from a carrier, earning additional battle stars.

Further modernisation (SCB-125) gave her an angled flight deck and enclosed hurricane bow. Redesignated an anti-submarine carrier (CVS-9), she operated in the Atlantic and Mediterranean, participated in the naval quarantine during the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962, and in October 1968 served as the prime recovery ship for the Apollo 7 mission, recovering astronauts Wally Schirra, Donn Eisele and Walter Cunningham after splashdown in the Atlantic.

Decommissioning

Essex was decommissioned on 30 June 1969, struck from the Naval Vessel Register in 1973, and sold for scrapping in 1975. Despite efforts to preserve her as a museum, she was broken up; a number of her artefacts, including one of her anchors and her ship's bell, survive in museums and memorials.
