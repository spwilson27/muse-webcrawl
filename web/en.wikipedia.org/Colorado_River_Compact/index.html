SubjectApportionment of Colorado River water between the Upper and Lower Basins

The Colorado River Compact is an interstate compact signed in 1922 by the seven U.S. states through which the Colorado River flows. It apportioned the river's water between an Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming) and a Lower Basin (Arizona, California, and Nevada), granting each basin the exclusive beneficial use of 7.5 million acre-feet of water per year. Together with later agreements, treaties, statutes, and court decisions, it forms the core of the "Law of the River".

Background and negotiation

Fearful that fast-growing California would claim the river's flow under the doctrine of prior appropriation, the other basin states pressed for an agreed division. Delegates chaired by Commerce Secretary Herbert Hoover negotiated the compact at Bishop's Lodge near Santa Fe in November 1922; Delph Carpenter of Colorado proposed the basin-based apportionment that made agreement possible. Arizona, disputing its share with California, refused to ratify until 1944.

Implementation

The Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928 authorised construction of Hoover Dam and the All-American Canal and divided the Lower Basin's share: 4.4 million acre-feet to California, 2.8 million to Arizona, and 300,000 to Nevada. The Upper Colorado River Basin Compact of 1948 subdivided the Upper Basin's share by percentage. A 1944 treaty guaranteed Mexico 1.5 million acre-feet annually.

Overallocation and modern stress

The negotiators assumed an average annual flow of about 16.4 million acre-feet, based on an unusually wet period; twentieth- and twenty-first-century flows averaged substantially less, and since 2000 a prolonged drought — worsened by climate change — has driven Lakes Mead and Powell to historic lows. The compact's fixed apportionments therefore exceed supply, prompting shortage-sharing agreements (including the 2007 Interim Guidelines and the 2019 Drought Contingency Plans) and ongoing renegotiation among the states, tribes, and Mexico.
