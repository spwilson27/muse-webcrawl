Pontifex maximus

Pontifex maximus (Latin for "greatest priest" or, traditionally, "greatest bridge-builder") was the chief high priest of the College of Pontiffs (Collegium Pontificum) in ancient Rome. The office was the most important position in the Roman state religion, held for life by a single person who supervised the priestly colleges, the Vestal Virgins, the calendar, and the official recording of events.

History

According to tradition, the office was created by Rome's second king, Numa Pompilius, in the 7th century BC. During the Republic the pontifex maximus was elected by a portion of the tribal assembly and was one of the few religious offices open to political competition; Julius Caesar famously won a fiercely contested election for the post in 63 BC. After Lepidus, from whom Augustus assumed the office in 12 BC, the title became a prerogative of the emperors, and it appears as "PONT MAX" on imperial coinage. The emperors held it until Gratian relinquished it around AD 382.

Duties

The pontifex maximus presided over the pontifical college, adjudicated sacred law, regulated the calendar (including intercalation before Caesar's reform), supervised adoptions and wills where they touched on sacral obligations, and kept the annales maximi, the official chronicle. His residence was the Domus Publica in the Roman Forum.

Later use

The title was later applied to the popes in Latin — as Pontifex Maximus or, in full, Summus Pontifex ("Supreme Pontiff") — reflecting the papacy's claim to inherit Roman religious authority. The abbreviation "PONT MAX" continued to appear on papal coins and inscriptions into modern times.
