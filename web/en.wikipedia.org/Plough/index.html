A plough (also spelled plow in American English) is a farm tool for loosening or turning soil before sowing seed or planting. Ploughing is the primary stage of tillage: it buries weeds and crop residues, aerates the soil and creates a seedbed. For millennia drawn by oxen or horses, ploughs are now almost universally pulled by tractors.

History

The earliest ploughs were digging sticks and hoes; by about 3000 BCE the scratch plough or ard, a wooden implement that scored a furrow without inverting the soil, was in use in Mesopotamia and Egypt. Iron shares improved it in antiquity. The heavy wheeled mouldboard plough, developed in northern Europe in the early Middle Ages, could turn the heavy, wet soils of the region and greatly raised agricultural productivity. In the 18th and 19th centuries, innovations from Jethro Tull's advocacy to John Deere's polished steel plough (1837) suited to sticky prairie soils mechanised ploughing; steam and then tractor power followed.

Types

The mouldboard plough cuts, lifts and inverts a furrow slice with its share, mouldboard and coulter. Disc ploughs use rotating concave discs for hard or stony ground; chisel ploughs loosen without inversion; reversible ploughs carry two sets of bodies so the tractor can work back and forth. Conservation agriculture increasingly favours reduced or no tillage to limit erosion and moisture loss, so conventional ploughing is declining in some regions, though it remains widespread.

Cultural significance

The plough is among humanity's most consequential inventions and a potent symbol of agriculture: it appears in idioms ("ploughshare"), in the constellation name the Plough (the Big Dipper asterism), and in heraldry and coinage.
