Aristide Briand

Born: 28 March 1862, Nantes, France
Died: 7 March 1932, Paris, France
Office: Prime Minister of France (eleven terms, 1909–1929); Minister of Foreign Affairs
Awards: Nobel Peace Prize (1926, shared with Gustav Stresemann)

Aristide Briand (28 March 1862 – 7 March 1932) was a French statesman who served eleven terms as Prime Minister of France and as foreign minister in numerous governments. He played a central role in the separation of church and state in 1905 and became, in the 1920s, a leading advocate of European reconciliation and collective security.

Early career

Born in Nantes to a modest bourgeois family, Briand trained as a lawyer and became active in socialist politics and trade-union journalism. As rapporteur of the 1905 law on the Separation of the Churches and the State, he steered a comparatively moderate secular settlement through the Chamber of Deputies. After breaking with the socialists, he led his first government in 1909 and suppressed a railway strike in 1910, an act that permanently strained his standing on the left.

First World War and after

Briand was prime minister during part of the First World War (1915–1917), overseeing the Salonika expedition and French war policy. In the 1920s he dominated French foreign policy: as foreign minister from 1925 to 1932 he negotiated the Locarno Treaties (1925), which stabilized Germany's western borders, and shared the 1926 Nobel Peace Prize with German foreign minister Gustav Stresemann. He also co-authored the Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928, by which signatories renounced war as an instrument of national policy.

European federation proposal

In a celebrated speech to the League of Nations in 1929 and a formal memorandum in 1930, Briand proposed a European federal union within the League's framework — an early forerunner of European integration, though it foundered amid the Great Depression and rising nationalism.

Death and legacy

Briand died in Paris on 7 March 1932, shortly after an unsuccessful campaign for the presidency. Remembered as an eloquent orator and a pragmatist of reconciliation, he is honoured as one of the founding fathers of the European idea.
