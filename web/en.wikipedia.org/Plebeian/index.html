Plebeians

In ancient Rome, the plebeians (Latin: plebs) were the general body of free Roman citizens who were not members of the patrician class — the hereditary aristocracy that monopolised political and religious office in the early Republic. Plebeians ranged from poor labourers and small farmers to wealthy merchants and landowners excluded from power only by birth.

Conflict of the Orders

Roman history from about 494 BC to 287 BC is marked by the Conflict of the Orders, the plebeians' struggle for political equality. Their chief weapon was secession — a mass withdrawal from the city that paralysed the economy and army. Concessions followed in stages: the creation of the tribunes of the plebs (494 BC), the publication of the Twelve Tables, the admission of plebeians to the consulship (367 BC), and finally the Lex Hortensia of 287 BC, which made plebiscites binding on the whole people.

Later Republic and Empire

Wealthy plebeian families merged with the patricians into a mixed senatorial aristocracy, the nobilitas, while "plebeian" came increasingly to denote the urban poor, sustained in imperial times by distributions of grain, bread, and circuses. The word survives in English as "pleb" and in terms such as plebiscite.
