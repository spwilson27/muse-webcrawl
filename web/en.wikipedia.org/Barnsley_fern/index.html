The Barnsley fern is a fractal named after the British mathematician Michael Barnsley, who first described it in his book Fractals Everywhere (1988). It is constructed using an iterated function system (IFS) and produces a self-similar structure that strikingly resembles the frond of a black spleenwort fern (Asplenium adiantum-nigrum).

The fern is one of the best-known examples of a V-variable fractal and demonstrates how a small set of affine transformations, applied probabilistically, can generate a complex natural-looking shape.

Construction

The Barnsley fern is generated by the chaos game: starting from a point, one of four affine transformations is chosen at random at each iteration, with probabilities heavily biased toward the transformation that draws the main stem and fronds. After many thousands of iterations the plotted points trace out the fern.

The four transformations

ProbabilityRole
1%The stem
85%Successively smaller leaflets
7%Largest left-hand leaflet
7%Largest right-hand leaflet

Each transformation has the form f(x, y) = (ax + by + e, cx + dy + f), with coefficient sets originally given by Barnsley. The first transformation maps points onto the stem; the second produces the main body of the fern, scaled and rotated copies along the stem; the third and fourth generate the two largest leaflets at the base.

Computer generation

Barnsley's fern was one of the first fractals widely reproduced on personal computers, and it is a standard exercise in introductory programming and computer graphics courses. A simple program plots the orbit of a point under the randomly chosen maps for 10,000 or more iterations.

Mutant varieties

By varying the coefficients, "mutant" ferns can be produced, including forms resembling other fern species such as Cyclosorus and Culcita. Barnsley and co-workers also developed superfractals, in which the coefficient sets themselves vary, giving rise to whole populations of related ferns.
