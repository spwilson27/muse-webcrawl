Antoine-Laurent de Lavoisier (26 August 1743 – 8 May 1794) was a French nobleman and chemist who was central to the chemical revolution of the 18th century. He is widely considered the "father of modern chemistry" for his role in establishing the law of conservation of mass and in disproving the phlogiston theory of combustion.

Scientific work

Through careful quantitative experiments Lavoisier showed that combustion and respiration involve combination with a gas he named oxygen, and that matter is conserved in chemical reactions. His Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789) listed 33 elements, introduced a systematic chemical nomenclature developed with colleagues such as Berthollet and Fourcroy, and defined an element operationally. With Pierre-Simon Laplace he used an ice calorimeter to study heat and animal respiration.

Public life and death

Lavoisier was an influential member of several government bodies and an investor in the Ferme générale, the private company that collected taxes for the crown. During the Reign of Terror he was arrested as a former tax farmer and was guillotined in Paris on 8 May 1794. The mathematician Lagrange is said to have remarked that it took only a moment to cut off his head, but a hundred years might not produce another like it.
