Cornett

The cornett (Italian: cornetto, not to be confused with the modern cornet) is an early wind instrument used in the late medieval, Renaissance, and Baroque periods. It is fingered like a woodwind instrument but sounded with a small cup-shaped mouthpiece like a brass instrument.

Traditionally made of wood — typically boxwood curved and covered in leather, though ivory examples survive — the cornett has six finger holes and one thumb hole. It was prized for its ability to blend with voices and was a principal virtuoso instrument in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries, used by composers including Giovanni Gabrieli and Heinrich Schütz in Venetian and German sacred music.

Decline and revival

The cornett fell from use during the eighteenth century as violins and oboes took over its roles. It was revived in the twentieth century by early-music performers, and modern players use historically informed reproductions in performances of Renaissance and Baroque repertoire.
