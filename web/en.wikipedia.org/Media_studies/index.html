Media studies

Media studies examines every major means of communication, from print to networked digital media.

Media studies is an academic discipline and field of study that deals with the content, history, effects, production and political economy of the media, especially the mass media. Drawing on the social sciences and the humanities, media studies examines media such as newspapers, magazines, radio, television, film, recorded music, advertising and the internet, asking how messages are produced, distributed, represented and received, and with what consequences for individuals, cultures and societies.

The field emerged in the twentieth century alongside mass-circulation newspapers, cinema, broadcasting and propaganda, and it has repeatedly reinvented itself as new media have appeared. Its characteristic questions include: Who owns and controls the media, and how does that shape content? How do media texts construct meaning and represent social groups? How do audiences interpret and use media, and how powerful are media effects? And how do technologies of communication themselves — print, telegraph, broadcast, network — restructure perception, politics and everyday life?

History

Origins: propaganda, effects research and the Frankfurt School

Scholarly attention to mass communication grew after the First World War, when the scale of wartime propaganda prompted researchers such as Harold Lasswell to analyse persuasive communication and its political effects. In the United States, the interwar and wartime research of Paul Lazarsfeld and the Columbia school applied surveys and experiments to voting, radio listening and persuasion, founding an empirical "administrative" tradition in communication research. At the same time, émigré scholars of the Frankfurt School — Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer above all — developed a critical account of the "culture industry", arguing in Dialectic of Enlightenment (1944/47) that commercial mass culture standardised consciousness and reinforced capitalist domination.

Medium theory and the Toronto school

A second foundational strand is associated with the Canadian scholars Harold Innis and Marshall McLuhan. Innis's Empire and Communications (1950) and The Bias of Communication (1951) argued that communication technologies bias civilisations toward control over space or over time. McLuhan popularised this insight in Understanding Media (1964), with the slogan "the medium is the message": the form of a medium, he claimed, shapes human association more profoundly than any particular content it carries. His distinction between "hot" and "cool" media and his prophecy of a "global village" created by electronic media remain staples of introductory courses.

British cultural studies

From the 1960s, the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies at the University of Birmingham, founded by Richard Hoggart and directed for a time by Stuart Hall, redirected attention from media effects to meaning-making. Hall's encoding/decoding model (1973) proposed that producers encode preferred meanings into media texts, but audiences decode them from dominant, negotiated or oppositional positions. Cultural studies researchers combined textual analysis, Gramscian theories of hegemony and ethnography of audiences to study news, television drama, youth subcultures and representations of race, class and gender.

Institutional growth and the digital turn

Media studies became a named university subject from the 1960s and 1970s onward — early institutional homes included the Polytechnic of Central London in Britain and departments of communication and film in North America — and expanded rapidly in the 1980s and 1990s, often absorbing film studies, journalism studies and cultural studies. Since the 1990s the rise of the internet, mobile media, games and social platforms has pushed the field toward digital media studies, platform studies, software studies and data studies, and toward questions of algorithms, surveillance, misinformation and artificial intelligence.

Major approaches

Media effects

The effects tradition studies what media do to audiences: agenda-setting (the press tells us what to think about), cultivation (heavy television viewing shapes beliefs about the world), priming and framing, social learning, desensitisation to violence, and "third-person" effects. Early assumptions of powerful, direct effects (the "hypodermic needle" caricature) gave way to models of limited and conditional effects, and later to renewed interest in strong effects in personalised, algorithmically curated environments.

Uses and gratifications and audience studies

Uses and gratifications research reversed the classic question to ask what people do with media, cataloguing needs for information, identity, social interaction and entertainment. Audience ethnography extended this turn, studying fans, households and online communities as active, creative interpreters and producers of media culture.

Political economy

Political economy of communication analyses ownership, advertising, markets and state policy as determinants of media content. Herman and Chomsky's "propaganda model" in Manufacturing Consent (1988) argued that five filters — ownership, advertising, sourcing, flak and ideology — systematically bias news in elite-serving directions. Contemporary political economy studies media concentration, platform monopolies and the economics of attention and data.

Textual, genre and representation studies

Drawing on semiotics, narratology and film theory, these approaches read media texts closely: how genres organise expectations, how narratives are structured, and how media represent gender, race, class, nation and sexuality — and with what ideological effects. Feminist media studies, critical race approaches and postcolonial media studies are major branches.

Medium theory and media archaeology

Following Innis and McLuhan, medium theorists study the material and sensory properties of media themselves, while media archaeology excavates forgotten and obsolete technologies to challenge linear histories of progress. Related work in science and technology studies treats media as infrastructures embedded in labour, energy and geopolitics.

Key concepts

  • Mediation and remediation — the idea that all communication is mediated, and that new media refashion older media forms.
  • Encoding/decoding — Hall's model of the production and reception of media meanings.
  • Gatekeeping and agenda-setting — the selection and salience of news.
  • Convergence — Henry Jenkins's account of content flowing across platforms and of participatory culture.
  • The public sphere — Jürgen Habermas's influential model of media and democratic deliberation, and its critics.
  • Surveillance and platform power — the extraction of data by digital platforms and its social consequences.

Criticism of the field

Media studies has periodically been attacked as a "soft" or vocational subject, and in the United Kingdom the phrase "media studies" became a tabloid byword for supposedly easy degrees — a reputation its defenders attribute to misunderstanding of a rigorous interdisciplinary field. Internal debates continue over its boundaries with communication studies, sociology, film studies and cultural studies; its relative neglect, at times, of production practice and political economy; and the challenges posed by global and non-Western media systems to theories built on Euro-American cases.
