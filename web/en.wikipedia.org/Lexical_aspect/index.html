Lexical aspect

Lexical aspect, also called Aktionsart, is a property of verbs and verb phrases that describes the inherent temporal structure of the situation they denote — whether an event is static or dynamic, has duration, and has a natural endpoint. It is distinguished from grammatical aspect, which is marked by the grammar (for example, the perfective/imperfective contrast in Slavic languages or the progressive in English) and presents a situation from a particular viewpoint. Lexical aspect is determined not by the verb alone but by the whole predicate, including its objects and adjuncts: "run" is open-ended, while "run a mile" has a built-in endpoint.

Vendler's classification

The philosopher Zeno Vendler (1957) proposed four classes of verbs based on their temporal properties:

  • States — static, durative, without endpoint: know, love, own. They do not normally occur in the progressive ("I am knowing the answer" is anomalous).
  • Activities — dynamic, durative, without a natural endpoint (atelic): run, push a cart, swim. "He ran for an hour" is natural; "in an hour" is not.
  • Accomplishments — dynamic, durative, with a natural endpoint (telic): build a house, run a mile, draw a circle. They take "in an hour" and if interrupted, the event has not occurred.
  • Achievements — dynamic, punctual, telic: win the race, reach the summit, notice, die. They occur at an instant and resist the progressive except in special readings.

Diagnostics and extensions

Standard tests include compatibility with the progressive, with "for an hour" versus "in an hour" adverbials, and the entailments of the past progressive ("was building a house" does not entail "built a house"). Later work added categories such as semelfactives (cough, knock — punctual but atelic) and active accomplishments, and analyses by Dowty, Moens and Steedman, and Smith (who separates situation type from viewpoint aspect) refined the theory. Compositional effects are central: adding a quantified object ("eat sandwiches" → activity; "eat three sandwiches" → accomplishment) or a goal phrase can shift a predicate's class.

Importance

Lexical aspect interacts with tense, aspect marking and adverbials in every language and is relevant to language acquisition (children first use perfective markers only with telic verbs — the "aspect hypothesis"), translation, and computational semantics.
