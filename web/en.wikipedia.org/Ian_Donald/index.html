Ian Donald CBE (27 December 1910 – 19 June 1987) was a Scottish physician and obstetrician who pioneered the use of diagnostic ultrasound in medicine. Working in Glasgow with the engineer Tom Brown and clinician John MacVicar, he adapted industrial ultrasonic flaw-detection technology to obstetrics, laying the foundations of the obstetric ultrasound scan now routine in pregnancy care worldwide.

Life and work

Donald was born in Cornwall into a Scottish medical family and was educated partly in South Africa before studying medicine at St Thomas's Hospital, London. He served in the Royal Air Force during the Second World War, where he gained familiarity with radar and sonar technology. After posts in London, he became Regius Professor of Midwifery at the University of Glasgow in 1954. Having encountered ultrasonic metal-flaw detectors used in shipbuilding, Donald, with Brown of the firm Kelvin & Hughes and obstetric registrar MacVicar, built scanning apparatus to distinguish abdominal cysts from tumours. Their landmark 1958 Lancet paper, "Investigation of abdominal masses by pulsed ultrasound," included early images of a fetus and is regarded as the founding publication of medical ultrasonography. Donald championed the technique despite initial scepticism and helped establish the world's first department of medical ultrasound.

Views and legacy

A devout Christian, Donald opposed the Abortion Act 1967 and supported the anti-abortion movement; ironically, the technology he created—by revealing fetal development—became central to public debate on the issue. He received many honours, including appointment as CBE, honorary fellowships, and the gold medal of the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists is named the field's foundational figure. He retired in 1976 and died in Essex in 1987.
