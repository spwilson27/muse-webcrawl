Ezra Pound

Ezra Weston Loomis Pound (30 October 1885 – 1 November 1972) was an American poet and critic, a major figure in the early modernist poetry movement. His works include Ripostes (1912), Hugh Selwyn Mauberley (1920), and the epic poem The Cantos, on which he worked for more than fifty years. As a literary editor and impresario in London and Paris, he helped to launch or shape the careers of T. S. Eliot, James Joyce, Robert Frost, Ernest Hemingway, and others, and was central to the Imagist movement.

Ezra Pound, modernist poet and editor

Modernism

Pound moved to London in 1908, where he became the driving force of Imagism, demanding that poetry be clear, precise, and free of superfluous words. His slogan was "Make it new." He heavily edited Eliot's The Waste Land (1922), substantially shaping the published poem. In 1924 he moved to Rapallo, Italy, and began The Cantos, a sprawling epic incorporating history, economics, Confucian philosophy, and fragments in many languages.

War and confinement

Pound remained in Italy during the Second World War and made hundreds of paid radio broadcasts for the fascist regime, denouncing the United States' involvement in the war and espousing antisemitic views. Arrested by American forces in 1945, he was charged with treason, found unfit to stand trial on grounds of insanity, and confined to St. Elizabeths Hospital in Washington, D.C., until 1958, when the indictment was dismissed following appeals by fellow writers. He returned to Italy and died in Venice in 1972. His legacy remains controversial because of his politics, though his influence on modern poetry is widely acknowledged.
