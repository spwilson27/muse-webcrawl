The Glengarry, or Glengarry bonnet, is a traditional Scottish cap made of thick-milled woollen material with a small pompom (toorie) on top, short ribbons hanging behind, and — in military versions — a diced band and regimental cap badge on the left side. Shaped like a boat and creased down the middle, it can be folded flat. The glengarry is worn as part of Highland dress and by Scottish military units, pipers and drummers worldwide.

Origins

The cap is said to have been devised in the early nineteenth century by Alexander Ranaldson Macdonell of Glengarry — chief of Clan MacDonell of Glengarry — who adapted the soft Kilmarnock bonnet into the creased, fore-and-aft form; a portrait of Macdonell by Henry Raeburn helped popularize it. It appeared as part of the uniform of Highland fencible and volunteer units and spread through the Scottish regiments of the British Army.

Military use

The British Army adopted the glengarry widely in the later nineteenth century: from 1868 it became the undress cap of most infantry regiments, English and Welsh as well as Scottish, usually in dark blue with regimental badges, before being replaced in line infantry by other forage caps around the turn of the century. Scottish regiments retained it, and it remains the head dress of the Royal Regiment of Scotland in certain orders of dress, as well as of Commonwealth Scottish units in Canada, Australia and elsewhere. The diced border — typically red, white and dark blue or black — derives from the Sillitoe tartan pattern also worn by police in Scotland.

Civilian wear

In civilian Highland dress the glengarry, usually plain black or navy with a red toorie, is worn with a tweed jacket for day wear and sometimes with evening dress; clan or regimental badges may be pinned to it. Pipe bands almost universally wear glengarries. A plain version without dicing is common, and the cap is also worn by members of Scottish police services (Police Scotland officers wear a related peaked cap with the diced band).
