Ofrenda

An ofrenda (Spanish: "offering") is a home altar or collection of objects placed on a ritual display during the annual Mexican celebration of Día de los Muertos (Day of the Dead). Families build ofrendas to honour and welcome the spirits of deceased loved ones who are believed to return to the world of the living on 1 and 2 November.

Elements

A traditional ofrenda is built in tiers — often two, three, or seven levels representing the steps the dead must climb to reach the world of the living — and typically includes:

  • Photographs and personal mementos of the deceased
  • Marigolds (cempasúchil), whose colour and scent are said to guide the spirits
  • Candles and incense (copal) to light the way and purify
  • Pan de muerto (bread of the dead), and the favourite foods and drinks of the departed
  • Water, for thirst after the long journey; salt, for purification
  • Papel picado, colourful perforated paper banners representing the air
  • Images of saints, crosses, and sugar skulls (calaveras)

Origins

The practice blends pre-Hispanic Aztec and Mesoamerican rituals honouring the dead with Catholic observances of All Saints' Day and All Souls' Day introduced by Spanish colonisers. Ofrendas are erected in homes, cemeteries, schools, and public spaces throughout Mexico and in Mexican diaspora communities, and public ofrendas may also commemorate victims of disasters or public figures.
