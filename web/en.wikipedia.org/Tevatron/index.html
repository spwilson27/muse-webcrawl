The Tevatron was a circular particle accelerator in the United States, at the Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory (Fermilab), east of Batavia, Illinois. It was the highest-energy particle collider in the world until the Large Hadron Collider at CERN surpassed it. The Tevatron collided protons and antiprotons in a 6.28 km (3.90 mi) ring at energies of up to 980 GeV per beam, creating centre-of-mass energies of 1.96 TeV — hence its name, from "tera-electronvolt" (TeV).

History

The Tevatron began operation in 1983 as an upgrade of Fermilab's Main Ring, using superconducting magnets — it was the first accelerator to employ large-scale superconductivity. Its two major experiments, CDF and DØ, collected proton–antiproton collision data over collider runs in 1988–89, 1992–96 (Run I), and 2001–2011 (Run II). The collider shut down on 30 September 2011, after the Large Hadron Collider had taken over the high-energy frontier and US funding was not renewed.

Discoveries

The Tevatron's most famous achievement was the discovery of the top quark, announced by the CDF and DØ collaborations in 1995. Its experiments also discovered the Ξb baryon, made precise measurements of the W boson and top quark masses that constrained the mass of the Higgs boson before its discovery, observed Bs meson oscillations, and excluded significant regions of Higgs mass. In 2012, Tevatron data contributed a modest supporting role to the Higgs discovery announcement at CERN.

Accelerator complex

Protons began in a Cockcroft–Walton pre-accelerator, then passed through a linac and the Booster ring before entering the Main Injector, which fed the Tevatron ring itself and the antiproton source. Antiprotons were produced by striking a target with 120 GeV protons and accumulated and cooled in dedicated rings. The Tevatron's roughly 1,000 superconducting niobium–titanium magnets were cooled by liquid helium to about 4.3 K and bent the beams around the ring while radiofrequency cavities provided acceleration.
