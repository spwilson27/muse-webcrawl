The Spreewald (Lower Sorbian: Błota, "the marshes") is a lowland region in the German state of Brandenburg, about 100 km south-east of Berlin, where the river Spree divides into a natural network of hundreds of small channels and canals. It is famous for its traditional irrigation system, punt-boat tourism, Sorbian culture and pickled gherkins, and is protected as a UNESCO biosphere reserve.

Geography

The Spreewald was formed during the last Ice Age, when meltwater spread the Spree into a broad, flat inland delta of alder woods, wet meadows and more than 200 small waterways (called Fließe) totalling roughly 1,500 km. Settlements such as the village of Lehde were historically reachable only by boat; many farms still use flat-bottomed punts (Kähne) propelled with long poles, and in some villages post and deliveries traditionally arrive by water.

Culture

The region is part of the settlement area of the Sorbs, a West Slavic minority; bilingual German–Lower Sorbian signage is common and Sorbian dress and customs are maintained. The best-known product is the Spreewald gherkin (Spreewälder Gurken), a protected geographical indication of the EU; cucumbers, horseradish and linseed oil are regional specialities. The Spreewald became a beloved day-trip destination from Berlin in the 19th century, and after German reunification tourism — punt tours from Lübbenau, canoeing, cycling and winter skating on frozen channels — became the region's economic backbone.

Nature conservation

The biosphere reserve protects black alder forests, wet meadows and species such as the Eurasian otter, beaver, black stork and numerous dragonflies. Traditional small-scale farming, with meadows mown by hand or light machinery, maintains the cultural landscape on which much of the wildlife depends.
