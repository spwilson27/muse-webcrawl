Dopamine agonist

A dopamine agonist (DA) is a compound that activates dopamine receptors in the absence of that receptor's physiological ligand, the neurotransmitter dopamine. Dopamine agonists activate signalling pathways through dopamine receptors and trimeric G proteins, ultimately leading to changes in gene transcription.

Medical uses

Dopamine agonists are used principally in:

  • Parkinson's disease — to compensate for the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. Examples include pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine, apomorphine and bromocriptine.
  • Hyperprolactinemia — bromocriptine and cabergoline suppress prolactin secretion by pituitary adenomas (prolactinomas).
  • Restless legs syndrome — pramipexole and ropinirole are approved for this indication, though long-term use can cause augmentation of symptoms.
  • Erectile dysfunction — apomorphine acts on central dopaminergic pathways.

Classification

Some dopamine agonists are ergoline derivatives (bromocriptine, cabergoline, pergolide, lisuride), originally derived from ergot alkaloids. Others are non-ergoline compounds (pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine, piribedil, apomorphine), which are generally preferred today because they lack the fibrotic side effects associated with ergot derivatives. Pergolide was withdrawn from the U.S. market in 2007 because of valvular heart disease caused by activation of 5-HT2B receptors.

Side effects

Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, orthostatic hypotension, drowsiness, hallucinations and headaches. A clinically important group of adverse effects are impulse control disorders — pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping and binge eating — which occur in a significant minority of patients treated for Parkinson's disease, and which usually resolve on dose reduction or discontinuation. Sudden sleep attacks have also been reported.
