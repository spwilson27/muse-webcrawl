John Logie Baird

Born13 August 1888, Helensburgh, Scotland
Died14 June 1946, Bexhill-on-Sea, England
OccupationEngineer, inventor
Known forFirst working television system

John Logie Baird (13 August 1888 – 14 June 1946) was a Scottish engineer and inventor of the world's first working television system and the first publicly demonstrated colour television system. He gave the first public demonstration of moving silhouette images by television at Selfridges in London in 1925 and of true television at the Royal Institution in January 1926.

Early life

Born in Helensburgh, the son of a Church of Scotland minister, Baird studied electrical engineering at the Royal Technical College in Glasgow. Ill health barred him from war service in 1914; he worked as an engineer and tried various businesses, including jam in Trinidad and soap, before settling in Hastings in 1923 to pursue television experiments with improvised equipment — tea chests, darning needles, sealing wax and a Nipkow scanning disc.

Television breakthroughs

In October 1925 Baird transmitted the first television picture with gradations of tone — of a ventriloquist's dummy, "Stooky Bill" — and then of office worker William Taynton, the first person televised. His 30-line mechanical television was adopted by the BBC for experimental broadcasts from 1929 to 1935, including the first transatlantic television transmission (1928, to New York), the first live outside broadcast (the Derby, 1931) and early colour demonstrations in 1928. From 1936 the BBC compared Baird's improved 240-line system with EMI-Marconi's fully electronic 405-line system and adopted the electronic system in 1937.

Later inventions and death

Baird continued pioneering work: colour television via his Telechrome tube (demonstrated 1944), stereoscopic and large-screen television, video recording on discs (Phonovision, 1927–28), fibre-optic concepts and radar-related research. He died in Bexhill-on-Sea in 1946 following a stroke. His mechanical television was superseded, but his role in achieving television — and his 1926 demonstration — is commemorated by the John Logie Baird Award and statues in Helensburgh.
