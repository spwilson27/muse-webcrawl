Pendennis Castle


Pendennis Castle, Falmouth

Pendennis Castle (Cornish: Penn Dinas) is an artillery fort constructed by Henry VIII near Falmouth, Cornwall, between 1540 and 1542. It formed part of the King's Device programme to protect against invasion from France and the Holy Roman Empire, and defended the anchorage of Carrick Roads, together with its sister castle at St Mawes across the water. It is now managed by English Heritage and is one of the best-preserved Henrician forts.

History

The original castle was a circular keep surrounded by a gun platform, placed on the Pendennis headland. Under Elizabeth I, facing renewed Spanish threats, it was greatly strengthened with an Italianate bastioned rampart. During the Civil War, Pendennis was one of the last Royalist strongholds in the west: after a five-month siege by Parliamentarian forces in 1646, its garrison was allowed to march out with the honours of war. The castle was refortified during the Napoleonic Wars, rearmed in the Victorian era, and used in both World Wars — as a coast-defence battery, training base, and in the Second World War a secret communications and observation station.

Today

The site preserves the Tudor keep, the Elizabethan ramparts, Georgian and Victorian barracks, and Second World War defences, with exhibitions on its four centuries of coastal defence. It overlooks Falmouth Bay and remains a popular visitor attraction.
