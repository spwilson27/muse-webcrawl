Base rate

In probability and statistics, the base rate (also called the prior probability) is the proportion of a population that belongs to a class of interest before any new evidence is considered — for example, the prevalence of a disease in a population, or the proportion of defective items in a production run. According to Bayes' theorem, the base rate is a crucial input when updating beliefs in light of new evidence, such as the result of a diagnostic test.

Base rates determine how test results should be interpreted.

Base rate fallacy

The base rate fallacy (or base rate neglect) is the tendency to ignore base rate information and focus instead on case-specific information when judging probabilities. A classic example: if a disease affects 1 in 1,000 people and a test has a false-positive rate of 5%, most people greatly overestimate the chance that a person who tests positive actually has the disease; in fact only about 2% of positive results are true positives, because the vast majority of positives come from the much larger healthy population.

Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky documented base rate neglect in experiments in the 1970s, such as the "engineers and lawyers" and cab problems. Base rate reasoning has practical importance in medicine, law, screening programmes, and risk assessment.
