London Buses

Red double-decker buses are an icon of London transport

London Buses is the subsidiary of Transport for London (TfL) that manages most bus services in London, England. Its red double-decker buses are an internationally recognised symbol of the city. The network comprises around 670 routes served by some 8,000 buses, carrying about 1.8 billion passenger journeys a year in recent years.

History

Horse-drawn omnibuses began operating in London in 1829 under George Shillibeer, copying a Parisian model. Motor buses appeared at the start of the 20th century, and the London General Omnibus Company became the dominant operator. In 1933 services passed to the London Passenger Transport Board, the ancestor of today's TfL. The iconic AEC Routemaster, introduced in 1956 with its open rear platform and separate conductor, remained in regular service until 2005.

Operations

Unlike most of Britain, London's buses were not deregulated in 1986. TfL specifies routes, fares and service levels and contracts operation to private companies — including Arriva, Go-Ahead, Stagecoach, Metroline, RATP and others — which run buses in the standard red livery. Fares are paid by Oyster card, contactless payment or travelcard; cash has not been accepted since 2014.

Fleet

The fleet is predominantly double-decker, including the New Routemaster (introduced 2012, commissioned under Mayor Boris Johnson), alongside single-deckers and articulated buses (withdrawn from London service by 2011). TfL has pursued electrification aggressively, building one of Europe's largest zero-emission bus fleets, with hydrogen buses also operating on some routes.
