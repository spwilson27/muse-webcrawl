P. L. Travers

P. L. Travers

Born: Helen Lyndon Goff, 9 August 1899, Maryborough, Queensland, Australia

Died: 23 April 1996 (aged 96), London, England

Occupation: Novelist, actress, journalist

Notable works: Mary Poppins (1934) and sequels

Pamela Lyndon Travers (born Helen Lyndon Goff; 9 August 1899 – 23 April 1996) was an Australian-British writer best known for the Mary Poppins series of children's books, featuring the magical English nanny of the title. She spent her later life in London, was a disciple of the spiritual teacher George Gurdjieff, and fiercely resisted — then famously clashed with Walt Disney over — the adaptation of her books into the 1964 film Mary Poppins.

Early life

Helen Goff was born in Maryborough, Queensland, to a bank manager father of Irish descent who died when she was seven, after which the family fell into genteel poverty. She wrote poetry as a teenager and worked as an actress and dancer with a touring Shakespearean company in Australia and New Zealand, and as a journalist, before emigrating to England in 1924. In London she wrote for newspapers and magazines, including the Irish Statesman under its editor George Russell (Æ), who introduced her to W. B. Yeats and to Irish literary and mystical circles.

Mary Poppins

Mary Poppins was published in 1934 and was an immediate success. Travers's Poppins differs sharply from the film version: vain, acerbic and mysterious, she arrives on the east wind at 17 Cherry Tree Lane to care for the Banks children, and leads them through episodes blending the mundane with myth and cosmic wonder. Seven further books followed, ending with Mary Poppins and the House Next Door (1988). Travers drew on childhood memories of her great-aunt in Sydney, on mythology, and on her growing interest in esoteric philosophy.

Disney adaptation

Walt Disney pursued the film rights for some twenty years; Travers agreed in 1961 only with script approval rights and a generous financial settlement. She disliked the resulting film's animation sequences, songs and softening of Poppins, and her battles with the studio were dramatised in the 2013 film Saving Mr. Banks. Her will barred any American remake and any further stage production without English writers; the later stage musical incorporated her conditions, and she refused consent for a film sequel in her lifetime (one was made in 2018).

Later life

Travers was a long-time student of Gurdjieff and contributed to the journal Parabola on myth and tradition. She adopted an Irish baby boy, Camillus, raising him as a single mother in London. She was appointed OBE in 1977. Travers died in Chelsea, London, in 1996, aged 96.
