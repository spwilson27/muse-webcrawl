The Alhambra Decree (also known as the Edict of Expulsion) was an edict issued on 31 March 1492 by the joint Catholic Monarchs of Spain, Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile, ordering all practising Jews to leave the kingdoms of Castile and Aragon — or convert to Christianity — by the end of July 1492. It was signed in the Alhambra palace at Granada, weeks after the city's conquest completed the Reconquista.

Background

Anti-Jewish violence in 1391 had driven mass conversions, creating a large class of conversos (New Christians) whose sincerity the Spanish Inquisition, founded in 1478, was charged with policing. The decree's preamble claimed that Jews who remained were causing conversos to relapse into Judaism; expulsion was presented as the remedy. Tomás de Torquemada, the Grand Inquisitor, is widely credited with a leading role in drafting it.

Terms and exodus

Jews were given four months to depart; they could sell property but were forbidden to export gold, silver or coined money, so many assets were sold for a fraction of their value. Estimates of those expelled vary widely, from about 40,000 to over 100,000. The exiles — the Sephardim — scattered to Portugal (whence many were expelled again in 1497), North Africa, Italy, the Netherlands and the Ottoman Empire, where Sultan Bayezid II welcomed them. They carried with them the Judeo-Spanish language, Ladino. Those who stayed converted, often remaining under Inquisition suspicion for generations.

Aftermath

The decree remained formally in force until annulled in 1968, in the spirit of the Second Vatican Council; Spain's 1992 commemoration of the quincentenary included a royal ceremony of reconciliation, and in 2015 Spain offered citizenship to descendants of the expelled Sephardim. The expulsion is remembered as one of the defining events in Jewish and Spanish history.
