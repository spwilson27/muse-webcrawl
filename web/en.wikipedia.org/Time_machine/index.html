Time machine


A Victorian-style time machine, as described by H. G. Wells

A time machine is a hypothetical device that would allow time travel — movement between different points in time in a manner analogous to movement between different points in space. The concept is a staple of science fiction and a topic of interest in philosophy and theoretical physics.

In fiction

The term and concept were popularised by H. G. Wells's 1895 novella The Time Machine, in which an inventor travels to the year 802,701 and encounters the Eloi and Morlocks. Earlier fiction featured more limited forms of time travel, such as the sleeper who wakes in the future (as in Edward Bellamy's Looking Backward, 1888) or the dream-like journey of Ebenezer Scrooge in Charles Dickens's A Christmas Carol (1843). Since Wells, time machines have appeared in countless novels, films and television series, including the TARDIS of Doctor Who and the DeLorean of Back to the Future.

In physics

Einstein's theory of general relativity permits solutions, such as traversable wormholes and certain rotating universes, that contain closed timelike curves — paths through spacetime that return to their starting point in time. Travelling along such a curve would, in principle, allow travel into the past. Physicists including Kip Thorne have studied whether such configurations could be physically realised; Stephen Hawking's chronology protection conjecture proposes that the laws of physics prevent the formation of time machines. Quantum effects near a would-be time machine may become unbounded, destroying it before it could be used.

No time machine has ever been built, and time travel to the past remains purely speculative; travel to the future at differing rates is, by contrast, an experimentally established consequence of time dilation.
