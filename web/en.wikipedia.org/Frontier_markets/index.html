Frontier markets

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Frontier markets are a category of financial markets in countries that are more developed than the least developed countries, but too small, illiquid or risky to be classified as emerging markets. The term was coined by Farida Khambata of the International Finance Corporation in 1992 and was popularised by index providers such as MSCI, which launched its Frontier Markets Index in 2007.

Characteristics

Frontier markets typically have smaller market capitalisations, lower trading volumes, and greater restrictions on foreign investment than established emerging markets. They often exhibit higher expected growth rates, lower correlations with developed markets, and correspondingly higher political, currency and liquidity risks. Examples of countries commonly classified as frontier markets have included Vietnam, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Kenya, Nigeria, Morocco, Romania and Kazakhstan.

Investment

Investors access frontier markets through dedicated mutual funds and exchange-traded funds tracking indices such as the MSCI Frontier Markets Index. Advocates argue the markets offer diversification and long-run growth as their economies "graduate" to emerging-market status — as countries such as Qatar and the United Arab Emirates did in 2014 — while critics point to governance concerns, capital controls, and the difficulty of exiting positions during crises.

Frontier markets list

Index providers periodically review classifications. Countries may be promoted from frontier to emerging status, demoted (as Argentina was in 2021, to "standalone" status), or reclassified in either direction following changes in market accessibility and liquidity. The category sits between emerging markets above it and the least developed, often uninvestable, economies below.
