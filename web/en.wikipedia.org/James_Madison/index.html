James Madison (March 16, 1751 – June 28, 1836) was an American statesman, diplomat, and Founding Father who served as the fourth president of the United States from 1809 to 1817. Madison is hailed as the "Father of the Constitution" for his pivotal role in drafting and promoting the Constitution of the United States and the Bill of Rights.

Early life

Madison was born in Port Conway, Virginia, the eldest son of a prosperous tobacco planter, and grew up at Montpelier in Orange County. He studied at the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), graduating in 1771, and developed interests in law, government, and religious liberty. Plagued by ill health, he did not serve in the Continental Army during the American Revolutionary War but entered politics, serving in the Virginia legislature and the Continental Congress.

Father of the Constitution

Dissatisfied with the weak Articles of Confederation, Madison organised support for constitutional reform. At the Philadelphia Convention of 1787 his Virginia Plan served as the basis for discussion, and his meticulous notes remain the principal record of the proceedings. With Alexander Hamilton and John Jay he wrote The Federalist Papers, the most influential commentary on the Constitution. In the first Congress he shepherded through the amendments that became the Bill of Rights (ratified 1791).

Politics in the new republic

Breaking with Hamilton over finance and foreign policy, Madison co-founded the Democratic-Republican Party with Thomas Jefferson. As Jefferson's Secretary of State (1801–1809) he supervised the Louisiana Purchase diplomacy and grappled with British and French seizures of American shipping, leading to the unpopular Embargo Act of 1807.

Presidency and the War of 1812

Madison succeeded Jefferson in 1809. Continuing tensions with Britain — impressment, trade restrictions, and conflict on the frontier — led Congress to declare war in June 1812. The War of 1812 brought defeats (including the burning of Washington in 1814, during which Dolley Madison famously rescued Washington's portrait) but also victories such as Andrew Jackson's at New Orleans in January 1815, fought after the Treaty of Ghent had ended the war. The conflict's conclusion boosted national confidence and Madison left office popular; he also signed the charter of the Second Bank of the United States in 1816.

Later life

Madison retired to Montpelier, serving as rector of the University of Virginia after Jefferson's death. He died on 28 June 1836, the last surviving signer of the Constitution. Madison was also a slaveholder throughout his life, holding over 100 enslaved people at Montpelier — a contradiction with his writings on liberty that historians continue to examine.
