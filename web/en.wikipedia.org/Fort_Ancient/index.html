Fort Ancient

The Fort Ancient culture is a Native American archaeological culture that lived in the Ohio River valley from roughly 1000 to 1750 CE. Named for the Fort Ancient earthworks site in southwestern Ohio (which was in fact built by the earlier Hopewell culture), Fort Ancient peoples were sedentary maize agriculturalists living in villages, many of them palisaded, along the Ohio River and its tributaries in present-day Ohio, Indiana, Kentucky and West Virginia.

Development

Archaeologists divide the culture into early, middle and late phases. Settlements grew larger and more nucleated over time, often with a central plaza surrounded by houses and burials. Fort Ancient peoples grew maize, beans and squash, hunted deer, and participated in exchange networks reaching the Mississippian cultures to the south and west. Their pottery, often shell-tempered, and distinctive triangular arrow points are characteristic artifacts.

Decline

By the mid-17th century, Fort Ancient settlements were abandoned, likely under pressures from disease, warfare and the disruptions of the Beaver Wars; descendant communities may include groups later known historically in the region.
