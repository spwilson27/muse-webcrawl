John the Evangelist (Greek: Ἰωάννης, Iōánnēs) is the name traditionally given to the author of the Gospel of John. Christians have traditionally identified him with John the Apostle, John of Patmos, and the Beloved Disciple. The exact identity remains a matter of scholarly debate.

Identity debates

Church tradition since at least the late 2nd century — notably Irenaeus — held that the Fourth Gospel was written by John son of Zebedee, one of the Twelve Apostles, at Ephesus late in the 1st century. Many modern scholars, however, consider the Gospel to be anonymous and the product of a "Johannine community", and distinguish the evangelist from the seer of Revelation and from the apostle. Others defend traditional authorship, at least in part.

Johannine writings

Five New Testament books are associated with "John": the Gospel of John, the three Epistles of John, and the Book of Revelation. The Gospel differs markedly from the Synoptic Gospels in style and content, presenting long theological discourses, the "I am" sayings, and a high Christology beginning with the prologue "In the beginning was the Word".

Traditions and veneration

According to tradition, John was the only apostle to die of natural causes, living to old age at Ephesus, where he was associated with the care of Mary, mother of Jesus. He is said to have been exiled to the island of Patmos under Domitian, where he received the visions of Revelation. His symbol in art is the eagle, and he is venerated as a saint throughout Christianity; his feast day in the West is 27 December.
