Lean manufacturing

Lean manufacturing is a method of manufacturing goods aimed primarily at reducing times within the production system as well as response times from suppliers and customers. It is closely related to another concept called just-in-time manufacturing. Lean manufacturing adopts the concept of reducing waste (Japanese: muda) to increase overall customer value.


A lean production line

Origins

Lean manufacturing derives largely from the Toyota Production System (TPS), developed by Taiichi Ohno and others at Toyota after World War II. The term "lean" was popularized by researchers at MIT's International Motor Vehicle Program and by the 1990 book The Machine That Changed the World.

Principles

James Womack and Daniel Jones identified five lean principles: specify value from the customer's perspective, identify the value stream, make value flow without interruptions, let customers pull value, and pursue perfection through continuous improvement (kaizen).

Common tools include kanban scheduling, 5S workplace organization, poka-yoke error-proofing, value-stream mapping, and single-minute exchange of dies (SMED).

Waste

Ohno identified seven wastes: transport, inventory, motion, waiting, overproduction, overprocessing, and defects. Eliminating these activities that consume resources without creating value is central to the lean philosophy.
