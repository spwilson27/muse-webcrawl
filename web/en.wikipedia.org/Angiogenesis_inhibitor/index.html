Angiogenesis inhibitor

An angiogenesis inhibitor is a substance that blocks angiogenesis—the growth of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones. Because solid tumours cannot grow beyond a few millimetres or metastasise efficiently without recruiting a blood supply, inhibiting angiogenesis became a major strategy in cancer therapy following Judah Folkman's hypothesis of 1971, and the same drugs are used to suppress pathological vessel growth in the eye.

Mechanisms

Most inhibitors target the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway, the dominant signal by which tumours summon vessels. Monoclonal antibodies such as bevacizumab bind VEGF itself, while antibody fragments like ranibizumab are injected into the eye for wet age-related macular degeneration. Small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors—sunitinib, sorafenib, pazopanib and others—block VEGF receptors and related signalling inside endothelial and tumour cells. Older agents include thalidomide and its derivatives, and experimental endogenous inhibitors such as endostatin and angiostatin.

Clinical use

Anti-angiogenic drugs are approved, usually in combination with chemotherapy, for colorectal, lung, kidney, liver, ovarian, and other cancers. Benefits are typically measured in months of progression-free survival rather than cures; tumours adapt by up-regulating alternative growth factors, increasing invasiveness, or co-opting existing vessels. Characteristic side effects follow from VEGF's normal roles: hypertension, bleeding, impaired wound healing, and gastrointestinal perforation.

Vessel normalisation

Research by Rakesh Jain showed that judicious anti-angiogenic dosing can transiently “normalise” chaotic tumour vasculature, improving delivery of chemotherapy and oxygen and potentially enhancing immunotherapy—a refinement now guiding combination trials.
