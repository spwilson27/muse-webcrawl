De Rham cohomology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, de Rham cohomology is a tool of algebraic topology and differential geometry, introduced by Georges de Rham in his 1931 doctoral thesis. It uses differential forms on a smooth manifold to detect its topological features: roughly, the de Rham cohomology groups measure the extent to which closed differential forms fail to be exact.

The construction begins with the de Rham complex, the sequence of spaces of smooth k-forms connected by the exterior derivative d, satisfying d² = 0. The k-th de Rham cohomology group HkdR(M) is the quotient of the closed k-forms (those with dω = 0) by the exact k-forms (those of the form ω = dη). A closed form represents a cohomology class; the form is exact exactly when its class is zero.

De Rham's theorem

De Rham's theorem states that the de Rham cohomology of a smooth manifold is naturally isomorphic to its singular cohomology with real coefficients. The isomorphism is given by integrating a differential form over singular cycles, and Stokes' theorem ensures that the pairing is well defined. A famous corollary is that the dimensions of the de Rham groups — the Betti numbers — are finite for compact manifolds. For a compact connected orientable n-manifold, the top group HndR is one-dimensional, reflecting that the integral of a volume form is a topological invariant.

Examples and applications

For a point or for ℝn, all positive-degree groups vanish (the Poincaré lemma), so closed forms are exact — the algebraic reason fundamental theorems of calculus extend to star-shaped regions. On a circle, H¹ is one-dimensional, generated by the angle form dθ; on a torus, H¹ is two-dimensional. In physics, de Rham classes classify possible electromagnetic fields and charges: the periods of the field strength 2-form F over 2-cycles correspond, via Dirac quantisation, to magnetic charges.
