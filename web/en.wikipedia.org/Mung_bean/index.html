Mung bean

The mung bean (Vigna radiata) is a plant species in the legume family (Fabaceae), grown principally for its small, green edible seeds. Native to the Indian subcontinent, it is widely cultivated in India, China, Southeast Asia and warm regions elsewhere.

Mung beans are used whole, hulled and split, sprouted, or ground into flour and starch, and feature in savoury and sweet dishes across Asia.

Cultivation and description

The plant is an erect annual herb 15–125 cm tall with trifoliate leaves, yellow flowers and hairy pods containing 10–15 small cylindrical green seeds. It is a short-season, warm-weather crop, often grown in rotation with cereals, and like other legumes it fixes nitrogen through root nodules. India is the largest producer and consumer.

Culinary uses

Sprouted mung beans ('bean sprouts') are eaten raw or lightly cooked in salads, stir-fries and spring rolls. Split and hulled beans form the yellow mung dal of Indian cooking, used in dals and khichdi. Whole beans are made into soups and curries; in Chinese and Filipino cooking they appear in sweet dessert soups, and mung bean paste fills mooncakes and other pastries. Mung bean starch is used to make transparent cellophane (glass) noodles and jellies.

Nutrition

Mung beans are rich in protein, dietary fibre, folate, iron and other micronutrients, and are low in fat. They are also grown as a sprouting seed and as animal fodder.
