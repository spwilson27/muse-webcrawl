Federal architecture

A Federal-style townhouse with characteristic fanlight and three-part windows.

Federal architecture is the name for the classicizing architecture built in the newly founded United States between c. 1780 and 1830, especially from 1785 to 1815. It is closely related to the contemporary British Adam style and to late Georgian architecture, and is sometimes grouped with it under the term Adamesque. The style is named for the Federal period in American history, not for buildings of the federal government, though it was widely used for public buildings as well as houses.

Characteristics

Federal-style buildings are typically built of brick on a simple rectangular or cubic plan, with a symmetrical façade, low-pitched or flat roofs hidden behind a balustrade, and restrained classical ornament. Signature motifs include the semi-circular or elliptical fanlight above the front door, three-part (Palladian-style) windows, decorative crown moulding with dentils, and delicate swags and urns derived from the work of Robert Adam. Inside, rooms are often oval or polygonal, with elaborate plasterwork and mantelpieces.

Leading architects and examples

Leading practitioners included Charles Bulfinch in Boston, who designed the Massachusetts State House; Samuel McIntire, the Salem woodcarver and architect; Alexander Parris; and John McComb Jr. in New York. Thomas Jefferson's work, such as the Virginia State Capitol, is usually classified as early Classical Revival or Jeffersonian. Federal architecture survives in large numbers in New England and the mid-Atlantic, in districts such as Boston's Beacon Hill and Salem's McIntire District.
