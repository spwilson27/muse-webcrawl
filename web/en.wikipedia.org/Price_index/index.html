Price index

A price index (plural: "price indices" or "price indexes") is a normalized average (typically a weighted average) of price relatives for a given class of goods or services in a given region during a given interval of time. It is a statistic designed to help compare how these price relatives, taken as a whole, differ between time periods or geographical locations. Price indices are used to measure inflation, adjust contracts and wages (indexation), and deflate nominal economic statistics to obtain real values.

A consumer price index tracks the cost of a basket of goods over time.

Types of price index

The best-known price index is the consumer price index (CPI), which measures changes in the retail prices of a basket of goods and services bought by households. The producer price index (PPI) measures prices received by domestic producers. Other indices include export and import price indices, construction price indices, and the GDP deflator, which covers all domestically produced goods and services.

Index formulas

Several formulas are used. The Laspeyres index weights prices by quantities from a base period; the Paasche index uses current-period quantities; the Fisher index, the geometric mean of the two, is often considered theoretically ideal. Chained indices update the basket frequently to reduce substitution bias — the tendency of fixed-basket indices to overstate cost-of-living increases as consumers substitute cheaper goods for dearer ones.

History

One of the earliest price indices was constructed by the English economist William Fleetwood in 1707 to answer a question about whether a student could take a certain oath about income limits set centuries earlier. Modern official indices date from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when governments began systematic cost-of-living measurement.
