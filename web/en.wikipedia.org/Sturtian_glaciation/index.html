Sturtian glaciation

The Sturtian glaciation was a worldwide glaciation during the Cryogenian Period, lasting from approximately 717 to 660 million years ago — a duration of some 57 million years that makes it the longest known ice age of the Phanerozoic-preceding record and one of the most severe glaciations in Earth's history. It is named after the tillite deposits of the Sturt Gorge region near Adelaide, South Australia, themselves named for explorer Charles Sturt.

Extent and duration

Glacial deposits of Sturtian age occur on nearly every continent, including at palaeolatitudes near the equator, supporting the Snowball Earth hypothesis that ice sheets reached tropical seas. Radiometric dating of volcanic ash beds bracketing the glacial sediments constrains the onset to about 717 million years ago and the termination to about 660 million years ago, although whether the glaciation was a single continuous event or several pulses remains debated.

Causes and termination

Proposed triggers include a drawdown of atmospheric carbon dioxide driven by enhanced silicate weathering of the vast basaltic province erupted during the breakup of the supercontinent Rodinia, together with the low luminosity of the Neoproterozoic Sun. Under snowball conditions, the silicate-weathering thermostat shuts down while volcanic outgassing continues, so CO₂ eventually accumulates to levels sufficient to trigger rapid deglaciation, leaving behind the distinctive cap carbonate layers found atop Sturtian glacial deposits worldwide.

Relationship to the Marinoan

The Sturtian is the earlier and longer of the two great Cryogenian glaciations; it was followed, after an interglacial interval of some 10–15 million years, by the shorter but equally severe Marinoan glaciation (c. 650–635 million years ago). Together they frame the Cryogenian and precede the appearance of large, complex multicellular life in the Ediacaran.
