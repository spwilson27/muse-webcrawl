Common practice period

In the history of Western classical music, the common practice period is the era of tonal music, spanning roughly from 1600 to 1910. It encompasses the Baroque, Classical, and Romantic periods, during which a shared system of harmonic and melodic conventions — functional tonality — governed composition across Europe and its cultural sphere.

Characteristics

Common practice music is based on major and minor keys, chord progressions organised around tonic, dominant, and subdominant functions, and conventions of voice leading and counterpoint. Standard forms such as the sonata, symphony, concerto, and fugue developed within this framework. Composers from Monteverdi and Bach through Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Brahms, and Wagner worked within — and progressively stretched — the system.

Decline

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, increasing chromaticism (as in the works of Wagner, Mahler, and Richard Strauss) loosened tonal conventions, and composers such as Debussy and Schoenberg moved toward atonality and other systems, ending the common practice era even though tonal music remains dominant in popular and film music.
