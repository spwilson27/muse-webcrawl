Trajan's Dacian Wars (101–102 and 105–106) were two military campaigns fought between the Roman Empire and Dacia during the reign of the Emperor Trajan. The conflicts were triggered by the continued strength and independence of the Dacian king Decebalus, whose kingdom north of the Danube had humiliated Rome during the reign of Domitian, and by Rome's interest in Dacia's gold mines.

The wars ended in a decisive Roman victory. Dacia was annexed as the Roman province of Dacia, and its conquest was celebrated in Rome with the construction of Trajan's Forum and Trajan's Column, whose spiral relief narrates the campaigns in stone.

Background

Under Domitian, Dacian forces had invaded Moesia and defeated a Roman army in 86 AD. The resulting settlement left Decebalus in possession of his kingdom and in receipt of Roman subsidies and engineers, an arrangement many Romans considered dishonourable. Trajan, a soldier-emperor, resolved to settle the Dacian question permanently.

First war (101–102)

Trajan crossed the Danube in 101 and advanced into Dacia, defeating Decebalus at the Third Battle of Tapae. After hard winter fighting, including a Dacian counterattack across the frozen Danube, Decebalus sued for peace in 102. He surrendered territory and demolished fortresses but retained his throne.

Between the wars

To secure the route into Dacia, Trajan's engineer Apollodorus of Damascus built Trajan's Bridge across the Danube at Drobeta—the longest arch bridge of the ancient world. Decebalus, meanwhile, rebuilt his forces and attacked Roman garrisons in 105, provoking the second war.

Second war (105–106)

Trajan's second campaign was a war of conquest. Roman forces besieged and stormed the Dacian capital, Sarmizegetusa Regia. Decebalus fled and, cornered by Roman cavalry, took his own life rather than be captured; his head was sent to Rome. Dacia was annexed and colonised, its gold mines passing into imperial hands.

Legacy

The conquest brought enormous wealth to Rome and extended the empire to its greatest territorial extent in Europe north of the Danube. Dacia remained a Roman province until Aurelian withdrew in the 270s. The wars are a foundational episode in Romanian national historiography, and Trajan's Column remains a principal source for the appearance of Roman and Dacian armies.
