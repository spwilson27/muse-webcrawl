Sources of the Self: The Making of the Modern Identity is a work of philosophy by Charles Taylor, published in 1989. Drawing on the author's Massey Lectures, it traces how the modern Western sense of selfhood — inward, expressive, and oriented by moral ideals — was formed over centuries.

Argument

Taylor argues that identity cannot be separated from orientations to the good: to be a self is to stand within a "moral space" defined by frameworks of value. He traces three major facets of modern identity: first, modern inwardness, from Augustine through Descartes and Locke's "punctual self"; second, the affirmation of ordinary life, rooted in the Reformation's sanctification of work and family; and third, the voice of nature as an inner moral source, developed by Romantic expressivism from Herder to modern art.

Method and reception

The book is both a work of moral philosophy and a vast intellectual history engaging literature, theology, and art. It is regarded as Taylor's magnum opus and a landmark response to purely naturalistic accounts of the self; its sequel, A Secular Age (2007), extends the story to the conditions of belief in the modern West.
