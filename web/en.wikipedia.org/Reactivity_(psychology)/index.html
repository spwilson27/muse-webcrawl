Reactivity (psychology)

Observation itself can alter participants' behaviour

Reactivity is a phenomenon in psychology and social research in which individuals alter their behaviour because they are aware that they are being observed or measured. Reactive measurement can threaten the validity of an experiment, because the observed behaviour may not reflect how participants would act naturally.

Forms

The best known form is the Hawthorne effect, named for studies at the Western Electric Hawthorne Works in the 1920s and 1930s, in which workers' productivity appeared to change in response to the attention of researchers. Related phenomena include demand characteristics, in which participants infer the purpose of a study and adjust their responses; experimenter expectancy effects; and the tendency of respondents in surveys and interviews to give socially desirable answers. In clinical and educational assessment, practice effects on repeated tests are another form of reactivity.

Mitigation

Researchers reduce reactivity through unobtrusive measures, such as behavioural traces and archival records; by concealing the true purpose of a study (with later debriefing); by using control groups and blind or double-blind designs; and by allowing participants to habituate to observation. In self-monitoring, reactivity is sometimes deliberately harnessed as a therapeutic tool, since the act of recording one's own behaviour (for example, food intake or smoking) tends to change it.
