Fallibilism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fallibilism is a broad epistemological doctrine associated with the philosophy of Charles Sanders Peirce, according to which claims can be accepted even though they cannot be proved with certainty. It holds that any claim justifiably used in science or inquiry could in principle turn out to be false, and that absolute certainty about empirical matters is unattainable. A fallibilist thus accepts beliefs as knowledge while remaining open to revision in light of new evidence.

Peirce's doctrine

Peirce developed fallibilism as part of his pragmatism and his theory of inquiry. Scientific knowledge, he argued, progresses through self-correcting investigation conducted by a community of inquirers over the long run; individual certainty is neither possible nor required. Fallibilism was thus opposed both to Cartesian scepticism, which demands indubitable foundations, and to dogmatism, which treats current beliefs as final.

Distinctions and influence

Fallibilism differs from scepticism: the sceptic questions whether we know anything, while the fallibilist maintains that we can know a great deal — just never with absolute, incorrigible certainty. It is also closely connected with, but distinct from, Karl Popper's falsificationism: Popper held that scientific theories can never be verified, only falsified, whereas Peircean fallibilism concerns the revisability of all belief.

The doctrine has been influential in philosophy of science, epistemology (where it is frequently contrasted with infallibilism about knowledge), and in liberal political thought, where the possibility of error supports open inquiry and freedom of expression.
