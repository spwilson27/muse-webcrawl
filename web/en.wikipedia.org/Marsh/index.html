Marsh

In ecology, a marsh is a wetland that is dominated by herbaceous rather than woody plant species. Marshes can often be found at the edges of lakes and streams, where they form a transition between the aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems. They are often dominated by grasses, rushes, or reeds. If woody plants are present they tend to be low-growing shrubs, and the marsh is sometimes called a carr. A wetland dominated by trees is a swamp, and one dominated by mosses and peat is a bog or mire.

Types

Marshes are classified by salinity and setting. Salt marshes occur on coasts flooded by tides, vegetated by salt-tolerant plants such as Spartina cordgrass. Tidal freshwater marshes lie further up estuaries. Inland freshwater marshes include wet meadows, prairie potholes, playa lakes, and the shallow edges of ponds and lakes, dominated by cattails, bulrushes, and sedges. Constructed marshes are built artificially to treat wastewater.

Hydrology and soils

The water in marshes may be fresh, brackish, or saline, and levels typically fluctuate seasonally or with the tides. Marsh soils are waterlogged and oxygen-poor, slowing decomposition and favouring the accumulation of organic matter; salt-marsh and mangrove sediments are significant stores of "blue carbon."

Ecology and value

Marshes are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. They provide habitat and nursery grounds for fish, shellfish, and migratory birds, filter pollutants and sediments from water, buffer coasts against storm surge and erosion, and sequester carbon. Despite this, large areas of marsh have been drained for agriculture and development; wetland restoration and salt-marsh restoration are now active conservation priorities worldwide.
