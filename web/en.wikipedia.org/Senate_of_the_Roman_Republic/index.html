Senate of the Roman Republic


The Curia Julia in the Roman Forum, meeting place of the Senate.

The Senate was the governing and advisory assembly of the aristocracy in the ancient Roman Republic. It was not an elected body but one whose members were appointed, initially by the consuls and later by the censors, drawn from former magistrates. Although its decrees (senatus consulta) were technically advisory, in practice the Senate directed finance, foreign policy, provincial administration, and much of the day-to-day government of the Republic.

Origins and membership

Tradition traced the Senate to the Roman monarchy, when it advised the king. Under the Republic its size varied: about 300 members for much of the Republic, expanded by Sulla to around 600, by Julius Caesar to 900, and reduced again under Augustus. Membership, held for life unless a senator was expelled by the censors for misconduct, was recruited from ex-quaestors and higher magistrates following reforms of the late Republic. The order of speaking followed seniority, beginning with the princeps senatus and former consuls.

Procedure

The Senate met in a consecrated space, most famously the Curia in the Forum, and could be summoned only by a magistrate with the right of consultation, such as a consul, praetor or tribune. Meetings began with religious observances; a presiding magistrate put a question (relatio), senators debated in order of rank, and a vote was taken by division — senators physically moving to the side of the speaker whose proposal they supported. A quorum was required for valid decrees, and proceedings were constrained to daylight hours.

Powers

The Senate controlled the treasury (aerarium), assigned provinces and commands to magistrates, received foreign embassies, and declared war and peace in practice by directing the magistrates who held formal authority. In emergencies it could pass the senatus consultum ultimum, the "final decree", urging magistrates to defend the Republic by any means. Its authority rested on auctoritas — prestige and accumulated precedent — rather than on legislative sovereignty, which formally lay with the popular assemblies.

Decline

From the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus in 133 BC, senatorial dominance was increasingly challenged by popular politicians and by military strongmen. The civil wars of the first century BC hollowed out the Senate's real power, and under Augustus it survived as a prestigious but subordinate institution of the Principate.
