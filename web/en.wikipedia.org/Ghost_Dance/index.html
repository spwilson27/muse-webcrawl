Ghost Dance

Lakota Ghost Dance participants photographed in 1890.

The Ghost Dance was a religious movement incorporated into numerous Native American belief systems near the end of the nineteenth century. Centred on a circle dance performed for up to five days, it promised the renewal of the world: the return of the dead, the restoration of game (especially the buffalo), and the removal or reconciliation of white settlers.

Origins

An earlier movement arose around 1870 with the Paiute prophet Wodziwob. The better-known revival began in 1889 with Wovoka (Jack Wilson), a Northern Paiute prophet who, during an illness coinciding with a solar eclipse, reported visions of a renewed earth. His doctrine was fundamentally peaceful: followers were to live honestly, work, and not fight.

Spread and suppression

Delegations travelled to Nevada from many tribes, and the dance spread rapidly across the Plains, changing as it was adopted. Among the Lakota, impoverished and confined to reservations, some adherents wore "ghost shirts" believed to repel bullets and anticipated the settlers' disappearance. Alarmed officials banned the dance; the attempted arrest of Sitting Bull led to his death, and tensions culminated in the Wounded Knee Massacre of 29 December 1890, in which the U.S. Army killed roughly 250–300 Lakota.

Legacy

Despite suppression, elements of the Ghost Dance persisted into the twentieth century, and it remains a powerful symbol of Native resistance and cultural survival.
