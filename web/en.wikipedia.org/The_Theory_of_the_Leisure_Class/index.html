The Theory of the Leisure Class: An Economic Study of Institutions (1899) is a book by the economist and sociologist Thorstein Veblen, which presents an evolutionary theory of social stratification and a critique of consumer capitalism. It is famous for introducing the concepts of conspicuous consumption and conspicuous leisure.

Summary

Veblen argued that in modern industrial societies, ownership is valued less for subsistence than for the social honor it confers. The "leisure class" demonstrates its status by abstaining from productive labor and by consuming goods wastefully and visibly — conspicuous consumption — in order to signal wealth and power to others. Tastes in dress, housing, manners, and even education are shaped, in his account, by the standard of reputability set by this class and emulated downward through society.

Veblen traced the leisure class to early "barbarian" cultures in which prowess in warfare and the seizure of property by force replaced subsistence work as the basis of prestige, and where captured women and slaves performed the productive labor. He contrasted this predatory ethos with the "instinct of workmanship" that drives productive efficiency.

Influence

The book founded a satirical and evolutionary strain of institutional economics and remains a classic of sociology. Its terms "conspicuous consumption", "pecuniary emulation", and "vicarious leisure" have entered general usage, and the work is frequently cited in discussions of consumer culture, advertising, and status goods.
