Adobe
MaterialSun-dried earth: clay, sand, water, often straw or dung
FormBricks moulded and dried in the sun

Adobe (from Spanish, ultimately from Arabic al-ṭūb, "the bricks") is an organic building material made from earth and organic materials — typically a mixture of clay-rich soil, sand, water, and fibrous material such as straw — formed into bricks and dried by the sun. Adobe is among the earliest building materials and remains widely used in arid regions of the world.

History

Mudbrick construction dates to the Neolithic; adobe-like bricks were used in ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley. Spanish colonists brought the term and techniques to the Americas, where earthen building had long indigenous traditions, giving rise to the Pueblo Revival and Santa Fe styles of the US Southwest. Monumental adobe architecture includes the tower houses of Shibam in Yemen, the Great Mosque of Djenné in Mali — the largest mud-brick building in the world — and the Chan Chan complex in Peru.

Properties and construction

Adobe walls have high thermal mass, absorbing heat by day and releasing it at night, suiting climates with large diurnal temperature swings. The material is cheap and low in embodied energy but vulnerable to rain erosion and moisture, requiring protective plasters, wide roof overhangs and periodic re-rendering. Unreinforced adobe performs poorly in earthquakes, which has led to building codes restricting it in seismic regions and to research into stabilised and reinforced adobe.

Modern use

Interest in sustainable building has revived adobe and related earth techniques (rammed earth, cob, compressed earth block). Adobe structures are common across the Middle East, North and West Africa, South and Central America, and the southwestern United States and Mexico.
