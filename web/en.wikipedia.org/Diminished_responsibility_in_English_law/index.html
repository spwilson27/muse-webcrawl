Diminished responsibility in English law

In English law, diminished responsibility is a partial defence to a charge of murder which reduces the offence to manslaughter. It was introduced by section 2 of the Homicide Act 1957 and substantially amended by section 52 of the Coroners and Justice Act 2009.

Diminished responsibility is argued in murder trials before the Crown Court.

The defence

Under the amended law, a person who kills another is not to be convicted of murder if they were suffering from an abnormality of mental functioning which arose from a recognised medical condition, substantially impaired their ability to understand the nature of their conduct, form a rational judgment, or exercise self-control, and provides an explanation for their act. The burden of proving the defence lies on the defendant, on the balance of probabilities.

Background

The defence was adopted from Scots law following recommendations that the strict rules of the M'Naghten insanity defence left no room for cases of mental disturbance falling short of legal insanity. Notable cases include R v Byrne (1960), concerning a sexual psychopath, and R v Golds (2016), on the meaning of "substantially impaired".
