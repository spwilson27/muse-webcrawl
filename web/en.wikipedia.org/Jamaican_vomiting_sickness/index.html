Jamaican vomiting sickness

Jamaican vomiting sickness, also known as ackee poisoning, is an acute illness caused by the toxins hypoglycin A and hypoglycin B in the fruit of the ackee tree (Blighia sapida), especially when the fruit is eaten unripe or improperly prepared. It was first recognised in Jamaica, where ackee is part of the national dish, ackee and saltfish.

Cause and mechanism

Hypoglycin A is metabolised to methylenecyclopropylacetic acid (MCPA), which inhibits several acyl-CoA dehydrogenases and blocks fatty-acid β-oxidation and gluconeogenesis. The resulting depletion of glucose produces profound hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis and hepatic dysfunction.

Symptoms

Onset is typically two to six hours after eating unripe ackee, beginning with abdominal discomfort followed by repeated, severe vomiting. After a brief apparent recovery, drowsiness, convulsions, coma and death may follow, particularly in malnourished children. Historically fatality rates were high; case fatality has fallen with supportive care.

Treatment and prevention

There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive, centred on intravenous glucose, correction of acidosis and electrolytes, and management of seizures. Prevention rests on eating only fully ripe ackee whose pods have opened naturally, discarding the seeds and membranes, and boiling the arils. Because of the risk, the import of raw ackee into the United States is restricted to approved manufacturers.
