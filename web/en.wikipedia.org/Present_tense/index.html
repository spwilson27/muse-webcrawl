Present tense

The present tense is a grammatical tense whose principal function is to locate a situation or event in present time. In English and many other languages, however, the present tense is also used for habitual actions, general truths, scheduled future events, and vivid narration of past events (the historical present). Linguists distinguish morphological tense — the form of the verb — from the time actually referred to, which need not coincide.

Uses in English

The English simple present expresses habitual or repeated action ("She walks to work"), general or scientific truths ("Water boils at 100 °C"), and states ("I know the answer"). It also serves for scheduled future events ("The train leaves at six"), performative utterances ("I hereby resign"), play-by-play commentary ("He shoots, he scores!"), and the historical present in storytelling. The present continuous (progressive), formed with be + present participle, usually describes events in progress at the moment of speaking ("It is raining") or planned future arrangements.

Forms

English verbs have two present-tense forms: the plain present, identical to the base form except for third-person singular -s ("he runs"), and auxiliary constructions such as the present continuous and present perfect ("has eaten"), the latter combining present tense of the auxiliary with a participle to express past events with current relevance. The verb be is irregular: am, is, are.

In other languages

Many languages mark the present tense richly by conjugation — Latin, Spanish, and Russian verbs, for example, inflect for person and number. Some languages, including Mandarin Chinese and many Southeast Asian languages, do not inflect verbs for tense at all, relying on context and aspect markers. Others divide the present into distinct forms for ongoing versus habitual action, or lack a morphological present in favor of a general non-past tense covering both present and future (as in Japanese).
