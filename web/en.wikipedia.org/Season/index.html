Season


The seasons result from Earth's axial tilt relative to its orbit around the Sun.

A season is a division of the year marked by changes in weather, ecology, and the amount of daylight. Temperate and polar regions conventionally recognise four seasons — spring, summer, autumn (fall), and winter — while many tropical regions have two, a wet season and a dry season governed by monsoon patterns. The word derives from Latin satio, "sowing," via Old French seson.

Cause

Seasons are caused by the tilt of Earth's rotational axis — about 23.4° — relative to the plane of its orbit around the Sun, not by changes in the Earth–Sun distance. As Earth orbits, each hemisphere in turn is tilted toward the Sun, receiving more direct sunlight and longer days (summer), then away from it (winter). The effect is opposite in the two hemispheres: when it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere it is winter in the Southern. The extremes are the solstices (around 21 June and 21 December), and the equinoxes (around 20 March and 22–23 September) mark the points when day and night are nearly equal.

Definitions

Astronomical seasons are defined by the solstices and equinoxes. Meteorological seasons, used by weather services for consistent statistics, group whole months: in the Northern Hemisphere spring is March–May, summer June–August, autumn September–November, and winter December–February. Cultural and ecological reckonings vary further: traditional calendars in China and Japan divide the year into 24 solar terms, Indigenous calendars in Australia recognise up to six seasons tied to local ecology, and Hindu calendars commonly count six ritu.

Effects

Seasonal change drives plant growth cycles, animal migration, hibernation, and breeding, and structures agriculture, clothing, festivals, and energy demand. Seasonal affective disorder links mood to reduced winter daylight at high latitudes. Polar regions experience the most extreme variation, with continuous daylight ("midnight sun") and darkness ("polar night") for parts of the year; equatorial regions, by contrast, have little temperature seasonality at all.

On other planets

Any planet with a tilted axis has seasons. Mars, with a tilt similar to Earth's, experiences recognisable if colder seasons; Uranus, tilted about 98°, has the most extreme seasons in the Solar System, each lasting about 21 Earth years.
