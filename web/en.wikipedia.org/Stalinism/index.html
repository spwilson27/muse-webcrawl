Stalinism

Stalinism is the ideology and system of government associated with Joseph Stalin, leader of the Soviet Union from the mid-1920s until his death in 1953. It combined Marxism–Leninism as interpreted by Stalin with a highly centralised command economy, rapid industrialisation, forced collectivisation of agriculture, one-party rule, pervasive state repression and a cult of personality around the leader. The term is also used more broadly for the political systems of states modelled on the Stalin-era USSR.

Key features

Stalin's doctrine of "Socialism in One Country" (1924) asserted that socialism could be built within the Soviet Union without waiting for world revolution. From 1928 the Five-Year Plans set compulsory targets for heavy industry and production, while collectivisation consolidated peasant farms into state and collective farms amid violent resistance and the famine of 1932–33, including the Holodomor in Ukraine. Political control was enforced by the NKVD through purges, show trials (notably the Moscow Trials of 1936–38), the Gulag camp system and mass deportations; the Great Purge of 1936–38 alone saw hundreds of thousands executed.

Foreign policy

Through the Comintern, Stalinism was exported to the ruling parties of the Eastern Bloc after 1945, and Stalinist parties and movements existed worldwide. The Sino-Soviet split of the 1960s followed the People's Republic of China's rejection of de-Stalinisation.

De-Stalinisation and legacy

After Stalin's death, Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956 denounced the cult of personality and many of Stalin's crimes, beginning de-Stalinisation in the USSR. Historians debate the death toll attributable to Stalinist policies — estimates of excess deaths range widely, with many scholarly estimates between roughly six and nine million for the 1930s alone when famine deaths are included. The term "Stalinist" is also used polemically to describe authoritarian left-wing politics.
