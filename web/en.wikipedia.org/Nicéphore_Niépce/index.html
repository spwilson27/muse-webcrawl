Joseph Nicéphore Niépce (7 March 1765 – 5 July 1833) was a French inventor and one of the earliest pioneers of photography. He is credited with creating the first permanent photograph from nature, the heliograph known as View from the Window at Le Gras, made in about 1826 or 1827 at his estate in Saint-Loup-de-Varennes, Burgundy.

Niépce's process, which he called heliography ("sun drawing"), used a plate coated with bitumen of Judea dissolved in oil of lavender. Light hardened the exposed areas over an exposure lasting many hours; the unexposed bitumen was then rinsed away, leaving the image. His earliest surviving heliograph on paper dates from 1822 (a copy of an engraving), while the Le Gras view is the oldest surviving camera photograph of a real-world scene.

Early life and the pyréolophore

Born into a wealthy family in Chalon-sur-Saône, Niépce served in the revolutionary armies and worked as an administrator in Nice and Sardinia before devoting himself to invention with his elder brother Claude. In 1807 the brothers patented the pyréolophore, an early internal combustion engine that powered a boat upstream on the river Saône using controlled explosions of lycopodium powder. Claude eventually moved to England promoting the engine and died in Kew in 1828.

Photography and partnership

Frustrated by his poor draughtsmanship, Niépce pursued ways to fix camera images, experimenting with silver chloride papers from 1816 before turning to bitumen. In 1827 he travelled to England, presented a memoir and specimens to the Royal Society (without disclosing the process, which blocked publication), and met botanist Francis Bauer. In December 1829 he signed a ten-year partnership with Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre to develop heliography. Niépce died of a stroke in 1833, six years before Daguerre announced the daguerreotype — a process different from, but built in part on, their joint work.

Recognition

For a century Niépce's priority was little known outside France. The historian Helmut Gernsheim rediscovered and publicised the Le Gras plate in 1952; it is now held by the Harry Ransom Center at the University of Texas at Austin. The Maison Nicéphore Niépce museum at Saint-Loup-de-Varennes preserves his workshop and laboratory.
