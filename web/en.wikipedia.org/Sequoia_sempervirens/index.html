Sequoia sempervirens, commonly known as the coast redwood, is an evergreen, long-lived conifer in the cypress family Cupressaceae and the sole living species of the genus Sequoia. It includes the tallest living trees on Earth: mature specimens commonly reach 60–90 metres (200–300 ft), and the tallest known, named Hyperion, stands about 116 metres (380 ft) tall. The species name sempervirens means "always green" or "everliving".

Distribution and habitat

Coast redwoods are native to a narrow coastal strip of northern California and extreme southwestern Oregon, within about 8–56 km (5–35 mi) of the Pacific Ocean, where summer fog supplies crucial moisture. They grow in deep, moist valley soils at elevations mostly below 300 m. Famous groves are protected in Redwood National and State Parks, Muir Woods National Monument, Big Basin Redwoods State Park and Humboldt Redwoods State Park.

Description

The bark is thick (up to 30 cm), fibrous, furrowed and rich in tannins, making mature trees notably resistant to fire and insects. Leaves are scale-like on older growth and flat needles on young shoots; the small cones are only about 2 cm long yet contain seeds that germinate on moist mineral soil. Redwoods also reproduce vegetatively, sprouting from the base and forming "fairy rings" of clones around a parent stump. Individuals commonly live 500–1,000 years, and the oldest known exceed 2,200 years.

History and conservation

Intensive logging after the California Gold Rush removed roughly 95% of the old-growth forest; redwood timber is prized for its decay resistance. Today remaining old growth is largely protected, and the species is listed as Endangered by the IUCN because of its restricted range and fragmentation. Redwoods also store more carbon per hectare than almost any other forest type.
