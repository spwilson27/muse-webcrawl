Enlightened absolutism

Enlightened absolutism, also called enlightened despotism, refers to the conduct and policies of European absolute monarchs during the 18th and early 19th centuries who were influenced by the ideas of the Enlightenment, espousing them to enhance their power. The concept originated during an age of absolutism, when monarchs were versed in the writings of Enlightenment thinkers and sought to centralise and rationalise their rule in light of those ideas.

Enlightened monarchs embraced the principles of the Enlightenment, especially its emphasis on rationality, and applied them to their territories. They tended to allow religious toleration, freedom of speech and the press, and the right to hold private property. They fostered the arts, sciences, and education. Despite their reforms, enlightened despots held that royal power emanated not from a divine right but from a social contract, whereby the ruler had a duty to govern in the best interests of the people — without surrendering any claim to absolute authority.

Major figures

The most prominent enlightened absolutists included Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Great of Russia, Joseph II and Leopold II of Austria, and Charles III of Spain. Frederick wrote of the king as the "first servant of the state." Catherine drafted proposals for a new law code, founded schools and hospitals, and corresponded with Voltaire and Denis Diderot, though she also expanded serfdom in practice.

Reforms

Typical reforms included the abolition or restriction of serfdom, codification of laws, the curtailment of torture and capital punishment, the reduction of noble and clerical privileges, the promotion of secular education, and the standardisation of administration. Joseph II of Austria was among the most radical: he issued the Edict of Tolerance (1781), abolished personal serfdom, and attempted thoroughgoing centralisation — reforms so sweeping that many were reversed after his death in 1790.

Criticism and legacy

Historians have long debated the sincerity and impact of enlightened absolutism. Critics note that rulers adopted only those Enlightenment ideas compatible with strengthening the state, and that fundamental inequalities of the old regime remained untouched. Nevertheless, the movement helped diffuse Enlightenment principles across Europe and foreshadowed the modernising reforms of the nineteenth century.
