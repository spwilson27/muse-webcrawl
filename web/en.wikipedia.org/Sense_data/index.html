Sense data

In the philosophy of perception, sense data are the kind of things that are allegedly directly perceived by the senses: colours, sounds, smells, hardnesses, and so on. According to the sense-datum theory, sense data are mental objects or states that stand between the perceiver and the external world, and perceptual knowledge is founded upon them. The theory was developed by philosophers such as Bertrand Russell, G. E. Moore, and H. H. Price in the early 20th century.

The argument from illusion

The principal motivation for sense-data theory is the argument from illusion: when a straight stick looks bent in water, or a round coin looks elliptical, something genuinely has the apparent properties (bent, elliptical), and since the physical object does not, what is immediately perceived must be a non-physical sense datum. Hallucinations, in which the subject perceives things that do not exist physically at all, strengthen the case.

Criticism

Sense-data theories were later attacked by J. L. Austin in Sense and Sensibilia (1962), who argued that the theory misdescribes ordinary perceptual experience, and they fell out of favour with the rise of direct realism and disjunctivism. Nevertheless, the concept continues to be discussed in contemporary philosophy of mind in connection with qualia and representationalism.
