Biological warfare

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biological warfare, also known as germ warfare, is the use of biological toxins or infectious agents such as bacteria, viruses, insects and fungi with the intent to kill, harm or incapacitate humans, animals or plants as an act of war. Biological weapons (often termed bio-weapons or bio-agents) are living organisms or replicating entities — viruses, which are not universally considered alive — that reproduce or replicate within their victims.

History

The use of disease as a weapon is ancient: armies poisoned wells, and at the 1346 siege of Caffa, plague-infected corpses were reportedly thrown into the city. In the 20th century, several states developed organised programmes. Imperial Japan's Unit 731 conducted lethal experiments and field attacks in China. During the Cold War, the United States and the Soviet Union maintained large offensive programmes; the Soviet Biopreparat system employed tens of thousands of people. An accidental release of anthrax from a Soviet facility at Sverdlovsk in 1979 killed at least 66 people.

Legal status

The 1925 Geneva Protocol prohibited the use of biological weapons, and the 1972 Biological Weapons Convention (BWC) — which entered into force in 1975 — bans their development, production, acquisition, stockpiling and transfer. Almost all states are parties, though the treaty lacks a formal verification regime. The deliberate release of a pathogen by non-state actors is termed bioterrorism; examples include the 2001 anthrax letter attacks in the United States.

Characteristics

Agents historically investigated include anthrax (Bacillus anthracis), plague, smallpox, botulinum toxin, tularemia and viral haemorrhagic fevers. Biological attacks are difficult to attribute, may have delayed effects because of incubation periods, and can target crops and livestock as well as people.
