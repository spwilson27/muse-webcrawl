Faraday's law of induction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Faraday's law of induction (or simply Faraday's law) is a law of electromagnetism predicting how a magnetic field interacts with an electric circuit to produce an electromotive force (emf) — a phenomenon known as electromagnetic induction. It states that the induced emf in any closed circuit is equal to the negative of the time rate of change of the magnetic flux through the circuit:

ℰ = − dΦB / dt

where ℰ is the electromotive force and ΦB is the magnetic flux. The negative sign expresses Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change that produces it.

History

The law was discovered by Michael Faraday in 1831 through experiments in which he showed that a changing current in one coil induced a current in a second coil, and that moving a magnet through a loop of wire generated electricity. Joseph Henry made similar observations independently in America. Faraday's discovery is the operating principle behind most electric generators, transformers, induction motors and many other electrical devices.

Maxwell–Faraday equation

In its differential form, generalised by James Clerk Maxwell, Faraday's law becomes one of the four Maxwell's equations: the curl of the electric field equals the negative partial derivative of the magnetic field with respect to time (∇ × E = −∂B/∂t). This form shows that a time-varying magnetic field creates a spatially varying electric field, and vice versa — the basis of electromagnetic waves. For a coil of N turns, the induced emf is multiplied by N, a fact exploited in transformer design.
