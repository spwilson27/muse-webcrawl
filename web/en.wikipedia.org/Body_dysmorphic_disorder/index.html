Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), sometimes called dysmorphophobia, is a mental disorder characterized by an obsessive preoccupation with one or more perceived defects or flaws in one's own appearance. The perceived flaws are typically not observable or appear only slight to others, but the preoccupation causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other areas of functioning. In the DSM-5, BDD is classified within the chapter on obsessive-compulsive and related disorders.

Signs and symptoms

People with BDD commonly engage in repetitive behaviors in response to their appearance concerns, such as mirror checking, excessive grooming, skin picking, reassurance seeking, or comparing their appearance with that of others. Any part of the body can be the focus of concern, although the skin, hair, and nose are especially common. Muscle dysmorphia, a form of BDD in which a person believes their body is too small or insufficiently muscular, predominantly affects males.

BDD is frequently comorbid with major depressive disorder, social anxiety disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. Suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are markedly elevated relative to the general population.

Causes

The exact cause of BDD is not known. Research points to a combination of genetic, neurobiological, psychological, and environmental factors, including abnormal visual processing favoring detail over holistic perception, a history of teasing or abuse, and cultural pressures regarding appearance.

Treatment

First-line treatments are cognitive behavioral therapy adapted for BDD — including exposure and response prevention and perceptual retraining — and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, often at relatively high doses and for longer durations than used for depression. Cosmetic procedures sought to correct the perceived defect generally do not relieve symptoms and are not recommended as treatment.

History

The condition was described in 1886 by the Italian psychiatrist Enrico Morselli, who coined the term dysmorphophobia. It was first recognized as a disorder by the American Psychiatric Association in 1980, in the third edition of the DSM.
