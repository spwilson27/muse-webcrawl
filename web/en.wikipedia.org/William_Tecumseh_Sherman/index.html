William Tecumseh Sherman

William Tecumseh Sherman (February 8, 1820 – February 14, 1891) was an American soldier, businessman, educator, and author. He served as a general in the Union Army during the American Civil War (1861–1865), achieving recognition for his command of military strategy as well as complaints about the harshness of the "scorched earth" policies he implemented against the Confederate States.

Civil War

Born in Lancaster, Ohio, and named for the Shawnee chief Tecumseh, Sherman graduated from West Point in 1840 and resigned from the army in 1853 before rejoining in 1861. He fought at the First Battle of Bull Run and at Shiloh, then worked closely with Ulysses S. Grant at Vicksburg. Succeeding Grant in the western theater in 1864, he captured Atlanta and then embarked on his famous March to the Sea, cutting a path of destruction through Georgia to break the Confederacy's capacity and will to fight — declaring that "war is cruelty" and must be waged on the enemy's resources. His Carolinas Campaign followed, and he accepted the surrender of Joseph E. Johnston's army in April 1865.

Later life

Sherman succeeded Grant as Commanding General of the Army (1869–1883), overseeing western campaigns against Plains tribes. Famously refusing political office — "I will not accept if nominated and will not serve if elected" — he retired to New York, published his Memoirs, and died there in 1891.
