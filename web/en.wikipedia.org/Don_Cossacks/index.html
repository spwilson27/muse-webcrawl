The Don Cossacks (Russian: Донские казаки, Donskiye kazaki) are Cossacks who settled along the middle and lower Don River in southern Russia. The Don Cossack Host was historically the largest and most influential of the Cossack hosts, and the Don Cossacks played a central role in the expansion of the Russian Empire, the conquest of Siberia and the Caucasus, and the defence of Russia's southern frontiers. Their name is sometimes used as a generic term for Cossacks as a whole.

History

Freebooting Slavic settlers, runaway serfs, and adventurers began settling the wild steppe along the Don from the late 15th and 16th centuries, forming self-governing military communities beyond the reach of Moscow. In return for defending the frontier against Crimean Tatar and Nogai raids, the tsars gradually recognised their autonomy and supplied them with grain, money, and gunpowder.

The Don Cossacks repeatedly rose in rebellion when the central government encroached on their freedoms: the great uprisings of Stenka Razin (1670–71), Kondraty Bulavin (1707–08), and Yemelyan Pugachev (1773–75) each drew heavily on Don manpower. Despite this, Don regiments served with distinction in the Napoleonic Wars, most famously under Ataman Matvei Platov, whose horsemen reached Paris in 1814, and in the Russo-Turkish wars and the First World War.

The Host and its government

The Don Voisko was governed by an elected ataman and a general assembly, the Krug, in which all Cossacks theoretically had a voice. The territory, the Voisko Oblast of the Don, was centred first on Cherkassk and after 1805 on Novocherkassk. By 1913 the Don Cossack population numbered well over a million. In 1920, after the Russian Civil War — in which the Don region, under Ataman Pyotr Krasnov, was a stronghold of the White movement allied for a time with the Don Republic — the Host was formally abolished and its lands subjected to decossackization.

Culture

Don Cossack culture blended frontier warfare with agriculture, fishing on the Don, and viticulture. Their polyphonic folk singing is renowned, and the Don Cossacks are the subject of Mikhail Sholokhov's epic novel And Quiet Flows the Don, which won the Nobel Prize in Literature. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union, Don Cossack societies were legally revived, and registered Don Cossacks now form part of Russia's registered Cossack hosts.
