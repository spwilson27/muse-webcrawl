Gorillas are herbivorous, predominantly ground-dwelling great apes that inhabit the tropical forests of equatorial Africa. The genus Gorilla is divided into two species, the eastern gorilla and the western gorilla, and either four or five subspecies. The DNA of gorillas is highly similar to that of humans, from 95 to 99% depending on what is included, and they are the next closest living relatives to humans after chimpanzees and bonobos.

Gorillas are the largest living primates, reaching heights of 1.25–1.8 metres and weights of 100–270 kg in the wild for adult males (silverbacks). They live in social groups called troops, led by a dominant silverback male, and are generally diurnal, spending much of the day feeding on stems, bamboo shoots, fruit and sometimes insects. At night they build nests of vegetation on the ground or in trees.

Behaviour and intelligence

Gorillas communicate through vocalisations, chest-beating displays, and gestures. They are capable of learning sign language in captivity (most famously Koko) and of using simple tools in the wild. Gestation lasts about 8.5 months, and infants are dependent on their mothers for several years; females give birth roughly every four years.

Conservation

All gorilla species and subspecies are classified as endangered or critically endangered on the IUCN Red List, threatened by habitat loss, poaching for bushmeat, disease (notably Ebola), and the effects of war. Conservation efforts include protected areas such as Virunga and Bwindi national parks, anti-poaching patrols, and ecotourism centred on habituated mountain gorilla groups, whose numbers have slowly recovered to over 1,000.
