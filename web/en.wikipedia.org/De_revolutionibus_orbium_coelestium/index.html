De revolutionibus orbium coelestium

De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres) is the foundational work of the heliocentric theory of the astronomer Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543). Published in Nuremberg in 1543, the year of Copernicus's death, it proposed that the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun, displacing the Earth from the centre of the universe and launching what historians call the Copernican Revolution and, more broadly, the Scientific Revolution.

Contents

The book is divided into six books. The first sets out the heliocentric arrangement and argues for the sphericity and motion of the Earth; later books develop the mathematical machinery — largely inherited from Ptolemy's Almagest, including epicycles — needed to compute the motions of the Moon and the five known planets. Copernicus's system explained retrograde motion as a natural consequence of the Earth's own movement, though it retained circular orbits and was not initially much more accurate than the Ptolemaic system it challenged.

Publication and reception

Copernicus had circulated the ideas decades earlier in a short manuscript, the Commentariolus, but delayed publication of the full work. The young astronomer Georg Joachim Rheticus persuaded him to publish and oversaw the printing, while the Lutheran theologian Andreas Osiander added an unsigned preface presenting the theory as a mere calculating device rather than a claim about reality. The book was initially read mainly by astronomers; the Catholic Church placed it on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1616, "until corrected", after the Galileo affair. Surviving copies, studied in a famous census by Owen Gingerich, show dense annotations proving the book was closely read by the leading astronomers of the age.
