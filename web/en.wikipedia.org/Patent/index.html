A patent is a type of intellectual property that gives its owner the legal right to exclude others from making, using, or selling an invention for a limited period of time, in exchange for publishing an enabling disclosure of the invention. In most countries, patent rights fall under private law and the patent holder must sue infringers to enforce them; the right is territorial, so protection must be obtained in each jurisdiction where it is sought.

History

Venice enacted the first known patent statute in 1474, granting ten-year privileges for new and inventive devices. England's Statute of Monopolies (1624) curtailed royal monopolies while preserving patents for "new manufactures", and the United States wrote patent power into its Constitution (1787), "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts". The Paris Convention (1883) established priority rights internationally, the Patent Cooperation Treaty (1970) simplified multinational filing, and the TRIPS Agreement (1994) set minimum standards — including a 20-year term — for WTO members.

Requirements and procedure

To be patentable, an invention must be novel (not previously disclosed), involve an inventive step or be non-obvious to a skilled person, be capable of industrial application or useful, and fall within patentable subject matter — abstract ideas, laws of nature and natural phenomena are excluded in many jurisdictions, and the patentability of software and business methods varies widely. An application contains a specification describing the invention and claims defining the scope of protection; examiners search prior art and may reject or require amendment before grant. Publication normally occurs 18 months after filing.

Rights, enforcement and criticism

A patent does not give its owner the right to practise the invention (other patents or regulations may block that), only the right to exclude others. Infringement litigation is notoriously expensive — the "smartphone wars" of the 2010s are a prominent example. Economists debate the system's balance: patents incentivise research and force disclosure, but critics cite patent thickets, "patent trolls" (non-practising entities), high drug prices and barriers to follow-on innovation, while open-source and defensive-patent movements seek alternative models. Utility patents are the standard form; many countries also offer shorter utility models, and separate systems cover designs and plant varieties.
