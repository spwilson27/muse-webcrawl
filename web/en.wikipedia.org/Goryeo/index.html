Goryeo

Goryeo was a Korean state founded in 918 during a fragmentation of the peninsula, unifying the Later Three Kingdoms in 936 and ruling most of the Korean Peninsula until 1392, when it was replaced by Joseon. The modern English name "Korea" derives from Goryeo, which itself perpetuated the name of the ancient kingdom of Goguryeo.

Founded by the general Wang Geon (King Taejo), Goryeo developed a sophisticated Buddhist culture, produced the celebrated green-glazed celadon pottery, and carved the Tripitaka Koreana — more than 80,000 wooden printing blocks of the Buddhist canon — now stored at Haeinsa temple and listed by UNESCO. Metal movable type printing was developed in Goryeo; the Jikji (1377) is the oldest extant book printed with metal type.

History

Goryeo repelled invasions by the Khitan Liao dynasty (993–1019) and maintained commercial and diplomatic relations with Song China. In 1170 a coup established decades of military rule under the Choe family. Mongol invasions beginning in 1231 devastated the country; the court fled to Ganghwa Island, and after 1270 Goryeo became a semi-autonomous vassal ("son-in-law") state of the Yuan dynasty. As Yuan power waned, King Gongmin attempted reforms and recovered territory, but court conflict, Red Turban invasions, and Japanese pirate (wakō) raids weakened the dynasty. In 1392 the general Yi Seong-gye overthrew it and founded Joseon.

Culture

Buddhism flourished as the state religion, while a Confucian civil-service examination system structured the bureaucracy. Goryeo celadon, inlaid with cranes and clouds under a pale green glaze, is considered among the finest ceramics in the world.
