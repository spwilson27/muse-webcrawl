The Western Zhou (Chinese: 西周; c. 1045 – 771 BC) was the first half of the Zhou dynasty of ancient China, succeeding the Shang dynasty. It began when King Wu of Zhou overthrew the Shang at the Battle of Muye and ended in 771 BC when Quanrong nomads sacked the Zhou capital at Haojing, killing King You and forcing the court to move east to Luoyi, beginning the Eastern Zhou period.

Government

The Western Zhou state was a kinship-based aristocratic order often described as a feudal system (fengjian): the king enfeoffed relatives and allies with regional states, which owed military service and tribute in return. The dynasty justified its rule with the Mandate of Heaven (Tianming), the doctrine that Heaven grants rule to the virtuous and withdraws it from the corrupt — a concept that shaped Chinese political thought for millennia.

Culture

The period is known for its finely cast ritual bronze vessels, many bearing inscriptions that are key historical sources. The core texts of early Chinese literature — the oldest parts of the Book of Documents and Book of Songs — date to this era, as do the political writings attributed to the Duke of Zhou, regent for the young King Cheng.

Decline

By the 9th century BC the Zhou kings' authority waned amid succession crises and wars with the Xianyun and other peoples. King Li was driven into exile in 841 BC, an event followed by the Gonghe Regency. In 771 BC the Marquess of Shen allied with the Quanrong to sack Haojing; the capital moved east and royal power declined into the Spring and Autumn period.
