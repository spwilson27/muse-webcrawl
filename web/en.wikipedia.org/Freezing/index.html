Freezing

Freezing, or solidification, is a phase transition in which a liquid turns into a solid when its temperature is lowered below its freezing point. For most substances, the melting point and freezing point are equal; water, the most familiar example, freezes at 0 °C (32 °F, 273.15 K) at standard atmospheric pressure.

Process

Freezing begins with nucleation, the formation of small ordered clusters of molecules or crystals that can grow into a solid phase. Many liquids can be supercooled below their nominal freezing point without solidifying until nucleation occurs. During freezing, a pure substance releases latent heat of fusion while its temperature remains constant until the whole mass has solidified. Unlike most substances, water expands on freezing — ice is less dense than liquid water — which is why ice floats and why freezing water can burst pipes and crack rock.

Applications

Freezing is used for food preservation, since low temperatures slow microbial growth and chemical spoilage; techniques range from household freezers to industrial blast freezing and freeze-drying. Cryopreservation applies controlled freezing to biological materials. In nature, freezing shapes landscapes through frost weathering and frost heave, and organisms in cold climates employ antifreeze proteins and other adaptations to survive or control ice formation.
