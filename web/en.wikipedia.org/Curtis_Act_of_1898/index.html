Curtis Act of 1898

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Curtis Act of 1898 was an amendment to the United States Dawes Act, enacted on June 28, 1898. Named for its sponsor, Senator Charles Curtis of Kansas (later Vice President), the Act extended the policy of allotment to the "Five Civilized Tribes" of Indian Territory — the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek (Muscogee), and Seminole — who had previously been exempt from the Dawes Act.

Provisions

The Act provided for:

  • The survey and allotment in severalty of the communal lands of the Five Tribes.
  • The abolition of tribal governments, to take full effect by March 4, 1906.
  • The termination of tribal laws and the transfer of tribal courts' jurisdiction to federal courts.
  • Federal control over tribal funds, schools, and the incorporation of towns in Indian Territory.

Consequences

Implementation through the Dawes Commission led to the enrolment of tribal citizens and the distribution of roughly 19 million acres, with "surplus" lands opened to non-Native settlement. The dissolution of tribal governments paved the way for Oklahoma statehood in 1907. The Act, together with the allotment policy generally, resulted in the loss of enormous areas of Native land and is regarded as a central instrument of forced assimilation.
