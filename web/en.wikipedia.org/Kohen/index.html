Kohen (Hebrew: כֹּהֵן, plural kohanim) is the Hebrew word for "priest" and refers to the hereditary Jewish priesthood whose members are traditionally believed to be direct patrilineal descendants of Aaron, the brother of Moses. In the Hebrew Bible, Aaron was appointed the first High Priest (Kohen Gadol), and the priestly office was assigned to his male descendants in perpetuity.

History

During the periods of the Tabernacle and the First and Second Temples in Jerusalem, kohanim performed the sacrificial service (avodah), including offering daily and festival sacrifices, burning incense, and blessing the people. They were supported by the Levites, who assisted in Temple service, and received certain gifts (terumah and tithes) from the Israelites in lieu of a territorial inheritance, as the Tribe of Levi received no portion of land.

Priestly division

King David is traditionally credited with organizing the kohanim into 24 priestly courses (mishmarot), each serving in rotation in the Temple for one week at a time. Lists of these courses appear in the Book of Chronicles and in later rabbinic literature.

Status after the Temple

After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, the sacrificial role of the kohanim ceased, but their hereditary status was preserved in Jewish law and practice. Kohanim retain certain privileges and restrictions: they are called first to the public reading of the Torah, they deliver the Priestly Blessing (Birkat Kohanim) during prayer services, and they officiate at the redemption of the firstborn (Pidyon HaBen). Traditional law also imposes restrictions on kohanim, including prohibitions on marriage to certain persons and on contact with the dead, except for close relatives.

Genetics

Studies of Y-chromosome markers among self-identified kohanim in the late twentieth century identified a set of haplotypes, termed the Cohen Modal Haplotype, found at elevated frequency among kohanim in both Ashkenazi and Sephardi communities. The findings were interpreted as consistent with a shared paternal ancestry dating back several thousand years, though subsequent research has refined this picture.
