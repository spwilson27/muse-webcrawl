LINER

A LINER spectrum, dominated by lines of weakly ionized atoms such as [O I] and [N II].

A LINER, or low-ionization nuclear emission-line region, is a type of galactic nucleus whose spectrum is defined by emission lines from weakly ionized or neutral atoms, such as O, O+, N+, and S+, in contrast to the strongly ionized lines seen in regions powered by hot young stars or quasars. The class was defined by Timothy Heckman in 1980. LINERs are extremely common: surveys suggest roughly one-third of all nearby galaxies host a LINER nucleus.

Properties

LINER spectra show strong forbidden lines of low-ionization species — [O I] 6300 Å, [N II] 6583 Å, [S II] 6716,6731 Å — relative to hydrogen Balmer lines, distinguishing them from H II regions and classic Seyfert galaxies on emission-line diagnostic diagrams such as the BPT diagram. They are found in galaxies of all Hubble types but especially in lenticular and elliptical galaxies and in the bulges of early-type spirals.

Power source debate

The energy source of LINER emission has been debated for decades. Candidate mechanisms include a low-luminosity active galactic nucleus accreting at low rates, fast shocks, and hot evolved stars — especially post-asymptotic-giant-branch stars, whose hard ultraviolet radiation can ionize extended gas in "retired" galaxies. High-resolution imaging and spectroscopy show that many LINERs contain compact central sources consistent with weak AGN, while in others the emission is extended and better explained by stellar photoionization. LINERs are thus likely a heterogeneous class unified by their spectra rather than a single physical mechanism.
