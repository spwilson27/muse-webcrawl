Yardang

A yardang is a streamlined protuberance carved from bedrock or consolidated sediment by the dual action of wind abrasion and deflation. Yardangs occur chiefly in deserts, where strong, unidirectional winds laden with sand sculpt the rock into elongated, inverted-hull shapes aligned with the prevailing wind.

Etymology and description

The word comes from the Turkic yar, "steep bank," and was introduced into English by the Swedish explorer Sven Hedin, who encountered classic examples around Lop Nur in China's Xinjiang region in the early 1900s. An ideal yardang resembles the hull of an upturned boat, typically about three to four times longer than it is wide, with a steep, blunt face toward the wind and a tapered tail downwind.

Formation

Wind-driven sand abrades softer sedimentary strata — lakebed clays, siltstones, and sandstones — while deflation removes loose grains. Differential erosion exploits joints and bedding, and undercutting at the base can steepen the windward prow. Large yardang fields, sometimes called "white dragon" or city-like clusters in Dunhuang's Yadan National Geopark, can extend for kilometres; individual mega-yardangs reach hundreds of metres long and tens of metres high.

Extraterrestrial yardangs

Yardangs have been identified on Mars — for example in the Medusae Fossae Formation — and possibly on Venus and Titan, where they record the direction of ancient winds and help reconstruct planetary climates.
