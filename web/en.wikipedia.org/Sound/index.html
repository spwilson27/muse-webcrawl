A sound wave as alternating compressions and rarefactions

In physics, sound is a vibration that propagates as an acoustic wave through a transmission medium such as a gas, liquid, or solid. In human physiology and psychology, sound is the reception of such waves and their perception by the brain. Sound waves are generated by a vibrating body, which creates alternating regions of compression and rarefaction in the surrounding medium.

Properties

Sound is characterised by frequency (perceived as pitch), wavelength, amplitude (related to loudness, measured in decibels), speed, and direction. The speed of sound depends on the medium: about 343 metres per second in air at 20 °C, about 1,480 m/s in water, and roughly 5,000 m/s in steel. Sounds below the human hearing range are called infrasound, and those above it ultrasound; both are exploited by animals and by technologies such as sonar and medical ultrasonography.

Perception

The ear converts pressure variations into neural signals: the eardrum vibrates, the ossicles transmit the motion to the cochlea, and hair cells transduce it by frequency position. The study of sound perception is psychoacoustics, which examines phenomena such as masking, localisation, and the missing fundamental. The scientific study of sound in general is called acoustics.
