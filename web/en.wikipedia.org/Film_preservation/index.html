Film preservation is the practice of rescuing, storing and maintaining motion picture films so that they survive and remain viewable for future generations. Because films were long regarded as disposable commercial products, and because their physical supports are chemically unstable, a large proportion of cinema history has been lost: studies estimate that roughly 70–75% of American silent feature films are completely lost, and many early sound films survive only in damaged or incomplete form.

Threats to film survival

Before 1951, films were shot and printed on cellulose nitrate stock, which is chemically unstable and highly flammable, decomposing into a sticky, then powdery, mass and sometimes igniting; many archives and studios lost entire collections to nitrate fires or deliberate destruction for silver recovery. Safety acetate stock replaced nitrate but suffers from "vinegar syndrome", autocatalytic deacetylation that shrinks and embrittles film. Colour dyes fade at different rates, leaving prints magenta-shifted, and magnetic soundtracks, videotape and digital files have their own obsolescence problems.

Practices

Preservation combines cold, dry, stable storage to slow decay; inspection and repair; duplication onto stable polyester stock or high-resolution digital scanning; and restoration, which uses surviving elements to reconstruct a film as close as possible to its original form. Institutions such as the members of the International Federation of Film Archives (FIAF, founded 1938), the Library of Congress and the BFI National Archive maintain vaults and conservation laboratories. The US National Film Registry, established 1988, selects culturally significant films for preservation. Funding shortages mean archivists must prioritise, and the "orphan film" movement advocates for neglected categories such as home movies, newsreels and educational films.

Digital preservation

Digitisation aids access but is not itself a preservation panacea: file formats, codecs and storage media become obsolete, requiring managed migration and redundant storage. For this reason many archives treat properly stored film elements as the most durable preservation medium, an approach summarised as "store the film, serve the file".
