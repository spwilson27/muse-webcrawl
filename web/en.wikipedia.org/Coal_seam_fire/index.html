Coal seam fire

CausesSpontaneous combustion, lightning, wildfire, mine accidents, burning waste
DurationYears to millennia
Notable examplesCentralia (USA), Jharia (India), Burning Mountain (Australia)

A coal seam fire is a burning of an outcrop or underground coal deposit. Coal fires often start through spontaneous combustion: exposed coal oxidizes, and if the heat produced cannot dissipate, the temperature rises to ignition. Other causes include lightning strikes, forest and bush fires, human accidents in and around mines, and the burning of refuse in abandoned mine workings or landfills overlying coal. Because they burn underground with a limited supply of oxygen, coal seam fires can smoulder at temperatures exceeding 500 °C and persist for extraordinarily long periods.

Notable fires

The most famous example is the fire beneath Centralia, Pennsylvania, ignited in 1962, which forced the abandonment of the town and continues to burn. The Jharia coalfield in Jharkhand, India, has suffered fires since 1916; dozens burn today across one of India's richest coal deposits, causing land subsidence and severe air pollution for a dense local population. In China, coal fires — particularly in Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia — consume an estimated tens of millions of tonnes of coal each year and are among the largest single sources of carbon emissions from any fire class.

The oldest known coal fire is Burning Mountain (Mount Wingen) in New South Wales, Australia, which has burned for approximately 6,000 years, advancing about a metre per year beneath the ground. Similar natural fires were noted by early explorers in the American West, and burning seams in the Powder River Basin have baked surrounding rock into red clinker that caps buttes across Wyoming and Montana.

Effects and control

Coal seam fires release carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, mercury, and other toxic substances; they sterilize land, trigger subsidence and sinkholes, and destroy the coal resource itself. Fighting them is difficult: methods include excavation of the burning material, flooding, injection of inert gases or grout, and sealing of cracks and shafts to starve the fire of oxygen — all frequently undermined by the extent of old mine workings. Many fires are simply monitored and left to burn out over decades or centuries.
