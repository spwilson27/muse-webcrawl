The United States is a charter member of the United Nations and one of five permanent members of the UN Security Council. The United States played a central role in the establishment of the United Nations at the San Francisco Conference in 1945, and the UN Headquarters is located in New York City.

History

President Franklin D. Roosevelt first coined the term "United Nations" in 1942 to describe the Allied nations fighting the Axis in the Second World War. The United States hosted the San Francisco Conference in April–June 1945, where the UN Charter was signed. The U.S. Senate ratified the Charter by a vote of 89–2, a sharp contrast with the rejection of the League of Nations after the First World War.

Role in the UN

As a permanent member of the Security Council, the United States holds veto power over substantive resolutions. It is also the largest financial contributor to the UN system, providing roughly 22 percent of the regular budget and a large share of peacekeeping funding — a source of recurring political debate in Washington. The United States has periodically withheld dues to press for reforms, and Congress has at times capped contributions.

Diplomacy and disputes

American relations with the UN have oscillated between enthusiastic multilateralism and skepticism. Notable episodes include the Korean War (fought under a UN flag after the Soviet Union boycotted the Security Council), the Cuban Missile Crisis debates, the 2003 invasion of Iraq without explicit Security Council authorization, and U.S. withdrawals from bodies such as UNESCO and the Human Rights Council at various times.
