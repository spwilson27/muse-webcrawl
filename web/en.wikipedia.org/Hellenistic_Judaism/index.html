Hellenistic Judaism

Hellenistic Judaism was a form of Judaism in the ancient world that combined Jewish religious tradition with elements of Greek (Hellenistic) culture and language. It flourished from the conquests of Alexander the Great in the late 4th century BC until late antiquity, principally in the Jewish diaspora of the eastern Mediterranean, above all in Alexandria in Egypt, but also within Judea itself.

Historical development

After Alexander's conquests, Jewish communities came under the rule of the Ptolemaic and Seleucid kingdoms, and Greek became the lingua franca of the eastern Mediterranean. Jews adopted Greek language, names, education and literary forms to varying degrees while maintaining their ancestral laws. In Judea, Hellenising reforms under the high priest Jason and the persecution under Antiochus IV provoked the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BC), yet even the Hasmonean state that followed made extensive use of Greek culture and institutions.

Literature

The most famous product of Hellenistic Judaism is the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible produced in Alexandria beginning in the 3rd century BC. Jewish authors wrote extensively in Greek: the philosopher Philo of Alexandria interpreted the Torah through Middle Platonism; the historian Josephus retold Jewish history for Greco-Roman readers; and apocryphal and pseudepigraphal works such as 2 Maccabees, the Wisdom of Solomon, 3 and 4 Maccabees and the Letter of Aristeas reflect the blending of biblical tradition with Greek genres and ideas.

Decline

Hellenistic Judaism was devastated by the destruction of the Second Temple in AD 70 and by the crushing of the great diaspora revolt of AD 115–117, which effectively ended the Jewish community of Alexandria. In the following centuries, rabbinic Judaism, which preserved its tradition mainly in Hebrew and Aramaic, became normative, while Greek-speaking Jewish literature survived chiefly because it was taken up and preserved by the early Christian church. The Septuagint remained the Old Testament of Greek Christianity, and Philo deeply influenced Christian theology.
