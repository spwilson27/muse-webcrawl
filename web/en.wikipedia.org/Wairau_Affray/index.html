Wairau Affray

The Wairau Valley, scene of the first violent clash of the New Zealand Wars era.

Wairau Affray
Date17 June 1843
LocationTuamarina, Wairau Valley, New Zealand
PartiesNew Zealand Company settlers and police vs Ngāti Toa

The Wairau Affray of 17 June 1843 was the first serious armed conflict between Māori and British settlers in New Zealand after the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi. It took place in the Wairau Valley, near modern Tuamarina in Marlborough, and left 22 Europeans and four Māori dead.

Background

The New Zealand Company claimed to have purchased the Wairau Plains from Ngāti Toa, but the chiefs Te Rauparaha and Te Rangihaeata disputed the sale and obstructed the Company's surveyors, burning a survey hut. In response, Nelson magistrates issued warrants for the chiefs' arrest on charges of arson, and an armed party of some 50 settlers and police sailed from Nelson to execute them.

The clash

At Tuamarina the two parties faced each other across a stream. During a tense negotiation, a shot — probably accidental — killed Te Rongo, a wife of Te Rangihaeata. Fighting erupted; the settlers, outmatched, surrendered, but Te Rangihaeata, invoking utu for his wife's death, ordered the execution of the prisoners, including Captain Arthur Wakefield, the Company's resident agent, and Captain Richard England.

Aftermath

Settlers demanded reprisals, but Governor Robert FitzRoy investigated and concluded that the Europeans had been in the wrong, since the land sale was unproven and the attempted arrest unlawful. His refusal to punish Ngāti Toa was bitterly resented by settlers but averted a wider war. The incident deepened the divide between settler and official views of Māori land rights that would shape the New Zealand Wars.
