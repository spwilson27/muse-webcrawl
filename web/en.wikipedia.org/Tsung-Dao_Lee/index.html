Tsung-Dao Lee (Chinese: 李政道; November 24, 1926 – August 4, 2024) was a Chinese-American theoretical physicist best known for proposing, together with Chen Ning Yang, that the weak interaction violates parity conservation. Their prediction was confirmed experimentally by Chien-Shiung Wu in 1957, and Lee and Yang received the Nobel Prize in Physics that same year. At 31, Lee was one of the youngest Nobel laureates in physics.

Early life and education

Born in Shanghai, Lee's education was disrupted by the Second Sino-Japanese War. He studied at Zhejiang University and National Southwestern Associated University before receiving a fellowship to study in the United States. At the University of Chicago he earned his PhD in 1950 under Enrico Fermi.

Career

After periods at Berkeley, Princeton's Institute for Advanced Study and elsewhere, Lee joined Columbia University in 1953, where he remained for the rest of his career, becoming a full professor at an exceptionally young age. With Yang, he worked on statistical mechanics (the Lee–Yang theorem) before turning to the theta–tau puzzle in particle physics, which led them to question parity conservation in weak interactions—long assumed to be a universal law.

Parity violation

In their landmark 1956 paper, Lee and Yang showed that parity conservation had never actually been tested in weak interactions and proposed experiments to test it. Wu's experiment on cobalt-60, announced in early 1957, confirmed that parity is violated, transforming the foundations of particle physics.

Later work and legacy

Lee made contributions across particle physics, field theory, statistical mechanics and astrophysics, and played an important role in rebuilding scientific exchange between China and the United States after 1979, helping to establish programmes that trained generations of Chinese physics students.
