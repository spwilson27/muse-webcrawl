Assemblage (art)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Assemblage
MediumFound objects, mixed media
Key figuresPablo Picasso, Kurt Schwitters, Joseph Cornell, Robert Rauschenberg, Louise Nevelson
Term coinedJean Dubuffet, 1953

Assemblage is an artistic form or medium usually created on a defined substrate that consists of three-dimensional elements projecting out of or from the substrate. It is similar to collage, a two-dimensional medium. It is part of the visual arts, and it typically uses found objects, but is not limited to these materials.

The term was coined by French artist Jean Dubuffet in 1953 to describe his own work, though antecedents include Pablo Picasso's three-dimensional constructions, Marcel Duchamp's readymades, and Kurt Schwitters's Merz installations built from scavenged debris.

Notable practitioners

Joseph Cornell became renowned for his shadow boxes containing poetic arrangements of found images and objects. Robert Rauschenberg's "combines" of the 1950s merged painting with everyday items such as quilts, tyres and a stuffed goat. Louise Nevelson stacked wooden boxes filled with found wood scraps, painted uniformly black, into monumental walls. In California, artists such as Edward Kienholz and Wallace Berman made assemblage a signature of West Coast art, while Betye Saar used it to explore memory and African American identity.

The Art of Assemblage (1961)

The Museum of Modern Art's 1961 exhibition The Art of Assemblage, curated by William C. Seitz, gave the medium institutional recognition, bringing together more than 250 works by 140 artists from Duchamp and Picasso to contemporary American practitioners.
