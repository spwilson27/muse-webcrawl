Inertial confinement fusion

Inertial confinement fusion (ICF) is a fusion energy process that initiates nuclear fusion reactions by compressing and heating targets, typically in the form of pellets containing deuterium and tritium. The compression is accomplished by delivering energy to the surface of the pellet, usually with powerful lasers or ion beams, causing the outer layer to ablate and drive the remaining fuel inward to extreme densities and temperatures.

In ICF, laser beams compress a millimetre-scale fuel pellet to conditions hotter and denser than the centre of the Sun.

Principle

ICF relies on the inertia of the fuel itself to confine it long enough for fusion to occur. When energy is deposited on the pellet's outer surface, the surface material is heated and expands violently outward; by Newton's third law, the remaining material is driven inward in a spherical implosion. The fuel is compressed to densities hundreds of times that of lead and heated to tens of millions of degrees, at which point fusion reactions begin in a central "hot spot" and propagate outward. The whole process lasts only a few nanoseconds before the pellet flies apart.

Approaches

In direct drive, laser beams strike the fuel capsule directly. In indirect drive, the capsule sits inside a small metal cylinder called a hohlraum; lasers heat the hohlraum walls, which emit X-rays that bathe and compress the capsule more uniformly. Other drivers studied include heavy-ion beams and pulsed-power devices such as the Z machine, which uses magnetic pressure (z-pinch) rather than light.

Facilities and milestones

The largest ICF facility is the National Ignition Facility (NIF) at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in California, which uses 192 laser beams delivering up to about 2 megajoules of ultraviolet light. In December 2022, NIF achieved a landmark result: a target released about 3.15 MJ of fusion energy from 2.05 MJ of laser energy delivered to it — the first controlled fusion experiment to produce more energy than the driver energy delivered to the fuel, known as scientific breakeven or ignition. The result has since been repeated with higher yields.

Other major facilities include the OMEGA laser at the University of Rochester, the Laser Mégajoule in France, and Shenguang-III in China. ICF research supports both fusion energy development and nuclear weapons stockpile stewardship, since the conditions created resemble those inside thermonuclear weapons.
