Problem gambling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Electronic gaming machines are strongly associated with gambling problems

Problem gambling, or ludomania, is an urge to gamble continuously despite harmful negative consequences or a desire to stop. Problem gambling is often defined by whether harm is experienced by the gambler or others, rather than by the gambler's behaviour. Severe problem gambling may be diagnosed as the clinical mental disorder gambling disorder when specific diagnostic criteria are met. Pathological gambling is classified in the DSM-5 as an addictive disorder, reflecting evidence that its symptoms resemble those of substance addictions.

Symptoms and diagnosis

According to the DSM-5, gambling disorder is characterised by persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behaviour leading to clinically significant impairment or distress, including preoccupation with gambling, needing to gamble with increasing amounts of money to achieve excitement, repeated unsuccessful attempts to cut back, restlessness or irritability when trying to stop, gambling to escape problems, chasing losses, lying to conceal involvement, jeopardising relationships or employment, and relying on others for bailout money. The Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) is a widely used screening tool in population surveys.

Prevalence and harm

Rates of problem gambling vary by jurisdiction and by the forms of gambling available; surveys in countries such as the United Kingdom, Australia and Canada typically find that around 0.5–2% of adults experience gambling problems, with a larger share at moderate risk. Harms include debt and bankruptcy, family breakdown, job loss, poor mental and physical health, crime and elevated suicide risk. Electronic gaming machines, casino games and online gambling are associated with higher rates of harm than lotteries.

Treatment and prevention

Treatments include cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing, twelve-step programmes such as Gamblers Anonymous, and self-exclusion schemes that allow people to bar themselves from gambling venues and websites. Some medications, including antidepressants and opioid antagonists, have shown benefit for comorbid conditions or urges. Prevention measures include regulation of advertising, stake and speed limits on machines, affordability checks and public health campaigns.
