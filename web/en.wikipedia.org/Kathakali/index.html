Kathakali

Kathakali (Malayalam: കഥകളി) is a major form of classical Indian dance-drama that originated in Kerala in the 17th century. It is renowned for its elaborate makeup, huge billowing skirts, ornate headdresses, detailed gestures and facial expressions, through which traditionally male actor-dancers enact stories from the Hindu epics and the Puranas.

Origins

Kathakali developed from earlier temple performance traditions such as Krishnanattam and Ramanattam, patronised by the rulers of southwestern India. The plays (attakatha) are written in a literary blend of Malayalam and Sanskrit called Manipravalam, sung by vocalists while the actors perform silently through mime.

Performance elements

Characters are identified by makeup types: green-faced pacha vesha for noble heroes and gods, red-bearded chuvanna tadi for villainous characters, and black-bearded types for demons. The actor's art rests on the nine facial expressions (navarasas) and an extensive vocabulary of hand gestures (mudras) set out in texts such as the Hastalakshanadeepika. Music is provided by singers with chenda and maddalam drums, cymbals and gongs; performances traditionally begin at dusk and continue all night. Training is long and physically demanding, centred at institutions such as Kerala Kalamandalam, founded by the poet Vallathol Narayana Menon in 1930.
