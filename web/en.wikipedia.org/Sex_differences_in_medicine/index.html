Sex differences in medicine

Many diseases differ in frequency, presentation and treatment response between the sexes.

Sex differences in medicine are the biological differences between females and males that affect health, disease and response to treatment. Beyond the reproductive system, sex influences anatomy, physiology, metabolism, the immune system and pharmacology throughout the body, and a growing field of sex-based medicine studies these effects.

Examples

Cardiovascular disease presents differently: women more often develop microvascular disease and may report symptoms other than classic chest pain during myocardial infarction, contributing historically to underdiagnosis. Autoimmune diseases such as lupus and multiple sclerosis are substantially more common in women, while men have higher rates of Parkinson's disease and some cancers unrelated to reproductive organs. Osteoporosis is more prevalent in postmenopausal women, and migraine is roughly three times as common in women as in men. Drug metabolism also differs — differences in body composition, gastric enzymes and cytochrome P450 activity mean some medicines, such as the sleep drug zolpidem, require lower recommended doses in women.

Research practice

For much of the twentieth century, clinical trials predominantly enrolled men, and results were generalised to women. Policy changes since the 1990s — including the US NIH Revitalization Act of 1993 and later requirements to consider sex as a biological variable in preclinical research — have pushed for inclusion of female participants and sex-disaggregated analysis. Researchers distinguish sex-based effects from gender-related social factors, such as occupational exposures and health-care seeking behaviour, which also shape outcomes.
