Haemophilus influenzae (formerly called Pfeiffer's bacillus) is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic coccobacillary bacterium of the family Pasteurellaceae. It was first described in 1892 by Richard Pfeiffer during an influenza pandemic and was mistakenly believed to be the cause of influenza — hence its name — until the viral aetiology of flu was established in 1933.

Biology

The organism requires two growth factors in culture, known as X factor (hemin) and V factor (NAD), and grows well on chocolate agar. Encapsulated strains are classified into serotypes a–f based on their capsular polysaccharide; non-encapsulated strains are called nontypable. H. influenzae was the first free-living organism to have its entire genome sequenced (1995), a landmark of the genomics era led by J. Craig Venter's team.

Disease

Before vaccination, serotype b (Hib) was a leading cause of bacterial meningitis and epiglottitis in young children, as well as pneumonia, septic arthritis and septicaemia. Introduction of the Hib conjugate vaccine from the late 1980s virtually eliminated invasive Hib disease in countries with high coverage. Nontypable strains remain common causes of otitis media, sinusitis and exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Treatment relies on antibiotics such as amoxicillin or cephalosporins, though beta-lactamase production and other resistance mechanisms are widespread.
