Yett

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A yett (from the Scots word for "gate") is a gate or grille of latticed iron bars, used for defensive purposes in castles and tower houses in Scotland. Unlike a portcullis, which is raised and lowered vertically, a yett is hinged like an ordinary door and is typically hung on the inner side of the entrance passage, immediately behind the wooden outer door.

Description

Yetts are constructed of interlocking iron bars, riveted together in a lattice pattern without welding, so that cutting a single bar does not compromise the whole grille. The bars pass alternately over and under one another, creating a surface that is extremely laborious to saw through. When the outer wooden door was burned or battered down by attackers, the iron yett behind it still barred the entrance while allowing defenders to see, shoot, or thrust spears through the openings.

History

Yetts were a characteristic feature of Scottish fortified houses from the 14th to the 17th centuries. Scottish legislation of 1456 required landowners building tower houses to furnish them with an iron yett, and many surviving examples remain in place at castles and peel towers across the country. Well-known surviving yetts can be seen at properties such as Smailholm Tower and other Borders strongholds. The word is cognate with the Old English geat, "gate".
