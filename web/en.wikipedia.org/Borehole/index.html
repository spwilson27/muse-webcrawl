Borehole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A borehole is a narrow shaft bored in the ground, either vertically, horizontally, or inclined. Boreholes are constructed for many different purposes, including the extraction of water, oil, or natural gas; the exploration of minerals; geotechnical and environmental site assessment; geothermal installations; and scientific research. The term is usually reserved for relatively narrow, deep holes, distinguishing them from wider excavations such as shafts.

Drilling methods

Boreholes are drilled by rotary drilling — in which a rotating bit cuts the rock while drilling fluid ("mud") circulates cuttings to the surface — by cable-tool percussion drilling, by augers in soft ground, and by diamond core drilling, which recovers an intact cylinder of rock for study. Directional drilling techniques allow boreholes to deviate from vertical, reaching targets kilometres away underground.

Applications

  • Water wells — tapping aquifers for drinking water and irrigation; hand-pumped boreholes are a major source of clean water in the developing world.
  • Oil and gas wells — exploration and production wells, typically cased with steel and cemented.
  • Geotechnical investigation — sampling soils and rock before construction of buildings, dams, and tunnels.
  • Monitoring wells — measuring groundwater levels and quality, including contamination plumes.
  • Scientific drilling — ice cores, ocean drilling, and deep continental holes.

Notable boreholes

The deepest artificial hole on Earth is the Kola Superdeep Borehole in Russia, which reached 12,262 metres (40,230 ft) in 1989 before being halted by unexpectedly high temperatures. Modern oil and gas wells, measured by total drilled length including horizontal sections, can exceed this in length though not in true vertical depth.
