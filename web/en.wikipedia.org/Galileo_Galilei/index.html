Galileo di Vincenzo Bonaiuti de' Galilei (15 February 1564 – 8 January 1642), commonly referred to as Galileo Galilei or simply Galileo, was an Italian astronomer, physicist and engineer, sometimes described as a polymath. He is considered a central figure in the transition from natural philosophy to modern science and in the Scientific Revolution.

Galileo improved the telescope and used it for observations that transformed astronomy: the four largest moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, mountains on the Moon, sunspots, and the multitude of stars in the Milky Way, published in Sidereus Nuncius (1610). He studied motion experimentally, formulating the law of falling bodies, the parabolic trajectory of projectiles, and an early principle of inertia.

Conflict over heliocentrism

Galileo's support for Copernican heliocentrism brought him into conflict with the Catholic Church. In 1616 the Inquisition declared heliocentrism heretical and Galileo was ordered not to hold or defend it. His Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (1632), apparently mocking the Pope's arguments in the character Simplicio, led to his trial in 1633; he was found "vehemently suspect of heresy", forced to recant, and spent the rest of his life under house arrest at Arcetri. Under confinement he wrote Two New Sciences (1638), his most rigorous work on mechanics and materials.

Scientific method and legacy

Galileo insisted on mathematical description and experiment over scholastic argument — famously that the book of nature is written in the language of mathematics. Albert Einstein called him the father of modern science. In 1992, following a commission of review, Pope John Paul II expressed regret for how the Galileo affair was handled. His name is honoured in the galileo unit of acceleration, NASA's Galileo spacecraft to Jupiter, and Europe's Galileo satellite navigation system.
