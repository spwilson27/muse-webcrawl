Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc.

Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc., 573 U.S. 682 (2014), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that closely held for-profit corporations are exempt from a federal regulation requiring employer-provided health insurance to cover contraceptives, where the owners religiously object, under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA).

Background

The Hobby Lobby craft-store chain, owned by the Green family, challenged the contraceptive mandate under the Affordable Care Act, objecting to certain contraceptive methods they believed could act as abortifacients. The case was consolidated with a similar challenge by Conestoga Wood Specialties, a Mennonite-owned company.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision written by Justice Samuel Alito, the Court held that RFRA's protections extend to closely held corporations, that the mandate substantially burdened the owners' religious exercise, and that the government had a less restrictive means available (the accommodation already offered to religious nonprofits). Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg's dissent warned of the ruling's expansive implications for employees' rights.

Significance

The decision was the first to recognise religious rights for for-profit corporations under RFRA and remains a central case in debates over religious liberty, corporate personhood, and reproductive healthcare in the United States.
