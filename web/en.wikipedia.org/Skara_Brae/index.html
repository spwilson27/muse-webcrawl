Skara Brae

LocationBay of Skaill, Mainland, Orkney, Scotland
PeriodNeolithic, occupied c. 3180–2500 BC
Discovered1850, after a storm removed covering sand
StatusUNESCO World Heritage Site (Heart of Neolithic Orkney)

Skara Brae is a stone-built Neolithic settlement on the Bay of Skaill in Orkney, Scotland. Older than Stonehenge and the Great Pyramids of Giza, it is one of the best-preserved prehistoric villages in Europe and is often called the "Scottish Pompeii" because of the remarkable state of its remains.

Discovery

The settlement was buried under sand dunes for millennia. In the winter of 1850, a severe storm stripped away the sand and grass, revealing the outlines of stone buildings. The local laird, William Watt, began excavating, and four houses were uncovered before work stopped; full scientific excavation came in the 1920s and 1930s under Vere Gordon Childe.

The houses

Eight houses survive, connected by covered stone passages. Because timber was scarce, everything — walls, roofs' supports, beds, shelves, seats and storage boxes — was built of stone, and much of the furniture survives in place: box beds, stone dressers, hearths and even primitive indoor drainage. The houses were sunk into protective middens of household waste, which insulated them against the Orkney weather.

Life and abandonment

The inhabitants farmed barley and wheat, kept cattle, sheep and pigs, and fished and gathered shellfish. Finds include pottery, bone tools, jewellery and carved stone balls of unknown purpose. The village was occupied for about 600 years before being abandoned around 2500 BC, possibly as the climate worsened; there is no evidence of a sudden catastrophe.
