Comfort women

Comfort women were women and girls forced into sexual slavery by the Imperial Japanese Armed Forces in occupied countries and territories before and during World War II. The system of 'comfort stations' operated from the early 1930s until 1945 across Asia and the Pacific.

History

Victims came primarily from Korea, China, and Japan, and also from the Philippines, Indonesia, Vietnam, and other occupied areas, as well as Dutch women in the East Indies. Estimates of numbers vary widely. Many women were deceived by false job offers or abducted, and endured severe violence and disease; many died or were killed, and survivors suffered lifelong trauma and stigma.

Postwar recognition

The issue became public in Japan and Korea in the early 1990s when survivors, notably Kim Hak-sun, testified publicly. The Japanese government's 1993 Kono Statement acknowledged military involvement and coercion, and the Asian Women's Fund (1995) offered atonement payments. A 2015 Japan–South Korea agreement proved controversial and was later effectively repudiated by Seoul. Memorials such as the Statue of Peace stand in Korea and worldwide.

Historiography

Scholars and international bodies, including UN reports, describe the system as sexual slavery and a war crime; debates in Japan over terminology and responsibility continue to affect regional diplomacy.
