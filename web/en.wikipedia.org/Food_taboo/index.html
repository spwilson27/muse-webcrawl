Food taboo

Many societies prohibit particular foods for religious, ethical, or customary reasons.

A food taboo is a prohibition against the consumption of certain foods or beverages, usually grounded in religion, culture, ethics, or custom. Food taboos are found in nearly every society, past and present. Well-known examples include the prohibition of pork in Judaism and Islam, the avoidance of beef among many Hindus, and restrictions on meat during Lent in some Christian traditions. Other taboos — such as those against eating horses, dogs, or insects — vary widely between cultures, so that a prized delicacy in one society may be taboo in another.

Religious prohibitions

Judaism's dietary laws, kashrut, forbid pork and shellfish, require animals to have cloven hooves and chew the cud, and prohibit consuming blood and mixing meat with dairy. Islam's halal rules likewise prohibit pork and blood and require animals to be slaughtered in a prescribed manner; alcohol is also forbidden. In Hinduism, cattle are venerated and beef is widely avoided, while many Hindus, Jains, and some Buddhists practise vegetarianism; Jain rules additionally avoid root vegetables whose harvest kills the plant. Seventh-day Adventists and some other groups also observe dietary restrictions derived from biblical law.

Cultural and regional taboos

Beyond formal religion, custom shapes strong aversions. Horse meat is legally sold and eaten in parts of Europe and Asia but is taboo in much of the English-speaking world. Dog meat, consumed historically in parts of East and Southeast Asia and elsewhere, is widely taboo and increasingly restricted. Insects are staple protein in many regions but provoke disgust in much of the West. Cannibalism is a near-universal taboo, with exceptions historically reported in ritual contexts.

Explanations

Anthropologists have proposed several explanations for food taboos. Religious explanations treat them as divine commands or marks of group identity. Ecological and materialist explanations, notably those of Marvin Harris, argue that taboos often encode practical adaptations — for example, that pigs were costly to raise in the arid Middle East, or that protecting cattle preserved draught power, milk, and dung in India. Structuralist accounts, such as Mary Douglas's analysis of Leviticus, interpret taboos as enforcing symbolic categories of purity and order. Taboos may also protect health, conserve resources, or reinforce social boundaries, and pregnancy-related food taboos documented in many cultures have been studied for both their protective and harmful effects on nutrition.
