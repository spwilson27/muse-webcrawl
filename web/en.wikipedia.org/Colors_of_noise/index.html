Colors of noise

In audio engineering, electronics and physics, colors of noise are informal names for random signals distinguished by their power spectral density — how their power is distributed over frequency. Each "color" is named by analogy with the color of light with a similar spectrum.

Principal colors

White noise has equal power per unit frequency (constant spectral density), like white light containing all wavelengths; it sounds like a bright hiss. Pink noise (1/f noise) has power inversely proportional to frequency, giving equal energy per octave; it sounds balanced and occurs widely in nature and electronics. Red noise, also called Brownian noise, has power proportional to 1/f² and is dominated by low frequencies, sounding like a deep rumble; it is the integral of white noise. Blue noise rises with frequency (power ∝ f), and violet noise rises as f², being the derivative of white noise.

Other colors

Grey noise is shaped to sound equally loud at all frequencies, following an equal-loudness contour. Green noise emphasises mid-frequencies around 500 Hz and is sometimes used to simulate natural ambience. Black noise is used loosely for silence or for spectra falling faster than 1/f². In image processing, "blue noise" also denotes dithering patterns with minimal low-frequency structure.

Applications

White and pink noise are used to test loudspeakers and room acoustics (pink noise has equal energy per octave, matching analyser bands), to mask distracting sounds, and in sound machines for sleep. 1/f noise is a fundamental phenomenon studied in electronics, where it appears as flicker noise in devices.
