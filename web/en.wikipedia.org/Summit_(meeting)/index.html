Summit (meeting)

A summit meeting (or just summit) is an international meeting of heads of state or government, usually with considerable media exposure, tight security, and a prearranged agenda. Notable summit meetings include Cold War meetings between the United States and the Soviet Union, meetings of the Group of Seven (G7) and Group of Twenty (G20), and the conferences of the European Council.

History

Summit diplomacy in its modern form is often traced to the conferences of the Second World War, such as the meetings at Yalta and Potsdam, where Allied leaders settled questions of the postwar order face to face. During the Cold War, summits between American presidents and Soviet leaders — Geneva (1955, 1985), Camp David (1959), Moscow (1972), and Reykjavík (1986) — became central instruments for managing arms control and superpower tensions.

Characteristics

Summits are typically preceded by months of preparation by officials known in G7/G8 contexts as "sherpas", who negotiate the agenda and draft communiqués in advance. Because leaders meet in person, summits can break deadlocks that lower-level diplomacy cannot, but they also carry the risk of public failure and tend to compress complex issues into personal chemistry. Agreements announced at summits range from detailed treaties to non-binding declarations.

Regular summit series

Many summits recur on fixed schedules. The G7 summit brings together leaders of major advanced economies annually; the G20 summit covers a broader membership including major emerging economies. The European Council meets at least four times a year. Regional bodies such as ASEAN, the African Union and APEC hold regular leaders' summits, and the United Nations convenes special summits on topics such as climate change, where the annual COP conferences function in part as summit meetings.

Criticism

Critics argue that summits can be heavy on ceremony and light on deliverables, that their communiqués often restate prior positions, and that the large security operations and protests surrounding them are costly. Supporters respond that regular personal contact among leaders builds trust and that summits set strategic direction for the bureaucracies that follow.
