Giuseppe Garibaldi (1807–1882) was an Italian general, revolutionary and republican who played a central role in the unification of Italy (the Risorgimento). Nicknamed the "Hero of the Two Worlds" for his military campaigns in both South America and Europe, he is regarded as one of Italy's founding fathers, alongside Count Cavour, King Victor Emmanuel II and Giuseppe Mazzini.

Early life and exile

Born in Nice, then part of the Kingdom of Sardinia, Garibaldi went to sea as a merchant sailor and was drawn into Mazzini's Young Italy movement. Condemned to death for his part in a failed mutiny in Genoa in 1834, he fled to South America, where he fought for the breakaway Riograndense Republic in Brazil and for Uruguay in its civil war, leading the Italian Legion — the origin of his famous red-shirted volunteers. His Brazilian wife Anita became a legendary companion in arms.

Italian campaigns

Returning to Italy in 1848, Garibaldi defended the Roman Republic against French forces in 1849, and in 1859 led the Hunters of the Alps for Piedmont-Sardinia against Austria. In 1860 he launched the Expedition of the Thousand: with about a thousand redshirt volunteers he sailed from Genoa, conquered Sicily and Naples from the Bourbons, and then handed the south to Victor Emmanuel II, enabling the proclamation of the Kingdom of Italy in 1861. Later campaigns to take Rome — halted by Piedmontese troops at Aspromonte in 1862 and by the French at Mentana in 1867 — ended when Rome was annexed in 1870.

Later life and legacy

Garibaldi retired largely to his island home of Caprera, wrote novels and memoirs, and in 1870–71 briefly fought for France against Prussia. A worldwide celebrity in his lifetime — admired from Lincoln (who offered him a command) to the British public — he remains a symbol of nationalist liberation movements, commemorated in countless statues, the Garibaldi biscuit, and even a fish named in his honour.
