Torse (heraldry)

In heraldry, a torse or wreath is a twisted roll of fabric laid about the top of the helmet and the base of the crest. It is a standard element of a full heraldic achievement, appearing between the helmet and the crest and serving to hold the crest in place and to conceal the join between them, much as the mantling conceals the back of the helmet.

Description and blazon

The torse is conventionally depicted as a rope-like twist of two tinctures — normally the principal metal and the principal colour of the arms, the same tinctures used for the mantling — with six twists or "knots" visible in the conventional rendering. It is blazoned, for example, as "On a wreath of the colours [or, of the liveries] …" followed by the description of the crest. In Scottish heraldry, the torse may be blazoned "of the liveries" and special forms are used by chiefs and peers; in some traditions a cap of maintenance (chapeau) is used instead of, or beneath, the torse.

Origin

The torse is generally understood to derive from the twisted band of cloth — sometimes explained as a favour, such as a lady's scarf — worn around the helmet by crusaders and tournament knights to help hold the crest and mantling in place and to reduce the heating of the steel helm by the sun. As heraldic art became codified, the practical band was conventionalised into the twisted wreath seen in modern achievements.

Variations

While the standard torse consists of alternating twists of one metal and one colour, variant torses appear in civic and continental heraldry with three tinctures or with the tinctures of a national flag. In English heraldry the torse is an almost invariable accompaniment to a crest; where no crest is granted, no torse is shown. The torse also appears on its own as a badge-like device in some families' rebus and crest traditions.
