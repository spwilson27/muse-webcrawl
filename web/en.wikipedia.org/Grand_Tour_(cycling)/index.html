The Grand Tours are the three most prestigious multi-week stage races in professional road cycling: the Tour de France, the Giro d'Italia, and the Vuelta a España. Each lasts three weeks, comprises 21 stages held over 23 days (with two rest days), and covers roughly 3,500 kilometres. They are the only stage races permitted to exceed two weeks under UCI regulations.

The races

The Tour de France, first held in 1903, is the oldest and most prestigious; its leader wears the yellow jersey (maillot jaune). The Giro d'Italia, founded in 1909, is held in May and awards the pink jersey (maglia rosa), reflecting the pink paper of the organising newspaper La Gazzetta dello Sport. The Vuelta a España, first run in 1935, moved to late summer in 1995; its leader wears the red jersey (la roja).

Classifications

Each race crowns an overall winner on cumulative time (general classification) and maintains subsidiary competitions: a points classification for sprinters (green jersey in the Tour, ciclamino in the Giro), a mountains classification (polka-dot jersey in the Tour, blue in the Giro), and a young rider classification (white jersey). Winning all three Grand Tours in a career is a rare feat; only a handful of riders—including Jacques Anquetil, Eddy Merckx, Bernard Hinault, and, later, Vincenzo Nibali and Chris Froome—have done so. Winning all three in a single calendar year has never been achieved.

History

The races were created to promote newspapers and became defining spectacles of European sport. Merckx, Hinault, and Miguel Indurain each won five Tours; Indurain also won the Giro–Tour double in consecutive years (1992–93), a double long considered among the hardest achievements in endurance sport.
