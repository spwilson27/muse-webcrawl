The Sargasso Sea is a region of the North Atlantic Ocean bounded entirely by ocean currents rather than land — the only sea in the world with no coastline. It is encircled by the Gulf Stream to the west, the North Atlantic Current to the north, the Canary Current to the east and the North Atlantic Equatorial Current to the south, forming the North Atlantic Gyre. Portuguese sailors named it for the floating golden-brown seaweed Sargassum that accumulates within its calm, clear, deep-blue waters.

Ecology

Mats of Sargassum natans and S. fluitans support a distinctive community of crabs, shrimp, fish and endemic species such as the sargassum fish, and provide nursery habitat for juvenile sea turtles, tuna and mahi-mahi. The Sargasso Sea is the spawning ground of the European and American eels, whose larvae drift for months on the surrounding currents to reach continental rivers — a migration puzzle solved only in the 20th century. Bermuda sits within its western portion.

History and lore

Columbus's fleet crossed the seaweed-strewn, windless waters in 1492, fuelling fears among his crews of being becalmed. The region later acquired a sinister literary reputation for trapping sailing ships, an exaggeration distinct from — but sometimes conflated with — the Bermuda Triangle legend to its south-west.

Environmental concerns

Because the gyre circulates slowly, floating debris concentrates in the Sargasso Sea; plastic pollution and microplastics are a growing concern, and since 2011 unusually large quantities of Sargassum have periodically washed ashore across the Caribbean (the "Great Atlantic Sargassum Belt"). The Sargasso Sea Commission, established under the 2014 Hamilton Declaration, promotes conservation of the area as high-seas habitat of international significance.
