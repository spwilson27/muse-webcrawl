Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary

Age: 66 million years ago
Former name: Cretaceous–Tertiary (K–T) boundary
Marker: Global iridium-rich clay layer

The Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) boundary, formerly called the Cretaceous–Tertiary (K–T) boundary, is a geological signature, usually a thin band of rock, dated to about 66 million years ago. It marks the end of the Mesozoic Era and the beginning of the Cenozoic, and coincides with one of the largest mass extinctions in Earth's history, including the disappearance of all non-avian dinosaurs.

The iridium layer

In 1980, a team led by Luis and Walter Alvarez reported that boundary clays at Gubbio, Italy, and worldwide contain anomalously high concentrations of iridium, an element rare in crustal rocks but common in asteroids. They proposed that a roughly 10 km asteroid struck the Earth, causing the extinction. The hypothesis was dramatically confirmed by the identification of the buried Chicxulub crater on Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, with shocked quartz, tsunami deposits, and tektites found at boundary sites around the Gulf of Mexico.

The extinction event

The K–Pg extinction eliminated an estimated 75% of species, including ammonites, mosasaurs, pterosaurs, and all dinosaurs except birds. Debate continues over the relative contribution of the impact versus the massive Deccan Traps volcanism in India, which spanned the boundary interval; the mainstream view assigns the impact the decisive role. Clay layers marking the boundary are exposed at classic sites such as Stevns Klint in Denmark (a UNESCO World Heritage Site), the Hell Creek region of Montana, and the Raton Basin of New Mexico and Colorado.
