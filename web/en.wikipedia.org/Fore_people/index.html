Fore people

The Fore people are an Indigenous group of the Okapa District in the Eastern Highlands Province of Papua New Guinea, numbering around 20,000. They speak the Fore language and traditionally practise subsistence agriculture centred on sweet potatoes, supplemented by hunting. The Fore became internationally known through the investigation of kuru, a fatal brain disease that reached epidemic proportions among them in the mid-twentieth century.

Kuru

Kuru caused trembling, loss of coordination and death, affecting women and children far more than men. Research by D. Carleton Gajdusek and others linked the disease to funerary endocannibalism: Fore mourners traditionally consumed the bodies of deceased relatives, with women and children receiving the brain, where the infectious agent was concentrated. When the practice ceased in the 1950s and 1960s, cases declined, though long incubation periods meant deaths continued for decades. Kuru was identified as a prion disease, work for which Gajdusek shared the 1976 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Genetic legacy

Studies of Fore survivors found selection for a variant of the prion protein gene that confers resistance to prion diseases, providing evidence of recent human evolution driven by the epidemic, and parallels have been drawn with the global distribution of similar protective variants.
