The Arctic Council is a high-level intergovernmental forum that addresses issues faced by the Arctic governments and the indigenous people of the Arctic region. At present, eight countries exercise sovereignty over the lands within the Arctic Circle: Canada, Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Norway, Russia, Sweden and the United States. These constitute the member states of the Council.

History

The Council was formally established with the Ottawa Declaration of 1996 as a forum for promoting cooperation, coordination and interaction among the Arctic states, with the involvement of Arctic indigenous communities, particularly on issues of sustainable development and environmental protection. It followed the Arctic Environmental Protection Strategy of 1991. Cooperation was largely suspended in 2022 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine, with limited project work later resuming without Russian participation for a time.

Structure

A distinctive feature of the Council is the category of Permanent Participants — six indigenous peoples' organisations, including the Inuit Circumpolar Council, the Saami Council and the Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North — which have full consultation rights. Six working groups (including AMAP, CAFF and PAME) carry out scientific and policy work. The chairmanship rotates every two years among the member states. Numerous non-Arctic states and organisations, including China, India and the European Union (as an ad hoc observer), hold observer status.

The Council has negotiated three legally binding agreements among the eight Arctic states: on search and rescue (2011), marine oil pollution preparedness and response (2013), and enhancing international Arctic scientific cooperation (2017).
