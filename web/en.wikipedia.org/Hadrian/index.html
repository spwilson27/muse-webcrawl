Hadrian (Publius Aelius Hadrianus Augustus; 24 January 76 – 10 July 138) was Roman emperor from 117 to 138. He is known for his extensive travels throughout the empire, his consolidation of Rome's frontiers — most famously Hadrian's Wall in northern Britain — and his patronage of architecture, including the rebuilding of the Pantheon in Rome. He is counted among the "Five Good Emperors".

Early life and accession

Hadrian was born to a Hispano-Roman family, probably at Italica in Hispania Baetica. His father was a cousin of the emperor Trajan, who became his guardian. After a career in the senate and army, Hadrian was adopted by Trajan on his deathbed — an adoption announced by the empress Plotina — and succeeded him in 117.

Reign

Hadrian abandoned Trajan's conquests in Mesopotamia and focused on defensible borders, touring nearly every province during two great journeys (121–125 and 128–132). He founded cities, rebuilt temples, and standardised imperial administration. His relationship with the Greek youth Antinous, whose drowning in the Nile in 130 devastated him, led to Antinous's deification and the founding of Antinoöpolis. In Judaea, the suppression of the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135) was brutal and reshaped the province.

Death and succession

Hadrian adopted Antoninus Pius as his successor, conditional on Antoninus adopting Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus. He died at Baiae in 138 and was buried in his mausoleum, now Castel Sant'Angelo.
