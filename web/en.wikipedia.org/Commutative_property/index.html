Commutative property

The commutative property is a property generally associated with binary operations: an operation is commutative if changing the order of the operands does not change the result. It is a fundamental property of many mathematical operations, and many proofs depend on it.

x + y = y + x   and   x × y = y × x

Addition and multiplication of real numbers are commutative: for example, 2 + 3 = 3 + 2 = 5 and 2 × 3 = 3 × 2 = 6. Subtraction and division are not: 5 − 3 ≠ 3 − 5 and 6 ÷ 3 ≠ 3 ÷ 6.

Examples and non-examples

Other commutative operations include addition of vectors and matrices, multiplication of matrices in restricted cases, the union and intersection of sets, and the logical connectives AND and OR. Function composition, matrix multiplication in general, string concatenation, and the cross product of vectors are not commutative. In everyday life, putting on socks and shoes is not commutative in outcome terms, whereas adding ingredients to a bowl usually is.

History and usage

The property was used implicitly from antiquity, but the term derives from the French commuter ("to exchange"), and "commutative" was applied to the law of addition and multiplication in the early nineteenth century, notably by François-Joseph Servois in 1814. In abstract algebra, structures whose main operation is commutative take special names: an abelian group is a group with a commutative operation, named after Niels Henrik Abel, and a commutative ring is a ring whose multiplication commutes. In quantum mechanics, operators that commute correspond to observables that can be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision.
