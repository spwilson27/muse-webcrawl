Charles's law

Charles's law (also known as the law of volumes) is an experimental gas law which states that, when the pressure on a sample of a dry gas is held constant, the volume of the gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. In mathematical form, V/T = constant, where V is volume and T is temperature measured in kelvins. Equivalently, V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ for two states of the same gas at the same pressure.

History

The law takes its name from the French scientist Jacques Charles, who formulated the relationship in unpublished work in the 1780s, building on his experiments with balloons. Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac published the relationship in 1802, crediting Charles, and it is therefore sometimes called the Charles–Gay-Lussac law. Extrapolating the linear relationship to zero volume led to the concept of absolute zero, estimated by extrapolation at about −273.15 °C.

Relation to the ideal gas law

Charles's law is one of the constituent gas laws, alongside Boyle's law and Gay-Lussac's law, that combine to give the ideal gas law, PV = nRT. In terms of kinetic theory, heating a gas at constant pressure increases the average kinetic energy of its molecules, so the gas expands to keep the pressure constant.

Applications

The law helps explain everyday phenomena such as the lifting power of hot-air balloons, why a balloon shrinks in cold weather, and the operation of some thermometers and engines.
