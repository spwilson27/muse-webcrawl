CHAdeMO

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

CHAdeMO is a DC fast-charging standard for electric vehicles, developed in Japan and first released in 2010. The name is an abbreviation of "CHArge de MOve" and a play on the Japanese phrase o-cha demo ikaga desu ka ("how about a cup of tea?"), suggesting a charge takes about as long as a tea break. It was the first widely deployed DC fast-charging system and is governed by the CHAdeMO Association, founded by TEPCO, Nissan, Mitsubishi, Fuji Heavy Industries (Subaru), and Toyota.

Technology

CHAdeMO uses a large round connector with separate power and signal pins, communicating with the vehicle over a CAN bus. Unlike later standards, it supported vehicle-to-grid (V2G) bidirectional power flow from early on. Version 2.0 (2018) raised capacity to 400 kW with liquid-cooled cables, and the harmonized ChaoJi standard, developed jointly with China, is designed for ultra-high-power charging and backward compatibility.

Adoption and decline

Early mass-market EVs such as the Nissan Leaf and Mitsubishi i-MiEV carried CHAdeMO ports, and tens of thousands of chargers were installed worldwide, especially in Japan and Europe. Outside Japan, however, most automakers adopted the Combined Charging System (CCS), and later the North American Charging Standard (NACS), leaving CHAdeMO in decline in Western markets while remaining common in Japan.
