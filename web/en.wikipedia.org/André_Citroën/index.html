André Citroën

André-Gustave Citroën (French: [ɑ̃dʁe ɡystav sitʁɔɛn]; 5 February 1878 – 3 July 1935) was a French industrialist and the founder of the French automobile manufacturer Citroën. Remembered as one of the great pioneers of mass production in Europe and a master of publicity, he introduced American-style assembly-line methods to France, built a vast dealer and service network, and lit his name across the Eiffel Tower in one of the most famous advertising stunts of the twentieth century.

Early life and education

Citroën was born in Paris, the fifth child of Levie Citroen, a Dutch Jewish diamond merchant who had taken the French spelling Citroën. His father died by suicide when André was six. A brilliant student fascinated by the works of Jules Verne and the construction of the Eiffel Tower, he graduated from the École Polytechnique in 1900 and served in the artillery. On a visit to relatives in Poland he saw a workshop using herringbone (chevron) gears, bought the patent, and in 1905 founded a gear-cutting firm whose double-helical gears later inspired the Citroën double-chevron logo.

Career

Citroën reorganised the Mors automobile firm from 1908, raising its output tenfold. During the First World War he built a vast munitions plant on the Quai de Javel in Paris that produced millions of shells, and after the armistice converted it to automobile production. The first Citroën car, the Type A 10 HP, was launched in 1919 — the first mass-produced car built in Europe with an assembly line, sold ready-to-drive with electric starter and lighting. Models such as the B2, the Rosalie and the revolutionary front-wheel-drive Traction Avant (1934) followed. Citroën pioneered instalment buying, road signs, sponsored expeditions such as the Croisière Noire across Africa and the Croisière Jaune across Asia, and from 1925 to 1934 spelled out his name in lights on the Eiffel Tower.

Downfall and death

The immense cost of developing the Traction Avant, combined with the Depression, bankrupted the company in 1934; the French government arranged its takeover by Michelin, its largest creditor. Citroën lost his fortune and his company, and died of stomach cancer in Paris on 3 July 1935. He is buried in Montparnasse Cemetery. A Paris park, the Parc André-Citroën, now occupies the site of his Javel factory.
