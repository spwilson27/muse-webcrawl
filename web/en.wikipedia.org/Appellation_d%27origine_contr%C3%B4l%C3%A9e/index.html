Appellation d'origine contrôlée

The appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC; French pronunciation: [apɛlasjɔ̃ dɔʁiʒin kɔ̃tʁole]; "controlled designation of origin") is a label that identifies an agricultural product whose stages of production and processing are carried out in a defined geographical area — the terroir — and using recognized and traditional know-how. It is a French standard for, among other products, wines, cheeses, butter, honey, and oysters, and it forms the basis of the European Union's protected designation of origin (PDO) system.

History

The origins of the AOC date to the 15th century, when Roquefort cheese was regulated by a parliamentary decree. Modern French law on designations of origin began with the law of May 6, 1919, protecting the place of origin of products. In 1935, the Comité National des Appellations d'Origine (now the Institut national de l'origine et de la qualité, INAO) was created to regulate the wine industry, driven by the efforts of Baron Pierre Le Roy of Château Fortia in Châteauneuf-du-Pape. The AOC designation was formalized in law on July 30, 1935.

Rules and scope

Each AOC product is governed by a decree specifying the geographical area, permitted varieties or breeds, production methods, yields, and quality standards. Products meeting the standard may carry the AOC label, and it is illegal to manufacture or sell a product under an AOC name if it does not meet the criteria. Well-known examples include Champagne, Cognac, Roquefort, Comté, and Camembert de Normandie.

International influence

The AOC system inspired analogous schemes worldwide, including Italy's DOC/DOCG, Spain's denominación de origen, and the EU-wide PDO and PGI frameworks. Designation-of-origin disputes remain a recurring topic in international trade negotiations.
