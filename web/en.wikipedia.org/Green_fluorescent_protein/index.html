Green fluorescent protein

The green fluorescent protein (GFP) is a protein consisting of 238 amino acid residues (26.9 kDa) that exhibits bright green fluorescence when exposed to light in the blue to ultraviolet range. It was first isolated from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria by Osamu Shimomura in the 1960s, where it occurs alongside the bioluminescent protein aequorin. The protein's chromophore forms spontaneously from its own amino acids, requiring only oxygen, which makes GFP uniquely useful as a genetically encoded marker.

Use in science

In 1994 Martin Chalfie's laboratory showed that the GFP gene could be expressed in other organisms, and Roger Tsien engineered improved and differently coloured variants. By fusing the GFP gene to a gene of interest, researchers can watch where and when that gene's protein is produced inside living cells and organisms. GFP and its derivatives have become indispensable reporter genes in cell biology, developmental biology and medicine. Shimomura, Chalfie and Tsien shared the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery and development of GFP.
