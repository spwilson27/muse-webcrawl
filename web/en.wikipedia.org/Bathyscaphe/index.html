Bathyscaphe

A bathyscaphe is a free-diving, self-propelled deep-sea submersible consisting of a crew cabin (a heavy pressure sphere) suspended beneath a large float filled with gasoline, which provides buoyancy because it is lighter than water and nearly incompressible. The design was developed by the Swiss scientist Auguste Piccard, adapting principles from his stratospheric balloon work.

Design

Unlike a submarine, a bathyscaphe cannot control its buoyancy with compressed air tanks at extreme depth. Instead it descends by venting air from ballast tanks and ascends by releasing iron pellet ballast held by electromagnets; if power fails, the pellets drop automatically and the craft rises. Batteries power electric propellers for limited horizontal movement.

Notable craft

The first bathyscaphe, FNRS-2, dove in 1948. Its successor FNRS-3 reached 4,050 metres in 1954. The most famous, Trieste, carried Jacques Piccard and U.S. Navy Lieutenant Don Walsh to the bottom of the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench on 23 January 1960, reaching about 10,900 metres — the deepest crewed descent of its era. The French bathyscaphe Archimède also made many deep scientific dives.

Legacy

Bathyscaphes proved that humans could reach the deepest parts of the ocean, but their poor manoeuvrability led to their replacement by more agile deep-submergence vehicles such as Alvin and by remotely operated vehicles.
