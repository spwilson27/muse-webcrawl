Paramedic

A paramedic is a healthcare professional who works mainly in emergency medical services (EMS), providing advanced pre-hospital assessment and treatment of patients with acute illness or injury, usually in ambulances and other out-of-hospital settings.

Role and skills

Paramedics perform advanced life support: airway management including endotracheal intubation, intravenous and intraosseous access, cardiac monitoring and defibrillation, 12-lead ECG interpretation, and administration of a range of emergency medications. They assess patients, make clinical decisions, and transport them to appropriate facilities, often working autonomously or in pairs with emergency medical technicians (EMTs).

History

The modern profession developed from battlefield medicine and from pioneering programmes of the 1960s–1970s, such as Freedom House Ambulance Service in Pittsburgh and the schemes led by physician Peter Safar and others, which demonstrated that trained lay providers could deliver hospital-level interventions before arrival. Television programmes such as Emergency! popularised the role in the United States.

Training and regulation

Requirements vary by country. In the United Kingdom, paramedics are registered health professionals, with entry via a university degree in paramedic science. In the United States, paramedic certification follows the National Registry of Emergency Medical Technicians pathway after roughly 1,200–1,800 hours of training. Many systems now employ paramedics in extended roles, including community paramedicine, urgent care and critical care retrieval.
