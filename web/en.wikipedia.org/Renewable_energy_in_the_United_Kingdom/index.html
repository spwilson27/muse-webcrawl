Renewable energy in the United Kingdom

Renewable energy in the United Kingdom has expanded rapidly since the early 2000s, driven by European and domestic targets, support schemes such as the Renewables Obligation and Contracts for Difference, and falling costs of wind and solar generation. Renewables grew from under 3 per cent of UK electricity in 2000 to around half of electricity generation in the early 2020s.

Wind power

Wind is the UK's largest renewable source. The country is a world leader in offshore wind, with large wind farms in the North Sea and Irish Sea — including Hornsea, among the world's largest — made possible by shallow waters, strong winds, and the Crown Estate leasing rounds. Onshore wind is concentrated in Scotland; for several years new onshore development in England was effectively halted by planning policy before reforms in the mid-2020s.

Solar, hydro, and biomass

Solar photovoltaics grew quickly under feed-in tariffs in the 2010s and now contribute a significant share, peaking at over a quarter of demand on sunny summer days. Hydroelectric power, developed mainly in the Scottish Highlands in the mid-twentieth century, is small but established, including pumped-storage stations such as Dinorwig in Wales. Biomass — notably the converted units at Drax power station burning imported wood pellets — is a substantial but debated source, and biogas and landfill gas also contribute.

Policy and targets

The Climate Change Act 2008 and the 2019 net-zero amendment committed the UK to eliminating net greenhouse-gas emissions by 2050. The government has targeted a decarbonised electricity system in the 2030s. Marine energy (tidal and wave) remains largely experimental despite the UK's strong tidal resource, exemplified by the MeyGen tidal array in the Pentland Firth.
