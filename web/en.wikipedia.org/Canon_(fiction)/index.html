Canon (fiction)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fiction, canon is the material accepted as officially part of the story in an individual universe of that story by its fan base. It is often contrasted with, or used as the basis for, works of fan fiction. The term is also used to describe the body of works considered authoritative within a fictional universe, as distinct from spin-offs, adaptations, or unofficial works.

Origins of the term

The use of "canon" in this sense derives from its application to religious scripture — the biblical canon — where it denotes the set of texts accepted as genuine and authoritative. The term was applied to fiction by analogy, notably in discussions of the Sherlock Holmes stories, where Ronald Knox playfully treated Arthur Conan Doyle's original works as scripture in 1911.

Canon and continuity

Canon is closely related to continuity, the consistency of characters, settings, and events across works. Franchise holders may issue official statements about what is canonical, sometimes reorganising earlier material through retroactive continuity ("retconning"). Debates over canon are common in large franchises such as Star Wars and Star Trek.
