Scopes trial

The Scopes trial, formally The State of Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes and commonly nicknamed the Scopes Monkey Trial, was a legal case held in July 1925 in Dayton, Tennessee. High-school teacher John T. Scopes was accused of violating Tennessee's Butler Act, which prohibited the teaching of human evolution in state-funded schools.

Background

The case was deliberately staged as a test case. The American Civil Liberties Union offered to defend anyone charged under the Butler Act, and civic leaders in Dayton, hoping to attract publicity and business to their town, persuaded Scopes to stand trial. Scopes, a substitute teacher, was not even certain he had ever taught evolution, but agreed to be prosecuted.

The trial

The prosecution was led in part by William Jennings Bryan, three-time Democratic presidential candidate and a leading fundamentalist spokesman. The defence included the celebrated trial lawyer Clarence Darrow. The proceedings drew hundreds of reporters and were among the first American trials broadcast live on radio. In the trial's most famous episode, Darrow called Bryan himself to the stand as an expert on the Bible and subjected him to a withering cross-examination about a literal reading of Genesis; the judge ultimately expunged the testimony. Because of the heat and the crowds, part of the trial was moved outdoors onto the courthouse lawn.

The jury deliberated for only a few minutes and convicted Scopes, who was fined $100. The defence had effectively invited the verdict in order to appeal. In 1927 the Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the Butler Act but overturned Scopes's conviction on a technicality — the judge, rather than the jury, had set the fine.

Aftermath and legacy

Bryan died in Dayton five days after the trial ended. The Butler Act remained on the books until 1967, and similar anti-evolution laws survived until the United States Supreme Court struck them down in Epperson v. Arkansas (1968). The trial became a lasting symbol of the conflict between science and religious fundamentalism in American public life, dramatised in the 1955 play and 1960 film Inherit the Wind, which used the case as an allegory for McCarthyism.
