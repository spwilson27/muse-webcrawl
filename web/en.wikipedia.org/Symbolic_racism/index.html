Symbolic racism

Survey-based research measures symbolic racism through agreement with statements about race and social policy.

Symbolic racism (also called modern racism or modern-symbolic racism) is a concept in social psychology describing a form of prejudice in the contemporary United States in which overt, "old-fashioned" racism — biological inferiority claims and support for formal segregation — has been largely replaced by a blend of anti-Black affect and traditional values such as individualism and the Protestant work ethic.

Theory

The concept was developed by David Sears, John McConahay, and colleagues in the 1970s–1980s. According to the theory, people scoring high in symbolic racism tend to believe that Black Americans violate cherished values — for example by demanding "special favours" or government help rather than working hard — and they express these beliefs abstractly and symbolically rather than through overt slurs. Scales measuring it include items about whether Black people are too demanding in their push for equal rights and whether discrimination remains a serious barrier.

Measurement and findings

Symbolic racism scores predict opposition to policies such as affirmative action, busing, and welfare spending, as well as voting behaviour, even after controlling for party identification and ideology, according to proponents. The scale is among the most widely used measures of racial attitudes in political science.

Criticism

Critics, including Christopher Tarman and David Sears's scholarly opponents such as Donald Sniderman and Paul Tetlock, argue the scale confounds racism with ordinary conservative values and non-racial policy preferences, and that its items may measure political ideology rather than prejudice. The debate over what the scale captures remains a major controversy in the study of public opinion.
