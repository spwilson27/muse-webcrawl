IPv4 address exhaustion

IPv4 address exhaustion is the depletion of the pool of unallocated IPv4 addresses. Because IPv4 uses 32-bit addresses, it can address at most about 4.3 billion devices, and growth of the Internet consumed that pool over several decades. The Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA) allocated its last free /8 blocks to the five regional Internet registries on 3 February 2011; the regional registries then exhausted their own free pools one by one — APNIC in 2011, RIPE NCC in 2012, LACNIC in 2014, ARIN in 2015, and AFRINIC later in the decade.

Mitigation

Several techniques delayed exhaustion: the transition from classful addressing to Classless Inter-Domain Routing (CIDR) in 1993, the reuse of private address space behind network address translation (NAT), and dynamic host configuration. Exhaustion created a transfer market in which IPv4 addresses are bought and sold, with prices per address rising substantially.

Long-term solution

The long-term solution is migration to IPv6, which uses 128-bit addresses and provides a vastly larger space. IPv6 deployment has grown steadily — a large share of users now reach major services over IPv6 — but IPv4 remains in widespread use through NAT and dual-stack configurations, and the two protocols are expected to coexist for many years.
