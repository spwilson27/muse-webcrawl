Brine (or briny water) is a high-concentration solution of salt (typically sodium chloride or calcium chloride) in water. In diverse contexts, brine may refer to salt solutions ranging from about 3.5% (a typical seawater concentration) up to about 26% (a typical saturated solution, depending on temperature). Brine forms naturally when seawater evaporates, in salt lakes, and in subsurface geological formations.

Properties

Dissolved salt lowers the freezing point of water, raises its boiling point, increases its density and electrical conductivity, and lowers its specific heat capacity. Concentrated brine remains liquid at temperatures well below 0 °C, which is why it is used for de-icing and refrigeration. Saturation for sodium chloride brine is about 26% by weight; beyond this, salt precipitates out.

Uses

Brining is a traditional method of food preservation and preparation: meats, fish, cheese and vegetables such as pickles and sauerkraut are soaked in brine to inhibit spoilage organisms and add flavour. Industrially, brine is the feedstock of the chloralkali process, producing chlorine, sodium hydroxide and hydrogen by electrolysis. It is also used as a heat-transfer and refrigeration fluid, in water softening and ion exchange regeneration, in drilling fluids, and in desalination — where disposing of the concentrated reject brine is an environmental concern.

Brine pools and natural brine

On the ocean floor, brine pools are depressions filled with extremely saline, oxygen-free water denser than seawater, toxic to most marine life. Subsurface brines are mined for lithium and other minerals, notably in the salt flats of South America.
