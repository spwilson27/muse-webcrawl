Sir Alec John Jeffreys, CH, FRS (born 9 January 1950) is a British geneticist who developed the techniques for genetic fingerprinting and DNA profiling, which revolutionised forensic science, paternity testing and immigration casework.

Discovery of DNA fingerprinting

Working at the University of Leicester, Jeffreys studied inherited variation in DNA. On 10 September 1984, examining an X-ray film image of DNA from different members of a technician's family, he noticed that regions of repetitive DNA — minisatellites — produced a pattern of bands unique to each individual yet shared in part between relatives. He immediately recognised the potential for identification: a "DNA fingerprint". His landmark paper appeared in Nature in 1985.

Applications

The technique's first forensic use, in 1986–87, exonerated a suspect and then identified Colin Pitchfork as the murderer of two Leicestershire schoolgirls — the first murder case solved by DNA evidence. DNA profiling (using fewer, more variable loci, and later PCR-based STR analysis) became a routine forensic tool, led to national DNA databases, and has been used to resolve paternity disputes, immigration cases, wildlife crime, identification of war victims and historical questions such as the fate of the Romanovs and of Josef Mengele.

Later work and honours

Jeffreys's later research used minisatellites to study mutation and recombination hotspots in the human genome. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1986, knighted in 1994, and received numerous prizes including the Royal Medal (2004) and the Copley Medal (2014). He retired from Leicester in 2012.
