Dosage compensation

Dosage compensation
FieldGenetics, developmental biology
Problem addressedUnequal sex-chromosome copy number between sexes
MechanismsX-inactivation (mammals); X upregulation (Drosophila); X downregulation (C. elegans)

Dosage compensation is the process by which organisms equalize the expression of genes between members of different biological sexes. In species with sex chromosomes — for example XX females and XY males — genes on the X chromosome would otherwise be present in two copies in one sex and one in the other, causing potentially harmful imbalances in gene product levels. Dosage compensation mechanisms have evolved independently in different lineages.

Mechanisms in model organisms

In placental mammals, females randomly inactivate one of their two X chromosomes early in development — the condensed, silent chromosome forms the Barr body — a process driven by the long non-coding RNA Xist and first hypothesized by Mary Lyon in 1961. In the fruit fly Drosophila, males instead upregulate transcription from their single X chromosome roughly twofold, via the MSL (male-specific lethal) ribonucleoprotein complex. In the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, hermaphrodites (XX) downregulate expression from both X chromosomes by about half using a dosage compensation complex related to condensin.
