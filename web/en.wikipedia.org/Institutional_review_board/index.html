Institutional review board


An institutional review board reviews research involving human subjects.

An institutional review board (IRB), also known as an independent ethics committee or research ethics board, is a committee that reviews and approves research involving human subjects to ensure that it is conducted ethically and that participants' rights and welfare are protected. IRBs are mandated in the United States under federal regulations — principally the Common Rule (45 CFR 46) and Food and Drug Administration rules — for research funded by the federal government or involving regulated products. Equivalent bodies exist in most countries, generally termed research ethics committees.

Function

Before a study begins, the IRB evaluates its protocol: whether risks to subjects are minimised and reasonable in relation to expected benefits, whether subject selection is equitable, and whether informed consent will be obtained and documented. Boards must include at least five members of varying backgrounds, including at least one scientist, one non-scientist and one person unaffiliated with the institution. Some minimal-risk research qualifies for exemption or expedited review; the board continues to monitor approved studies and can suspend or terminate them.

Background

The IRB system grew out of twentieth-century research scandals and codes of research ethics, from the Nuremberg Code (1947) and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) to the US Belmont Report (1979), which articulated the principles of respect for persons, beneficence and justice after revelations such as the Tuskegee syphilis study. Critics have debated whether IRB review, designed for biomedical research, is well suited to ethnography, oral history and other social-science methods, and whether it can chill legitimate inquiry.
