Butt joint

A butt joint is a woodworking joint in which the end of a piece of material is simply placed (butted) against another piece and joined, usually with glue, screws or nails. It is the simplest and weakest of joints, since the bond relies largely on glue on end grain, which absorbs adhesive and forms a poor connection.

Construction

Because of its weakness, the butt joint is usually reinforced when strength matters: common reinforcements include dowels, biscuits, screws driven at an angle (pocket holes), splines, corrugated fasteners, or metal angle plates. Butt joints are nevertheless common in cabinet face frames, box construction, framing for drywall, and other applications where loads are modest or other parts provide support.

Variants

Related forms include the mitred butt joint, in which both pieces are cut at matching angles, and the end-to-side butt joint used in carcass construction. In welding, the "butt joint" is also the basic configuration in which two plates lie in the same plane and are welded along their edges.
