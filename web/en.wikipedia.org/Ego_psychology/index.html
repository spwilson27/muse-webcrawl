Ego psychology

Freud's structural model: id, ego, and superego

Ego psychology is a school of psychoanalysis rooted in Sigmund Freud's structural id-ego-superego model of the mind. An individual interacts with the external world as well as responds to internal forces. Many psychoanalysts use a theoretical construct called the ego to explain how this is done through various ego functions. Adherents of ego psychology focus on the ego's normal and pathological development, its management of libidinal and aggressive impulses, and its adaptation to reality.

History

Sigmund Freud initially considered the ego to be a sense organ for perception of both external and internal stimuli. He considered the ego a group of functions that included defense, information synthesis, and memory. Freud's structural theory, set forth in The Ego and the Id (1923), furthered the understanding of the ego. After Freud, Heinz Hartmann became the first theorist to write more specifically about the healthy ego's adaptive functions. Hartmann proposed that ego development proceeds in a "conflict-free sphere" and emphasised the ego's autonomous functions, such as perception, motor skills, intention, and anticipation.

Other major contributors included Anna Freud, whose The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936) systematised the study of defence mechanisms, and David Rapaport, who sought to give ego psychology a systematic theoretical and empirical foundation. Erik Erikson extended the approach across the lifespan with his theory of psychosocial stages of development.

Key concepts

Ego psychology emphasises the ego's conflict-free sphere, the average expectable environment, adaptation, and reality testing. Defence mechanisms, as described by Anna Freud, are viewed not only as pathological but as normal, adaptive functions. Later figures such as Edith Jacobson and Margaret Mahler applied ego-psychological ideas to the development of self and object representations, laying groundwork for object relations theory.
