Intonation (linguistics)

In linguistics, intonation is the variation in pitch used when speaking — the rise and fall of the voice over phrases and sentences. Intonation is a central part of prosody, alongside stress, rhythm and tempo, and it carries meanings that words alone do not: it can signal questions, emphasis, emotion, attitude and the structure of conversation.

Functions

Intonation serves several overlapping functions. Its grammatical function helps distinguish sentence types — in English a final rise commonly marks yes–no questions while a fall marks statements — and marks clause boundaries. Its discourse function manages turn-taking, highlights new versus given information, and shows how utterances relate to each other. Its attitudinal function conveys feelings such as surprise, irony, doubt or enthusiasm, and its accentual function places prominence on important syllables and words.

Description

Analysts describe intonation with units such as the tone unit or intonation phrase, identifying a prominent nucleus (tonic syllable) bearing the main pitch movement — falls, rises, fall-rises and rise-falls in the British tradition of analysis. Autosegmental-metrical phonology instead treats intonation as sequences of high (H) and low (L) tones aligned with stressed syllables and phrase edges.

Variation across languages

All spoken languages use intonation, but its grammatical load differs. In tone languages such as Mandarin, pitch primarily distinguishes word meaning, yet intonation still operates over whole utterances. Languages differ in typical question intonation: some use final rises, others falls or particles instead. Regional varieties of English are recognisable by features such as the high rising terminal ("uptalk") associated with Australian, New Zealand and some North American speech.
