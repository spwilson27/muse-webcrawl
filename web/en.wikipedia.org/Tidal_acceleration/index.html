Tidal acceleration

Tidal acceleration is an effect of the tidal forces between an orbiting natural satellite (such as the Moon) and the planet it orbits (such as Earth). The acceleration causes a gradual transfer of angular momentum: the satellite slowly recedes from the planet, while the planet's rotation gradually slows. The process also works in reverse — known as tidal deceleration — when the satellite's orbital period is shorter than the planet's rotation period (as with Mars's moon Phobos) or when the orbit is retrograde (as with Neptune's moon Triton).

Earth–Moon system

The Moon raises tidal bulges in Earth's oceans. Because Earth rotates faster than the Moon orbits, friction drags the bulge slightly ahead of the line joining Earth and Moon. The bulge's gravity pulls the Moon forward in its orbit, increasing its orbital energy and pushing it outward, while the reaction torque on Earth slows Earth's rotation. Lunar laser ranging experiments using retroreflectors left by the Apollo missions measure the Moon's recession at about 3.8 centimetres per year, and the length of Earth's day is increasing by roughly 2.3 milliseconds per century from this cause.

Historical evidence

Records of ancient eclipses show that Earth's rotation has indeed slowed over thousands of years, and fossil evidence from corals and tidal rhythmites indicates that hundreds of millions of years ago the year contained more than 400 shorter days, with the Moon much closer. Early in its history the Moon was so close that tides were far stronger; in the remote future, if the system survived the Sun's evolution, Earth and Moon would become mutually tidally locked, as the Moon already is to Earth and as the Pluto–Charon pair already are to each other.
