Status quo bias

Status quo bias is an emotional bias in decision making: a preference for the current state of affairs, such that individuals tend to choose options that keep things the same, or to stick with a previously chosen option even when alternatives would be better. The bias was named and systematically studied by William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser in a 1988 paper.

Explanations

Proposed causes include loss aversion (losses relative to the status quo loom larger than gains), the mere-exposure effect, cognitive dissonance, and the cognitive costs of evaluating change. Related phenomena include the endowment effect, default effects (for example, opt-out versus opt-in organ donation and retirement savings enrolment), and omission bias.

Applications

Status quo bias has been documented in consumer choice, politics, health care decisions, and organizational inertia. Policymakers exploit default effects in "nudge" interventions — for instance, automatic enrolment in pension plans substantially raises participation compared with optional enrolment — while critics note the ethical questions raised by steering choices through defaults.
