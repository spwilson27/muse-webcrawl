Apollo 8 (December 21–27, 1968) was the first crewed spacecraft to leave low Earth orbit and reach the Moon. The crew — commander Frank Borman, command module pilot James Lovell, and lunar module pilot William Anders — orbited the Moon ten times without landing, becoming the first humans to see the far side of the Moon and to witness an Earthrise. The mission was the second crewed flight in the United States Apollo space program.

"Earthrise," photographed by William Anders on December 24, 1968

Background

Apollo 8 was originally planned as a test of the Lunar Module in Earth orbit, but delays in the module's development and intelligence suggesting the Soviet Union was preparing a crewed circumlunar flight led NASA to redesign the mission as a lunar orbital flight using only the Command and Service Module. The bold decision, made in August 1968, kept the program on schedule for a 1969 landing.

Mission

Launched atop the first crewed Saturn V, Apollo 8 reached the Moon on December 24, 1968. During a live television broadcast on Christmas Eve, the crew read from the Book of Genesis — the most-watched television broadcast of its time. Anders' color photograph of Earth rising over the lunar horizon, known as Earthrise, became one of the most influential environmental photographs ever taken. After ten orbits, the crew fired the engine to return home and splashed down in the Pacific on December 27, recovered by the USS Yorktown.

Legacy

The success of Apollo 8 paved the way for Apollo 11's landing seven months later. The mission's crew were named Time magazine's Men of the Year for 1968.
