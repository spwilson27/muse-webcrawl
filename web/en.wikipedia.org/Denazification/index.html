Denazification

Denazification (German: Entnazifizierung) was an Allied initiative to rid German and Austrian society, culture, press, economy, judiciary, and politics of the Nazi Party and of Nazi ideology following the Second World War. It was carried out by removing those who had been Nazi Party members from positions of power and influence, disbanding or rendering impotent the organizations associated with Nazism, and trying prominent Nazis for war crimes.

German civilians were required to complete questionnaires about their Nazi-era activities

Implementation

Each occupying power approached denazification differently. In the American zone, millions of adults completed a detailed questionnaire (Fragebogen), and German-run tribunals (Spruchkammern) classified people into five categories from "major offender" to "exonerated". The British and French zones were generally less thorough, while the Soviet zone combined purges with a broader transformation of property and institutions.

Wind-down

By the late 1940s, the onset of the Cold War shifted Allied priorities toward rebuilding West Germany as an ally. Amnesty laws in 1949 and 1954, and Article 131 of the Basic Law, allowed many former officials to return to public service. In East Germany, denazification was declared complete early, with former Nazis integrated under the regime's antifascist doctrine.

Assessment

Historians debate the programme's depth and fairness: it processed enormous numbers of people and broke the institutional power of Nazism, yet many compromised officials — judges, civil servants, businessmen — resumed their careers, and the reckoning with individual guilt was often shallow. The term itself became a byword for the difficulty of purging an ideology from a society.
