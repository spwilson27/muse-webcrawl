The electron is a subatomic particle with a negative one elementary electric charge. Electrons belong to the first generation of the lepton particle family and are generally thought to be elementary particles because they have no known components or substructure. The electron's mass is approximately 1/1836 that of the proton.

Quantum mechanical properties of the electron include an intrinsic angular momentum (spin) of a half-integer value, expressed in units of the reduced Planck constant, ħ. No two electrons can occupy the same quantum state, in accordance with the Pauli exclusion principle. Electrons exhibit wave–particle duality: they can collide with other particles and can be diffracted like light.

History

The electron was identified as a particle in 1897 by J. J. Thomson and his team at the Cavendish Laboratory, who showed that cathode rays consisted of negatively charged particles far lighter than hydrogen atoms. Robert Millikan's oil-drop experiment (1909) measured the elementary charge. The concept of an indivisible quantity of electric charge had been proposed earlier by G. Johnstone Stoney, who coined the name "electron" in 1891.

Role in atoms and technology

Electrons surround atomic nuclei and, through their configurations, determine nearly all of chemistry. Electric current is the flow of electrons in conductors, and electronics — vacuum tubes, then transistors — rests on controlling that flow. Electron beams are used in microscopy, welding, and cathode-ray displays.
