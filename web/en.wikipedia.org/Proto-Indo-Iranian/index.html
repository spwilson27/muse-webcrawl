Proto-Indo-Iranian is the reconstructed common ancestor of the Indo-Iranian languages, the largest branch of the Indo-European family, comprising the Indo-Aryan languages (such as Sanskrit, Hindi and Bengali), the Iranian languages (such as Persian, Pashto and Kurdish) and the Nuristani languages. Its speakers, the Proto-Indo-Iranians, are generally identified with the late Bronze Age steppe cultures east of the Caspian Sea.

Origins and history

Proto-Indo-Iranian developed from late Proto-Indo-European on the Eurasian steppe, probably among the Sintashta culture (c. 2200–1900 BCE) of the southern Urals, famed for the earliest known spoke-wheeled war chariots, and its successor the Andronovo culture, which spread across Central Asia. From there, Indo-Aryan speakers moved toward the Indian subcontinent — a related group appears as the elite of the Mitanni kingdom in Syria by c. 1500 BCE — while Iranian speakers gradually spread over the Iranian plateau, entering recorded history with the Medes and Persians in the 1st millennium BCE.

Language and culture

Proto-Indo-Iranian is reconstructed from the close correspondences between Vedic Sanskrit and Avestan, the languages of the Rigveda and the Avesta, whose ritual vocabularies overlap extensively — including the sacred drink soma/haoma, fire worship and cognate deities (some of whom, such as Indra, appear as demons in the Zoroastrian tradition). Characteristic sound changes distinguish the branch, including the merger of PIE vowels *e, *o, *a into *a and the palatalisation of velars. The speakers called themselves something like *arya- ("Aryan"), a name preserved in Iran and in Vedic usage.
