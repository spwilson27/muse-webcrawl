Hospice care is a philosophy and model of care for people facing a life-limiting illness, prioritising comfort, dignity and quality of life over cure. It addresses physical symptoms — especially pain — together with psychological, social and spiritual needs, and supports families during illness and bereavement. Care may be delivered in dedicated inpatient hospices, at home, in hospitals or in care homes.

History

The word derives from the Latin hospes (guest/host), and medieval hospices sheltered pilgrims and the sick. The modern movement began with Dame Cicely Saunders, who founded St Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967 and articulated the concept of "total pain". The first American hospice opened in Connecticut in 1974, and hospice became a Medicare benefit in the United States in 1982.

Care model

Hospice teams typically include doctors, nurses, social workers, chaplains and volunteers. In the U.S. Medicare model, eligibility generally requires a prognosis of six months or less and a choice to forgo curative treatment for the terminal condition. Worldwide, hospice and palliative care remain unevenly available, prompting advocacy for wider access.
