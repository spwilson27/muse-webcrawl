ATP synthase

ATP synthase (EC 7.1.2.2)
Function: synthesis of ATP from ADP and phosphate
Location: inner mitochondrial membrane, thylakoid membrane, bacterial plasma membrane
Mechanism: rotary catalysis driven by a proton gradient

ATP synthase is an enzyme that creates the energy storage molecule adenosine triphosphate (ATP). It catalyses the formation of ATP from adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate, using energy stored in an electrochemical gradient of protons across a membrane — a process called chemiosmosis. It is found in the inner membranes of mitochondria, the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts, and the plasma membranes of bacteria.

Structure

The enzyme consists of two main components. The FO portion is embedded in the membrane and contains a ring of c subunits that rotates as protons flow through it. The F1 portion projects into the matrix (or cytoplasm) and contains three α and three β subunits arranged around a central γ shaft. Rotation of the shaft forces the β subunits through conformational changes — loose, tight and open states in the binding change mechanism proposed by Paul Boyer — binding ADP and phosphate, forming ATP and releasing it.

Significance

ATP synthase is often described as a molecular turbine and is one of the most abundant and essential enzymes on Earth; nearly all cellular energy transactions are funded by ATP it produces through oxidative phosphorylation and photophosphorylation. Research on its mechanism contributed to the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1997, awarded to Paul D. Boyer and John E. Walker, with Jens C. Skou recognised for work on a related ion-transporting enzyme. The enzyme can also run in reverse, hydrolysing ATP to pump protons, a function used by some bacteria to maintain membrane potential.
