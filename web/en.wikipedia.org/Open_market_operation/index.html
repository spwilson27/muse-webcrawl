Open market operation

Open market operations
Tool ofMonetary policy
Conducted byCentral banks
InstrumentsGovernment securities, repos, reverse repos
EffectAdjusts reserves and short-term interest rates

An open market operation (OMO) is the buying or selling of government securities in the open market by a central bank in order to implement monetary policy. It is traditionally the principal tool with which central banks influence short-term interest rates and the money supply.

When a central bank buys securities, it pays by crediting the reserve accounts of banks, expanding reserves and putting downward pressure on the overnight interbank rate. When it sells securities, reserves are drained and rates rise. By steering the overnight rate—such as the U.S. federal funds rate—the central bank influences the whole structure of interest rates, credit conditions, and ultimately inflation and employment.

Types of operations

Outright purchases and sales permanently change the central bank's portfolio. Temporary operations include repurchase agreements (repos), in which the central bank lends reserves against collateral for a short term, and reverse repos, which temporarily drain reserves. In the United States, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) sets policy, and the Open Market Trading Desk at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York executes the transactions with primary dealers.

Quantitative easing

When short-term rates reached the zero lower bound after the 2008 financial crisis, several central banks undertook large-scale outright purchases of longer-term government bonds and other assets—quantitative easing—to expand their balance sheets and lower long-term rates. The unwinding of these portfolios, or quantitative tightening, proceeds largely through letting securities mature without replacement.
