Oleg the Wise

Oleg (Old East Slavic: Ѡлегъ; Old Norse: Helgi), known as Oleg the Wise or Oleg the Prophet, was a Varangian prince who ruled all or part of the Rus' people during the late 9th and early 10th centuries, traditionally as regent of Novgorod from 879 and prince of Kiev from 882 until his death, conventionally dated to 912.

Rule

According to the Primary Chronicle, Oleg was a kinsman of Rurik and became guardian of Rurik's son Igor. In 882 he sailed down the Dnieper, captured Smolensk and Lyubech, and took Kiev by trickery, killing its rulers Askold and Dir and declaring Kiev "the mother of Rus cities". He is credited with subjugating neighbouring East Slavic tribes — Drevlians, Severians, and Radimichs — and imposing tribute.

Expedition against Constantinople

In 907, the chronicle says, Oleg led a great fleet against Constantinople; the Byzantines barred the harbour with a chain and, after Oleg's men allegedly dragged their ships overland on wheels and raised sails on them, sued for peace. The resulting treaty (confirmed in 911) granted the Rus favourable trading terms. Oleg is said to have hung his shield on the gates of Constantinople. Byzantine sources do not mention the 907 attack, and some historians doubt its details, though the 911 treaty text preserved in the chronicle is widely accepted as genuine.

Death legend

The chronicle relates that pagan priests prophesied Oleg would be killed by his favourite horse. Oleg had the horse sent away, but years later, visiting its bones, he stepped on the skull, from which a snake emerged and bit him fatally. The legend inspired Alexander Pushkin's ballad The Song of the Wise Oleg (1822).
