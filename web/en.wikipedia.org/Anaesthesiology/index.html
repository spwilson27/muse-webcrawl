Anaesthesiology

Anaesthesiology (also spelled anesthesiology in American English) is the medical specialty concerned with the perioperative care of patients before, during and after surgery. It encompasses anaesthesia — the provision of general, regional and local techniques that render patients insensible to pain — as well as intensive care medicine, critical emergency medicine and pain medicine.

Scope of practice

The anaesthesiologist (anaesthetist) evaluates patients before surgery, plans and delivers anaesthesia, monitors and supports vital functions such as breathing, circulation and fluid balance during operations, and supervises recovery. Beyond the operating theatre, anaesthesiologists run intensive care units, provide labour analgesia including epidurals, staff chronic pain services, and assist in resuscitation and emergency care throughout the hospital.

History

Modern anaesthesia began with the public demonstration of ether anaesthesia by William T. G. Morton in Boston in 1846, followed by James Young Simpson's introduction of chloroform in 1847. The specialty developed rapidly with endotracheal intubation, muscle relaxants, intravenous agents such as thiopental and propofol, and modern monitoring standards including pulse oximetry and capnography, which together made anaesthesia dramatically safer over the 20th century.

Types of anaesthesia

General anaesthesia produces reversible unconsciousness and amnesia. Regional anaesthesia — spinal, epidural and peripheral nerve blocks — numbs a region of the body while the patient remains awake or sedated. Local anaesthesia and procedural sedation are used for minor procedures.
