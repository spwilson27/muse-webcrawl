Transylvania

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Transylvania (Romanian: Transilvania or Ardeal; Hungarian: Erdély; German: Siebenbürgen) is a historical and cultural region in Central Europe, today part of Romania. It is bounded by the Carpathian Mountains and the Apuseni Mountains, and its principal cities include Cluj-Napoca, Brașov, Sibiu and Târgu Mureș. The region is widely known in popular culture for its association with the legend of Dracula.

History

Transylvania was part of the Dacian kingdom, then the Roman province of Dacia after Trajan's conquest in 106 AD. In the Middle Ages it became part of the Kingdom of Hungary; after the Battle of Mohács (1526) it emerged as an autonomous principality under Ottoman suzerainty. Following the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699) it passed to Habsburg rule. In 1920, the Treaty of Trianon — a companion settlement to the Treaty of Trianon process that redrew Hungary's borders — assigned Transylvania to Romania, a transfer briefly reversed in the north by the Second Vienna Award (1940) and restored after the Second World War.

Population

The region's population is majority ethnic Romanian, with a significant Hungarian minority (including the Székely), as well as Roma and smaller German (Transylvanian Saxon) communities, much reduced by emigration in the late 20th century. Romanian, Hungarian and German are all historically spoken.

Culture

Transylvania is noted for its fortified churches built by Saxon settlers, several of them UNESCO World Heritage Sites, its medieval towns, folk traditions, and mountain landscapes. Bram Stoker's 1897 novel Dracula, drawing loosely on the Wallachian prince Vlad III, fixed the region's Gothic image in the Western imagination.
