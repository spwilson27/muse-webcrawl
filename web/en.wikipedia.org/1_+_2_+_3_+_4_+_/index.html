The series 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯, the sum of all positive integers, is the archetypal divergent series of mathematics. Because its terms grow indefinitely and its partial sums — the triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21, … — increase without limit, it does not converge to any finite number under the standard definition of the sum of an infinite series.

Partial sums

The sum of the first n natural numbers is given by the closed formula:

1 + 2 + ⋯ + n = n(n + 1)/2

— a result famously (if apocryphally) derived by the young Carl Friedrich Gauss by pairing terms from the two ends of the sum. Since this expression tends to infinity as n increases, the infinite series diverges.

The −1/12 phenomenon

Despite its divergence, generalisations of summation — including Ramanujan summation and analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function, for which ζ(−1) = −1/12 — consistently attach the rational value −1/12 to the series. This coincidence of methods has made the series a celebrated popular topic, and the value plays a genuine role in theoretical physics, notably in Casimir-effect calculations and in deriving the 26-dimensional critical dimension of bosonic string theory.

Critically, saying "the sum is −1/12" without qualification is a shorthand: it means the series is assigned −1/12 by those extended summation methods, not that the partial sums approach −1/12.
