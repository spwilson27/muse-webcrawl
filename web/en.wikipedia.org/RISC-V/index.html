RISC-V (pronounced "risk-five") is an open standard instruction set architecture (ISA) based on established reduced instruction set computer (RISC) principles. Unlike most other ISA designs, RISC-V is provided under royalty-free open licenses that do not require fees for use. Anyone may design, manufacture, and sell RISC-V chips and software.

The project began in 2010 at the University of California, Berkeley, under researchers including Krste Asanović and David Patterson; the name marks it as the fifth generation of Berkeley RISC designs. Stewardship now rests with RISC-V International, a non-profit association based in Switzerland with thousands of member companies and institutions.

Design

RISC-V has a modular design: a small mandatory base integer ISA — RV32I (32-bit), RV64I (64-bit), or RV128I — is combined with optional standard extensions, identified by letters. Common extensions include M (integer multiplication and division), A (atomics), F and D (single- and double-precision floating point), C (compressed 16-bit instructions), and V (vector operations). The common combination RV64GC ("G" for the general set) runs mainstream operating systems including Linux. Because implementers may add their own extensions or omit standard ones, the ISA scales from tiny embedded microcontrollers to high-performance and supercomputing processors, while the frozen base guarantees software compatibility.

Adoption

Freed from licensing fees and export restrictions attached to proprietary ISAs such as ARM and x86, RISC-V has been widely adopted in microcontrollers and embedded systems (for example in products from Espressif, Western Digital, Nvidia, and Alibaba), in Indian and European processor initiatives, and increasingly in data-centre accelerators and consumer devices. Billions of RISC-V cores ship each year. Its openness has also made it a focus of geopolitical interest, as governments weigh dependence on foreign-controlled architectures.

Software ecosystem

RISC-V is supported by the GCC and LLVM compiler toolchains and by the Linux kernel and major distributions, although its desktop and mobile software ecosystem remains less mature than those of x86 and ARM.
