Greek theatre, where the roles of protagonist, deuteragonist, and tritagonist originated.

In literature and drama, the deuteragonist is the second most important character in a narrative, after the protagonist and before the tritagonist. The term comes from the Greek deuteragōnistēs, meaning "second actor," and derives from ancient Greek theatre, in which plays traditionally had only one to three speaking actors who divided all the roles between them.

Origins in Greek drama

Aeschylus is credited with introducing the second actor into tragedy, a development that reduced the role of the chorus and made dialogue and conflict between characters possible. With the addition of a third actor by Sophocles, the hierarchy of protagonist, deuteragonist, and tritagonist was established; the lead actor took the principal role, while the deuteragonist played secondary parts such as confidants, messengers, or antagonists within a given scene.

Modern usage

In contemporary criticism the deuteragonist is the character with the second-greatest importance or screen time in a story. A deuteragonist may be an ally or a foe of the protagonist and often has an independent character arc of their own. Examples commonly cited include Dr. Watson to Sherlock Holmes and Samwise Gamgee to Frodo Baggins; in ensemble works, the boundary between protagonist and deuteragonist can be deliberately blurred.
