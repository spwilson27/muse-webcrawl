Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)

Location: Tacoma Narrows, Washington, US
Type: Suspension bridge
Main span: 2,800 ft (853 m)
Opened: 1 July 1940
Collapsed: 7 November 1940

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge of 1940 was a suspension bridge in the US state of Washington spanning the Tacoma Narrows strait of Puget Sound between Tacoma and the Kitsap Peninsula. Opened on 1 July 1940, it collapsed into the strait on 7 November of the same year in one of the most famous structural failures in engineering history — earning it the nickname "Galloping Gertie".

Design and behaviour

Designed by Leon Moisseiff, the bridge used shallow, solid plate girders instead of open trusses to stiffen the deck. The solid girders deflected wind rather than letting it pass through, and the deck was unusually narrow and light. Even before opening, workers noticed the bridge undulating vertically in moderate winds; attempts were made to damp the motion with cables and hydraulic buffers.

Collapse

On the morning of 7 November 1940, in winds of about 40 mph (64 km/h), the bridge began twisting torsionally as well as undulating. The motion grew until the main span tore apart and fell into the water below. The only fatality was a dog; the collapse was captured on film, which is still widely studied. The cause was aerodynamic flutter — a self-exciting oscillation in which the twisting deck fed energy from the wind into the structure (often popularly but imprecisely described as resonance).

Legacy

The failure transformed bridge engineering: wind-tunnel testing of models became standard, decks were stiffened and made aerodynamically open, and the disaster remains a standard case study in physics and engineering courses. A replacement bridge opened in 1950, joined by a parallel span in 2007.
