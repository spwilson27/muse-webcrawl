Mechanical advantage

Mechanical advantage is a measure of the force amplification achieved by using a tool, mechanical device or machine system. The device trades off input forces against movement to obtain a desired amplification in the output force. The model for this is the law of the lever. Machine components called mechanical elements or linkages and mechanisms are designed to transform a given input force and movement into a desired output force and movement.

A lever provides mechanical advantage by trading distance for force.

Definition

The mechanical advantage MA of a machine is the ratio of output force to input force:

MA = output force / input force.

For an ideal machine with no friction or losses, the mechanical advantage equals the velocity ratio: the distance the effort moves divided by the distance the load moves. Real machines lose energy to friction, so their actual mechanical advantage is always less than the ideal.

Simple machines

  • Lever: MA equals the ratio of the effort arm length to the load arm length.
  • Pulley system: MA is approximately equal to the number of rope segments supporting the moving block.
  • Inclined plane: MA equals the length of the slope divided by its height.
  • Screw: a rotating inclined plane; MA depends on the pitch and the radius at which force is applied.
  • Wheel and axle, gears, and hydraulic systems likewise trade force against distance (or pressure and area).

Gear trains

In a gear train or chain drive, the mechanical advantage (or gear ratio) is determined by the ratio of the numbers of teeth, or equivalently the pitch diameters, of the output and input gears. A gear ratio greater than one increases torque at the expense of rotational speed, and vice versa.
