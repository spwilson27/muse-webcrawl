Dynamic soaring

Dynamic soaring is a flying technique used to gain energy by repeatedly crossing the boundary between air masses of different velocities. It is used by soaring birds, especially albatrosses and giant petrels crossing oceans, and by pilots of radio-controlled gliders, which hold the outright speed records for unpowered model aircraft.

Principle

Near a surface such as the ocean, wind speed increases with height (a wind gradient or shear layer). A bird climbs into the faster-moving air while heading upwind, gaining airspeed, then turns and descends downwind into slower air, again converting the difference into airspeed. By repeating this cycle — popularized in explanation by Lord Rayleigh in 1883 — the bird extracts energy from the shear and can travel hundreds of kilometres without flapping.

Birds

Wandering albatrosses are the masters of the technique, covering vast distances over the Southern Ocean with minimal effort, their long, narrow wings ideally suited to high-speed gliding between wave troughs and crests.

Radio-controlled gliders

In the early 2000s, RC glider pilots began dynamic soaring on hilltop shear layers, flying tight circuits across the boundary between windward and lee-side air. Speeds climbed rapidly: models exceeded 800 km/h (500 mph), and in 2021 a Spencer Lisenby flight reached 882 km/h (548 mph), among the fastest speeds achieved by any unpowered aircraft.
