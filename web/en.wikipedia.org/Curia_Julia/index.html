Curia Julia

The Curia Julia (Latin: Curia Iulia) is the third named curia, or senate house, in the ancient city of Rome. It was begun by Julius Caesar in 44 BC, replacing the Curia Hostilia he had demolished to make way for his Forum, and was completed and dedicated by Augustus in 29 BC. Standing in the Roman Forum beside the Comitium, it is one of the best-preserved structures of ancient Rome.

History

The Senate met in the Curia Julia throughout the imperial period. The building was restored by Domitian and, after being destroyed by fire during the reign of Carinus in AD 283, was rebuilt by Diocletian in essentially its present form. In 630, Pope Honorius I converted it into the church of Sant'Adriano al Foro, which preserved the structure through the Middle Ages and beyond. The church's baroque additions were removed in the 1930s under the Fascist government to restore the building's Roman appearance.

Architecture

The curia is a rectangular brick-faced concrete hall, approximately 27 metres long, 18 metres wide, and 21 metres high, in proportions prescribed by Vitruvian ideals. Inside, three broad, low steps on each side accommodated the senators' seats — room for about 300 senators — facing a central floor paved in colored marble (opus sectile). The bronze doors now in the building are replicas; the originals were transferred in the 17th century to the Lateran Basilica. Against the rear wall stood the Altar of Victory, erected by Augustus and the focus of a famous dispute between pagans and Christians in the late fourth century AD.

Two large marble reliefs, the Anaglypha Traiani, depicting imperial benefactions, are associated with the building and displayed in the Forum.
