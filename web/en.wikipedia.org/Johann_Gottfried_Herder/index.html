Johann Gottfried Herder

Johann Gottfried von Herder (25 August 1744 – 18 December 1803) was a German philosopher, theologian, poet and literary critic. A leading figure of the Sturm und Drang and of Weimar Classicism, he championed the idea that language and culture express the unique character of each people (Volk), profoundly influencing Romanticism, historicism and the modern study of folklore and national cultures.

Life

Herder was born in Mohrungen in East Prussia, the son of a schoolmaster. He studied at the University of Königsberg, where he attended the lectures of Immanuel Kant and formed a formative friendship with Johann Georg Hamann. He worked as a clergyman and schoolteacher in Riga, travelled in France, and in Strasbourg in 1770 met the young Goethe, whom he introduced to Shakespeare, Ossian and folk poetry — an encounter that helped ignite the Sturm und Drang. From 1776, through Goethe's influence, he served as court preacher and superintendent of the church in Weimar.

Thought

In his Treatise on the Origin of Language (1772) Herder argued, against both divine-origin theories and simple imitation, that language arises from human reflection (Besonnenheit). His Ideas for the Philosophy of History of Humanity (1784–1791) presented history as the unfolding of diverse cultures, each to be understood in its own terms and each contributing to Humanität, the full realisation of human potential. His collections of folk songs, Volkslieder (1778–79), coined the term and inspired folk revivals across Europe.

Legacy

Herder's historicism and cultural pluralism influenced thinkers from Hegel to the Slavophiles and the Grimms, and his stress on language as constitutive of thought anticipated modern hermeneutics and anthropology, though his concept of the Volk was later put to nationalist uses he would not have endorsed.
