Asch conformity experiments

The Asch conformity experiments were a series of psychological experiments conducted by Solomon Asch during the 1950s. The experiments revealed the degree to which a person's own opinions are influenced by those of a group. Asch found that people were willing to ignore reality and give an incorrect answer in order to conform to the rest of the group.

In the line task, participants judged which comparison line matched a standard line.

Procedure

In the experiments, a naive participant was placed in a room with several confederates of the experimenter. The group was shown a card with a standard line and then a card with three comparison lines of different lengths, and asked which line matched the standard. On certain "critical trials", the confederates unanimously gave a clearly wrong answer before the participant responded.

Results

Across the critical trials, about 75 per cent of participants conformed at least once, and the overall rate of conformity was roughly one third of responses. In a control condition with no confederates giving wrong answers, participants erred on fewer than 1 per cent of trials. The presence of even a single "truthful partner" who broke unanimity sharply reduced conformity.

Interpretation

Asch distinguished between normative influence (conforming to be liked or accepted) and informational influence (assuming the group must be right). The experiments remain a landmark demonstration of group pressure and are frequently compared with the later Milgram experiment on obedience.
