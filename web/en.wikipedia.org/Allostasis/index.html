Allostasis

Allostasis is the process of achieving stability, or homeostasis, through physiological or behavioral change. It was coined by Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer in 1988 as "stability through change." Whereas homeostasis maintains a fixed set point, allostasis anticipates demand and adjusts set points — such as blood pressure, cortisol, and body temperature — according to anticipated need.

Mechanisms

Primary mediators include the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the autonomic nervous system, metabolic hormones, and the immune system. Efficient allostasis involves turning responses on when needed and off promptly afterward.

Allostatic load

When mediators are overused or dysregulated — frequent activation, failure to habituate, prolonged response, or inadequate response triggering compensatory hyperactivity in other systems — wear and tear accumulates. This cumulative cost, termed allostatic load by Bruce McEwen, is associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, depression, and cognitive decline, and can be indexed by biomarkers such as cortisol, C-reactive protein, blood pressure, and waist-hip ratio.

Criticism and development

Some researchers regard allostasis as a useful reframing of homeostasis rather than a distinct process. The concept has been widely adopted in stress research, psychiatry, and public health, including studies of how socioeconomic adversity becomes biologically embedded.
