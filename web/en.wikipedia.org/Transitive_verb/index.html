Transitive verb

[Image: Diagram of subject–verb–object structure]
images/transitiveverb.png

A transitive verb is a verb that takes a direct object — that is, a verb whose action is carried over to a recipient or target expressed in the clause. In the English sentence "She reads the report", reads is transitive and the report is its direct object. Verbs that do not take a direct object are called intransitive, and many verbs are ambitransitive, usable either way (compare "He sings" with "He sings the anthem").

Transitivity across languages

Grammars describe transitivity differently across languages. In English, the test is often whether the verb can appear in the passive ("the report was read"). Many languages mark transitivity morphologically: in Japanese, pairs of transitive/intransitive verbs are distinguished by suffixes (e.g. akeru "open (something)" vs aku "be open"). Ergative languages treat the subjects of transitive verbs differently from those of intransitives, reversing the alignment found in English.

Ditransitive and ambitransitive verbs

Ditransitive verbs take two objects — a direct and an indirect one ("She gave him the keys"). English permits the dative alternation ("She gave the keys to him"), while some languages encode the two objects with case. Ambitransitive verbs shift meaning or valency depending on use: "The cook broke the plates" (transitive) versus "The plates broke" (intransitive, describing the event without an agent).
