Silicate minerals are rock-forming minerals made up of silicate groups. They are the largest and most important class of minerals and make up approximately 90 percent of Earth's crust. In mineralogy, silica (silicon dioxide, SiO₂) is usually considered a silicate mineral rather than an oxide.

Silicate minerals all contain silicon and oxygen. The fundamental structural unit is the silicon–oxygen tetrahedron, in which a silicon atom is surrounded by four oxygen atoms. These tetrahedra may occur isolated or linked by sharing oxygen atoms into pairs, rings, single and double chains, sheets, or three-dimensional frameworks, giving rise to the standard classification.

Classification

Nesosilicates have isolated tetrahedra (e.g., olivine, garnet, zircon). Sorosilicates have paired tetrahedra (e.g., epidote). Cyclosilicates form rings (e.g., beryl, tourmaline). Inosilicates form chains — single chains in pyroxenes, double chains in amphiboles. Phyllosilicates form sheets, including the micas, chlorite, and clay minerals such as kaolinite. Tectosilicates, or framework silicates, include quartz and the feldspars — the most abundant mineral group in the crust.

Importance

Silicates dominate the composition of igneous, metamorphic, and many sedimentary rocks, and their weathering regulates the long-term carbon cycle by consuming atmospheric CO₂. Many silicates are economically important: feldspar and clay in ceramics and glass, asbestos (now restricted for health reasons) in insulation, and gemstones such as emerald, topaz, and garnet.
