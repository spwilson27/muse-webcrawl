Pyroclastic surge

A pyroclastic surge is a fluidized mass of turbulent gas and rock fragments that flows along the ground at high speed during some volcanic eruptions. It is one of the two principal types of pyroclastic density current, the other being the pyroclastic flow; surges are more dilute, more turbulent, and less confined by topography than flows, and can surmount ridges and travel across water.

Types

Volcanologists distinguish ground surges, generated by the collapse of an eruption column near the vent; ash-cloud surges, which separate from the top of a moving pyroclastic flow; and base surges, first recognized in underwater nuclear tests, which radiate outward from the base of an explosion column, especially in phreatomagmatic eruptions where magma interacts with water. Base surges produced the devastation at Taal in the Philippines and contributed to the destruction around Mount St. Helens in 1980.

Deposits and hazards

Surge deposits are thin (centimeters to a few meters), fine-grained, and often show cross-bedding and dune-like bedforms from the turbulent flow. Temperatures in surges range from below 100 °C in some wet base surges to several hundred degrees; the 1902 eruption of Mount Pelée sent a surge — the nuée ardente of contemporary accounts — over Saint-Pierre, Martinique, killing some 28,000 people. Because surges can travel at speeds exceeding 100 km/h, extend several kilometres beyond accompanying flows, and cannot be outrun, they are among the deadliest volcanic phenomena; the directed blast of Mount St. Helens devastated 600 km² largely through surge-like currents.
