The OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (officially the Convention on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International Business Transactions) is an anti-corruption convention adopted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. Signed in Paris on 17 December 1997 and in force from 15 February 1999, it requires signatory states to criminalise the bribery of foreign public officials in international business transactions — the "supply side" of transnational bribery.

Background

Before the convention, only the United States, through the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act of 1977, criminalised the bribery of foreign officials; in several European states, foreign bribes were even tax-deductible as business expenses. American businesses complained that this put them at a competitive disadvantage, and sustained US pressure through the OECD — building on a 1994 recommendation and a 1996 recommendation to end tax deductibility — produced the convention.

Provisions

Parties must make it a criminal offence for any person intentionally to offer, promise or give any undue pecuniary or other advantage to a foreign public official in order to obtain or retain business or other improper advantage. The convention also requires effective, proportionate and dissuasive sanctions, corporate liability, accounting and auditing provisions to prevent the concealment of bribes, mutual legal assistance, and extradition. It covers bribery through intermediaries and applies to officials of any public enterprise or public international organisation.

Monitoring

Enforcement is monitored by the OECD Working Group on Bribery through a rigorous peer-review system conducted in phases: Phase 1 examines legislation, Phase 2 its practical application, Phase 3 enforcement and cross-cutting issues, and Phase 4 — launched in 2016 — country-specific issues including enforcement, detection and corporate liability. The Working Group's public reports and the threat of a "bis" (repeat) review or a high-level mission have been credited with driving enforcement reforms.

Enforcement and criticism

Enforcement has been uneven across parties. The United States, Germany, the United Kingdom (under the Bribery Act 2010) and Switzerland have brought many of the largest cases; major corporate resolutions include Siemens (2008) and Airbus (2020). Transparency International's periodic "Exporting Corruption" reports have repeatedly found only a minority of parties actively enforcing the convention, even though its parties account for the majority of world exports and outward foreign direct investment.
