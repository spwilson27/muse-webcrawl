Interference (wave propagation)


Overlapping waves from two sources produce an interference pattern of maxima and minima.

In physics, interference is a phenomenon in which two or more coherent waves combine, by the superposition principle, to form a resultant wave of greater, lower or the same amplitude. Where crest meets crest the waves reinforce (constructive interference); where crest meets trough they cancel (destructive interference). The resulting pattern of alternating maxima and minima is characteristic of waves of every kind — light, sound, water and quantum-mechanical matter waves — and is unrelated to the concept of an interface in computing.

Mechanism

Interference requires the sources to maintain a stable phase relationship (coherence). The amplitude at a point depends on the path difference from the sources: a difference of whole wavelengths yields constructive interference, and of half-odd wavelengths yields destructive interference. Thomas Young's double-slit experiment of 1801 demonstrated the interference of light and provided decisive evidence for its wave nature; with electrons and even molecules, the same experiment later demonstrated wave–particle duality.

Applications

Interference underlies interferometry, used in instruments from the Michelson interferometer — central to the Michelson–Morley experiment and to gravitational-wave detectors such as LIGO — to radio telescope arrays. Thin-film interference produces the colours of soap bubbles and oil slicks and is exploited in anti-reflective coatings; acoustic interference is harnessed in noise-cancelling headphones and must be managed in concert-hall design.
