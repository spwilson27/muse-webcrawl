Citizen's arrest

A citizen's arrest is an arrest made by a private citizen — that is, a person who is not acting as a sworn law-enforcement official. In common law jurisdictions, citizen's arrests are generally permitted where a serious offence, such as a felony, has been committed, or where an offence is being committed in the arrester's presence.

The concept derives from English common law, under which private persons were not merely permitted but historically obliged to assist in apprehending offenders, for instance by joining the hue and cry.

Legal rules by jurisdiction

In England and Wales, the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 permits "any person" arrests for indictable offences, subject to strict conditions: the offence must actually have been committed, and it must not be reasonably practicable for a constable to make the arrest instead. In the United States, the rules vary by state; most states allow a private person to arrest for a felony, and many for a misdemeanour committed in their presence. A person who makes an unlawful citizen's arrest may be liable for false imprisonment and civil damages.

Shopkeeper's privilege

A related doctrine, the shopkeeper's privilege, allows merchants to detain suspected shoplifters for a reasonable time while investigating.
