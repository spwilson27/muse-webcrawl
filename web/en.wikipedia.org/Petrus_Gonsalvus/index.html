Petrus Gonsalvus

Petrus Gonsalvus (Spanish: Pedro González; 1537–1618), referred to by the naturalist Ulisse Aldrovandi as "the man of the woods", was a Canary Islander who became a celebrated member of the court of King Henry II of France. He was born with hypertrichosis, a condition causing excessive hair growth over the body, and his life and family — several of whose children inherited the condition — are among the best documented early cases.

Life

Gonsalvus was born on Tenerife and brought to the court of Henry II of France as a boy of about ten, initially as a curiosity. The king instead had him educated: he learned Latin and courtly manners and rose to a respected position in the royal household, receiving a salary and serving in the king's sommellerie. After Henry's death he remained under the protection of Catherine de' Medici and later passed to the household of Margaret of Parma in the Netherlands, and finally to Italy, where he lived under the Farnese dukes of Parma and spent his last years at Capodimonte near Lake Bolsena.

Family

The French court arranged his marriage to a woman named Catherine, who did not have the condition; the couple had seven children, four of whom — including his daughters Antonietta ("Tognina") and Maddalena — inherited hypertrichosis. Portraits of the family were painted at several European courts, notably a famous series of the children at Ambras Castle in Innsbruck, and the family was studied by physicians including Aldrovandi, making them the first recorded family with the condition, sometimes called "Ambras syndrome".

In culture

Gonsalvus's story is often cited as a possible inspiration for the fairy tale Beauty and the Beast (published by Gabrielle-Suzanne Barbot de Villeneuve in 1740), though the connection is speculative. His portraits remain frequently reproduced images of difference and dignity in Renaissance court culture.
