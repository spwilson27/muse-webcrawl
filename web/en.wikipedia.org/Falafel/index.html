Falafel

Falafel (Arabic: فلافل) is a deep-fried ball or patty-shaped fritter made from ground chickpeas, fava beans, or both, seasoned with herbs and spices such as parsley, coriander and cumin. A staple of Middle Eastern cuisine, it is commonly served in a pita or flatbread wrap with salads, pickles and tahini sauce, and is eaten throughout the world as a popular vegetarian street food.

Origin and history

Falafel is most often traced to Egypt, where it was probably developed by Coptic Christians — who called it ta'amiya when made with fava beans — as a meatless dish for Lent. From Egypt it spread through the Levant, where chickpeas or a mixture of the two legumes became common. In the twentieth century it became an iconic street food across the Arab world and in Israel and Palestine, where its cultural ownership is sometimes contested. Migrants carried falafel to Europe and North America, where it became a fixture of vegetarian and Middle Eastern restaurants.

Preparation

Dried legumes are soaked, ground with herbs, onions and spices, formed into balls or patties — often with a special scoop called an aleb falafel — and deep-fried until crisp and brown outside and green within (from the herbs). It is typically served as a sandwich in pita with tomato, lettuce, pickles and tahini, or as part of a meze plate with hummus. Baked and air-fried versions exist as lower-fat alternatives.
