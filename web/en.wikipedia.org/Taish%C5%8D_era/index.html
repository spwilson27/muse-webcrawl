Taishō era

Taishō era (大正時代, Taishō jidai) — period of Japanese history corresponding to the reign of Emperor Taishō, from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926. Preceded by Meiji, followed by Shōwa.

The Taishō era (大正時代, Taishō jidai) is a period of Japanese history corresponding to the reign of Emperor Taishō, from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926, which began with the death of Emperor Meiji and ended with the ascension of the Emperor Hirohito (Shōwa). It roughly coincides with the era known as "Taishō Democracy", a period of liberal politics and cultural flourishing.

Overview

During the First World War Japan fought on the Allied side, expanded its influence in Asia, and enjoyed an economic boom. The 1918 Rice Riots and postwar recession spurred political ferment: universal male suffrage was enacted in 1925, party cabinets alternated in power, and Hara Takashi became the first commoner prime minister in 1918. Urban mass culture blossomed — cinemas, cafés, jazz, and new literature — even as the era also saw the Great Kantō earthquake of 1923 and the passage of the Peace Preservation Law in 1925.

Taishō democracy

The term describes the trend toward party government and expanded civil participation between roughly 1905 and 1932, exemplified by the political scientist Yoshino Sakuzō's advocacy of minponshugi ("government by the people"). Its momentum faded in the early Shōwa period as militarism rose.
