Rheingau is one of 13 designated German wine regions (Anbaugebiete) producing quality wines (QbA and Prädikatswein). It stretches along the north bank of the Rhine west of Frankfurt, chiefly between Wiesbaden and Lorch, where the river turns and the Taunus hills shelter south-facing vineyards. Despite accounting for only about 3 per cent of German wine production, the Rheingau is historically one of the most prestigious wine regions in the world, and is synonymous with Riesling.

South-facing Riesling vineyards slope down to the Rhine.

History

Viticulture here is documented from Roman times and was developed by the Church: the Cistercian abbey of Kloster Eberbach (founded 1136) became one of medieval Europe's largest wine estates, and its Steinberg vineyard was enclosed by a wall. At Schloss Johannisberg, the practice of late harvesting (Spätlese) is traditionally dated to 1775, when a delayed harvest permit led to the discovery of the virtues of noble rot; the estate is also credited with pioneering Auslese and with planting Riesling exclusively from 1720.

Wines

About four-fifths of plantings are Riesling, unusual even by German standards; Spätburgunder (Pinot noir) dominates the red plantings, notably around Assmannshausen, whose Höllenberg is famed for red wine. Rheingau Rieslings tend to be fuller-bodied than Mosel wines, with ripe stone-fruit flavours balanced by firm acidity, in styles from bone-dry (trocken) to lusciously sweet Trockenbeerenauslese. Quality designations include the VDP classification of Erste Lage and Grosse Lage sites and the regional "Erstes Gewächs".

Geography and climate

For some 30 km the Rhine flows westward, so the vineyards on its north bank face due south across the water, gaining reflected light and moderated temperatures; the Taunus mountains protect them from cold northern winds. Soils vary from slate and quartzite to loess and loam, contributing to distinct village styles in places such as Rüdesheim, Geisenheim, Johannisberg, Hallgarten and Rauenthal.
