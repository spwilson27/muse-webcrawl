The Shannon hydroelectric scheme was a major development by the Irish Free State in the 1920s to harness the power of the River Shannon for electricity generation. Built between 1925 and 1929 under the direction of the Irish engineer Thomas McLaughlin and constructed by the German firm Siemens-Schuckert, its centrepiece is the power station at Ardnacrusha in County Clare. At a cost of about £5.2 million — a very large fraction of the young state's annual budget — it was the flagship project of the new Irish state and, at its opening, one of the largest hydroelectric schemes in the world.

Design

The scheme exploits the fall of the Shannon between Lough Derg and the tidal estuary near Limerick. A weir at Parteen diverts most of the river's flow into a 12.6 km headrace canal, which carries the water to the powerhouse at Ardnacrusha, where it drops about 30 metres through turbines before rejoining the old river channel via a tailrace. The original plant had three vertical Francis turbines of about 21 MW each and one smaller unit; a fourth, a Kaplan turbine, was added in 1934, bringing capacity to roughly 86 MW. A double lock at Ardnacrusha — with a lift of about 30 m, among the largest in Europe at the time — maintained navigation, and a fish pass was included to allow salmon to bypass the works.

Construction

Works began in 1925 and employed several thousand workers, including many German specialists; a purpose-built workers' community and narrow-gauge railways served the site. The project faced labour disputes and difficult ground conditions but was completed ahead of much contemporary expectation. Ardnacrusha began supplying power in 1929, feeding the new national grid — the "Shannon Scheme" lines that carried electricity first to Dublin and Cork and then progressively across the country under the newly founded Electricity Supply Board (ESB, 1927).

Impact

For its first years Ardnacrusha supplied the overwhelming majority of Ireland's electricity, and it catalysed rural electrification (a decades-long programme completed in the 1970s). The scheme was a potent symbol of Irish independence and modernization — proof that the Free State could execute world-scale engineering — and McLaughlin became one of the state's most celebrated engineers. In 2002 the American Society of Civil Engineers recognized Ardnacrusha as an International Historic Civil Engineering Landmark, and the station continues in operation under the ESB, its output now a modest share of national demand but still a notable renewable resource. Environmental effects included obstruction of salmon and eel migration on the Shannon, mitigated by fish passes and traps-and-trucked transport of eels.
