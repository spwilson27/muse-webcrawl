Fever of unknown origin

Fever of unknown origin (FUO), also called pyrexia of unknown origin (PUO), is a condition in which a patient has an elevated temperature (fever) whose cause remains undiagnosed after initial investigation. The classic definition, proposed by Petersdorf and Beeson in 1961, requires a temperature above 38.3 °C on several occasions, lasting at least three weeks, with no diagnosis despite one week of inpatient investigation.

Classification

Modern classifications divide FUO into four categories: classic FUO; nosocomial (hospital-acquired) FUO; neutropenic (immune-deficient) FUO; and FUO associated with HIV infection. Each category has a characteristic differential diagnosis and diagnostic tempo, with neutropenic and nosocomial fevers requiring urgent evaluation.

Causes

The differential diagnosis contains over 200 conditions, conventionally grouped into infections (such as tuberculosis, endocarditis, and occult abscesses), malignancies (especially lymphoma), non-infectious inflammatory diseases (such as adult-onset Still's disease, giant cell arteritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus), and miscellaneous causes including drug fever and factitious fever. In many series, infections remain the single largest group, while a substantial minority of cases resolve without a diagnosis ever being found.

Workup

Evaluation emphasises careful history and repeated physical examination to identify "potentially diagnostic clues", followed by stepwise testing: blood cultures, imaging, and serology, escalating to PET/CT scanning and tissue biopsy when simpler studies fail. Unfocused empirical therapy, apart from antipyretics for comfort, is generally discouraged because it can mask the underlying disease.
