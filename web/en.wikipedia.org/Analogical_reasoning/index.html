Analogical reasoning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Analogical reasoning is a form of reasoning based on the perception of a common relational structure between two situations or domains: because a source (or analogue) and a target share a pattern of relations, further features of the source are inferred to hold in the target as well. Analogy is central to learning, problem solving, scientific discovery, legal argument, and everyday cognition, though analogical inferences are plausible rather than deductively certain.

Structure

Cognitive theories, notably Dedre Gentner's structure-mapping theory, describe analogy as a mapping between a familiar source domain and a less familiar target domain. Good analogies preserve systems of relations (especially higher-order causal relations) rather than superficial object attributes — the "systematicity principle". Keith Holyoak and Paul Thagard's multiconstraint theory adds that mappings are guided by structural consistency, semantic similarity, and the reasoner's pragmatic goals.

Uses

  • Science — analogies such as the Rutherford–Bohr model of the atom (modelled on the solar system) and the wave theory of light generated testable hypotheses.
  • Law — common-law reasoning proceeds by analogising new cases to precedents.
  • Education — analogies scaffold learning by connecting the unfamiliar to the familiar.
  • Artificial intelligence — case-based reasoning and computational models of analogy apply mapping algorithms to problem solving.

Development and criticism

Children's analogical ability develops with relational knowledge and executive control. Philosophers note that analogical arguments cannot be evaluated by form alone: their strength depends on relevant similarities and known differences, as in debates over analogical arguments about animal minds or the existence of other minds.
