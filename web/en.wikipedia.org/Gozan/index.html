The Gozan (五山, literally "Five Mountains") was a network of state-sponsored Zen Buddhist temples in medieval Japan, modelled on the Chinese Five Mountains system. Ranking the leading Rinzai Zen monasteries of Kyoto and Kamakura, the system was overseen by the shogunate and flourished from the late Kamakura period through the Muromachi period.

The two sets of five

In Kyoto, the five temples were Tenryū-ji, Shōkoku-ji, Kennin-ji, Tōfuku-ji, and Manju-ji, with Nanzen-ji ranked above them all as a special "temple above the five". In Kamakura, the five were Kenchō-ji, Engaku-ji, Jufuku-ji, Jōchi-ji, and Jōmyō-ji. Rankings shifted over time as shoguns favoured different temples.

Culture

Gozan monks were not only religious figures but diplomats, administrators, and traders — conducting missions and commerce with China — and leading cultural figures. The "literature of the Five Mountains" (Gozan bungaku), written in Chinese verse and prose, and the temples' influence on ink painting, calligraphy, gardening, and tea culture, made the Gozan a central conduit of continental learning into Japan.
