Growing block universe

In the growing block view, the past and present exist while the future does not yet.

The growing block universe, or growing block theory, is a theory of time in the philosophy of time, holding that past and present events exist, while future events do not yet exist. The universe is pictured as a four-dimensional block of spacetime that continually grows as time passes: the present is the leading edge of the block, where new slices of reality come into being, and the future is literally nothing — not merely unknown.

Place among theories of time

The growing block stands between the two other main ontological views. Presentism holds that only the present exists. Eternalism (the static "block universe") holds that past, present and future are all equally real, and passage of time is an illusion of consciousness. The growing block, championed by C. D. Broad in Scientific Thought (1923) and developed by later philosophers, is meant to preserve both the reality of the past (against presentism) and the openness and coming-into-being of the future (against eternalism).

Criticisms

Critics press several problems. First, the theory seems to conflict with special relativity, which denies any privileged universal "now" along which the block could grow uniformly. Second, there is the epistemic problem raised by David Braddon-Mitchell: if the block grows, how can we know that we are located at its edge — that our "now" is the real present — rather than in its interior, especially since from the inside things would look the same? Defenders respond with various refinements, including relativistic versions based on a preferred foliation or local becoming.
