History of Malaysia

The history of Malaysia spans from prehistoric settlements in the Malay Peninsula and northern Borneo, through Indianised kingdoms and the Malacca Sultanate, to Portuguese, Dutch and British colonisation, and the formation of the modern federation in 1963.

Malacca was one of the great entrepôts of Southeast Asia.

Early history

Archaeological evidence, including the Perak Man skeleton, shows human habitation tens of thousands of years old. From the first centuries AD, trading kingdoms such as Langkasuka and the maritime empire of Srivijaya flourished, influenced by Indian religion and culture. Islam spread from the 13th century, and around 1400 Parameswara founded Malacca, which became a great trading port linking China, India and the Spice Islands.

Colonial era

The Portuguese captured Malacca in 1511; the Dutch, allied with Johor, took it in 1641. Britain entered with the founding of Penang in 1786 and Singapore in 1819 by Stamford Raffles. The Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824 divided the Malay world between British and Dutch spheres. The British gradually extended control over the Malay states through the Residential system, forming the Federated Malay States in 1895, while Sarawak was ruled by the Brooke "White Rajahs" and North Borneo by a chartered company.

War and independence

Japanese forces occupied Malaya from 1942 to 1945. Post-war, the Malayan Union proposal met strong Malay opposition, leading to the Federation of Malaya in 1948 and a communist insurgency known as the Malayan Emergency (1948–1960). The federation gained independence on 31 August 1957 under Prime Minister Tunku Abdul Rahman.

Malaysia

On 16 September 1963 Malaya, Singapore, Sarawak and North Borneo (Sabah) federated as Malaysia; Singapore separated in 1965. The new state faced Indonesian Konfrontasi and communal tensions that erupted in the riots of 13 May 1969, followed by the New Economic Policy. Rapid industrialisation under Mahathir Mohamad transformed the economy from the 1980s.
