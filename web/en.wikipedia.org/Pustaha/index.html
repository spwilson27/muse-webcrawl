Pustaha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pustaha is the magic book of the datu, the ritual specialists and priests of the Batak peoples of North Sumatra, Indonesia. Written in the Batak script in an archaic ritual language, pustaha contain the accumulated knowledge of the datu: divination, magic formulas, medicine, calendars and instructions for rituals.

Form and materials

Pustaha are made from a single long strip of bark — usually of the agarwood (Aquilaria) tree — folded accordion-style between two wooden covers, sometimes elaborately carved with lizard or serpent motifs associated with the protective deity Boraspati. The text is written in black ink made from soot and plant extracts. Larger examples can be several metres long when unfolded.

Contents

The books record the hadatuan, the art of the datu: techniques of divination using the calendar (parhalaan) and astrology; protective and aggressive magic; recipes for medicines and poisons; and myths, genealogies and instructions for making ritual objects such as the tunggal panaluan staff. The writing system and subject matter reflect centuries of Indian influence on Batak religion, blended with indigenous beliefs.

Collections

Because Batak ritual knowledge was closely guarded, surviving pustaha were largely collected by missionaries, colonial officials and ethnographers in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Significant collections are held by museums and libraries in the Netherlands, Germany and Indonesia, and the manuscripts are important sources for the study of pre-Christian and pre-Islamic Batak religion and of Southeast Asian manuscript traditions.
