Peripeteia

Aristotle's Poetics introduced peripeteia as the reversal of fortune in tragedy.

Peripeteia (Greek: περιπέτεια, "a sudden turn of events") is a reversal of circumstances, or turning point, in a story. The concept originates with Aristotle's Poetics, where it is defined as "a change by which the action veers round to its opposite, subject always to our rule of probability or necessity".

In Aristotle

Aristotle considered peripeteia, together with anagnorisis (recognition), a defining feature of the finest tragedies. His prime example is Sophocles' Oedipus Rex, in which a messenger arrives intending to comfort Oedipus by revealing that Polybus was not his father — and instead, by revealing the truth of his origins, produces the opposite effect, precipitating Oedipus's downfall.

Usage

The term is applied to sudden reversals of fortune in drama and fiction generally — the hero's apparent triumph turning to disaster, or the villain's scheme recoiling upon its author. In modern narrative theory it overlaps with the plot twist, though Aristotle's notion specifically involves a reversal produced plausibly and necessarily by the action itself.
