Oscar Wilde

Born: 16 October 1854, Dublin, Ireland
Died: 30 November 1900, Paris, France
Occupation: Playwright, novelist, poet
Notable works: The Picture of Dorian Gray, The Importance of Being Earnest

Oscar Fingal O'Fflahertie Wills Wilde (16 October 1854 – 30 November 1900) was an Irish poet and playwright. After writing in different forms throughout the 1880s, he became one of the most popular playwrights in London in the early 1890s. He is best remembered for his epigrams and plays, his novel The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890), and the circumstances of his criminal conviction for gross indecency for consensual homosexual acts, imprisonment, and early death from meningitis at age 46.

Life and career

Wilde was born in Dublin, the son of the surgeon Sir William Wilde and the poet Jane Wilde, and studied classics at Trinity College Dublin and Magdalen College, Oxford, where he became associated with the Aesthetic movement and the philosophy of "art for art's sake". After a successful lecture tour of the United States in 1882, he published poems, fairy tales (The Happy Prince and Other Tales, 1888), and essays including "The Decay of Lying" and "The Critic as Artist". His society comedies — Lady Windermere's Fan (1892), A Woman of No Importance (1893), An Ideal Husband and The Importance of Being Earnest (both 1895) — made him the toast of London theatre.

Trials and imprisonment

In 1895 Wilde sued the Marquess of Queensberry for libel after Queensberry accused him of homosexuality; the trial exposed Wilde's private life, the suit collapsed, and Wilde was himself arrested and convicted of gross indecency. He served two years' hard labour, mostly at Reading Gaol, an experience he recorded in De Profundis (written 1897) and The Ballad of Reading Gaol (1898). Released in 1897, he lived in exile in France under the name Sebastian Melmoth and died in Paris in 1900. He is buried at Père Lachaise Cemetery.

Legacy

Wilde is regarded as one of the great wits of the English language, and his plays remain staples of the theatre. His conviction is now widely seen as unjust; in 2017 he was among the men pardoned posthumously under the UK's "Turing law".
