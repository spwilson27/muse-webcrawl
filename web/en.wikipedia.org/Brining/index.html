Brining

Brining is a food-processing technique in which food — typically meat or cheese — is treated with brine, a solution of salt in water, by soaking, injecting or spraying. Brining is used both to preserve food and to improve its flavour, moisture content and tenderness when cooked.

Wet and dry brining

In wet brining, the food is submerged in a salt solution, commonly around 3–6% salt by weight of the water, sometimes with sugar and spices, for hours or days under refrigeration. In dry brining, the food is coated with dry salt (often kosher salt) and left uncovered in the refrigerator; the salt first draws out moisture, then dissolves in it and is reabsorbed, achieving a similar effect with a more concentrated flavour.

Mechanism

Salt affects meat in several ways. Through osmosis and diffusion it seasons the interior of the food. It also dissolves some muscle proteins (notably myosin), which loosens the protein structure and increases the meat's capacity to hold water during cooking, so brined meat loses less moisture and tastes juicier. In cheese making, brining — as with feta, mozzarella and many hard cheeses — controls bacterial growth, firms the rind and regulates moisture.

Uses

Brining is traditional for corned beef and pickled herring, common for poultry such as Thanksgiving turkey and for pork chops, and essential in the production of brined cheeses. Vegetables are brined in pickling and fermentation (as in sauerkraut and kimchi), although those processes are usually classed under pickling rather than brining alone.
