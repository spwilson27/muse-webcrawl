Flutter (aeronautics)

Flutter is an aeroelastic phenomenon: a dynamic instability of an elastic structure in a fluid flow, caused by positive feedback between the body's deflection and the forces exerted by the fluid. It is a potentially explosive vibration that can cause structural failure in bridges, aircraft wings, chimneys, and other structures.

Mechanism

In aircraft flutter, a wing's bending and torsional modes couple through the airflow. When the phase relationship becomes unfavourable, the structure extracts energy from the airstream on each oscillation cycle, and amplitude grows until damping or structural failure intervenes. Flutter occurs above a critical speed for a given configuration; certification rules require demonstrating a flutter-free margin well beyond an aircraft's design dive speed.

Types

Classic bending–torsion flutter involves coupled wing modes. Control-surface flutter involves a hinged surface such as an aileron oscillating about its hinge line, often prevented by mass balancing ahead of the hinge. Other forms include stall flutter, transonic "buzz," panel flutter on thin skins, and whirl flutter of propeller–nacelle systems.

History

Flutter contributed to several early aviation accidents and was studied intensively in the 1930s, notably after incidents such as the 1931 crash of a Fokker F-10 in Kansas. The collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940, though strictly an aeroelastic torsional divergence/flutter event, made the phenomenon famous. Modern analysis uses computational fluid dynamics coupled with structural models, wind-tunnel testing, and instrumented flight flutter tests with excitation systems.

Prevention

Design remedies include increasing stiffness, redistributing mass, balancing control surfaces, limiting speed, and — in modern fly-by-wire aircraft — active flutter suppression using sensors and control laws.
