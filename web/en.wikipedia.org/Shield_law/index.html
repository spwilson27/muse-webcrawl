Shield law

Shield law
AreaMedia law, freedom of the press
PurposeProtect journalists from compelled disclosure of confidential sources and unpublished material
United StatesNo federal statute; statutes in most states (first: Maryland, 1896)
Key caseBranzburg v. Hayes (1972)

A shield law is a statute that gives journalists a privilege — absolute or qualified — to refuse to testify about, or disclose, confidential sources and unpublished information obtained in newsgathering. The rationale is that a free press depends on informants who would dry up if reporters could be forced to unmask them. Shield laws are most developed in the United States, where they exist at state level, but analogous protections appear in many other democracies.

United States

Maryland enacted the first American shield law in 1896 after a reporter was jailed for refusing to name a source. Most states — 49 states and the District of Columbia by most counts — now have shield laws or court-recognised reporter's privileges, though they vary widely: some cover all media, others were written for print and broadcast and raise questions about bloggers and freelancers; some are absolute, while most are qualified, yielding when a litigant shows the information is highly relevant, unavailable elsewhere, and needed for a compelling interest. Several states recognise the privilege through judicial interpretation of state constitutions instead.

Case law

In Branzburg v. Hayes (1972), the US Supreme Court held 5–4 that the First Amendment does not exempt reporters from the duty of every citizen to testify before a grand jury. Justice Powell's influential concurrence, however, suggested a balancing test, and lower federal courts have widely read the case to leave room for a qualified privilege in many settings — especially in civil cases. Famous confrontations include Judith Miller of The New York Times, jailed 85 days in 2005 for refusing to identify the source who exposed CIA officer Valerie Plame, and the 2013 seizure of Associated Press phone records, which renewed debate on federal protections.

Federal proposals

The United States has no federal shield statute; bills such as the Free Flow of Information Act have repeatedly passed one chamber of Congress without enactment. Instead, internal Justice Department guidelines restrict subpoenas to the media. A bipartisan bill styled the PRESS Act, which would broadly bar compelled disclosure by journalists, passed the House of Representatives unanimously in 2024 but did not become law.

Elsewhere

Many European countries protect sources through statute or constitutional jurisprudence — Sweden's protections are especially strong and date to the 18th century — and the European Court of Human Rights recognised source protection as a basic condition of press freedom in Goodwin v. United Kingdom (1996). Limits typically appear in national-security and serious-crime cases.
