Igneous rock

Common igneous rocks: granite, basalt, obsidian
SubdivisionsIntrusive (plutonic); extrusive (volcanic)
ExamplesGranite, gabbro, basalt, rhyolite, pumice, obsidian
PrecursorMagma or lava

Igneous rock (from Latin ignis, fire), or magmatic rock, is one of the three main rock types, the others being sedimentary and metamorphic. Igneous rocks are formed through the cooling and solidification of magma or lava. The magma can be derived from partial melts of existing rocks in either a planet's mantle or crust. Typically, the melting is caused by one or more of three processes: an increase in temperature, a decrease in pressure, or a change in composition.

Geological significance

Igneous and metamorphic rocks make up 90–95% of the top 16 km of the Earth's crust by volume. Igneous rocks form about 15% of the Earth's current land surface. Most of the Earth's oceanic crust is made of igneous rock, principally basalt produced at mid-ocean ridges.

Classification

Igneous rocks are divided into two main categories. Intrusive (plutonic) rocks result when magma cools and crystallises slowly within the crust, producing coarse-grained textures such as granite and gabbro. Extrusive (volcanic) rocks result from magma reaching the surface as lava and cooling rapidly, producing fine-grained or glassy textures such as basalt, rhyolite, pumice and obsidian. Rocks are further classified by mineral composition — felsic rocks are rich in silica and feldspar, mafic rocks rich in magnesium and iron — and by silica content, from ultramafic (<45% SiO2) to felsic (>63%).

Formation environments

Magma forms at divergent plate boundaries, where decompression melting of the mantle produces basaltic oceanic crust; at convergent boundaries, where water released from a subducting slab lowers the melting point of the mantle wedge, generating volcanoes of arcs and, through differentiation, granitic continental crust; and at intraplate hotspots such as Hawaii and Iceland.

Textures

Crystal size records cooling history: slow cooling yields large, interlocking crystals (phaneritic texture), rapid cooling yields microscopic crystals (aphanitic) or glass. Porphyritic texture — large phenocrysts in a fine groundmass — records two stages of cooling. Volcanic fragments ejected during eruptions form pyroclastic rocks such as tuff.
