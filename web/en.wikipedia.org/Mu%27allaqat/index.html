The Muʿallaqat (Arabic: al-Muʿallaqāt, "the suspended ones") is a collection of seven — in some accounts ten — long pre-Islamic Arabic odes (qaṣīdas) regarded as the finest poetry of the era and foundational works of Arabic literature.

Name and legend

According to tradition the poems were written in gold on Coptic linen and suspended (hung) on the walls of the Kaaba in Mecca — hence the name, also interpreted as "the prized/hanging poems" or as necklaces strung with jewels. Modern scholars treat the Kaaba story as legend but agree on the poems' early canonization by collectors such as Hammad al-Rawiya in the 8th century.

The poems

The traditional seven poets are Imru' al-Qays, Tarafa ibn al-ʿAbd, Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma, Labid ibn Rabiʿa, ʿAmr ibn Kulthum, ʿAntara ibn Shaddad and al-Harith ibn Hilliza. Some lists add al-Aʿsha, al-Nabigha and ʿAbid ibn al-Abras. Each ode typically opens with a lament at the deserted campsite of the beloved, moves through a journey section describing the poet's camel or horse and the desert, and ends with boast, praise or satire.
