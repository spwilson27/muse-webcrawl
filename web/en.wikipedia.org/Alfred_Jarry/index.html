Alfred Jarry (8 September 1873 – 1 November 1907) was a French playwright, novelist and poet whose grotesque comedy Ubu Roi (1896) scandalised Paris and is now considered a founding work of modern avant-garde theatre, anticipating Dada, Surrealism and the Theatre of the Absurd. He also invented the mock-philosophical "science" of 'pataphysics — the science of imaginary solutions.

Life and Ubu

Born in Laval, Brittany, Jarry developed the character of Père Ubu from schoolboy puppet plays mocking a physics teacher in Rennes. Staged in Paris on 10 December 1896, Ubu Roi opened with the word "Merdre!" and depicted the cowardly, gluttonous Ubu murdering his way to the Polish throne — a savage parody of bourgeois greed and authority. The single riotous performance made Jarry notorious. He wrote further Ubu plays and adopted Ubu as a personal alter ego, speaking of himself in the royal "we".

Other work and 'pataphysics

Jarry's novels include Days and Nights (1897), Messalina (1901) and The Supermale (1902); his posthumous Exploits and Opinions of Dr. Faustroll, Pataphysician (1911) codified 'pataphysics, "the science of the particular", which later inspired the Collège de 'pataphysique (founded 1948) and artists including Marcel Duchamp. Famed for his eccentric life — cycling, fencing, absinthe, and a pistol he fired at social gatherings — Jarry died in poverty in Paris at 34, of tuberculosis aggravated by alcohol and ether use.

Legacy

Jarry's influence extends through Surrealism, Eugène Ionesco and the absurdists, and his spiral-bellied figure of Ubu remains an emblem of anarchic satire.
