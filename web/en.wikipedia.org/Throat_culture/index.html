Throat culture

Throat culture
PurposeDetect bacterial infection of the throat, especially group A Streptococcus
SpecimenSwab of the tonsils and posterior pharynx
TurnaroundUsually 24–48 hours

A throat culture is a laboratory diagnostic test that evaluates a swab sample taken from the back of the throat for the presence of a bacterial infection. It is most commonly performed to identify group A streptococcal infection (Streptococcus pyogenes), the cause of streptococcal pharyngitis ("strep throat"), but can also detect other organisms such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Corynebacterium diphtheriae and various fungi.

Procedure

A health professional depresses the patient's tongue and rubs a sterile cotton swab over the tonsils and the back of the throat (posterior pharynx), taking care to contact any red or exudative areas while avoiding the tongue and cheeks. The swab is then placed in a transport medium and sent to the laboratory, where the sample is streaked onto a blood agar plate and incubated, typically for 24 to 48 hours. Beta-haemolytic colonies suggest group A streptococci, which can be confirmed by further tests such as the bacitracin susceptibility test or a PYR test.

Clinical role

Throat culture has long been considered the reference ("gold") standard for diagnosing strep throat, with high specificity and sensitivity when properly collected. Its main disadvantage is the delay of one to two days before results are available, which is why many clinics first use a rapid antigen detection test: negative rapid results in children and adolescents are commonly backed up by a throat culture, while positive rapid results are generally treated without culture confirmation. Identifying and treating group A streptococcal pharyngitis helps prevent complications such as rheumatic fever and peritonsillar abscess.

Limitations

A positive culture does not always distinguish true infection from asymptomatic carriage, and a poorly collected swab, prior antibiotic use or delays in transport can produce false negatives. Culture also cannot detect the viruses that cause the majority of sore throats.
