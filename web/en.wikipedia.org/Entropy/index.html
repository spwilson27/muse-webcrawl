Entropy

Entropy is a scientific concept, as well as a measurable physical property, most commonly associated with a state of disorder, randomness, or uncertainty. In thermodynamics, entropy is a state function whose change in a reversible process equals the heat transferred divided by the absolute temperature, ΔS = ∫ dQrev/T. The second law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

History

The concept was developed by Rudolf Clausius in the 1850s–1860s, building on the work of Sadi Carnot on heat engines; Clausius coined the name in 1865 from the Greek entropia, "a turning towards". Ludwig Boltzmann later gave entropy a statistical interpretation, relating it to the number of microscopic configurations (microstates) consistent with a macroscopic state: S = kB ln W, the formula inscribed on his tombstone.

Statistical interpretation

In statistical mechanics, entropy measures the amount of information needed to specify the exact microstate of a system given its macroscopic description. Josiah Willard Gibbs generalized the formula to an ensemble average, and it was further extended by John von Neumann to quantum systems.

Information theory

Claude Shannon introduced an analogous quantity in information theory in 1948 — the entropy of a message source — measuring the average information content or unpredictability of a symbol sequence. The mathematical similarity between the two formulas led Shannon, on von Neumann's advice, to adopt the name "entropy".
