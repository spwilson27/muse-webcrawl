Anaerobic respiration

Anaerobic respiration is respiration using electron acceptors other than molecular oxygen (O2). Like aerobic respiration, it involves an electron transport chain that generates a proton gradient to drive ATP synthesis, but the terminal electron acceptor has a lower reduction potential than oxygen, so less energy is obtained per unit of substrate oxidized.

Electron acceptors

Depending on the organism, the terminal electron acceptors include nitrate (denitrification), sulfate (sulfate reduction, producing hydrogen sulfide), carbon dioxide (methanogenesis in archaea, producing methane), ferric iron (Fe(III)), fumarate, and various other oxidized compounds. These processes are carried out principally by bacteria and archaea in oxygen-free environments such as waterlogged soils, sediments, wetlands, and the guts of animals.

Comparison with fermentation

Anaerobic respiration should not be confused with fermentation, which does not use an electron transport chain or chemiosmosis and generates ATP only by substrate-level phosphorylation. Because it exploits full redox chains, anaerobic respiration yields considerably more ATP than fermentation, though less than aerobic respiration.

Significance

Anaerobic respiratory processes are major drivers of the global nitrogen, sulfur, and carbon cycles: denitrification returns nitrogen gas to the atmosphere, sulfate reducers dominate carbon mineralization in marine sediments, and methanogens produce much of the world's methane.
