Ionian Islands

The Ionian Islands (Greek: Ιόνια νησιά) are a group of islands in the Ionian Sea, west of mainland Greece. They are traditionally called the Heptanese ("Seven Islands"), though the group includes many smaller islands as well as the seven principal ones: Corfu, Paxos, Lefkada, Ithaca, Kefalonia, Zakynthos, and Kythira (the last lying off the southern tip of the Peloponnese).

History

The islands were settled by Greeks in antiquity; Ithaca was the legendary home of Odysseus. They later came under Roman, Byzantine, and Norman rule. From the late 14th century most of the islands belonged to the Republic of Venice, which ruled Corfu for some 400 years and repelled several Ottoman sieges. Venice's long rule left a lasting Italianate imprint on architecture, music, and cuisine.

After the fall of Venice in 1797, the islands passed through French, Russo-Ottoman, and renewed French control before becoming a British protectorate, the United States of the Ionian Islands, in 1815. Britain ceded the islands to Greece in 1864 as a gesture of goodwill on the accession of King George I.

Geography and culture

The islands are greener and wetter than the Aegean, with olive groves and cypresses, and experience frequent seismic activity. Kefalonia is the largest. The region's culture reflects Venetian, French, and British influences, visible in Corfu's old town, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and in traditions such as kantades music and carnival. Today the islands form one of Greece's 13 administrative regions and are major tourist destinations.
