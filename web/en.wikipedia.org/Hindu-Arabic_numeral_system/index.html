The Hindu–Arabic numeral system is a positional decimal numeral system for representing numbers using ten digits. Developed in India, it was transmitted to Europe through Arabic-language works on arithmetic, hence its double name. It is the most common system for the symbolic representation of numbers in the world today.

Principles

The system is positional: the value of a digit depends on its place, so the 2 in 20, 200 and 0.02 means two tens, two hundreds and two hundredths respectively. It is also decimal, grouping by powers of ten. Crucially it includes a symbol for zero, which serves both as a placeholder and as a number in its own right. These features make arithmetic algorithms — addition, subtraction, multiplication and long division — far simpler than with additive systems such as Roman numerals.

History

Positional notation with nine digits appeared in India by the early centuries CE; the astronomer Brahmagupta gave rules for computing with zero in 628 CE. Inscriptions at Gwalior (876 CE) show a recognisable early zero. Persian and Arab mathematicians adopted the system: al-Khwarizmi's On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals (c. 825) described it, and his name, Latinised as "Algoritmi", gave us the word algorithm.

In Europe the system spread slowly. Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) championed it for merchants, but abacists and algorists disputed for centuries; Florence even banned the new numerals in 1299 over forgery fears. Printing and commerce finally settled the contest by the 16th century.

Variants

The glyphs evolved regionally: Western Arabic numerals (0–9) used in Europe and the Americas, Eastern Arabic numerals (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) used in much of the Middle East, and many Indic scripts with their own digit forms, all sharing the same positional logic. See also decimal and numeral system.
