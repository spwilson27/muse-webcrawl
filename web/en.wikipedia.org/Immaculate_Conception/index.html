Immaculate Conception

Defined8 December 1854, bull Ineffabilis Deus
Defined byPope Pius IX
Feast day8 December
ConcernsConception of Mary, free from original sin

The Immaculate Conception is the belief that the Virgin Mary was free of original sin from the moment of her conception. It is one of the four Marian dogmas of the Catholic Church. It should not be confused with the virginal conception of Jesus, which concerns Christ's conception by the Holy Spirit. The dogma was defined by Pope Pius IX in 1854, and the feast is celebrated on 8 December.

Doctrine

According to the dogma, Mary was preserved exempt from all stain of original sin by a singular grace and privilege granted by God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ. She is held to have remained free from personal sin throughout her life as well. The doctrine does not claim that Mary did not need a saviour, but that she was redeemed in a more perfect manner, in advance.

History

The belief developed in medieval devotion and theology. Franciscan scholars, notably Duns Scotus, defended it against objections associated with Thomas Aquinas, arguing that preservation from sin was a more fitting redemption than liberation after falling into it. The Council of Trent left the question open, and devotion spread widely in Spain and its empire.

Definition and reception

Pius IX defined the dogma in the bull Ineffabilis Deus on 8 December 1854, after consulting the world's bishops. Four years later, the apparitions at Lourdes — where the figure reportedly identified herself as "the Immaculate Conception" — were taken by many Catholics as confirmation. Most Protestant churches reject the dogma as lacking scriptural warrant, and Eastern Orthodoxy, while honouring Mary's purity, does not accept the Latin doctrine of original sin on which it rests.
