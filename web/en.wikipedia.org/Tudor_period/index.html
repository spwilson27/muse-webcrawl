The Tudor period in English history lasted from 1485 to 1603, covering the reign of the Tudor dynasty. It is often associated with the English Renaissance, the Reformation, and the emergence of England as a significant maritime and colonial power.

Political history

The period began with Henry VII's victory at Bosworth Field. Under the Tudors the power of the central monarchy grew at the expense of the nobility, Parliament's role expanded, and the administration of the kingdom became more professional. The Acts of Union legally incorporated Wales into England.

Religion

Religious change defined the era. Henry VIII's break with Rome in the 1530s created the Church of England; Edward VI's reign pushed Protestant doctrine further, Mary I restored Catholicism and persecuted Protestants, and Elizabeth I's religious settlement of 1559 established a broadly Protestant church with compromises intended to unify the realm.

Society and culture

Population growth, inflation, enclosure and the growth of London reshaped daily life. The later Tudor years saw an extraordinary flowering of literature and drama, including the works of William Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe and Edmund Spenser, alongside advances in exploration and cartography.
