"The Lottery" is a short story written by American author Shirley Jackson, first published in the June 26, 1948, issue of The New Yorker. The story describes a fictional small town which observes an annual rite known as "the lottery", in which a member of the community is selected by chance. The shocking consequence — the selected person is stoned to death by the remaining villagers — is revealed only at the end.

The New Yorker received a torrent of letters from readers outraged, confused, and disturbed by the story, many of whom cancelled their subscriptions. The story has been dramatized several times and adapted for television, stage, and film. It is regarded as one of the most famous and widely anthologized short stories in American literature.

Plot summary

In a small village of about 300 residents, the locals gather on the morning of June 27 for the annual lottery. Children arrive first and begin collecting stones, stuffing their pockets and piling them in the square. The lottery is conducted by Mr. Summers, a civic-minded man who also runs the coal business, assisted by Mr. Graves, the postmaster, who brings the black wooden box containing the paper slips.

The heads of families draw slips from the box one by one. Bill Hutchinson draws the slip marked with a black spot. His family — his wife Tessie and their three children — must then draw again among themselves. Tessie draws the marked slip. As she protests that the process was unfair, the villagers close in and stone her to death, with Mrs. Delacroix selecting a stone so large she needs both hands, and even Tessie's young son Davy given a few pebbles to throw.

Themes

The story explores the dangers of blindly following tradition, mob psychology, scapegoating, and the capacity for violence lurking beneath the surface of ordinary, respectable communities. Jackson juxtaposes the cheerful, mundane details of village life — gossip about tractors and taxes — with the ritual's barbaric conclusion. Scholars have also read the story as a commentary on conformity, patriarchy, and humanity's innate inhumanity.

Reception and interpretation

Initial reader reaction was overwhelmingly negative; Jackson later recalled that many readers wrote asking where such lotteries were held and whether they could attend. Over time, however, the story became a staple of American high school and college curricula, valued for its irony, symbolism (the battered black box, the three-legged stool, the stones), and open-ended meaning. Jackson herself consistently declined to explain the story's significance, saying only that she hoped "by setting a particularly brutal ancient rite in the present and in my own village, to shock the story's readers with a graphic dramatization of the pointless violence and general inhumanity in their own lives."

Adaptations

The story was adapted as a radio play (1951), a ballet (1953), a one-act opera by William H. Schimmel, a 1969 short film directed by Larry Yust (the most famous version, long shown in classrooms), and television movies in 1996 and later versions. It also inspired episodes of television series and remains a frequent subject of parody and homage.
