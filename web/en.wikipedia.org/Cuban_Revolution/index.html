The Cuban Revolution (Spanish: Revolución cubana) was the military and political effort to overthrow Fulgencio Batista's dictatorship which reigned as the government of Cuba between 1952 and 1959. It began with the assault on the Moncada Barracks on 26 July 1953 and ended on 1 January 1959, when Batista fled the country and the cities of Santa Clara and Santiago de Cuba were taken by rebels, led by Fidel Castro's 26th of July Movement.

Background

Fulgencio Batista seized power in a military coup in March 1952, cancelling elections and governing with the support of the United States and Cuban business elites. Corruption, inequality and repression fuelled opposition among students, workers and political parties.

The insurrection

After the failed Moncada attack, Castro was imprisoned and then exiled to Mexico, where he organised a new expedition. On 2 December 1956 the yacht Granma landed 82 expeditionaries in Oriente Province; most were killed or captured, but a small core — including Castro, his brother Raúl and Ernesto "Che" Guevara — survived in the Sierra Maestra and built a guerrilla army. Urban underground movements and a second front in the Escambray Mountains broadened the insurrection. In late December 1958 Guevara's column won the decisive Battle of Santa Clara, and Batista fled on New Year's Day 1959.

Aftermath

The revolutionary government carried out agrarian reform, nationalised foreign-owned property and aligned Cuba with the Soviet Union, leading to the United States embargo, the failed Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961 and the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962. The revolution transformed Cuban society and made the island a focal point of Cold War politics for decades.
