Exurb

An exurb is a community lying beyond the suburbs of a city, within its commuting belt but retaining a semi-rural character and low population density. The word is a portmanteau of "extra" and "urban", coined in the 1950s by the American writer A. C. Spectorsky in his book The Exurbanites (1955), which described wealthy New Yorkers settling in the countryside beyond the suburbs.

Characteristics

Exurbs are defined less by formal boundaries than by commuting patterns and density: residents typically travel to the metropolitan area for work, while the landscape remains one of farmland, woodland and large residential lots. Because land is cheap, exurban houses tend to be large, and residents accept long commutes in exchange for space, perceived safety and rural amenities. In the United States, exurban counties were among the fastest-growing parts of many metropolitan areas during the housing booms of the 1990s and 2000s, and again during the remote-work shift of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Criticism

Urban planners criticise exurban development as an extreme form of sprawl: it consumes farmland and habitat, requires long car journeys with high emissions, and is expensive to serve with water, sewerage and schools. Defenders respond that it reflects households' preferences for space and that exurbs can provide a transition between city and countryside.
