Folklore of the United States

Folklore of the United States, also called American folklore, comprises the traditional beliefs, customs, tales, music and sayings that have developed in the United States over centuries. It draws on Indigenous Native American traditions, the cultures of European settlers, the heritage of enslaved Africans and their descendants, and the contributions of later immigrant communities, all reshaped by the American landscape and experience.

Tall tales and folk heroes

American folklore is famous for its tall tales — humorous stories of exaggerated feats. Legendary figures include Paul Bunyan, the gigantic lumberjack accompanied by Babe the Blue Ox; John Henry, the steel-driving man who raced a steam drill; Johnny Appleseed, who planted orchards across the frontier; and Davy Crockett, whose fame rested partly on embellished almanac stories. Real historical figures such as Daniel Boone and Wild Bill Hickok were absorbed into folklore during their lifetimes.

Regional and ethnic traditions

Each region preserves distinctive traditions: New England sea tales and witchcraft lore from Salem; Appalachian ballads and Jack tales; cowboy songs and legends of the American West; Cajun and Creole traditions in Louisiana; African American folktales featuring tricksters such as Br'er Rabbit; and the urban legends of the modern era, such as alligators in sewers and vanishing hitchhikers.

Native American traditions

Indigenous peoples maintain rich oral traditions far older than the United States itself, including creation stories, trickster cycles involving Coyote and Raven, and ceremonies tied to the land. These traditions are living cultural heritage, and their collection and publication have raised important questions about ownership and consent.

Study

The American Folklore Society was founded in 1888. Major collectors include Joel Chandler Harris, Zora Neale Hurston and Alan Lomax, whose field recordings preserved American folk music for the Library of Congress.
