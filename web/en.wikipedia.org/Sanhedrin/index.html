Sanhedrin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sanhedrin (Hebrew: סַנְהֶדְרִין, from Greek synedrion, "assembly") was the supreme council and tribunal of the Jewish people in the Land of Israel during the Second Temple period and afterwards. Tradition held that it consisted of 71 members — the high priest or nasi (president) presiding over 70 elders — modelled on Moses and the seventy elders of Numbers 11.

Functions

The Great Sanhedrin in Jerusalem tried capital cases, supervised the Temple service and calendar, and issued rulings on Jewish law binding on the nation. Lesser sanhedrins of 23 judges sat in larger towns. After the destruction of the Temple in 70 AD, the Sanhedrin reconstituted itself at Yavneh under Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai and became the centre of rabbinic Judaism, eventually moving to Galilee (Usha, Shefaram, Beit Shearim, Sepphoris, and Tiberias).

History

The institution's origins are debated; it is attested in Hasmonean and Herodian times, appears in the Gospels in accounts of the trial of Jesus, and continued under the patriarchate until the Roman emperor Theodosius II suppressed it around 425 AD. Sanhedrin is also the name of a tractate of the Mishnah and Talmud describing the court system. In 1807 Napoleon convened a "Grand Sanhedrin" of French Jewish notables, borrowing the ancient name.
