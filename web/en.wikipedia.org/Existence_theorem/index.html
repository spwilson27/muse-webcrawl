Existence theorem

An existence theorem guarantees that an object with stated properties exists, even when no explicit construction is given.

In mathematics, an existence theorem is a theorem which asserts the existence of a certain object. It might be a statement which begins with the phrase "there exist(s)" — an existential claim. Pure existence theorems assert that an object exists without giving an explicit example or a method for constructing it, in contrast to constructive proofs, which provide an algorithm or explicit formula producing the object.

Constructive and non-constructive proofs

Many existence proofs proceed indirectly: assuming no such object exists leads to a contradiction, or a compactness or counting argument shows the object must occur somewhere in a large space. Classic examples include proofs that use the axiom of choice, such as the existence of a basis for every vector space, and non-constructive probabilistic arguments in combinatorics, where showing that a randomly chosen object has the desired property with positive probability proves existence without exhibiting one.

Examples

Well-known existence theorems include the fundamental theorem of algebra (every non-constant polynomial has a complex root), the intermediate value theorem, existence and uniqueness theorems for solutions of ordinary differential equations such as the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, and the implicit function theorem. Debates between classical and constructive mathematics, going back to the intuitionism of L. E. J. Brouwer and the formalism of David Hilbert, have centred on how much weight to give to non-constructive existence results.
