Erikson's stages of psychosocial development

Erikson's stages of psychosocial development are a psychoanalytic theory of personality development proposed by Erik Erikson, in collaboration with Joan Erikson. The theory describes eight stages through which a healthily developing human passes from infancy to late adulthood, each characterised by a psychosocial conflict that can be resolved positively or negatively.

The eight stages

The stages are: (1) trust vs. mistrust in infancy; (2) autonomy vs. shame and doubt in early childhood; (3) initiative vs. guilt in the preschool years; (4) industry vs. inferiority in school age; (5) identity vs. role confusion in adolescence; (6) intimacy vs. isolation in young adulthood; (7) generativity vs. stagnation in middle adulthood; and (8) ego integrity vs. despair in late adulthood.

Influence

Erikson's model extended Freud's psychosexual stages by emphasising social and cultural influences across the whole lifespan. The concept of the adolescent "identity crisis", popularised by Erikson, became a central idea in developmental psychology. A ninth stage, addressing the challenges of very old age, was added by Joan Erikson in an extended edition of their work.

Criticism

Critics note that the stages are loosely defined, culturally specific to Western societies, and difficult to test empirically, though the framework remains widely used in education and counselling.
