A potato chip (known as a crisp in British and Irish English) is a thin slice of potato that is deep fried, baked or dehydrated until crunchy. Potato chips are one of the world's most popular snack foods, sold worldwide in sealed bags with a wide range of flavourings, from plain salted to salt and vinegar, cheese and onion, and numerous regional varieties.

Origins

Recipes for shaved potatoes fried crisp appear in English cookery books of the early 19th century, including William Kitchiner's The Cook's Oracle (1817). A famous American legend credits chef George Crum of Saratoga Springs, New York, with inventing chips in 1853 after a customer complained that his fried potatoes were too thick. Commercial production grew in the 20th century with mechanical peelers and continuous fryers; Herman Lay built a major distribution business in the American South, and his company eventually became Frito-Lay, maker of Lay's chips.

Production and varieties

Chips are made by slicing washed potatoes to about 1–2 mm, rinsing off surface starch, frying in oil at around 180 °C until golden, then salting or seasoning. Kettle-cooked chips are fried in batches for a harder, crunchier texture. "Stackable" chips such as Pringles are formed from a potato-based dough. Regional forms include thick-cut chips, ruffled (crinkle-cut) chips and tortilla-style potato snacks. Plain chips contain roughly 530 kcal per 100 g, along with significant fat and salt, which has prompted baked and reduced-fat variants and regulatory attention to the acrylamide that forms during frying.
