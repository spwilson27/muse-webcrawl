Religion in Ireland

St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin.

Religion in Ireland has historically been dominated by Christianity, above all the Roman Catholic Church, whose influence over Irish society, education and politics was profound for most of the twentieth century. The island's religious geography is entangled with its political division: the Republic of Ireland is majority Catholic, while Northern Ireland (part of the United Kingdom) has large Catholic and Protestant (Church of Ireland, Presbyterian and Methodist) populations whose identities aligned historically with nationalist and unionist politics.

Demographics

In the Republic's 2022 census, about 69% identified as Roman Catholic — down from over 90% in the 1970s — about 14% reported no religion, and the Church of Ireland accounted for roughly 2%, with growing Orthodox, Muslim and other communities through immigration. In Northern Ireland's 2021 census, Catholics (about 42%) for the first time slightly outnumbered those of Protestant and other Christian backgrounds (about 37%), with around 17% reporting no religion.

History and change

Ireland was Christianised from the fifth century, traditionally by St Patrick. The Reformation left the established Church of Ireland Protestant while most of the population remained Catholic, enduring penal laws until Catholic emancipation in 1829. After independence, the 1937 Constitution recognised the "special position" of the Catholic Church (removed by referendum in 1972). From the 1990s, clerical abuse scandals, urbanisation and social liberalisation sharply reduced practice and authority: referendums legalised divorce (1995), same-sex marriage (2015) and abortion (2018), each against Church teaching.
