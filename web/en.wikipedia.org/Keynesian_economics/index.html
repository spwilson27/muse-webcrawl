Keynesian economics

Keynesian economics is a macroeconomic theory of total spending in the economy (aggregate demand) and its effects on output, employment, and inflation, developed by British economist John Maynard Keynes during the 1930s in an attempt to understand the Great Depression. It is set out principally in his General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936).

Core ideas

Keynes argued that aggregate demand determines overall economic activity, that markets can settle at underemployment equilibrium, and that wages and prices are sticky. Because private spending can collapse in downturns, government fiscal policy — public spending and tax cuts, even financed by deficits — should be used to stabilize demand. He emphasized the consumption function, the multiplier, liquidity preference in interest-rate determination, and the role of uncertainty and "animal spirits" in investment.

Development

Post-war Keynesianism, formalized in the IS–LM model by John Hicks and others and combined with classical theory in the neoclassical synthesis, dominated policy in Western economies until the stagflation of the 1970s. Responses included monetarism, New Keynesian economics (with microfoundations and price rigidities), and post-Keynesian schools stressing fundamental uncertainty.

Influence

Keynesian ideas underpinned Bretton Woods, post-war welfare-state economics, and the fiscal stimulus responses to the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.
