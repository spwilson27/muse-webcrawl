Voltaire (born François-Marie Arouet; 21 November 1694 – 30 May 1778) was a French Enlightenment writer, philosopher and historian, famous for his wit, his advocacy of civil liberties — including freedom of speech and religion — and his criticism of the Catholic Church and the French institutions of his day.

Life

Arouet adopted the pen name Voltaire in 1718. His satires repeatedly brought him into conflict with authority: he was imprisoned in the Bastille and exiled to England (1726–1728), where exposure to British thought and Newton's science inspired his Lettres philosophiques (1734). He later lived at the court of Frederick the Great in Potsdam, and spent his final decades at Ferney, near Geneva, becoming a wealthy and celebrated figure across Europe.

Works

Voltaire was extraordinarily prolific, writing plays, poetry, novels, essays, history and scientific works. His best-known work, the satirical novella Candide (1759), mocks the optimistic philosophy that "all is for the best". Other major works include Zadig (1747), the Dictionnaire philosophique (1764) and histories such as The Age of Louis XIV.

Advocacy

In his later years Voltaire campaigned against judicial injustice, most famously in the Calas affair, in which the Protestant Jean Calas was executed on a false charge of murdering his son; Voltaire's efforts led to Calas's posthumous exoneration. His remains were transferred to the Panthéon in Paris in 1791 during the French Revolution.
