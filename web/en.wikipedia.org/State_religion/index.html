State religion

A state religion (also called an official religion or established church) is a religion or creed officially endorsed by a sovereign state. A state with an official religion, while not secular, is not necessarily a theocracy, since officials of the state religion need not hold governmental power.

History

State sponsorship of religion is ancient: Egyptian pharaohs embodied divine kingship, and Ashoka promoted Buddhism in the Mauryan Empire. In 380 CE the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the state church of the Roman Empire. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) let rulers choose the religion of their realm (cuius regio, eius religio).

Current state religions

Examples include the Church of England in England, Lutheran churches in parts of Scandinavia, Sunni Islam in several Middle Eastern states, Theravada Buddhism in Cambodia and Sri Lanka, and Roman Catholicism in Costa Rica and Malta. Iran and Vatican City are commonly described as theocracies.

Disestablishment

Many countries have disestablished former state churches, including Sweden (2000) and Norway (2012, partially). The First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution prohibits an establishment of religion at the federal level.
