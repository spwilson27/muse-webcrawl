Archaic humans is a broad category denoting any of a number of varieties of Homo that existed from roughly 600,000 to 200,000 years ago, in the period after Homo erectus and before or contemporaneous with early anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens). Archaic humans are distinguished from anatomically modern humans by features such as a thicker skull, prominent brow ridges (supraorbital torus), and the general absence of a strongly protruding chin.

Terminology and classification

The term "archaic Homo sapiens" was once widely used for these fossils, but many palaeoanthropologists now assign them to separate species, most commonly Homo heidelbergensis. The category has no single agreed taxonomic rank; instead it groups Middle Pleistocene hominins that combine derived traits shared with later humans (such as enlarged braincases of 1,100–1,400 cm³) with primitive traits retained from H. erectus.

Major groups

  • Homo heidelbergensis — known from Africa and Europe; often proposed as the last common ancestor of Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans.
  • Neanderthals (Homo neanderthalensis) — occupied Europe and western Asia until about 40,000 years ago.
  • Denisovans — known mainly from ancient DNA and a few fossils from Siberia and Tibet.
  • Homo naledi — a small-brained species from South Africa that survived surprisingly late, to roughly 236,000 years ago.
  • Homo rhodesiensis — proposed for African fossils such as the Kabwe (Broken Hill) skull.

Interbreeding

Ancient DNA studies have shown that archaic humans interbred with anatomically modern humans as the latter dispersed out of Africa. Present-day non-African genomes carry roughly 1–4% Neanderthal ancestry, and some Oceanian populations carry additional Denisovan ancestry. Genes inherited from archaic humans have been linked to immunity, skin and hair traits, and adaptation to high altitude.

Brain expansion

Archaic humans show a marked increase in endocranial volume compared with Homo erectus, alongside more sophisticated tool traditions such as the prepared-core (Levallois) technique and hafted spears; the wooden Schöningen spears, around 300,000 years old, are attributed to archaic humans.
