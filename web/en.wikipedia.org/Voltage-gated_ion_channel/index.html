Voltage-gated ion channel

Voltage-gated ion channels are a class of transmembrane proteins that form ion channels which open or close in response to changes in the electrical membrane potential near the channel. They are crucial to the function of electrically excitable cells such as neurons and muscle cells, where they generate and propagate action potentials. Major families are selective for sodium (NaV), potassium (KV), calcium (CaV), or chloride ions.

Voltage-gated channels open and close as membrane potential changes.

Structure

Voltage-gated channels are typically built from subunits whose transmembrane segments form a central pore. A key feature is the voltage-sensing domain: the fourth transmembrane segment (S4) carries regularly spaced positively charged arginine residues that move within the membrane in response to voltage changes, coupling the electric field to the opening of the pore's activation gate. Sodium and calcium channels are formed from a single large polypeptide with four homologous domains, whereas most potassium channels assemble from four separate subunits.

Gating and physiology

Depolarisation opens voltage-gated sodium channels, allowing sodium influx that drives the rising phase of the action potential; many then enter a non-conducting inactivated state from which they recover only after repolarisation. Voltage-gated potassium channels open more slowly and restore the resting potential, while voltage-gated calcium channels couple electrical signals to neurotransmitter release, muscle contraction, and gene expression. Dysfunction of these channels causes channelopathies including epilepsy, periodic paralysis, long QT syndrome, and chronic pain syndromes, and they are targets for anaesthetics, anticonvulsants, and antiarrhythmic drugs.
