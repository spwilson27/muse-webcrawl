Holden Caulfield

Holden Caulfield is a fictional character created by American author J. D. Salinger. He is the teenage protagonist and narrator of Salinger's 1951 novel The Catcher in the Rye, having first appeared in short stories in the 1940s. Alienated, judgemental, and grieving, Holden has become one of the most famous literary symbols of adolescent rebellion and angst, and the red hunting hat he wears is an enduring icon of the novel.

Appearances

A character named Holden Caulfield appears in Salinger's stories "Last Day of the Last Furlough" (1944) and "This Sandwich Has No Mayonnaise" (1945), where he is a soldier reported missing in the Second World War, and in the novella-length "The Ocean Full of Bowling Balls" (1947, unpublished in his lifetime), which recounts the death of his brother. The Catcher in the Rye reimagines him as the sixteen-year-old narrator who, having been expelled from his Pennsylvania prep school, Pencey Prep, spends several days adrift in New York City before a breakdown; he tells the story retrospectively from a rest home in California.

Character

Holden is intelligent, sensitive, and an unreliable, digressive narrator. He divides the world into innocent children and "phony" adults, reserving his harshest contempt for hypocrisy, pretension, and cruelty, yet he is himself frequently dishonest — he introduces himself as a compulsive liar — and judgemental. Beneath his cynicism lies unresolved grief for his younger brother Allie, who died of leukaemia when Holden was thirteen, and a terror of growing up: his defining fantasy, drawn from a mishearing of Robert Burns's "Comin' Through the Rye", is to stand in a field of rye catching children before they fall off a cliff — preserving their innocence forever. His ten-year-old sister Phoebe, the person he most admires, ultimately helps him accept that children must be allowed to fall. Iconic details include his red hunting hat, his obsession with where the Central Park ducks go in winter, and his disgust at graffiti in Phoebe's school.

Reception and legacy

Holden quickly became the emblem of postwar teenage alienation, and The Catcher in the Rye has remained a staple — and frequent target of book challenges — in schools. Critics debate whether Holden is a perceptive moralist, a traumatised depressive, or an unreliable narrator whose judgements the reader should resist; Salinger, famously reclusive, refused film adaptations, reportedly feeling the character could only exist on the page. Holden's voice — colloquial, repetitive, intimate — influenced generations of first-person fiction and remains one of the most imitated in American literature.
