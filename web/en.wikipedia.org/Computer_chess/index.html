Computer chess

IBM's Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov in 1997

Computer chess is the field of artificial intelligence and engineering devoted to building machines and software that play chess. It includes chess engines, dedicated chess computers, and research into search algorithms and evaluation functions.

History

Theoretical foundations were laid by Claude Shannon's 1950 paper "Programming a Computer for Playing Chess" and Alan Turing's hand-executed program Turochamp. Early programs of the 1950s–60s, such as Kotok–McCarthy and Mac Hack, played weak amateur chess. By the 1980s, programs like Belle and Deep Thought reached master strength. In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue defeated world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game rematch — a landmark in AI history. In the 2000s, commercial engines on personal computers surpassed the best humans; matches between elite humans and top engines effectively ceased to be competitive.

How engines work

Classical engines combine alpha–beta search over a move tree with pruning techniques (null-move pruning, transposition tables, killer heuristics), opening books, endgame tablebases, and a hand-crafted evaluation function assessing material, piece placement, king safety, and pawn structure. Since 2017–2018, neural-network approaches pioneered by DeepMind's AlphaZero and adopted in engines such as Stockfish (via NNUE evaluation) have dominated, giving engines Elo ratings far above 3400 in computer rating lists.

Impact

Engine analysis has transformed chess preparation and post-game study at all levels, and detection of engine use has become central to anti-cheating efforts in online and over-the-board play.
