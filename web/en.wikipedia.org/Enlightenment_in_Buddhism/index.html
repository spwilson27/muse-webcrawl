Enlightenment in Buddhism

Enlightenment in Buddhism (Sanskrit: bodhi, literally "awakening") is the understanding of the true nature of reality and the cessation of suffering, attained by the Buddha and, following his teaching, by arhats and bodhisattvas. The Buddha described awakening as the direct knowledge of the Four Noble Truths and of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), together with the destruction of the mental defilements (āsavas).

Terminology

The term bodhi derives from the Sanskrit root budh, "to wake, know, perceive". In the early texts the Buddha is said to have attained sambodhi, "perfect awakening", on the night of his enlightenment at Bodh Gaya. In Mahayana Buddhism, enlightenment is broadened into Buddhahood (buddhatva), the complete awakening of a Buddha, distinguished from the liberation of an arhat by the removal of even subtle obscurations and the development of omniscience.

The Buddha's awakening

According to tradition, Siddhartha Gautama sat beneath the Bodhi tree and resolved not to rise until he had found the truth. During the three watches of the night he recollected his past lives, saw beings dying and being reborn according to their karma (karma), and finally understood the dependent origination of suffering and its cessation, realizing nirvana.

Stages of awakening

The Theravada tradition describes four stages of enlightenment on the path to arhatship: stream-enterer (sotāpanna), once-returner (sakadāgāmin), non-returner (anāgāmin) and arhat. Each stage is marked by the abandonment of some of the ten fetters. In Mahayana, the bodhisattva path is structured into ten grounds (bhūmis) culminating in Buddhahood.
