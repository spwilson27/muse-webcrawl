Ladybird beetles (Coccinellidae)
OrderColeoptera
FamilyCoccinellidae
Speciesabout 6,000 described

Ladybird beetles, also called ladybugs (North America) or ladybirds (Britain), are small beetles of the family Coccinellidae. They are noted for their round, often brightly coloured bodies — frequently red or orange with black spots — and are widely regarded as beneficial insects because most species prey on aphids and other plant pests.

Description

Ladybirds range from under 1 mm to about 18 mm long. Their wing cases (elytra) meet in a straight line down the back, and the pronotum partially covers the head. Bright coloration advertises their unpalatability: when threatened, many species exude bitter, alkaloid-laden haemolymph from their leg joints ("reflex bleeding").

Biology

Both adults and larvae of predatory species consume large numbers of aphids, scale insects and mites; a single seven-spot ladybird may eat thousands of aphids in its life. Females lay batches of yellow eggs near prey colonies. Some species are herbivorous or feed on mildew.

Relation to humans

Ladybirds are beloved in folklore and are deliberately released for biological pest control. The vedalia beetle's rescue of California citrus from cottony cushion scale in 1889 is a classic success story. However, introduced species such as the harlequin ladybird (Harmonia axyridis) have become invasive, outcompeting native species and overwintering in houses.
