Temporary Assistance for Needy Families

Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) is a United States federal assistance program that provides cash aid and support services to low-income families with children. It was created by the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996, signed by President Bill Clinton, and began operating on July 1, 1997. TANF replaced the earlier Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC) programme, converting federal welfare into a fixed block grant to the states with substantial state discretion over eligibility and benefits.

Structure

The federal government provides about $16.5 billion annually in block grants to states, which must also spend their own "maintenance of effort" funds. States design their own programmes within broad federal goals: assisting needy families so children can be cared for at home, reducing dependency by promoting work and marriage, preventing out-of-wedlock pregnancies, and encouraging two-parent families. The federal programme is administered by the Office of Family Assistance within the Administration for Children and Families of the Department of Health and Human Services.

Work requirements and time limits

TANF introduced requirements that recipients engage in work activities after at most 24 months of assistance, and a general five-year (60-month) lifetime limit on federally funded benefits, though states may exempt a portion of their caseload for hardship and may continue aid with state funds. States face penalties if specified percentages of their caseloads are not participating in countable work activities. Sanctions for non-compliance may reduce or terminate a family's benefit.

Caseload and effects

Caseloads fell sharply after 1996, from more than 12 million recipients under AFDC to roughly 2 million in recent years, a decline attributed both to the strong late-1990s economy, expanded earned-income tax credits, and the new programme's rules. Critics argue that the fixed block grant has lost much of its real value to inflation, that relatively little TANF money now reaches families as basic cash assistance, and that the programme responds weakly to recessions; supporters credit it with increasing employment among single mothers and reducing welfare dependency.
