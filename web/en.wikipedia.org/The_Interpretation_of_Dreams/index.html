The Interpretation of Dreams (German: Die Traumdeutung) is an 1899 book by Sigmund Freud, the founder of psychoanalysis, in which the author introduced his theory of the unconscious as it relates to dream interpretation, and first articulated the concept later known as the Oedipus complex. Issued by the Viennese publisher Franz Deuticke in November 1899 (postdated 1900), it sold slowly at first but went through eight editions in Freud's lifetime and became the foundational text of psychoanalysis. Freud called it his most significant work.

Summary of the theory

Freud argued that dreams are "the royal road to a knowledge of the unconscious activities of the mind" and that every dream represents the disguised fulfilment of a repressed wish. He distinguished the manifest content (the dream as remembered) from the latent dream-thoughts (the wishes and memories beneath), connected by the "dream-work": mechanisms of condensation, displacement, symbolism or "considerations of representability", and secondary revision. Interpretation proceeds by the dreamer's free association to each element of the manifest dream rather than by a fixed dictionary of symbols. The book's extended specimen analysis — the "Irma injection" dream of July 1895 — served as its demonstration case, and its seventh chapter presented Freud's first full model of the mental apparatus.

Influence and criticism

The book launched psychoanalysis as a movement and reshaped twentieth-century culture's understanding of dreams, symbolism, and sexuality; it also drew sustained criticism for unfalsifiability, the centrality it assigns infantile sexuality, and the reliability of its clinical evidence. Later sleep research emphasising neurobiology (such as the activation-synthesis model) largely displaced Freudian dream theory in academic psychology, while its literary and cultural influence endures.
