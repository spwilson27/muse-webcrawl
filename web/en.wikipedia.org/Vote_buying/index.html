Vote buying

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Vote buying (also called electoral clientelism or patronage politics) occurs when a political party or candidate distributes money or resources to a voter in an upcoming election with the expectation that the voter will reciprocate by voting for the benefactor. It is a form of electoral fraud and is illegal in most democracies, though its prevalence and enforcement vary widely.

Forms

Vote buying ranges from handing out cash, food, goods or favours shortly before an election, to longer-term clientelistic relationships in which brokers — local intermediaries — deliver state resources, jobs or services to communities in return for reliable bloc voting. Related practices include "turnout buying", paying supporters to vote (or opponents to abstain) rather than to switch sides. Because the secret ballot prevents buyers from verifying how a vote was cast, enforcement typically relies on brokers' local knowledge, social pressure, or, in some documented cases, photographing ballots with camera phones.

History and extent

Vote buying was commonplace in 18th- and 19th-century Britain — candidates openly "treated" electors — until the Corrupt and Illegal Practices Prevention Act 1883 and the adoption of the secret ballot curtailed it. Modern survey research finds vote buying remains widespread in parts of Latin America, Africa, South and Southeast Asia and Eastern Europe, with reported rates in some countries reaching a quarter or more of voters being offered benefits. International observer missions routinely report it as a central integrity problem.

Effects and countermeasures

Scholars debate its impact: vote buying can distort representation towards the poor voters most willing to sell, entrench machine politics, and drain public resources into patronage, though some argue payments function partly as a signal of a candidate's credibility. Countermeasures include voter education campaigns, election monitoring, strict campaign-finance enforcement, and secrecy protections such as bans on phones in voting booths.
