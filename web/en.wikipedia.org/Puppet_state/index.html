Puppet state

A puppet state, puppet government, or puppet regime is a state that is nominally independent but is in practice completely dependent upon an outside power and subject to its orders. Puppet states have nominal sovereignty but effectively act as appendages of a foreign power, which exercises control through economic, political, or military means, often while avoiding the costs and international condemnation of outright annexation or military occupation.

Characteristics

Puppet regimes are typically installed or sustained by a stronger state, which selects or approves their leadership, stations troops or advisers, and dictates key decisions on foreign policy, defense, and often domestic affairs. The patron power in return gains strategic territory, resources, legitimacy for its intervention, and a local administration to enforce its will. The term is pejorative: governments so accused almost always deny the label, and its application is often disputed.

Historical examples

Frequently cited examples include Manchukuo, established by Imperial Japan in Manchuria in 1932 and headed by the former Qing emperor Puyi, which was recognized by few states and condemned by the League of Nations; the Slovak Republic and the Independent State of Croatia, client states aligned with Nazi Germany during the Second World War; Vichy France, whose degree of autonomy remains debated; and the satellite states of the Soviet Union in Eastern Europe, though their levels of independence varied considerably. The term has also been applied to South Vietnam during the Vietnam War, and in contemporary disputes — Russia's opponents describe the Donetsk and Luhansk people's republics as puppets, while Russia and its allies apply the term to Kosovo and to Western-backed governments.

Related concepts

A puppet state differs from a satellite state, which retains greater autonomy; from a client state, which is influenced but substantially independent; from a protectorate, whose dependent status is formalized by treaty; and from a puppet ruler installed within an existing state. Modern international law generally prohibits acquiring territory by force, and puppet states created through aggression are rarely granted recognition, under doctrines such as the Stimson Doctrine of non-recognition.
