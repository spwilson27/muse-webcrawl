Tempering (chocolate)

Tempering is a process of heating and cooling chocolate to stabilise it for making candies and confections. It gives chocolate a smooth and glossy finish, a pleasant snap when broken, and prevents the dull greyish-white appearance known as fat bloom. Tempering works by controlling the crystallisation of cocoa butter, encouraging it to set in one particular stable crystal form.

Cocoa butter crystals

Cocoa butter is polymorphic: it can crystallise in six forms, conventionally numbered I to VI, with progressively higher melting points and stability. Form V (beta crystals, melting at about 34 °C / 93 °F) is the target of tempering: it produces chocolate that is glossy, firm, and contracts slightly as it sets, aiding release from moulds. Untempered chocolate sets with a mixture of lower crystal forms, giving a soft, dull, bloom-prone product.

Process

A classic tempering curve for dark chocolate involves three stages:

StageTemperature (dark chocolate)Purpose
Melting45–50 °C (113–122 °F)Melt out all existing crystals
Coolingabout 27 °C (81 °F)Form seed crystals of forms IV and V
Reheating31–32 °C (88–90 °F)Melt out unstable form IV, leaving form V

Milk and white chocolates are worked at slightly lower temperatures because milk fat and milk solids alter crystallisation. Common methods include tabling (working melted chocolate on a cool marble slab), seeding (stirring finely chopped already-tempered chocolate into melted chocolate), and the use of tempering machines that hold the chocolate on the correct curve. Temper is checked by dipping a knife or strip of parchment: properly tempered chocolate sets quickly with a glossy, streak-free surface.

Bloom

Improperly tempered or poorly stored chocolate develops fat bloom, a whitish surface haze of recrystallised cocoa butter, or sugar bloom, caused by moisture dissolving and recrystallising surface sugar. Bloomed chocolate remains safe to eat but loses its texture and appearance; it can be re-tempered.
