Alençon lace is a handmade needle lace that originated in the town of Alençon in Normandy, France. Regarded as the "queen of laces", it is a form of point lace in which motifs are worked with a needle over a parchment pattern, outlined with a raised cord (the cordonnet) and joined by a fine hexagonal mesh ground known as the réseau. In 2010 the craftsmanship of Alençon needle lace was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

Lacemaking in Alençon can be traced to the sixteenth century, but the industry was transformed in 1665 when Jean-Baptiste Colbert, minister to Louis XIV, established a royal workshop in the town as part of his policy of replacing imported Venetian lace with French products. Workers were recruited from Venice, and the local product — initially called point de France — soon developed its own distinctive character. By the eighteenth century Alençon lace was among the most expensive textiles in Europe, used for court dress, christening robes and religious vestments.

The French Revolution severely damaged the industry, as lace fell out of fashion and luxury trades collapsed. A revival in the nineteenth century, assisted by the mechanisation of the mesh ground (producing the cheaper point d'Alençon imitations), kept production going, but hand-made Alençon lace remained a costly speciality. Today a small number of lacemakers at the Atelier national du Point d'Alençon preserve the traditional technique.

Technique

Traditional Alençon lace is made entirely with a needle and a single thread. The design is pricked out on parchment; the motifs are built up with buttonhole stitches in graduated densities (the modes), outlined with a cordonnet of horsehair or thread covered in buttonhole stitch, and connected by a réseau of twisted hexagonal meshes. A single square centimetre can take several hours to complete, and a finished piece may pass through the hands of as many as seven specialists, each responsible for one stage of the work.
