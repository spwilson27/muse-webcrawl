Hugh Bamford Cott (6 July 1900 – 18 April 1987) was a British zoologist, authority on natural and military camouflage, and scientific illustrator. His 1940 book Adaptive Coloration in Animals became the standard reference on camouflage in nature and influenced its military application during the Second World War.

Career

Cott studied zoology at Cambridge, where he later became a lecturer and a Fellow of Selwyn College, and served as director of the Cambridge Museum of Zoology's Strickland Curator of birds. He undertook fieldwork in the Amazon, Africa (including expeditions to the Zambezi) and Egypt, studying crocodiles, frogs and bird coloration, and was a skilled photographer and artist who illustrated his own works.

Camouflage

Cott applied principles from nature — countershading, disruptive coloration, coincident disruption and differential blending — to military problems. At the start of the Second World War he argued, controversially, that the Royal Air Force should adopt disruptive camouflage for aircraft. He served as a camouflage instructor with the Royal Engineers in Egypt, where his natural-history approach influenced deception work in the Western Desert.
