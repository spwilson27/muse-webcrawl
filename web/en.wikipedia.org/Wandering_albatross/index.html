The wandering albatross (Diomedea exulans), also called the snowy albatross or white-winged albatross, is a large seabird of the albatross family found across the Southern Ocean. It was among the first albatrosses described, in 1758 by Carl Linnaeus, and it has the largest measured wingspan of any living bird, verified at up to 3.5 metres (11 feet), with exceptional unverified reports larger still.

Description and flight

Adults have white plumage with black-and-white wings that become progressively whiter with age, especially in males; the bill is large and pink with a salt gland above the nasal passage. Wandering albatrosses spend most of their lives in flight, gliding for hours over the ocean using dynamic soaring and slope soaring, and satellite tracking has recorded individuals circumnavigating the Southern Ocean in a matter of weeks and travelling thousands of kilometres on a single foraging trip.

Breeding and diet

The species breeds on subantarctic islands including South Georgia, Crozet, Kerguelen, and Macquarie Island. Pairs form long-term, often lifelong, bonds and breed only every two years, raising a single chick that takes over a year to fledge. The diet consists mainly of cephalopods, fish, and crustaceans, taken at the surface, and the birds frequently follow ships. Populations have declined owing largely to mortality in longline fisheries, and the species is listed as Vulnerable.

In culture

The bird is the central figure of Samuel Taylor Coleridge's poem The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, from which comes the metaphor of an "albatross around one's neck" for a burdensome psychological weight.
