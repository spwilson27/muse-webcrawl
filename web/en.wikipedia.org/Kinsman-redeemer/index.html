Kinsman-redeemer

Kinsman-redeemer

Hebrewgoel (גואל)
SubjectAncient Israelite law and custom
Key textsLeviticus 25; Ruth; Deuteronomy 25

The kinsman-redeemer (Hebrew goel, "redeemer") is a concept in the Hebrew Bible for a male relative who, according to ancient Israelite tribal law, had the privilege or responsibility to act on behalf of a relative who was in trouble, danger or need of vindication — redeeming family land that had been sold, buying a relative out of slavery, or marrying a childless widow to continue the family line.

Duties in the Law

Leviticus 25 provides that if an Israelite becomes poor and sells part of his property, his nearest relative, the redeemer, shall buy back what was sold, keeping land within the clan until the Jubilee year. The goel also redeemed relatives sold into servitude and, as the "avenger of blood" (goel ha-dam), pursued the killer of a family member, subject to the protections of the cities of refuge.

The Book of Ruth

The most famous narrative of the kinsman-redeemer is the Book of Ruth, where Boaz acts as goel for the widowed Naomi and Ruth: a nearer relative declines the duty, so Boaz redeems Elimelech’s land and marries Ruth, becoming the great-grandfather of King David.

Theological use

In Isaiah, God is repeatedly called the Redeemer (goel) of Israel, and in Christian theology the concept informs the idea of Christ as redeemer of humanity.
