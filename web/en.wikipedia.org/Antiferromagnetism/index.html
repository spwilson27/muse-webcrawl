Antiferromagnetism

In an antiferromagnet, neighbouring spins point in opposite directions and cancel

In materials that exhibit antiferromagnetism, the magnetic moments of atoms or molecules, usually related to the spins of electrons, align in a regular pattern with neighbouring spins pointing in opposite directions. This ordering arises from the exchange interaction and is the magnetic analogue of ferromagnetism, in which neighbouring moments align in the same direction. Because the opposing moments cancel, antiferromagnets have zero net magnetisation.

Properties and the Néel temperature

Antiferromagnetic order typically exists at low temperatures and vanishes above a critical temperature called the Néel temperature (TN), named after Louis Néel, who developed the theory in the 1930s and received the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physics partly for this work. Above TN the material behaves as a paramagnet. Examples include chromium (TN ≈ 311 K), manganese oxide (MnO) and iron oxide (FeO). Magnetic susceptibility of an antiferromagnet peaks at the Néel temperature rather than diverging.

Detection and applications

Because they have no net moment, antiferromagnetic structures were historically hard to detect; neutron diffraction, which first revealed the ordering in MnO in 1949, remains a principal tool. Antiferromagnetic materials are used in exchange-bias layers that pin the magnetisation of read heads in hard disk drives, and the field of antiferromagnetic spintronics exploits their insensitivity to stray fields and ultrafast (terahertz) spin dynamics.
