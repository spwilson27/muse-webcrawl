Camargue

The Camargue is a natural region located south of Arles, in the south of France, between the Mediterranean Sea and the two arms of the Rhône delta. It is western Europe's largest river delta and one of its most important wetlands.

Geography and ecology

The region covers some 930 km² (360 sq mi) of salt marshes, lagoons (étangs), reed beds, pastures, and rice fields between the Petit Rhône and the Grand Rhône. The central Étang de Vaccarès is the core of the Camargue National Reserve, established in 1927 and incorporated into the Parc naturel régional de Camargue in 1970. The Camargue is a major bird habitat — the only regular breeding site in France for the greater flamingo — and a stopover for hundreds of migratory species.

Agriculture and traditions

The Camargue is known for its semi-wild white Camargue horses, ridden by the gardians (the region's herdsmen, often described as French cowboys), and for black Camargue bulls raised for the course camarguaise, a bloodless bull game distinct from Spanish bullfighting. Salt has been harvested for centuries at Salin-de-Giraud and Aigues-Mortes, and the northern Camargue produces much of France's rice. Fleur de sel de Camargue is prized as a finishing salt.

Culture

The town of Saintes-Maries-de-la-Mer is a place of pilgrimage, notably for Romani people who gather each May for the feast of Saint Sarah. The culture of the gardians and the manades (bull and horse ranches) was popularised internationally by films and by conservation work of the Tour du Valat research station.
