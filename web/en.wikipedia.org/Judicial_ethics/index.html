Judicial ethics

Judicial ethics is the branch of legal and professional ethics concerned with the moral duties and proper conduct of judges. It comprises the principles and rules that require judges to be independent, impartial, and of integrity; to conduct themselves with propriety both in and out of court; to be competent and diligent; and to treat all participants in proceedings with equality and respect. Judicial ethics sustains public confidence in the judiciary, which depends largely on moral authority rather than coercive power.

Core principles

The most widely adopted international statement is the Bangalore Principles of Judicial Conduct (2002), developed by a group of senior judges under the auspices of the United Nations and endorsed by the UN Human Rights Commission in 2003. They identify six values: independence, impartiality, integrity, propriety, equality, and competence and diligence. A judge must not allow family, social, political, or financial relationships to influence decisions; must avoid conflicts of interest and recuse (disqualify) themselves where a reasonable observer might doubt their impartiality; must not accept gifts or favours connected with their office; and must restrict political activity and public comment on pending cases.

Codes and enforcement

Many jurisdictions codify these duties: the American Bar Association's Model Code of Judicial Conduct and the Code of Conduct for United States Judges in the US; the Guide to Judicial Conduct in England and Wales; and comparable instruments elsewhere. Breaches may be investigated by judicial conduct bodies, with sanctions ranging from advice and reprimand to removal from office, usually through impeachment or a disciplinary tribunal. In the United States, federal judges may also face proceedings under the Judicial Conduct and Disability Act of 1980.

Issues

Recurring questions include judges' use of social media, acceptance of hospitality and sponsored travel, financial disclosure, post-retirement employment, courtroom demeanour, delays in judgments, and the balance between judicial free speech and the appearance of bias. Debates over a binding ethics code for the justices of the U.S. Supreme Court — which adopted its own code in 2023 — illustrate the continuing public importance of the subject.
