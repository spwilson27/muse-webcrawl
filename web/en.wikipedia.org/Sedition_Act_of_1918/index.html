The Sedition Act of 1918 was an Act of the United States Congress, signed by President Woodrow Wilson on 16 May 1918, that extended the Espionage Act of 1917 to cover a broad range of offences, notably speech and the expression of opinion that cast the government or the war effort in a negative light. Passed during the First World War, it forbade the use of "disloyal, profane, scurrilous, or abusive language" about the United States government, its flag, or its armed forces, or language intended to cause Americans to view the government with contempt.

Provisions and enforcement

Violations carried fines of up to $10,000 and imprisonment of up to twenty years. The Act empowered the Postmaster General to refuse mail delivery to publications deemed in violation. Together with the Espionage Act, it was used to prosecute roughly two thousand people, including Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs, who received a ten-year sentence for an anti-war speech in 1918 and ran for president from prison in 1920. Many convictions were later commuted by President Warren G. Harding.

Repeal and legacy

Congress repealed the Sedition Act on 13 December 1920, though the underlying Espionage Act remained in force. The prosecutions under the two acts provoked the founding generation of American civil-liberties advocacy — the American Civil Liberties Union was founded in 1920 largely in response — and the Supreme Court cases they generated, such as Schenck v. United States (1919), began the modern development of First Amendment free-speech doctrine.
