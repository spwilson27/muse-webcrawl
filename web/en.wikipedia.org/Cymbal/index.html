Cymbal

A cymbal is a common percussion instrument consisting of a thin, normally round plate of metal, usually bronze. Most cymbals have an indefinite pitch, though ancient and orchestral examples can be tuned. Cymbals are played by striking them with a drumstick, brush, or mallet, by clashing pairs together, or by bowing.

Structure

A cymbal comprises the bell (the raised centre dome), the bow (the main body), and the edge. Concentric grooves called tonal grooves are lathed into the surface, and hammering shapes the profile and sound. The centre hole mounts the cymbal on a stand or, for hand cymbals, carries a strap.

Types

In a drum kit, the principal cymbals are the hi-hat (a pair clashed by pedal and stick), crash cymbals for accents, and the larger ride cymbal for patterns. Orchestras use clash (hand) cymbals and suspended cymbals. Specialist types include splash, china, and effects cymbals.

Manufacture

Major makers such as Zildjian — founded in Constantinople in 1623 by the Armenian alchemist Avedis Zildjian — cast bronze blanks that are rolled, hammered, and lathed. Traditional bell bronze (B20) contains roughly 80% copper and 20% tin.

History

Cymbals were used in ancient Egypt, Israel, Greece, and Rome, and spread along trade routes. They entered the European orchestra through eighteenth-century "Turkish" (Janissary) music, appearing in works by Gluck, Haydn, and Mozart before becoming standard in the nineteenth century.
