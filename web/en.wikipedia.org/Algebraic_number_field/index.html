An algebraic number field (or simply number field) is a finite-degree field extension of the field ℚ of rational numbers. Equivalently, a number field is a field that contains ℚ and has finite dimension as a vector space over ℚ. The study of number fields and their rings of integers is the central subject of algebraic number theory.

Definition and examples

If K is a number field of degree n = [K : ℚ], then K is generated over ℚ by a single algebraic number θ (the primitive element theorem). Familiar examples include the quadratic fields ℚ(√d) for square-free integers d, such as the Gaussian rationals ℚ(i), and the cyclotomic fields ℚ(ζn) obtained by adjoining a root of unity. Every element of a number field is an algebraic number, and every finite extension of ℚ arises this way.

Ring of integers

Within a number field K, the algebraic integers form a ring OK, the ring of integers, which is a Dedekind domain: every non-zero ideal factorises uniquely into prime ideals, even though unique factorisation of elements may fail (as in ℤ[√−5]). The failure is measured by the ideal class group, whose finiteness is a fundamental theorem; the class number is 1 precisely when OK has unique factorisation. Dirichlet's unit theorem describes the structure of the group of units of OK.

Invariants and theory

Important invariants of a number field include its discriminant, its signature (numbers of real and complex embeddings), and its Dedekind zeta function, which encodes the distribution of prime ideals and satisfies a functional equation. Ramification theory describes how rational primes split in OK, and class field theory — one of the great achievements of twentieth-century mathematics — classifies the abelian extensions of a number field in terms of its arithmetic.
