Stolon

In biology, stolons are horizontal connections between organisms, or horizontal stems that grow at or just below the soil surface and produce new cloned plants at their nodes or tips. In botany, above-ground stolons are often called runners. Stolons are a major means of vegetative (asexual) reproduction and of colonial spread.

In plants

The strawberry is the classic example: it sends out slender runners that arc to the ground, root at a node, and establish a daughter plant that is genetically identical to the parent. Spider plants produce plantlets on stolons, and many grasses — including bermuda grass and creeping bentgrass — spread aggressively by stolons, which is why they form turf. Similar structures growing underground are called rhizomes; the distinction is that stolons run along or just under the surface and are often thinner and longer-internoded.

In fungi and animals

In fungi, a stolon is a horizontal hypha connecting groups of sporangiophores, as in the bread mould Rhizopus stolonifer. In animals, colonial organisms such as some hydrozoans, bryozoans and tunicates spread by stolons — creeping tissue that buds new zooids — and some worms and rotifers reproduce along stolon-like structures.

Significance

Stoloniferous growth lets a plant colonise open ground quickly, share resources between connected ramets, and persist through disturbance, at the cost of genetic uniformity. Gardeners exploit stolons to propagate strawberries and ground covers, and control them where stoloniferous grasses become weeds.
