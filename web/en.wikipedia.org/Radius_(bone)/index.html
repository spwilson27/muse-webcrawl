The radius is one of the two large bones of the forearm, the other being the ulna. It lies on the lateral (thumb) side of the forearm, extending from the elbow to the wrist. Unlike the ulna, which is the main bone of the elbow joint, the radius carries most of the load at the wrist and rotates around the ulna to allow pronation and supination of the hand.

Structure

The proximal end consists of a disc-shaped head that articulates with the capitulum of the humerus and the radial notch of the ulna, a neck, and the radial tuberosity, where the biceps brachii tendon inserts. The shaft is triangular in cross-section and connects to the ulna via the interosseous membrane. The distal end is broad and quadrangular, articulating with the scaphoid and lunate bones of the wrist; its lateral side projects as the styloid process.

Function

Together with the ulna, the radius supports the forearm and transmits forces from the hand to the arm. About 80% of axial load across the wrist passes through the radius. Its rotation around the relatively fixed ulna turns the palm downward (pronation) or upward (supination).

Clinical significance

Distal radius fractures are among the most common fractures in adults. A Colles' fracture — a fracture of the distal radius with dorsal displacement, classically from a fall onto an outstretched hand — produces a characteristic "dinner fork" deformity. Fractures of the radial head commonly follow falls onto the hand as well, and the combination of a radial head fracture with dislocation of the distal radioulnar joint is known as an Essex-Lopresti injury.
