Rugby union in France is a popular team sport, particularly in the south-west of the country, where it rivals association football in popularity. The game was introduced to France in 1872 by English merchants and students at Le Havre, and the first domestic club, Le Havre Athletic Club, was founded the same year. Racing Club de France and Stade Français, both Parisian, were among the earliest clubs and dominated the early championship.

History

The French championship was first contested in 1892, when Racing Club defeated Stade Français in a one-off final refereed by Pierre de Coubertin. France joined the Five Nations Championship in 1910, and the national side — known as Les Bleus — won its first outright title in 1959. France has since won numerous championships and Grand Slams, hosted the Rugby World Cup in 2007 and 2023, and reached three World Cup finals (1987, 1999 and 2011), losing each time.

Domestic competitions

The top domestic league is the Top 14, widely regarded as one of the strongest club competitions in the world, sitting above Pro D2 and the amateur federal divisions. Historic clubs include Stade Toulousain, the most successful French club in European competition, ASM Clermont Auvergne, Racing 92, Montpellier and Toulon. French clubs have won the European Rugby Champions Cup many times, beginning with Toulouse in 1996.

National team

The France national team competes annually in the Six Nations Championship against England, Ireland, Scotland, Wales and Italy, and tours the Southern Hemisphere regularly. Its traditional home ground is the Stade de France in Saint-Denis, near Paris. Famous French players include Jean Prat, Serge Blanco, Philippe Sella, Fabien Galthié, Thierry Dusautoir and Antoine Dupont.

Popularity and culture

Rugby is deeply embedded in the culture of the south-west — the region sometimes called l'Ovalie — where small towns support their clubs passionately, while the sport's heartlands also extend to the Paris region and parts of the south-east. The French style of play has historically been celebrated as le beau jeu, emphasising flair, handling skill and improvisation.
