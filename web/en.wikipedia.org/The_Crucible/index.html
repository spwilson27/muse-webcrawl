The Crucible

The Crucible is a 1953 play by American playwright Arthur Miller. It dramatises the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in colonial Massachusetts and was written as an allegory of McCarthyism, when the US government persecuted alleged communists.

Plot

In Salem, a group of girls led by Abigail Williams is caught dancing in the woods and deflects punishment by accusing neighbours of witchcraft. As accusations multiply, the farmer John Proctor — who has had an affair with Abigail — tries to expose the fraud. Arrested himself, he refuses to sign a false confession, choosing to die with his name intact: "Because it is my name! Because I cannot have another in my life!"

Background and reception

The play premiered on Broadway in January 1953 to a mixed reception but won the Tony Award for Best Play; a 1954 revival, after cuts, was more successful. Miller was himself called before the House Un-American Activities Committee in 1956 and convicted of contempt for refusing to name others (the conviction was overturned on appeal). The play has become a staple of school and professional theatre; Miller adapted it for the 1996 film starring Daniel Day-Lewis and Winona Ryder.

Themes

The Crucible examines mass hysteria, theocracy, reputation, integrity and the danger of ideology-driven courts — themes that have kept it in production wherever political persecution is debated.
