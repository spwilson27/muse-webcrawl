Time-domain astronomy

Time-domain astronomy is the study of how astronomical objects change with time. It encompasses variable stars, supernovae and other transient phenomena, exoplanet transits, asteroseismology, active galactic nuclei, gamma-ray bursts, and the counterparts of gravitational-wave and neutrino events. Although variability has been observed for centuries — novae and comets were recorded in ancient chronicles, and variable-star astronomy dates to the nineteenth century — the field has been transformed by wide-field digital sky surveys that image the sky repeatedly and alert observers to changes within minutes or hours.

Surveys and methods

Modern time-domain surveys include the Palomar Transient Factory and its successor the Zwicky Transient Facility, Pan-STARRS, ASAS-SN, ATLAS and Catalina, as well as space missions such as Kepler and TESS for exoplanet transits and light curves, and Gaia for repeated all-sky photometry and astrometry. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) is expected to issue millions of alerts per night. Because most alerts are variable stars, artefacts or moving objects, automated brokers filter and classify the stream, and robotic telescopes carry out rapid follow-up spectroscopy. Multimessenger astronomy — combining light with gravitational waves (as in the neutron-star merger GW170817), neutrinos and cosmic rays — is one of the field's drivers.

Science

Key discoveries include the systematic use of Type Ia supernovae (which revealed the accelerating expansion of the universe), kilonovae from neutron star mergers, tidal disruption events of stars by supermassive black holes, fast radio bursts (millisecond transients first recognised in archival pulsar data), and thousands of exoplanets detected by the transit method. Variable stars such as Cepheids and RR Lyrae stars remain fundamental distance indicators.
