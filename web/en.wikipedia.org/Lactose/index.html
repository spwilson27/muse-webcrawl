Lactose

FormulaC12H22O11
Molar mass342.30 g/mol
AppearanceWhite solid
Solubility in water21.6 g/100 mL

Structure of lactose: galactose β(1→4) linked to glucose

Lactose, or milk sugar, is a disaccharide sugar synthesised in the mammary glands and found naturally in milk. It is composed of one molecule of D-galactose and one of D-glucose joined by a β(1→4) glycosidic linkage. Milk typically contains 2–8 percent lactose by weight; cow's milk contains about 4.7 percent.

Digestion

In the small intestine, lactose is hydrolysed by the enzyme lactase into glucose and galactose, which are absorbed into the bloodstream. In most mammals, lactase production falls sharply after weaning; humans who retain high lactase activity into adulthood are described as lactase persistent, a trait common in populations with a long history of dairying, such as northern Europeans and some African and Middle Eastern pastoralist groups. Where lactase is deficient, undigested lactose reaches the colon and is fermented by bacteria, producing the symptoms of lactose intolerance.

Uses

Isolated as a white, mildly sweet powder (roughly 16–20 percent the sweetness of sucrose), lactose is used as a filler and excipient in pharmaceutical tablets, as a carrier in homeopathic preparations, in infant formula, and as a substrate in fermentation and food processing. Whey, a by-product of cheese-making, is its major commercial source. Bacteria such as Lactobacillus ferment lactose to lactic acid, the basis of yogurt and cheese production.

History

Lactose was isolated from whey by Fabrizio Bartoletti in 1619 and characterised chemically in the following centuries; its component monosaccharides were identified in the nineteenth century.
