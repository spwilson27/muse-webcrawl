The Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty (ABM Treaty) was an arms control treaty between the United States and the Soviet Union on the limitation of anti-ballistic missile systems used in defending areas against ballistic missile-delivered nuclear weapons. Signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 by President Richard Nixon and General Secretary Leonid Brezhnev as part of the SALT I negotiations, it was intended to preserve the doctrine of mutual assured destruction by preventing either side from building a nationwide missile shield.

Provisions

Each party was originally permitted two ABM deployment areas — one around its capital and one around an ICBM field — each limited to 100 interceptor missiles. A 1974 protocol reduced this to a single site. The Soviet Union defended Moscow; the United States completed a site at Grand Forks, North Dakota, but closed it in 1976 as ineffective. The treaty also banned sea-based, air-based, space-based and mobile land-based ABM systems and components.

Withdrawal

The treaty became contentious during the 1980s Strategic Defense Initiative and again in the 1990s over U.S. plans for national missile defence against limited attacks. In December 2001 President George W. Bush gave notice of withdrawal under the treaty's six-month clause, and the United States withdrew on 13 June 2002 — the first time it had withdrawn from a major arms control treaty. Russia criticised the move, though responses were muted; the U.S. subsequently deployed ground-based interceptors in Alaska and California.
