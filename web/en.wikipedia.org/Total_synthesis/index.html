Total synthesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Retrosynthetic scheme for the total synthesis of taxol]

Total synthesis is the complete chemical synthesis of a complex organic molecule, usually a natural product, from simple, commercially available precursors. It is distinguished from semisynthesis, which starts from an advanced natural intermediate, and it historically served both to prove a proposed structure and to supply scarce compounds for study.

History

The discipline is often traced to Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea from inorganic salts, but its modern form began with landmark constructions such as R. B. Woodward's syntheses of quinine (1944, with Doering), cholesterol and cortisone (1951), and culminated in the Woodward–Eschenmoser synthesis of vitamin B₁₂ (1972), a collaboration of about 100 researchers that drove the development of the Woodward–Hoffmann rules. E. J. Corey's introduction of formal retrosynthetic analysis in the 1960s — and syntheses such as prostaglandins and ginkgolide B — earned the 1990 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Notable achievements

Famous targets include strychnine (Woodward, 1954), reserpine, the tumour-promoting phorbol esters, K. C. Nicolaou's and Robert Holton's near-simultaneous syntheses of the anticancer drug taxol (1994), palytoxin — for years the most complex molecule ever synthesised — and brevetoxin. Total syntheses remain a proving ground for new reactions and strategy, even where they cannot compete economically with fermentation or semisynthesis for production.

Strategy

A synthesis may be linear, with each step's yield multiplying against the total, or convergent, assembling fragments late to shorten the longest sequence. Key concerns include stereocontrol (many natural products have numerous stereocentres), protecting groups, and the ideality of the route — its step count, atom economy and scalability.
