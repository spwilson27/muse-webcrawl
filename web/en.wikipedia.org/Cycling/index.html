Cycling

Cycling, also known as bicycling or biking, is the activity of riding a bicycle or other types of cycles, including unicycles, tricycles, and human-powered vehicles. It is used for transport, recreation, exercise, and sport. The bicycle, developed in its recognisable safety form in the 1880s, became one of the most efficient machines ever devised for converting human effort into motion, and cycling remains a principal mode of travel in many countries, notably the Netherlands and Denmark, where dedicated infrastructure supports very high modal shares.

History

The wooden draisine of 1817, the pedal-driven velocipede of the 1860s, and the high-wheel "penny-farthing" of the 1870s preceded John Kemp Starley's safety bicycle of 1885, which combined a chain drive to the rear wheel, wheels of similar size, and later pneumatic tyres. The resulting 1890s bicycle boom transformed personal mobility, influenced road paving, and played a noted role in women's emancipation. Cycling clubs and racing emerged almost immediately; the Tour de France was first held in 1903.

Types of cycling

  • Utility cycling — everyday travel for commuting, deliveries, and errands; supported by bike lanes, bike-sharing systems, and cargo bikes.
  • Recreational cycling — leisure riding, touring, and long-distance randonneuring.
  • Road cycling — racing on paved roads, including Grand Tours, classics, and criteriums, governed internationally by the UCI.
  • Mountain biking — off-road riding and racing on rough terrain, including cross-country and downhill disciplines.
  • Track cycling — racing on banked velodromes, an Olympic discipline since the first modern Games.
  • BMX and cyclo-cross — short-course disciplines combining riding with jumps or dismounts and obstacles.

Equipment

Modern bicycles use a diamond frame of steel, aluminium, titanium, or carbon fibre; derailleur or hub gearing; rim or disc brakes; and drop or flat handlebars depending on purpose. Helmets, lights, and locks are common accessories. Electrically assisted e-bikes, which provide motor support while pedalling, have grown rapidly since the 2010s and extended cycling to longer distances and hillier terrain for many riders.

Health, environment, and society

Cycling is a low-impact aerobic exercise associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity. As transport it produces essentially no direct emissions, requires far less road and parking space than cars, and is inexpensive. Cities promote it to reduce congestion and air pollution. Set against these benefits are safety concerns: most serious cyclist injuries involve motor vehicles, and risk levels vary greatly with infrastructure quality. Studies generally find that the health benefits of regular cycling far outweigh the injury and pollution risks.

Cycling culture

Cycling is embedded in national cultures to differing degrees. In the Netherlands roughly a quarter or more of all trips are made by bicycle, supported by a dense network of separated paths and bicycle parking. Copenhagen similarly plans around the bicycle. Elsewhere, advocacy groups campaign for safer streets, and events such as critical mass rides and car-free days promote cycling visibility. Bicycle messengers, touring traditions, and a large amateur sportive scene sustain a broad global subculture.
