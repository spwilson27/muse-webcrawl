Scalding


Scalding injury

Scalding is a form of thermal burn resulting from heated fluids such as boiling water or steam. Most scalds are considered first- or second-degree burns, but third-degree burns can occur, especially with prolonged contact. The term is from the Latin word calidus, meaning hot.

Causes

Most scalds occur in the home, particularly in the kitchen and bathroom. Common causes include spilled hot beverages, boiling water from kettles and saucepans, steam from cooking, and excessively hot tap water. Young children and older adults are at greatest risk because their skin is thinner and their reactions to danger may be slower.

Severity

The severity of a scald depends on the temperature of the liquid and the duration of contact. Water at 60 °C (140 °F) can cause a third-degree burn in about three seconds, while at 69 °C (156 °F) the same burn can occur in under a second. Steam is especially dangerous because it carries additional latent heat released on condensation.

Treatment

First aid involves removing the person from the source of heat, cooling the burn under cool running water for at least 20 minutes, removing clothing or jewellery near the injury, and covering the area with a sterile non-adhesive dressing. Severe scalds require emergency medical attention.

Prevention

Prevention measures include setting domestic water heaters to a maximum of about 49 °C (120 °F), using thermostatic mixing valves, keeping hot drinks away from children, and turning saucepan handles inward on stoves.
