Kerr metric

A rotating (Kerr) black hole drags spacetime around with it.

The Kerr metric is an exact solution of the Einstein field equations of general relativity describing the geometry of spacetime around a rotating, uncharged mass. It was discovered by Roy Kerr in 1963 and generalises the earlier Schwarzschild metric, which describes a non-rotating mass. The Kerr geometry is believed to represent the final state of a rotating black hole after gravitational collapse, in accordance with the no-hair theorems.

Structure

The metric depends on two parameters: the mass M and the angular momentum per unit mass a = J/M. Unlike the Schwarzschild case, the event horizon is accompanied by an outer surface called the ergosphere, within which spacetime is dragged so strongly — the frame-dragging or Lense–Thirring effect — that nothing can remain stationary relative to a distant observer. Inside the horizon lies a ring singularity. The horizon exists only if a ≤ GM/c²; a solution with greater spin would describe a naked singularity, which cosmic censorship conjectures cannot arise from realistic collapse.

Physical effects

Rotation makes black-hole physics richer: energy can in principle be extracted from the ergosphere by the Penrose process, and accretion flows, jets and gravitational-wave signals from merging binaries all depend on the spins of the holes involved. Geodesic motion in Kerr spacetime is integrable thanks to a hidden symmetry expressed by the Carter constant, discovered by Brandon Carter in 1968. Astrophysical measurements — from X-ray spectroscopy of accretion discs to the Event Horizon Telescope images of M87* and Sagittarius A* — indicate that real black holes spin at substantial fractions of the maximal Kerr rate.

Extensions

Charged rotating black holes are described by the Kerr–Newman metric. The Kerr solution remains one of the most important exact solutions in relativity and the standard model for astrophysical black holes.
