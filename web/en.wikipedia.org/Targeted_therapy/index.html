Targeted therapy

Targeted therapies block specific molecules involved in cancer growth and spread.

Targeted therapy or molecularly targeted therapy is one of the major modalities of medical treatment (pharmacotherapy) for cancer, others being hormonal therapy and cytotoxic chemotherapy. As a form of molecular medicine, targeted therapy blocks the growth of cancer cells by interfering with specific targeted molecules needed for carcinogenesis and tumor growth, rather than by simply interfering with all rapidly dividing cells, as with traditional chemotherapy.

Because most agents for targeted therapy are biopharmaceuticals, the term biological therapy is sometimes synonymous with targeted therapy when used in the context of cancer. However, the modalities can be combined; antibody-drug conjugates combine biologic and cytotoxic mechanisms into one targeted therapy.

Types

The main categories of targeted therapy are small molecules and monoclonal antibodies. Small-molecule inhibitors are typically designed to interfere with enzymatic domains of target proteins, such as the tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Imatinib, used to treat chronic myelogenous leukemia, was the first highly effective targeted small molecule. Monoclonal antibodies are larger proteins that bind to targets on the cell surface, such as trastuzumab, which targets the HER2 receptor in certain breast cancers.

Mechanism

Most targeted therapies are either small-molecule drugs or monoclonal antibodies. Small molecules can penetrate the cell membrane and interact with targets inside the cell, usually signal transduction proteins. Monoclonal antibodies act on extracellular or cell-surface targets, and may recruit immune effector functions or deliver cytotoxic payloads.

Side effects

Although targeted therapies are often described as more selective than conventional chemotherapy, they have their own side-effect profiles, including skin rashes, diarrhea, hypertension, and impaired wound healing. Resistance to targeted agents commonly develops through secondary mutations in the target or activation of alternative pathways.
