Slave narrative

The slave narrative is a type of literary genre involving the (written) autobiographical accounts of enslaved persons, particularly Africans enslaved in the Americas, though many other examples exist. Over 6,000 such narratives are estimated to have been produced from the 18th century to the present; around 150 were published as separate books or pamphlets, and they were a major instrument of the abolitionist movement.

Early narratives

Early British and American examples include The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano (1789) and the narrative of Mary Prince (1831), the first account by a Black woman published in Britain. In North America the genre reached its height between the 1830s and the end of the Civil War, with classics such as Frederick Douglass's Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (1845), Solomon Northup's Twelve Years a Slave (1853) and Harriet Jacobs's Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl (1861).

Characteristics and later collections

Slave narratives typically follow a passage from bondage to freedom, assert the narrator's humanity and literacy (often beginning "Written by Himself"), and expose the violence and hypocrisy of slavery, frequently countering pro-slavery claims. In the 1930s the US Federal Writers' Project collected over 2,300 oral interviews with formerly enslaved people. The genre's conventions influenced later African-American autobiography and the modern neo-slave narrative, in novels such as Toni Morrison's Beloved.
