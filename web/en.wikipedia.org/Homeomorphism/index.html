Homeomorphism

In topology, a homeomorphism is a continuous function between topological spaces that has a continuous inverse function. Two spaces are called homeomorphic if a homeomorphism exists between them; such spaces are indistinguishable from the standpoint of topology, since a homeomorphism preserves all topological properties — connectedness, compactness, dimension (by invariance of domain), and the structure of open sets. The concept is the isomorphism of the category of topological spaces, and it formalises the intuitive idea of stretching, bending, and twisting a shape without tearing or gluing.

Definition

A function f : X → Y between topological spaces is a homeomorphism if it is bijective, continuous, and its inverse f−1 is continuous. Equivalently, f is a bijection that maps open sets to open sets (an open map) and is continuous. Care is needed because a continuous bijection need not be a homeomorphism: the map [0, 1) → S1 given by t ↦ (cos 2πt, sin 2πt) is a continuous bijection from a half-open interval onto the circle, but its inverse is discontinuous at (1, 0). A homeomorphism from a space to itself is called a self-homeomorphism; the set of these forms a group under composition, central to the study of mapping class groups and dynamical systems.

Examples and non-examples

Any open interval (a, b) is homeomorphic to the real line ℝ (via, for instance, a scaled tangent function), and to any other open interval; an open disk is homeomorphic to ℝ². A square's boundary and a circle are homeomorphic, as are a sphere and a cube's surface. The famous example equates a torus with a coffee mug: each has exactly one hole, and one can be deformed into the other continuously. By contrast, a sphere is not homeomorphic to a torus (their fundamental groups differ: π₁(S²) is trivial, π₁(T²) ≅ ℤ × ℤ), a circle is not homeomorphic to a figure-eight, and removing a point disconnects ℝ but not ℝ² — an elementary argument that spaces of different dimensions are not homeomorphic, proved in general by Brouwer's invariance of domain theorem (1912).

Properties and invariants

Properties preserved by homeomorphisms are precisely the topological properties: connectedness and path-connectedness, compactness, separability, the separation axioms, and algebraic invariants such as the fundamental group, homology and cohomology groups, and the Euler characteristic. Homeomorphism is an equivalence relation, and classifying spaces up to homeomorphism is a founding programme of topology: it succeeds completely for closed surfaces (classified by orientability and genus) and, through Perelman's proof of the Poincaré conjecture (2002–03), for simply connected closed 3-manifolds, but is intractable in general — there is no algorithm to decide whether two arbitrary manifolds of dimension ≥ 4 are homeomorphic.

Related notions

Weaker than homeomorphism is homotopy equivalence, which identifies spaces that can be deformed into one another allowing non-injectivity (a disk and a point are homotopy equivalent but not homeomorphic). Stronger notions include diffeomorphism, which requires smoothness and distinguishes exotic spheres — smooth manifolds homeomorphic but not diffeomorphic to the standard sphere, discovered by Milnor in 1956. In other branches of mathematics, analogous structure-preserving bijections (isomorphisms, isometries, biholomorphisms) play the same role.
