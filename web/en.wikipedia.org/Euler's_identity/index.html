Euler's identity

In mathematics, Euler's identity (also known as Euler's equation) is the equality:

eiπ + 1 = 0

where e is Euler's number (the base of natural logarithms), i is the imaginary unit, and π is pi, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. The identity is a special case of Euler's formula in complex analysis, eix = cos x + i sin x, evaluated at x = π.

Significance

Euler's identity is often cited as an example of deep mathematical beauty because it links five fundamental mathematical constants — 0, 1, e, i, and π — together with the three basic arithmetic operations (addition, multiplication, and exponentiation), each used exactly once. The physicist Richard Feynman called it "the most remarkable formula in mathematics." A 1988 poll of readers of The Mathematical Intelligencer ranked it the most beautiful theorem in mathematics.

Explanation

On the complex plane, eiθ traces the unit circle as θ varies. At θ = π, the point lies exactly opposite 1 on the real axis, at −1; hence eiπ = −1, and adding 1 gives 0. The identity thus expresses a geometric rotation by 180 degrees in algebraic form.

History

The identity appears in the work of Leonhard Euler, whose Introductio in analysin infinitorum (1748) systematically developed the exponential function for complex numbers. Euler's formula had been anticipated in part by Roger Cotes in 1714.
