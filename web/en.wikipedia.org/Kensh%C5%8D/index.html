Kenshō

A Zen monk in meditation, seeking insight into one's true nature.

Kenshō (Japanese: 見性, "seeing one's true nature") is a term from the Zen tradition of Japanese Buddhism referring to an initial insight or awakening experience in which a practitioner perceives the nature of the self and reality. It is often described as "seeing into one's own nature," that is, perceiving one's Buddha-nature.

Terminology

The term combines ken, meaning "seeing," with shō, meaning "nature" or "essence." Kenshō is commonly translated as "enlightenment" or "self-realization," though Zen teachers often distinguish it from satori, which is sometimes held to denote a deeper or more complete awakening. Kenshō may come in many degrees, ranging from a shallow glimpse to a profound view into the emptiness of phenomena.

Training

In Rinzai Zen, kenshō is cultivated through intensive practice, including zazen (seated meditation), kōan study, and sanzen (interviews with a teacher). A famous formulation of the aims of Zen attributed to Bodhidharma includes "direct pointing at the human mind" and "seeing one's nature and becoming a Buddha." Sudden experiences of kenshō are traditionally checked and acknowledged by a Zen master.

After kenshō

Kenshō is regarded as the beginning rather than the end of Zen training. Practice after awakening, sometimes called shōtaichōyō, is needed to deepen the insight and integrate it into daily life. The Zen ox-herding pictures illustrate this progression from searching for the ox to returning to the marketplace with helping hands.
