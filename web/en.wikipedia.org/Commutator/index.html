Commutator

In mathematics, the commutator gives an indication of the extent to which a certain binary operation fails to be commutative. There are different definitions used in group theory and ring theory.

Group theory

The commutator of two elements, g and h, of a group G, is the element

[g, h] = g−1h−1gh.

Some authors prefer the definition [g, h] = ghg−1h−1. The commutator is equal to the group's identity element if and only if gh = hg, i.e. if the two elements commute. In general, gh = hg[g,h].

The set of all commutators of a group is not in general closed under the group operation, but the subgroup of G generated by all commutators is called the derived group or the commutator subgroup of G. Commutators are used to define nilpotent and solvable groups and the largest abelian quotient group.

Ring and algebra theory

The commutator of two elements a and b of a ring or an associative algebra is defined by

[a, b] = ab − ba.

It is zero if and only if a and b commute. In linear algebra, if two endomorphisms of a space are represented by commuting matrices with respect to one basis, then they are so represented with respect to every basis. By using the commutator as a Lie bracket, every associative algebra can be turned into a Lie algebra.

The anticommutator of a and b is defined by {a, b} = ab + ba. It is especially used in Jordan algebras.

Identities

Commutators satisfy a number of identities in both group and ring contexts. In a ring or algebra, notable identities include:

  • [a, b] = −[b, a] (antisymmetry)
  • [a + b, c] = [a, c] + [b, c] (linearity)
  • [a, bc] = [a, b]c + b[a, c] (the Leibniz rule)
  • [a, [b, c]] + [b, [c, a]] + [c, [a, b]] = 0 (the Jacobi identity)

In quantum mechanics, the canonical commutation relation between position and momentum operators, [x̂, p̂] = iħ, is a foundational example; commutators of observables measure their incompatibility and underlie the uncertainty principle.
