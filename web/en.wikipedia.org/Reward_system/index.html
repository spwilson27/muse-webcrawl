Reward system

The reward system is a group of interconnected structures in the brain that are activated by rewarding stimuli — such as food, sex, social interaction and addictive drugs — and that reinforce the behaviours leading to those stimuli. It is central to motivation, learning and pleasure.

Anatomy

The core circuit is the mesolimbic pathway, in which dopaminergic neurons of the ventral tegmental area project to the nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum). Other important components include the amygdala, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex and the lateral hypothalamus. The neurotransmitter dopamine plays a central role, particularly in signalling reward prediction and motivation ("wanting"), while endogenous opioids are more closely associated with pleasure itself ("liking").

Function

The reward system drives organisms toward biologically useful outcomes and supports associative learning through reward prediction errors — differences between expected and received rewards that update future behaviour. Primary rewards satisfy survival needs; secondary rewards, such as money, acquire value through association.

Disorders

Dysfunction of the reward system is implicated in addiction, in which drugs of abuse hijack dopaminergic signalling; in depression, where reduced reward sensitivity produces anhedonia; and in conditions such as schizophrenia and ADHD.
