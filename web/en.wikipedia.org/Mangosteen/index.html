Mangosteen

Scientific classification
Kingdom:Plantae
Family:Clusiaceae
Genus:Garcinia
Species:G. mangostana

The mangosteen (Garcinia mangostana), also known as the purple mangosteen, is a tropical evergreen tree with edible fruit native to Maritime Southeast Asia. It has been cultivated since ancient times and is widely grown in countries such as Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and the Philippines. The fruit is frequently called the "queen of fruits," a title sometimes paired with the durian as the "king of fruits."

Description

The mangosteen tree grows from 6 to 25 metres (20 to 82 feet) tall. The fruit is round, dark purple when ripe, roughly the size of a tennis ball, with a thick, bitter, inedible rind (exocarp). Inside, the edible endocarp consists of four to eight white, juicy segments arranged like the sections of a citrus fruit. The flavor is described as sweet and tangy, with notes reminiscent of peach, strawberry, and citrus.

Cultivation

Mangosteen is an ultra-tropical plant: it cannot tolerate temperatures below about 4 °C (40 °F) and requires abundant rainfall and humidity. The tree is slow-growing and may take 6 to 12 years to bear fruit when grown from seed. Because the seeds are apomictic, new trees are essentially genetic clones of the mother plant. Propagation is therefore almost entirely by seed, and the species shows very little genetic diversity.

Uses

The fruit is usually eaten fresh. It is also canned, juiced, and used in desserts and traditional medicine in Southeast Asia. The rind contains xanthones, a class of polyphenolic compounds that have attracted scientific interest, though health claims based on them remain unproven. Mangosteen was long banned from import into the United States out of concern over fruit-fly pests; the ban was lifted in 2007 after irradiation treatment was approved.
