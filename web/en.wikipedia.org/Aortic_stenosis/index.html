Aortic stenosis (AS or AoS) is the narrowing of the exit of the left ventricle of the heart (where the aorta begins), such that problems result. It may occur at the aortic valve as well as above and below this level. It typically gets worse over time. Symptoms often come on gradually with a decreased ability to exercise occurring first. If heart failure, loss of consciousness, or heart-related chest pain occur due to AS, the outcomes are worse.

Causes and pathophysiology

In developed countries the commonest cause is age-related calcific degeneration of a normal three-leaflet valve; a congenitally bicuspid aortic valve (present in about 1–2% of people) calcifies earlier, typically presenting in the fifth or sixth decade. Rheumatic fever remains a major cause worldwide. The fixed obstruction raises left ventricular afterload, causing concentric hypertrophy; over time this leads to diastolic and then systolic dysfunction, angina from supply–demand mismatch, exertional syncope, and heart failure — the classic symptom triad whose onset historically marked a poor prognosis (average survival measured in a few years) without valve replacement.

Diagnosis and treatment

Examination may reveal a harsh crescendo–decrescendo systolic murmur radiating to the carotids and a slow-rising, weak pulse. Echocardiography quantifies valve area (severe: <1.0 cm²) and transvalvular gradients. No drug therapy alters progression; definitive treatment is aortic valve replacement, either surgical (SAVR) or transcatheter (TAVI/TAVR), with the choice guided by age, surgical risk and anatomy. Balloon valvuloplasty has a limited, mainly palliative or bridging role in adults.
