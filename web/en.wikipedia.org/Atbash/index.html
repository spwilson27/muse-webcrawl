Atbash

Atbash (אתב"ש) is a monoalphabetic substitution cipher originally used to encrypt the Hebrew alphabet. It can be modified for use with any known writing system with a standard collating order. The cipher works by mapping the first letter of the alphabet to the last, the second to the second-last, and so on; its name derives from the first, last, second and second-last letters of the Hebrew alphabet — Aleph, Tav, Beth, Shin.

Biblical examples

Several instances appear in the Hebrew Bible. In the Book of Jeremiah, "Sheshach" (ששך) is Atbash for "Babel" (בבל, Babylon), and "Lev Kamai" (לב קמי) encodes "Kasdim" — the Chaldeans. Scholars debate whether these were deliberate cipher substitutions or later scribal conventions.

Latin alphabet

Applied to the Latin alphabet, Atbash maps A↔Z, B↔Y, C↔X and so forth, so that "HELLO" becomes "SVOOL". The transformation is an involution: applying it twice restores the original text, making the cipher its own inverse.

Properties

As a fixed substitution with no key, Atbash offers essentially no security — it is a single specific case of the affine cipher (a = b = 25 in modular arithmetic where the mapping is x ↦ −x − 1). Its interest is historical and recreational: it appears in puzzle hunts, children's codes and popular fiction.

Modern use

Atbash features in works such as Dan Brown's novels and in classroom cryptography exercises, and is implemented in many cipher-tool collections alongside Caesar and ROT13 ciphers.
