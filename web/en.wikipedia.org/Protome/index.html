Protome

Griffin protomes adorned large bronze cauldrons in the ancient Greek world.

A protome (Greek: προτομή, protomē, "forepart") is a decorative element in ancient art consisting of the head and upper part of an animal or a human figure, used especially as an ornament on vessels, furniture and architecture. Protomes of griffins, lions, bulls and horses were cast in bronze and attached to the rims of large cauldrons dedicated at sanctuaries such as Olympia and Delphi during the Orientalising period of Greek art (roughly the 8th–7th centuries BC).

Origins and use

The griffin protome cauldron is thought to derive from Near Eastern models transmitted through North Syria and Urartu. In Greek sanctuaries these cauldrons, mounted on tripods, were among the most prestigious votive offerings of their age; Herodotus describes a colossal example dedicated by the Samians in the Heraion. Protomes also appear in Achaemenid Persian art — the double-protome capitals of Persepolis, in which two bulls or griffins face outward from a column, are a famous architectural variant — and in Etruscan and Roman decorative work.

In later terminology

The term survives in archaeology and art history for any bust-like termination depicting only the forepart of a creature, and is occasionally extended to similar elements in other traditions, such as the lion protomes of ancient Egyptian and Mesopotamian furniture.
