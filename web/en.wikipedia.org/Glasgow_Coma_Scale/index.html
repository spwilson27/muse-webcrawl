Glasgow Coma Scale


Assessing a patient's responses.

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a clinical scale used to reliably measure a person's level of consciousness after a brain injury. It was developed in 1974 by neurosurgeons Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett at the University of Glasgow.

Elements

The scale assesses three aspects of responsiveness, each scored separately and summed to give a total between 3 (deep unconsciousness) and 15 (fully awake):

ResponseBest scoreExamples
Eye opening4Spontaneous (4); to speech (3); to pain (2); none (1)
Verbal response5Orientated (5); confused (4); inappropriate words (3); incomprehensible sounds (2); none (1)
Motor response6Obeys commands (6); localises pain (5); withdraws (4); abnormal flexion (3); extension (2); none (1)

Interpretation

Brain injury is classically classified as severe (GCS 3–8), moderate (9–12) or minor (13–15). A GCS of 8 or below is a common threshold for intubation, as the patient may be unable to protect their airway. Scores are often reported by component, e.g. "E3 V4 M5 = 12".

Use and limitations

The GCS is used worldwide in trauma and acute care, including as part of the APACHE II score. Its limitations include difficulty scoring intubated or sedated patients and poor inter-rater reliability for the verbal component in some settings. A paediatric version modifies the verbal scale for children.
