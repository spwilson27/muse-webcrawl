Asabiyyah

Asabiyyah (Arabic: عصبية, ʿaṣabiyya) is an Arabic term for social cohesion, solidarity, group feeling or tribal loyalty. It is most famously developed in the work of the 14th-century historian and philosopher Ibn Khaldun, whose Muqaddimah (1377) made asabiyyah the central mechanism of his cyclical theory of the rise and fall of dynasties and civilizations.

Concept

Before Ibn Khaldun, the term was often used in a negative sense, denoting blind partisanship or factionalism condemned in Islamic teaching. Ibn Khaldun gave it a systematic sociological meaning: asabiyyah is the bond of cohesion among humans in a group, strongest among nomadic and tribal peoples whose harsh conditions of life require mutual defence and cooperation.

Ibn Khaldun's theory

In the Muqaddimah, Ibn Khaldun argues that dynasties arise when a group with strong asabiyyah — typically desert nomads — conquers settled peoples whose solidarity has decayed. Once in power, the ruling group gradually becomes corrupted by luxury and urban comfort; its asabiyyah weakens over roughly three or four generations (about 120 years), until the dynasty falls to a new group with fresh solidarity. Religion, in his analysis, can strengthen and extend asabiyyah by uniting groups under a shared cause.

Legacy

The concept has been compared to later sociological ideas such as Durkheim's collective consciousness and mechanical solidarity, and it remains influential in studies of tribalism, nationalism, state formation and social capital. Modern scholars have applied asabiyyah to topics ranging from the durability of political regimes to group cohesion in organizations.
