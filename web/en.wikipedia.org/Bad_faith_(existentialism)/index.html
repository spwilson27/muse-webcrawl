Bad faith (existentialism)

Bad faith (French: mauvaise foi) is a concept in existentialist philosophy, developed most fully by Jean-Paul Sartre in his 1943 work Being and Nothingness. It describes the phenomenon in which a person deceives themselves in order to deny their own freedom and avoid the anxiety that accompanies responsibility for their choices.

Concept

Sartre argued that human consciousness is defined by both facticity — the given facts of one's situation, body and past — and transcendence, the freedom to project oneself beyond those facts. Bad faith occurs when a person treats themselves as a fixed thing with a determined nature, thereby denying their freedom, or conversely denies the facts of their situation by treating them as unreal. Sartre's famous example is a waiter who plays at being a waiter too completely, performing the role as though it were an inescapable essence rather than a chosen activity.

Development

Simone de Beauvoir adapted the concept in The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947), exploring how individuals and societies evade freedom, and applied related ideas in The Second Sex. Bad faith remains a central notion in existentialism and has influenced discussions of authenticity, self-deception and moral responsibility.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether self-deception of the kind Sartre describes is coherent, since deceiver and deceived are the same mind, and whether Sartre's account of radical freedom exaggerates the power of individuals to transcend social and material constraints.
