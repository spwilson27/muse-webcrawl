Joseph-Louis Lagrange

Born: Giuseppe Lodovico Lagrangia, 25 January 1736, Turin
Died: 10 April 1813 (aged 77), Paris
Known for: Lagrangian mechanics, Lagrange points, calculus of variations, Lagrange's theorem

Joseph-Louis Lagrange (born Giuseppe Lodovico Lagrangia, 25 January 1736 – 10 April 1813), also reported as Giuseppe Luigi Lagrangia, was an Italian-French mathematician, physicist and astronomer, later naturalized French. He made significant contributions to the fields of analysis, number theory, and both classical and celestial mechanics.

Life

Born in Turin, then part of the Kingdom of Sardinia, Lagrange showed early brilliance in mathematics and was appointed professor at the Royal Artillery School of Turin at nineteen. At Euler's recommendation he became a member of the Berlin Academy, and in 1766 succeeded Euler as director of mathematics there, where he remained for twenty years. In 1787, at the invitation of Louis XVI, he moved to Paris. He survived the French Revolution and became a leading figure of the École Polytechnique.

Work

Lagrange's masterpiece, the Mécanique analytique (1788), recast Newtonian mechanics into an analytical framework free of diagrams, built on the principle of virtual work and generalized coordinates. The resulting Lagrangian mechanics remains a cornerstone of physics. He founded the calculus of variations jointly with Euler, introduced the method of Lagrange multipliers, proved results on the solvability of equations, and made major contributions to number theory, including a proof that every natural number is the sum of four squares. In celestial mechanics he identified the five Lagrange points and studied the stability of planetary orbits.

Legacy

Lagrange was honoured by Napoleon, who made him a senator and count of the Empire. His name is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower, and the lunar crater Lagrange and the asteroid 1006 Lagrangea commemorate him.
