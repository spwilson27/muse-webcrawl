Stare decisis underpins precedent in common-law courts.

Stare decisis is a legal doctrine through which judges are obliged to respect the precedent established by prior decisions. The words originate from the Latin phrase stare decisis et non quieta movere: "to stand by decisions and not disturb the undisturbed". In a legal context it means that courts should abide by precedent and not disturb settled matters.

Principle

The doctrine operates both vertically — lower courts must follow the rulings of higher courts in their hierarchy — and horizontally, where a court generally follows its own previous decisions. It promotes consistency, predictability and equality of treatment in the law, and underlies the common-law method of deciding cases by analogy to earlier ones.

Binding and persuasive precedent

A precedent is binding when it comes from a superior court within the same hierarchy and the material facts are analogous. Decisions from other jurisdictions, or remarks not essential to the outcome (obiter dicta), are merely persuasive. The part of a judgment that binds is its ratio decidendi, the rule of law on which the decision rests.

Departing from precedent

Higher appellate courts may overrule their own precedents, though they do so rarely: the UK House of Lords announced in the Practice Statement of 1966 that it would depart from its earlier decisions "when it appears right to do so", and the US Supreme Court weighs factors such as workability, reliance and the quality of the precedent's reasoning. Legislatures may also override judge-made rules by statute.
