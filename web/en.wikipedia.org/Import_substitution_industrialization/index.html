Import substitution industrialization (ISI) is a trade and economic policy that advocates replacing foreign imports with domestic production. Under ISI, developing countries use tariffs, quotas, subsidies and state investment to nurture local industries — especially manufacturing — so that goods once bought abroad are made at home, reducing dependence on foreign markets and, in theory, capturing more value within the national economy.

Intellectual origins

Elements of the strategy reach back to Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures and Friedrich List's "infant industry" argument: young industries need temporary protection to survive against established foreign competitors. ISI as a coherent development doctrine emerged in the mid-20th century from the work of the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLAC) under Raúl Prebisch. The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis held that the terms of trade for primary commodities decline relative to manufactures over time, so countries exporting raw materials and importing manufactures face a structural disadvantage unless they industrialise.

Implementation

Latin American countries such as Brazil, Mexico and Argentina adopted ISI in stages: first substituting "easy" consumer goods (food processing, textiles), then attempting the harder stage of intermediate and capital goods (steel, chemicals, automobiles), often through state-owned enterprises. Governments shielded firms with high tariffs and import licences, kept exchange rates overvalued to cheapen imported machinery, and channelled credit to favoured sectors. Similar policies were pursued in post-independence India, Egypt, Turkey and much of sub-Saharan Africa.

Results and criticism

ISI delivered rapid industrial growth in its early decades and built substantial manufacturing capacity. But by the 1960s–70s its weaknesses were widely noted: protected firms, facing small domestic markets and little competition, often remained inefficient and technologically dependent on imported inputs; overvalued currencies hurt agriculture and exports; and reliance on foreign borrowing left ISI economies exposed in the debt crisis of the 1980s. Economists contrasted Latin America's stagnation with the rapid growth of East Asian economies that shifted toward export-oriented industrialization.

Defenders reply that ISI was undermined by external shocks and premature liberalisation, and that virtually all now-rich countries industrialised behind some protection. The strategy remains influential in development debates, and elements of it — local content rules, strategic protection, industrial policy — have returned to fashion worldwide.
