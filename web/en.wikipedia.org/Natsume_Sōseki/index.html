Natsume Sōseki (born Natsume Kin'nosuke, 1867–1916) was a Japanese novelist widely regarded as the greatest writer in modern Japanese literature. His novels explore the psychological tensions of individuals caught between traditional Japanese values and Western modernity during the Meiji era. His portrait appeared on the Japanese 1,000-yen banknote from 1984 to 2004.

Life

Born in Edo (now Tokyo) as the unwanted youngest child of a large family, Sōseki was fostered out in infancy. He studied English literature at Tokyo Imperial University and later taught school in Matsuyama and Kumamoto — experiences he drew on in the comic novel Botchan (1906). From 1900 to 1903 he studied in London on a government scholarship, an unhappy period marked by poverty and isolation but also intense reading. On his return he lectured at his university while beginning to write fiction.

Career

Sōseki won early fame with the satirical I Am a Cat (1905), narrated by a supercilious household cat. In 1907 he resigned his university post to write full-time for the Asahi Shimbun newspaper, an unprecedented step for a serious author. His mature works include the trilogy Sanshirō, And Then, and The Gate, and the late novels Kokoro (1914), his most famous work, and the unfinished Light and Darkness.

Legacy

Plagued by stomach ulcers and nervous illness, Sōseki died in 1916 at the age of 49. His exploration of egoism, loneliness, and fractured relationships shaped the Japanese I-novel tradition and generations of writers, including his disciples Ryūnosuke Akutagawa and Masao Kume.
