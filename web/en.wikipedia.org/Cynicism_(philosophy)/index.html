Cynicism (philosophy)

Cynicism is a school of ancient Greek philosophy whose practitioners believed that the purpose of life is to live in virtue, in agreement with nature, and that happiness comes from self-sufficiency and freedom from possessions, social conventions, and the opinions of others. The name derives from the Greek kynikos, "dog-like", a nickname the early Cynics embraced.

FounderAntisthenes (c. 445–365 BCE)
Most famous memberDiogenes of Sinope
Era4th century BCE – 5th century CE
Key ideasVirtue, self-sufficiency (autarky), frank speech (parrhesia), cosmopolitanism

Origins

Antisthenes, a pupil of Socrates, taught in the Cynosarges gymnasium at Athens and laid the school's foundations. His follower Diogenes of Sinope became its emblematic figure, living in poverty in a large ceramic jar, carrying a lantern in daylight "looking for an honest man", and famously telling Alexander the Great to stand out of his sunlight.

Philosophy

Cynics held that virtue is the only good and is sufficient for happiness; wealth, reputation, and pleasure were indifferent or harmful. They practised ascetic training, shamelessness toward convention (anaideia), and outspokenness. Diogenes declared himself a "citizen of the world" (kosmopolites), coining the term cosmopolitanism. Later figures include Crates of Thebes, his wife Hipparchia, and, in the Roman era, Demetrius and Demonax.

Influence

Cynicism strongly influenced Stoicism, founded by Zeno of Citium, a pupil of Crates. Its critique of convention and its ideal of the self-sufficient sage echoed into later philosophy and early Christian asceticism. In modern usage, "cynicism" has shifted to mean a distrustful attitude toward others' motives — a sense quite different from the ancient school.
