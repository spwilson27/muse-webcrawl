Induced seismicity

CausesWastewater injection, hydraulic fracturing, reservoirs, mining, geothermal energy
MechanismPore-pressure changes reactivate faults
Notable examplesOklahoma (2011 Prague, 2016 Pawnee), Basel 2006

Induced seismicity is earthquake activity caused or triggered by human activity that alters stresses and fluid pressures in the Earth's crust. Most induced earthquakes are small, but some have exceeded magnitude 5 and caused damage, prompting regulation of subsurface industrial operations.

Mechanisms

Injecting fluid underground raises pore pressure, reducing effective normal stress on faults and allowing them to slip. Other triggers include reservoir impoundment (the weight and seepage of large lakes), extraction of oil, gas, groundwater or geothermal heat, mining, and hydraulic fracturing, which typically induces microseismicity but occasionally larger events.

Notable cases

A sharp rise in earthquakes in Oklahoma and other central U.S. states in the 2010s was linked mainly to deep disposal of oilfield wastewater; the 2011 Prague (M5.7) and 2016 Pawnee (M5.8) earthquakes damaged buildings. A geothermal project at Basel, Switzerland, was halted in 2006 after thousands of small quakes. Reservoir-induced seismicity is suspected at Koyna Dam, India, site of a damaging M6.3 quake in 1967.

Monitoring and mitigation

Regulators use "traffic-light" protocols that reduce or stop injection when seismicity crosses thresholds, along with seismic monitoring, injection-rate limits and setback rules. Research continues into forecasting maximum magnitudes and distinguishing induced from natural events.
