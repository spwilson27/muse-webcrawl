Niklas Luhmann (December 8, 1927 – November 6, 1998) was a German sociologist and philosopher of social systems theory. His theory of society, developed across some seventy books and hundreds of articles, treats modern society as composed of self-reproducing ('autopoietic') systems of communication—such as the economy, law, science, politics and art—each operating with its own binary code.

After a career in public administration, Luhmann became professor of sociology at the newly founded University of Bielefeld in 1968, a post he held until his retirement. He famously kept a vast card index (Zettelkasten) of around 90,000 notes, which he credited as a partner in his thinking.

Theory

Luhmann radicalized Talcott Parsons's systems theory by making communication—not action or individuals—the basic element of social systems, and by borrowing the concept of autopoiesis from biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela. His magnum opus Social Systems (1984) and the posthumous Theory of Society (1997) present a complete account of modern, functionally differentiated society. His public debate with Jürgen Habermas in the 1970s was a major event in German social theory.
