Perturbation theory

In mathematics and applied science, perturbation theory comprises methods for finding an approximate solution to a problem by starting from an exact solution of a related, simpler problem and adding corrections that account for a small "perturbation". The approximate solution is usually expressed as a power series in a small parameter, whose first terms give the dominant behaviour and higher terms refine it. The technique is used when no exact closed-form solution exists.

Origins

Perturbation methods grew out of celestial mechanics, where astronomers such as Lagrange and Laplace computed the small gravitational influences of planets on each other's orbits; their application to the Moon's motion and the discovery of Neptune from irregularities in the orbit of Uranus were celebrated successes. Henri Poincaré's work showed that the series involved can diverge and that perturbed systems can behave chaotically.

Quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics, perturbation theory splits a Hamiltonian into a solvable unperturbed part and a small perturbation, then corrects the energy levels and eigenstates order by order. Time-independent perturbation theory treats static changes; time-dependent theory handles transitions and underlies calculations such as Fermi's golden rule. Perturbative expansions also form the computational backbone of quantum field theory, where interaction terms are expanded diagrammatically using Feynman diagrams, although the resulting series are typically asymptotic rather than convergent.

Regular and singular problems

A perturbation problem is regular if the approximate solution converges uniformly to the true one; it is singular when the small parameter multiplies the highest derivative or the solution develops boundary layers, requiring techniques such as matched asymptotic expansions.
