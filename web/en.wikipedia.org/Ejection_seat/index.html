Ejection seat

An ejection seat is a system designed to rescue the pilot or crew of an aircraft, usually a military one, in an emergency. The seat is propelled out of the aircraft by an explosive charge or rocket motor, carries its occupant clear of the aircraft, and then deploys a parachute for a safe descent. Modern seats can operate at zero altitude and zero airspeed ("zero-zero" capability).

History

Early concepts date to the 1910s; the Romanian inventor Anastase Dragomir patented a parachuted cell concept tested in 1929. The first practical seats were developed during the Second World War: German Heinkel and Swedish Saab designs used compressed air and later explosive charges. In 1946 Bernard Lynch made the first live test of a Martin-Baker seat in Britain. Martin-Baker remains a leading manufacturer, and its seats have saved thousands of aircrew.

Operation

Pulling the ejection handle typically fires the canopy jettison or shattering system, then a catapult (explosive cartridge and later a rocket pack) launches seat and occupant. Restraints pull the legs in and an oxygen supply is provided. After clearing the aircraft, a drogue parachute stabilizes and slows the seat before the main parachute deploys and the occupant separates from the seat, which falls away. Barometric and time-delay devices automate the sequence for different altitudes and speeds.

Risks

Ejection subjects the body to forces of roughly 12–20 g, and spinal injuries are a known risk; historically only about half of ejections at very high speed or low altitude were fully successful. Early Soviet and Western space programs also used ejection seats — Vostok cosmonauts ejected before landing, and early Gemini concepts considered them before adopting escape towers and other systems.
