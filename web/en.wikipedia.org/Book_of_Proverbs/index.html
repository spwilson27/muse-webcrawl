Book of Proverbs

Series: Wisdom literature (Ketuvim)

Traditionally attributed to: Solomon

Chapters: 31

Language: Biblical Hebrew

The Book of Proverbs (Hebrew: מִשְׁלֵי, Mishlei) is a book of the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament, a collection of moral and philosophical sayings gathered over centuries. It belongs to the wisdom literature and is traditionally attributed largely to King Solomon. Its purpose, stated in its opening verses, is to impart wisdom, discipline, and understanding, beginning from the principle that "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge."

Structure

The book falls into several collections. Chapters 1–9 are discourses in praise of Wisdom, personified as a woman calling in the streets, contrasted with folly. Chapters 10–22 contain the "proverbs of Solomon," hundreds of short, mostly two-line sayings on diligence, honesty, speech, family life, and wealth. Later sections add the "words of the wise," proverbs copied by the scribes of King Hezekiah, the words of Agur, the words of King Lemuel, and the closing acrostic poem in praise of the capable wife (chapter 31).

Themes

Proverbs presents an ordered moral universe in which wise, righteous conduct generally leads to prosperity and folly to ruin, though the sayings are observations of typical outcomes rather than guarantees. Recurring topics include the power of the tongue, the dangers of laziness, pride, and drunkenness, justice for the poor, and the choice of companions.

Composition

Scholars date the collections' compilation from the monarchic period to the post-exilic era, drawing on older Israelite and neighbouring Egyptian wisdom traditions such as the Instruction of Amenemope. See also the Book of Psalms and the Book of Joshua.
