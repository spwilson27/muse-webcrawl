Rowing, sometimes called crew in the United States, is a sport in which athletes race boats — narrow racing shells — propelled by oars. Rowers sit facing backwards, sliding on movable seats, and drive the boat with a stroke cycle divided into the catch, drive, finish and recovery. Crews may be steered by a coxswain (cox) or steered by the rowers themselves.

Boat classes

There are two disciplines: in sweep rowing each rower uses one oar with both hands; in sculling each rower uses two smaller oars. Standard classes range from the single scull (1x) through doubles and pairs, quads and fours, to the eight (8+), which carries eight rowers and a coxswain and is the fastest class, reaching speeds above 22 km/h. Olympic and World Championship racing is held over 2,000 metres on buoyed, straight courses.

Technique and training

Power comes mainly from the legs, transmitted through the trunk swing to the arms. Because boats run best with all crew members moving in perfect unison, timing and technique are prized alongside strength and endurance. Off-water training centres on the rowing ergometer ("erg"), particularly the Concept2 machine, and winter competition increasingly includes indoor rowing championships.

History and competition

Competitive rowing developed in early nineteenth-century England: the Boat Race between Oxford and Cambridge began in 1829 and Henley Royal Regatta in 1839. The governing body now known as World Rowing was founded in 1892, and rowing has been an Olympic sport for men since 1900 and for women since 1976. Major events include the annual World Rowing Championships, the World Cup series and, for juniors and under-23s, dedicated world championships. Variants include coastal rowing, para-rowing and lightweight categories.
