The Grand Canyon is a steep-sided canyon carved by the Colorado River in Arizona, United States. It is 277 miles (446 km) long, up to 18 miles (29 km) wide and attains a depth of over a mile (6,093 feet or 1,857 metres). The canyon and adjacent rim are contained within Grand Canyon National Park, the Kaibab National Forest, the Grand Canyon–Parashant National Monument, and the Hualapai, Havasupai and Navajo reservations.

For thousands of years the area has been inhabited by Native Americans, who built settlements within the canyon and its caves. The Pueblo people considered the Grand Canyon a holy site and made pilgrimages to it. The first European known to have viewed it was García López de Cárdenas in 1540, a member of the Coronado expedition.

Geology

The Grand Canyon exposes an exceptionally complete sequence of Proterozoic and Paleozoic strata, uplifted with the Colorado Plateau and incised by the river over roughly the last 5–6 million years. From the rim downward the principal formations include the Kaibab Limestone, Toroweap Formation, Coconino Sandstone, Hermit Shale, the Supai Group, the cliff-forming Redwall Limestone, and, in the inner gorge, the ancient Vishnu Schist and Zoroaster Granite of the basement, some 1.8 billion years old. The canyon was a key site in the development of geological thought about deep time and river incision, notably through the surveys of John Wesley Powell, who led the first recorded passage of the canyon by boat in 1869.

Tourism

Grand Canyon National Park receives around five to six million visitors a year. The South Rim, developed with the historic Grand Canyon Village, the El Tovar Hotel and Mary Colter's Desert View Watchtower, is open year-round; the higher North Rim is open seasonally. Activities include rim hiking, mule trips, rafting the Colorado River, and viewing from the glass Skywalk on the Hualapai Reservation at Grand Canyon West.
