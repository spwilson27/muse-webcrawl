The Haymarket bombing of May 4, 1886, a landmark event in labour history.

The Haymarket affair, also known as the Haymarket massacre and the Haymarket riot, was the aftermath of a bombing that took place at a labour demonstration on May 4, 1886, at Haymarket Square in Chicago, Illinois, United States. The rally, held to protest police killings of strikers at the McCormick Reaper Works the day before and to demand the eight-hour workday, turned violent when an unknown person threw a dynamite bomb at police as they moved to disperse the crowd; gunfire followed, and at least seven police officers and four civilians were killed.

Trial and executions

Eight anarchists — including August Spies, Albert Parsons, Adolph Fischer, George Engel, and Louis Lingg — were tried for conspiracy to murder. The prosecution presented little evidence linking the defendants to the bomb itself, relying instead on their writings and speeches. All eight were convicted; seven were sentenced to death and one, Oscar Neebe, to 15 years in prison. Lingg died in his cell, apparently by suicide, and four men — Spies, Parsons, Fischer, and Engel — were hanged on November 11, 1887. The remaining death sentences were commuted to life imprisonment.

Pardons and legacy

In 1893 Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the surviving defendants and issued a report condemning the trial as a miscarriage of justice, a decision that ended his political career. The Haymarket events became a symbol of the international labour movement: the Second International chose May 1 as International Workers' Day in commemoration, and the executed men are memorialized by the Haymarket Martyrs' Monument in Forest Park, Illinois, designated a National Historic Landmark. The identity of the bomb thrower — often speculated to be Rudolph Schnaubelt — was never proven.
