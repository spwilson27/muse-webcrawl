Graphon

In the study of dense graphs, a graphon (also known as a graph limit) is a symmetric measurable function W : [0,1]² → [0,1] that captures the limit of a convergent sequence of dense graphs. The theory of graphons, developed principally by László Lovász and co-authors in the 2000s, provides a continuous analogue of graph theory in which large finite graphs are approximated by functions, allowing analytic and probabilistic tools to be applied to combinatorial questions.

Definition and sampling

Given a graphon W, a random graph on n vertices can be generated by assigning each vertex i an independent uniform random value xi in [0,1] and then joining vertices i and j with probability W(xi, xj). Sequences of graphs converge (in the sense of homomorphism densities, or equivalently in the cut distance) exactly when they are described by a common limiting graphon. Every graph can itself be viewed as a step-function graphon, embedding finite graphs into the same space.

Properties

Graphons were introduced in work by Borgs, Chayes, Lovász, Sós, Szegedy and Vesztergombi on convergent graph sequences and the limits of dense graph sequences. The space of graphons is compact under the cut metric after identifying functions related by measure-preserving transformations. The theory connects graph theory with probability (exchangeable random graphs, and the Aldous–Hoover representation), statistics (estimating network models), and analysis; the related sparse-graph theory uses graphings and local convergence instead.

Applications

Graphons serve as nonparametric models for real-world networks, underpinning methods for estimating large network structure from sampled data, and they appear in property testing, extremal combinatorics and statistical physics. Lovász's monograph Large Networks and Graph Limits (2012) is the standard reference for the subject.
