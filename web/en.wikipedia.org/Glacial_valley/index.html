Glacial valley

A glacial valley is a valley formed by the erosive action of a glacier moving over land. Unlike river valleys, which typically have a V-shaped cross-section, glacial valleys characteristically have a steep-sided, flat-bottomed U-shaped profile, because the ice mass grinds down and widens the valley floor as it advances.

Formation

When a glacier flows through a pre-existing river valley, it straightens the course, removes interlocking spurs, and deepens and widens the channel. Abrasion by rock debris embedded in the ice, together with plucking of bedrock from the valley sides and floor, produces the classic U-shape. Tributary valleys, eroded less deeply because their glaciers carried less ice, are left as hanging valleys high above the main trough, often marked by waterfalls after the ice melts.

Features

Glacial valleys commonly contain ribbon lakes occupying rock basins gouged by the ice, and may be flooded by the sea to form fjords where the glacier carved below sea level. Depositional landforms such as moraines and drumlins may partly fill the valley floor. Well-known examples include Yosemite Valley in California, the Lauterbrunnen Valley in Switzerland, and the fjords of Norway and New Zealand.
