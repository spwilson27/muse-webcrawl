Cyril (born Constantine, 826/827–869) and Methodius (815–885) were brothers from Thessalonica in the Byzantine Empire who became missionaries and Christianized the Slavs. They are known as the "Apostles to the Slavs" and are credited with devising the Glagolitic alphabet, the first alphabet used to transcribe Old Church Slavonic.

The Moravian mission

In 863, at the request of Prince Rastislav of Great Moravia, who sought missionaries who could preach in the Slavic language, the Byzantine emperor Michael III sent the brothers to Moravia. Before leaving, Constantine created the Glagolitic script and translated the Gospels and liturgical books into Slavonic. Their mission provoked opposition from German clergy who insisted on the Latin liturgy, and the brothers travelled to Rome, where Pope Adrian II approved the Slavonic liturgy. Constantine died in Rome in 869, taking the monastic name Cyril.

Methodius and the legacy

Methodius returned to Moravia as archbishop and continued the mission until his death in 885, despite imprisonment by Frankish bishops. Afterwards his disciples were expelled from Moravia; they found refuge in Bulgaria, where they developed the script now called Cyrillic in Cyril's honour and spread Slavonic literacy throughout the Orthodox Slavic world.

In 1980 Pope John Paul II declared Cyril and Methodius co-patrons of Europe. Their feast day is a public holiday in Bulgaria, North Macedonia, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, and Russia, celebrated as a day of Slavic culture and letters. See also Old Church Slavonic and Glagolitic script.
