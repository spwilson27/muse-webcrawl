Sir Walter Scott, 1st Baronet (15 August 1771 – 21 September 1832), was a Scottish novelist, poet and historian who is credited with popularizing historical fiction. Many of his works remain classics of European and Scottish literature, notably the novels Ivanhoe (1819), Rob Roy (1817), Waverley (1814), Old Mortality (1816), The Heart of Mid-Lothian (1818), and The Bride of Lammermoor (1819), along with the narrative poems Marmion (1808) and The Lady of the Lake (1810).

Life and career

Scott was born in Edinburgh, survived a childhood bout of polio that left him lame, and trained as a lawyer like his father. He began his literary career collecting and publishing Scottish border ballads in The Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802–03), then achieved fame with his narrative poems. In 1814 he published Waverley, set during the Jacobite rising of 1745, initially anonymously; its success launched the series known as the Waverley novels and established the template for the modern historical novel, influencing writers across Europe and America.

Scott built his great house at Abbotsford on the River Tweed and was made a baronet in 1820. A financial crash in 1825–26, involving his publishing partners, left him with enormous debts, which he resolved to repay by writing; he produced a vast output, including a nine-volume biography of Napoleon, before his death at Abbotsford in 1832. The debts were paid off from his posthumous earnings.

Influence

Scott's novels shaped the image of Scotland abroad, revived interest in the Middle Ages, and helped fashion a romantic Scottish identity; he stage-managed George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822, the first by a reigning monarch in centuries, popularizing tartan pageantry. The Scott Monument in Edinburgh is one of the largest monuments to a writer in the world.
