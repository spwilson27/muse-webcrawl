Semi-presidential republic

System of governmentMixed presidential–parliamentary
ExecutiveDual: president and prime minister
Term coinedMaurice Duverger, 1970s
Well-known exampleFifth French Republic (from 1958)

A semi-presidential republic is a system of government in which a president exists alongside a prime minister and a cabinet, with the latter two responsible to the legislature. It combines features of presidential and parliamentary systems: the president is usually directly elected for a fixed term and holds significant powers, while the prime minister manages day-to-day government and must retain the confidence of parliament.

Definition

The French political scientist Maurice Duverger defined semi-presidentialism by three criteria: the president is elected by universal suffrage, possesses quite considerable powers, and faces a prime minister and ministers who hold executive and governmental power and can remain in office only if parliament does not oppose them. Scholars such as Robert Elgie later simplified the definition to the mere coexistence of a directly elected fixed-term president and a prime minister responsible to the legislature.

Variants and cohabitation

Analysts distinguish premier–presidential systems, where the prime minister is accountable exclusively to parliament, from president–parliamentary systems, where the cabinet answers to both the president and the legislature. In France, periods when the president and parliamentary majority come from opposing camps — called cohabitation — shift effective power toward the prime minister.

Examples

Semi-presidential systems are found in France, Portugal, Poland, Romania, Ukraine, Taiwan, Egypt and a number of post-Soviet and African states, though the actual balance of power varies widely in practice.
