Earned run average

In baseball statistics, earned run average (ERA) is the average of earned runs allowed by a pitcher per nine innings pitched (i.e., the traditional length of a game). It is calculated as:

ERA = 9 × (earned runs ÷ innings pitched)

Runs scored because of defensive errors (including pitchers' own errors) or passed balls are counted as unearned and excluded, so ERA is intended to measure a pitcher's own effectiveness independently of his fielders.

History

The statistic originated in the early 1900s, when relief pitchers were rare and starters were expected to complete games; Henry Chadwick is often credited with devising it, and the National League first tabulated ERA in 1912. As bullpen use increased, ERA comparisons across eras became complicated by changes in rules, ballparks and strategy.

Interpretation

League-average ERA has varied widely over time: it hovered near or below 3.00 in the "dead-ball era", exceeded 4.50 in the high-offence 1930s and 2000s, and was remarkably low in 1968 (the "Year of the Pitcher"). In modern Major League Baseball, an ERA under 3.00 is considered excellent, while the best single seasons include Bob Gibson's 1.12 in 1968 and Dutch Leonard's record 0.96 in 1914. Career leaders (with minimum innings) include Ed Walsh (1.82). Advanced metrics such as FIP and ERA+ adjust ERA for defence, park and league context.
