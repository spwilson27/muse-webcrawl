Microfabrication

Microfabrication is the process of fabricating miniature structures of micrometre scales and smaller. Historically, the earliest microfabrication processes were used for integrated circuit fabrication, also known as semiconductor manufacturing or semiconductor device fabrication. In the last two decades microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), microsystems, micromachines and their subfields have re-used, adapted or extended microfabrication methods. These subfields include microfluidics/lab-on-a-chip, optical MEMS (also called MOEMS), RF MEMS, PowerMEMS, BioMEMS and their extension into nanoscale dimensions.

Origins

Microfabrication has its origins in the semiconductor industry. The planar process developed by Jean Hoerni at Fairchild Semiconductor in the late 1950s, combined with the photolithographic techniques already used to make printed circuit boards, allowed the batch production of transistors and integrated circuits with features measured in micrometres.

Key processes

  • Photolithography — transferring a pattern from a photomask to a light-sensitive chemical photoresist on the substrate.
  • Thin-film deposition — growing or depositing material layers by techniques such as chemical vapor deposition (CVD), physical vapor deposition (PVD), sputtering, evaporation and electroplating.
  • Etching — removing material selectively by wet chemical etching or dry plasma etching (reactive-ion etching).
  • Doping — introducing impurities into a semiconductor by diffusion or ion implantation to modify its electrical properties.
  • Thermal oxidation — growing a layer of silicon dioxide on a silicon wafer.
  • Polishing — chemical-mechanical polishing (CMP) planarizes the wafer surface between steps.
  • Bonding and packaging — joining wafers or dies and encapsulating finished devices.

Cleanrooms

Microfabrication is carried out in cleanrooms, where airborne particles are filtered to levels specified by standards such as ISO 14644. Operators wear full-body garments ("bunny suits") to avoid contaminating the wafers, since a single dust particle can destroy a device.

Applications

Beyond integrated circuits, microfabrication produces accelerometers and gyroscopes for phones and cars, inkjet printer heads, pressure sensors, micromirror arrays for projectors, lab-on-a-chip diagnostic devices, and microfluidic channels used in chemical and biological research.
