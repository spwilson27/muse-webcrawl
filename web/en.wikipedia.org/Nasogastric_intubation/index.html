Nasogastric intubation

Nasogastric intubation is a medical process involving the insertion of a plastic tube (nasogastric tube or NG tube) through the nose, down the oesophagus and into the stomach. The related orogastric intubation passes the tube through the mouth instead. The procedure is performed by trained medical personnel and is used for feeding, administration of medication, or gastric decompression and drainage.

Uses

  • Enteral feeding for patients who cannot swallow safely but have a functioning gut.
  • Administration of medications and activated charcoal (in poisoning, usually orogastric).
  • Aspiration/decompression of stomach contents in bowel obstruction, ileus, or after surgery.
  • Diagnostic sampling of gastric contents and gastric lavage in selected cases.

Procedure and verification

The lubricated tube is passed through a nostril while the patient sips water and swallows; the external length is usually estimated by measuring from nose to earlobe to xiphisternum (the NEX measurement). Correct placement must be verified before anything is given through the tube — most reliably by testing aspirate pH (≤5.5 on CE-marked paper) or by X-ray; the old "whoosh test" (listening for injected air) is unreliable and no longer recommended. Misplacement into the lungs can be fatal if feed is then administered, so such events are classified as "never events" in NHS practice.

Complications

Minor complications include nosebleeds, sinusitis and sore throat. Serious complications include aspiration, tube misplacement into the trachea and lungs, oesophageal perforation, and erosion of the nasal tissue with prolonged use. Finer, softer tubes (such as Dobhoff tubes) are used for longer-term feeding; wider-bore Ryles tubes suit drainage and short-term use.
