A CT scan, or computed tomography scan (formerly computed axial tomography or CAT scan), is a medical imaging technique used to obtain detailed internal images of the body. CT scanners use a rotating X-ray tube and a row of detectors placed in a gantry to measure X-ray attenuations by different tissues inside the body. A computer processes the measurements using tomographic reconstruction algorithms to produce cross-sectional (tomographic) images — "slices" — of the body, which can be combined into three-dimensional views.

History

The mathematical theory behind CT was developed by Allan McLeod Cormack, while the first commercially viable scanner was built by Godfrey Hounsfield at EMI Laboratories in England. The first patient scan was performed in 1971 at Atkinson Morley Hospital in London; Cormack and Hounsfield shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Tissue density on CT images is expressed in Hounsfield units (HU), with water defined as 0 and air as −1000.

Uses

CT is widely used for diagnosing trauma injuries, cancers and their staging, cardiovascular disease, infections and musculoskeletal disorders, and for guiding biopsies and interventions. CT pulmonary angiography is a standard test for pulmonary embolism, and CT colonography can screen for colorectal cancer. Because it involves ionising radiation, its use is balanced against risk — particularly in children — under the ALARA principle ("as low as reasonably achievable"), and MRI or ultrasound may be preferred where they give equivalent information.

Procedure

The patient lies on a motorised table that moves through the gantry while the X-ray source rotates. Iodinated contrast agents may be given intravenously or orally to enhance blood vessels and organs. Modern multislice scanners acquire whole-body images in seconds.
