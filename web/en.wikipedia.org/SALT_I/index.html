SALT I is the common name for the first round of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks between the United States and the Soviet Union, culminating in two agreements signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 during President Richard Nixon's summit visit: the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty and the Interim Agreement on the Limitation of Strategic Offensive Arms. SALT I was a centrepiece of Cold War détente and the first agreement to place limits on nuclear weapons delivery systems.

Background

By the late 1960s both superpowers had achieved rough nuclear parity, and the expense and danger of an unconstrained arms race — especially in anti-ballistic missile defences and multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs) — drove both sides to the negotiating table. Talks opened in Helsinki in November 1969 and alternated between Helsinki and Vienna.

The agreements

ABM Treaty

The Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty limited each side to two ABM deployment sites (later reduced by protocol to one), each with no more than 100 interceptor missiles, on the theory that nationwide missile defence would undermine deterrence and spur an offensive arms race. The treaty was of unlimited duration (the United States withdrew in 2002).

Interim Agreement

The five-year Interim Agreement froze the number of intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM) and submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) launchers at existing levels — roughly 1,618 ICBMs and 740 SLBMs for the USSR, and 1,054 ICBMs and 656 SLBMs for the United States — but did not limit MIRVed warheads, bombers, or missile modernisation, gaps that critics attacked as allowing the arms race to continue qualitatively.

Legacy

SALT I established verification by "national technical means" (reconnaissance satellites) as an accepted practice and created the Standing Consultative Commission. It paved the way for the Vladivostok framework and SALT II in 1979, and is remembered as both a landmark of arms control and an illustration of its limits.
