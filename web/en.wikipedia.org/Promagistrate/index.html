Promagistrate

In ancient Rome, a promagistrate (Latin: pro magistratu) was a person who was granted the power (imperium) of a magistrate after their term of office expired, generally so that they could govern a province or command an army. The most common promagistracies were the proconsul and the propraetor, former consuls and praetors respectively whose authority was prorogued by the Senate or the people. The prefix pro- means "in place of" or "on behalf of," indicating that the holder acted with the authority of the magistracy he had previously held.

Origins and development

Prorogation first appeared during the Second Punic War era, when Rome's expanding military commitments made it impractical to replace experienced commanders annually. The first recorded prorogation was that of Publius Cornelius Scipio in 211 BC. Over time, governing provinces as a proconsul or propraetor became a standard stage in a senatorial career, and under the lex Pompeia of 52 BC a five-year interval was required between a magistracy in Rome and a provincial governorship.

Role in the late Republic

Provincial commands brought wealth, military glory, and client networks, making them fiercely contested. Long commands — such as Julius Caesar's decade in Gaul as proconsul — gave ambitious men armies loyal to themselves, a dynamic that contributed to the civil wars of the late Republic. Under the Principate, the emperor monopolized the major promagisterial appointments, with legates governing imperial provinces in his name while the Senate continued to allot proconsular provinces such as Asia and Africa.
