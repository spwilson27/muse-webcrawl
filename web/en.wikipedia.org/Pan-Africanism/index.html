Pan-Africanism

The Pan-African flag, with red, black and green stripes, adopted by the UNIA in 1920.

Pan-Africanism is a worldwide movement that aims to encourage and strengthen bonds of solidarity between all indigenous peoples and diasporas of African ancestry. It holds that unity is vital to the economic, social and political progress of people of African descent and seeks to unify and uplift them. The movement's roots lie in the struggles of African peoples against enslavement and colonisation, and it took organisational form in the Pan-African Congresses of the 20th century.

Origins

Ideas of African unity circulated among enslaved and free black communities from the 18th century onward. The term "Pan-African" gained currency around the first Pan-African Conference, held in London in 1900 and organised by the Trinidadian barrister Henry Sylvester Williams. W. E. B. Du Bois, who attended, became the movement's most prominent intellectual figure.

Congresses and leaders

Du Bois helped organise Pan-African Congresses in 1919 (Paris), 1921, 1923, 1927 and — most consequentially — 1945 in Manchester, England, where future independence leaders Kwame Nkrumah, Jomo Kenyatta and Peter Abrahams participated. In the diaspora, Marcus Garvey's Universal Negro Improvement Association (UNIA) popularised Pan-African symbols, including the red, black and green flag adopted in 1920, and the slogan "Africa for the Africans".

Independence era and the African Union

Pan-Africanism powered the decolonisation era: Nkrumah's Ghana (independent 1957) championed continental union, and in 1963 thirty-two states founded the Organisation of African Unity in Addis Ababa. The OAU was succeeded in 2002 by the African Union, which now comprises 55 member states and regards the African diaspora as its "sixth region". The movement continues to shape African politics, culture and institutions.
