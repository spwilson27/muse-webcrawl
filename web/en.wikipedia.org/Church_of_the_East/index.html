Church of the East

The Church of the East (Classical Syriac: ʾĒḏtā ḏ-Maḏenḥā), also known historically as the Nestorian Church or the Persian Church, was an Eastern Christian church that developed among Syriac-speaking Christians in the Sasanian Empire and Mesopotamia. Headed by the Catholicos-Patriarch of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, it separated from the churches of the Roman world following the Council of Ephesus (431) and its own synods of the 5th century.

History

Tradition traces the church's origins to the apostles Thomas, Bartholomew and the disciples Addai and Mari in 1st-century Mesopotamia. The church organised itself under the Catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon and in 424 declared its independence from western sees. Under the Sasanians, and later under the Abbasid Caliphate with its patriarchate in Baghdad, the Church of the East carried out one of the largest missionary expansions of the pre-modern world, establishing dioceses along the Silk Road in Persia, Central Asia, India (the Saint Thomas Christians) and Tang-dynasty China, where the Xi'an Stele of 781 records its arrival. Its influence declined sharply after the persecutions of Timur in the 14th century.

Modern successors

The church's tradition is continued today by the Assyrian Church of the East, the Ancient Church of the East, the Chaldean Catholic Church and the Syro-Malabar Church of India.
