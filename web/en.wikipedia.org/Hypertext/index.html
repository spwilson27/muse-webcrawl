Hypertext

Hypertext is text displayed on a computer or other electronic device with references (hyperlinks) to other text that the reader can immediately access, usually by a mouse click or keypress. Apart from plain text, hypertext may contain tables, images, and other presentational elements, and the structure formed by hyperlinks is often described as a web.

History

The concept descends from Vannevar Bush's description of the Memex in his 1945 essay "As We May Think". Ted Nelson coined the terms "hypertext" and "hypermedia" in the 1960s and developed the Xanadu project, while Douglas Engelbart's team at SRI built the oN-Line System (NLS), demonstrated in 1968. Later systems included HyperCard (1987) and Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web (1989–1991), which made hypertext a mass medium through HTML and the browser.

Characteristics

Hypertext documents may be static or dynamic, and their links may be unidirectional or bidirectional. Non-linear reading is a defining property: readers choose their own paths through a body of linked material rather than following a fixed sequence, an idea with roots in literary works that experiment with branching narrative.

Academic study

Hypertext has been studied in computer science, literature, and education. The annual ACM Conference on Hypertext and Social Media, founded in 1987, is the field's main scholarly venue. Literary theorists have connected hypertext fiction to poststructuralist ideas about the open, decentered text.
