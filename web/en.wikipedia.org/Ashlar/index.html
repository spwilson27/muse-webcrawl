Ashlar

Ashlar is finely dressed (cut, worked) stone, either an individual stone that has been worked until squared, or a structure built from such stones. It is the finest stone masonry unit, generally cuboid in shape. The term is used both for the stone itself and for masonry built with it, characterised by precise joints and smooth faces.

Description

Ashlar blocks are quarried, cut to regular dimensions, and dressed so that the exposed face is flat and the beds and joints are true. In classical orders of architecture, ashlar walls could be laid without mortar, the stones held by their own weight and sometimes by metal cramps. Rusticated ashlar deliberately leaves the face rough and projecting, contrasting with the smooth joints.

History

Fine ashlar masonry appears in Bronze Age and classical construction, including Minoan palaces, Egyptian temples, and Inca walls such as those of Cusco, where polygonal ashlar was fitted with extraordinary precision. It remained a prestige material through the Renaissance and into modern monumental building.

Freemasonry

In Freemasonry, the rough ashlar and the perfect (or smooth) ashlar are important symbols: the rough ashlar represents the unrefined mind, and the perfect ashlar the mind improved by education and self-discipline.
