Niagara Falls is the collective name for three waterfalls that straddle the international border between the Canadian province of Ontario and the U.S. state of New York, draining Lake Erie into Lake Ontario through the Niagara River. From largest to smallest, they are the Horseshoe Falls (mostly on the Canadian side), the American Falls and the Bridal Veil Falls. Combined, they have the highest flow rate of any waterfall system in North America and a vertical drop of more than 50 metres.

Formation and geology

The falls were formed when glaciers receded at the end of the last ice age, and water from the newly formed Great Lakes carved a path over the Niagara Escarpment. The falls have since retreated about 11 kilometres upstream through erosion, a process slowed in the 20th century by flow diversion for hydroelectric power generation under international treaty, which reserves a scenic flow over the falls by day in tourist season.

Industry and tourism

Niagara became one of the world's earliest major tourist attractions in the 19th century and a famed honeymoon destination. Its waters powered pioneering hydroelectric stations — including Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse's Adams Power Plant (1895), which first delivered alternating current to Buffalo — making the falls central to the electrification of North America. The cities of Niagara Falls, Ontario and Niagara Falls, New York face each other across the gorge, connected by the Rainbow Bridge. Daredevils have challenged the falls since Annie Edson Taylor went over in a barrel in 1901; such stunts are now illegal.
