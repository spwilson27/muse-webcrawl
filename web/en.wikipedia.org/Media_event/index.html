Media event

A media event, also known as a pseudo-event, is an event, occurrence, or experience created for the purpose of attracting attention from the media. A media event may also include an event that would not have been of interest to the media had it not been promoted deliberately. The term was coined by historian Daniel Boorstin in his 1961 book The Image: A Guide to Pseudo-Events in America.

A press conference structured as a media event.

Definition and history

Boorstin defined a pseudo-event by four characteristics: it is not spontaneous but planned; it is arranged primarily for the purpose of being reported; its relation to the underlying reality is ambiguous; and it usually becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. Classic examples include press conferences, product launches, photo opportunities, political debates, and ribbon-cutting ceremonies.

In their influential 1992 book Media Events: The Live Broadcasting of History, Daniel Dayan and Elihu Katz used the term differently, for pre-planned ceremonial broadcasts — coronations, state funerals, Olympic Games, royal weddings, and moon landings — that interrupt routine, are broadcast live, and are watched by large audiences with a sense of reverence and social integration.

Criticism

Critics argue that the proliferation of media events allows political and corporate actors to manage impressions and set agendas, displacing spontaneous news and substantive reporting. Media events are a staple of political campaigning, public relations, and modern publicity campaigns.
