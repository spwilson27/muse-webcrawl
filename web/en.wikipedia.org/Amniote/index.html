Amniote

The amniotic egg, with its protective membranes, allowed vertebrates to reproduce away from water.

Amniotes are tetrapod vertebrate animals belonging to the clade Amniota, a large group that comprises the vast majority of living terrestrial and semiaquatic vertebrates. Amniotes are distinguished from the other living tetrapod group, the amphibians (lissamphibians), by the development of three extraembryonic membranes — the amnion, chorion, and allantois — and a thick, keratinized skin.

Evolution

The amniotic egg was a key innovation in vertebrate evolution, freeing reproduction from standing water: the embryo develops within a fluid-filled sac (protected by the amnion), while the chorion and allantois manage gas exchange and waste. The earliest amniotes appeared in the Carboniferous period, around 340 million years ago, and soon diverged into two major lineages: the Synapsida (which includes mammals) and the Sauropsida (which includes reptiles and birds).

Classification

The living amniotes number more than 24,000 species, including roughly 6,500 mammals, over 11,000 birds, and more than 11,000 non-avian reptiles. In mammals (except monotremes), the amniotic membranes are retained even though development takes place inside the mother's body, forming the amniotic sac in which the fetus grows.
