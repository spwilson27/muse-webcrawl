The Berlin Decree was issued by Napoleon Bonaparte on 21 November 1806, following the French victory over Prussia at the Battle of Jena–Auerstedt and the occupation of Berlin. It inaugurated the Continental System (French: Blocus continental), Napoleon's grand strategy of economic warfare against the United Kingdom: since he could not defeat the Royal Navy at sea — Trafalgar had been lost the year before — he would ruin Britain by closing the whole continent of Europe to its trade.

Provisions

The decree declared the British Isles to be in a state of blockade; forbade all commerce and correspondence with Britain; made British subjects found in French-occupied territory prisoners of war; declared all British merchandise and colonial produce lawful prize; and closed ports under French control to any vessel that had touched at a British port. The follow-up Milan Decree (17 December 1807) extended the system, providing that neutral ships submitting to British searches or paying British duties would be treated as British property.

British response

Britain replied with the Orders in Council of 1807, requiring neutral trade with Europe to pass through British ports and pay British customs. The two blockading systems squeezed neutral commerce — especially that of the United States, whose Embargo Act of 1807 and eventual declaration of war on Britain in the War of 1812 were partly consequences of this economic crossfire.

Effects and failure

The Continental System hurt Britain less than Napoleon hoped — British exports were redirected to the Americas, the Levant, and smuggling networks, notoriously via Heligoland — while it damaged the economies of continental Europe, bred resentment in France's allies and satellites, and required ever-widening coercion to enforce. Enforcement drove Napoleon to annex Holland and the Hanseatic ports, to pressure Portugal (a prelude to the Peninsular War), and, when Russia withdrew from the system in 1810–12, to launch the disastrous invasion of Russia in 1812. The Berlin Decree thus stands as a classic case of economic warfare producing unintended strategic consequences.
