Roman–Persian Wars

The Roman–Persian Wars were a series of conflicts between the Greco-Roman world and successive Iranian empires — first the Parthian Empire and then the Sasanian Empire — lasting from 54 BC to AD 629, a span of nearly seven centuries. They form one of the longest-lasting rivalries of the ancient world, fought chiefly over control of Mesopotamia, Armenia, Syria, and the trade routes of the Near East.

Background

Rome and Persia were the two great powers of western Eurasia for centuries. Their frontier, generally running through upper Mesopotamia and the Armenian highlands, was among the most heavily militarised borders of the ancient world. Neither side was able to achieve a lasting conquest of the other's heartland, and wars tended to end with a return to something close to the status quo ante.

Roman–Parthian Wars (54 BC – AD 217)

The conflict began with the invasion of Parthia by Marcus Licinius Crassus, which ended in catastrophe at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC, where the Roman army was annihilated and Crassus killed. Mark Antony also invaded Parthian territory in 36 BC with little success. Under the Roman Empire, emperors such as Trajan, Lucius Verus, and Septimius Severus campaigned successfully in Mesopotamia, twice capturing the Parthian capital Ctesiphon, though Rome could never hold lower Mesopotamia permanently. The last Roman–Parthian war ended with the Parthian victory at Nisibis in 217.

Roman–Sasanian Wars (224–628)

In 224 the Parthian Empire was overthrown by Ardashir I, founder of the more aggressive and centralised Sasanian Empire, which saw itself as the heir of the ancient Achaemenid Empire and sought to restore Persia's old frontiers. Warfare became more intense: the Sasanians captured the emperor Valerian at the Battle of Edessa in 260, an unprecedented humiliation for Rome. In the late third century the balance swung back under Diocletian and Galerius, and the Peace of Nisibis (299) gave Rome territory in northern Mesopotamia and influence in Armenia.

The fourth century saw long wars between Constantius II and Shapur II, and Julian's disastrous Persian expedition of 363, in which the emperor was killed. Thereafter the two empires, increasingly occupied elsewhere, maintained a relatively stable frontier for about a century, dividing Armenia between them in 387.

The wars of the sixth and seventh centuries

War resumed under Justin I and Justinian I in the 520s–540s, with campaigns by generals such as Belisarius, and Khosrow I's sack of Antioch in 540. The last and greatest war began in 602, when Khosrow II, ostensibly avenging the murdered emperor Maurice, overran the Byzantine East, conquering Syria, Egypt, and Anatolia and besieging Constantinople in 626. The emperor Heraclius responded with daring counteroffensives deep into Persia, culminating in the Byzantine victory at the Battle of Nineveh in 627 and the overthrow of Khosrow II. The peace of 629 restored the old frontiers.

Consequences

The final war exhausted both empires financially and militarily, leaving the Near East vulnerable to the Arab Muslim conquests of the 630s and 640s, which destroyed the Sasanian Empire entirely and stripped Byzantium of Syria and Egypt. The Roman–Persian Wars thus shaped the transition from the ancient to the medieval world.
