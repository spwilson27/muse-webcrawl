Citric acid

Citric acid
IUPAC name: 2-hydroxypropane-1,2,3-tricarboxylic acid
Formula: C6H8O7
Molar mass: 192.12 g/mol
Appearance: White crystalline solid
E number: E330

Citric acid is a weak organic acid that occurs naturally in citrus fruits, especially lemons and limes, where it is responsible for their sour taste. It is an intermediate in the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), the metabolic pathway used by all aerobic organisms to generate energy. More than two million tonnes of citric acid are manufactured annually, chiefly for use as a food additive (E330), flavoring, preservative, and chelating agent.

History

Citric acid was first isolated in 1784 by the Swedish chemist Carl Wilhelm Scheele, who crystallized it from lemon juice. Industrial production from Italian citrus fruit began in the 19th century. In 1917 the American food chemist James Currie discovered that the mold Aspergillus niger could produce citric acid efficiently from sugar; Pfizer began industrial-scale fermentation production using this method in 1919, and microbial fermentation remains the dominant production process today.

Properties and biochemistry

Citric acid is a tricarboxylic acid with three carboxyl groups and pKa values of 3.13, 4.76, and 6.40. In biochemistry, citrate is formed in the first step of the citric acid cycle by the condensation of acetyl-CoA with oxaloacetate. Citrate also acts as a regulator of glycolysis, inhibiting phosphofructokinase when energy is plentiful.

Uses

About half of world production goes into beverages, and much of the rest into other foods, as an acidulant and flavoring. Citric acid is used in effervescent tablets, as a chelating agent in detergents and cleaners (including for descaling), in cosmetics and pharmaceuticals, and medically as an anticoagulant in stored blood (as citrate) and as potassium citrate to treat kidney stones.
