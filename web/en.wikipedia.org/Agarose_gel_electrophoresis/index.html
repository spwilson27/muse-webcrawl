Agarose gel electrophoresis

Agarose gel electrophoresis is a method of gel electrophoresis used in biochemistry, molecular biology, genetics, and clinical chemistry to separate a mixed population of macromolecules such as DNA or proteins in a matrix of agarose, a polysaccharide extracted from seaweed. Fragments are separated by size as they migrate through the porous gel under an applied electric field.

Principle

DNA is uniformly negatively charged, so fragments migrate toward the positive electrode (anode). The agarose matrix acts as a sieve: shorter fragments move faster and farther than longer ones, so distance migrated is approximately inversely proportional to the logarithm of fragment size. Gel concentration (typically 0.7–2%) is chosen according to the size range to be resolved; pulsed-field gel electrophoresis extends the range to very large DNA molecules.

Procedure and visualization

Agarose is dissolved in buffer (TAE or TBE), cast with a comb to form sample wells, and the DNA loaded with a tracking dye and density agent. After running, DNA is visualized with intercalating fluorescent stains such as ethidium bromide or safer alternatives (GelRed, SYBR Safe) under ultraviolet or blue light, and sizes are estimated against a DNA ladder of known fragments. Bands can be excised and the DNA recovered for cloning or sequencing.

Applications

The technique is a routine workhorse for checking PCR products, restriction digests, plasmid preparations, and RNA integrity, and historically underpinned DNA fingerprinting and Southern blotting.
