Tiryns is a Mycenaean archaeological site in Argolis in the Peloponnese, Greece, a few kilometres north of Nafplio. It was a hill fort with occupation ranging back to the Neolithic and reaching its height between 1400 and 1200 BC, when it was one of the major centres of the Mycenaean world, with a palace, famous Cyclopean walls and underground cisterns.

Mythology

In Greek mythology Tiryns was founded by Proetus, and its massive walls were said to have been built by the Cyclopes — hence "Cyclopean" masonry. The city was the home of Heracles during his service to King Eurystheus, from which he set out to perform his Twelve Labours, and it figures in the stories of Perseus and Bellerophon.

Archaeology

The site was excavated by Heinrich Schliemann in 1884–1885, after his earlier work at Mycenae, and subsequently by Wilhelm Dörpfeld and the German Archaeological Institute. The citadel occupies a low rocky hill about 18 metres high. The fortification walls, up to 8 metres thick in places and built of boulders weighing several tonnes, enclose a palace complex whose megaron preserved traces of frescoes, including the famous frieze of women in procession. A remarkable feature is the pair of vaulted galleries in the south wall and the secret staircase descending to an underground spring that secured the water supply during sieges.

History

Tiryns flourished in the Late Bronze Age as a port and palace centre. After the collapse of the Mycenaean palace system around 1200 BC the lower town continued to be inhabited, and a temple was later built over the palace megaron. By the time Pausanias visited in the 2nd century AD, Tiryns was in ruins, though he still marvelled at its walls. Together with Mycenae, Tiryns was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999.
