Portraits of Shakespeare

The Chandos portrait, attributed to John Taylor, is one of the most famous images claimed to depict Shakespeare.

No portrait of William Shakespeare (1564–1616) is known to have been painted from life and to survive with certainty. Nevertheless, many images have been widely reproduced and accepted over the centuries as depictions of him. Scholars distinguish between portraits with plausible claims to authenticity and the many later fabrications, fakes, and misattributions.

The main candidates

The two images most widely accepted as genuine representations are the engraving by Martin Droeshout that appeared in the First Folio of 1623, published seven years after Shakespeare's death, and the sculpted funerary monument in Holy Trinity Church, Stratford-upon-Avon, which was in place by 1623. Ben Jonson's verses facing the Droeshout engraving state that the artist "had a strife with Nature, to out-do the life," implying a likeness.

The Chandos portrait, held by the National Portrait Gallery in London, is traditionally attributed to John Taylor and dated to about 1610. It was the first portrait acquired by the gallery. Other candidates include the Cobbe portrait, the Sanders portrait, and the so-called Flower portrait, the last of which was shown by X-ray analysis to be a 19th-century painting over an older religious image.

The Shakespeare portrait industry

The scarcity of authenticated images fed a flourishing trade in supposed portraits during the 18th and 19th centuries. Portraits of other Jacobean gentlemen were relabelled as Shakespeare, and features were altered to match the Droeshout type. The "standard" Shakespeare image — a balding man with a high forehead, pointed beard, and earring — derives largely from the Chandos and Droeshout images combined.
