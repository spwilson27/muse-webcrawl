Cork is a lightweight, buoyant material obtained from the bark of the cork oak (Quercus suber), a tree native to the western Mediterranean. It is composed largely of suberin, a hydrophobic substance, and its honeycomb of air-filled cells makes it elastic, impermeable to liquids, and an excellent thermal and acoustic insulator.

Harvesting

Cork is unusual among tree products because the tree is not felled: the outer bark is stripped by hand every nine to twelve years without killing the oak, which regenerates a new layer. A cork oak may live for over 200 years and be harvested many times. Portugal produces roughly half of the world's cork, with Spain the other major producer.

Properties

Cork's cellular structure — millions of tiny sealed chambers of air per cubic centimetre — gives it low density, resilience, and resistance to fire and water. It is also hypoallergenic and does not absorb dust. These properties made Robert Hooke's microscopic examination of cork famous: his 1665 Micrographia coined the word "cell" from the structures he saw in cork tissue.

Uses

The best-known use of cork is as stoppers for wine bottles, a role it has held since the seventeenth century; natural cork remains the premium closure despite competition from screw caps and synthetic stoppers. Cork is also used for flooring and wall tiles, bulletin boards, fishing floats, shoe soles, gaskets, and as insulation, including in aerospace applications. Waste cork is ground into agglomerated products, so almost none of the harvest is wasted.
