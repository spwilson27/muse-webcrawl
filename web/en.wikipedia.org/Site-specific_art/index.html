Site-specific art

MovementContemporary art
Related movementsLand art, environmental art, installation art
Key figuresRobert Smithson, Richard Serra, Christo and Jeanne-Muse, Maya Lin

Site-specific art is artwork created to exist in a certain place. The artist takes the location into account while planning and creating the work, so that the site — its landscape, architecture, history or social context — becomes part of the work's meaning. If removed from its location, a site-specific work would lose much or all of its significance.

Origins

The term was promoted in the 1970s by artists working against the idea of the autonomous, portable artwork. Robert Smithson's earthworks, such as Spiral Jetty (1970) in Utah's Great Salt Lake, could exist only in their remote settings. Minimalist sculptors like Richard Serra argued that moving a work designed for a particular space destroyed it — a claim tested in court when Serra's Tilted Arc was removed from a New York plaza in 1989.

Characteristics

Site-specific works may be permanent or temporary. Christo and Jeanne-Muse's wrappings of buildings and landscapes, such as The Gates in Central Park (2005), were designed for a single location and a limited duration. Other works engage the social life of a place: community-based projects and public memorials, such as Maya Lin's Vietnam Veterans Memorial, derive their force from their setting.

Site-specificity in other media

The concept has been extended to theatre, dance and performance, where productions are staged in found spaces — warehouses, swimming pools, city streets — chosen to interact with the drama. Critics have also distinguished between works merely placed in a site and works genuinely generated by it.
