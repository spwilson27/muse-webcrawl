Fruit

In botany, a fruit is the seed-bearing structure in flowering plants (angiosperms) that is formed from the ovary after flowering. Fruits are the means by which flowering plants disseminate their seeds. Edible fruits in particular have long propagated using the movements of humans and other animals in a symbiotic relationship that is the means for seed dispersal for the one group and nutrition for the other.

In common language usage, "fruit" normally means the seed-associated fleshy structures (or produce) of plants that typically are sweet or sour and edible in the raw state, such as apples, bananas, grapes, lemons, oranges, and strawberries. In botanical usage, the term includes many structures that are not commonly called fruits in everyday language, including nuts, bean pods, corn kernels, tomatoes, and wheat grains.

Botanical and culinary usage

Many common language terms used for fruits are not botanically accurate. Botanically, a fruit is the mature ovary together with its contents and accessory parts; the wall of the ovary, the pericarp, may differentiate into an outer exocarp, a middle mesocarp, and an inner endocarp. Fruits are classified as simple, aggregate, or multiple depending on how they develop from flowers. By contrast, many items treated as vegetables in cooking — such as tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, and squashes — are botanical fruits, while the culinary category also counts some botanical fruits, like rhubarb stalks, as fruit despite their not being fruits at all botanically.

Development

A fruit results from the fertilisation of a flower and the maturation of the ovary; as the seeds develop from the ovules, the ovary wall typically thickens. Plant hormones such as gibberellins, auxin, and ethylene regulate growth and ripening. Some fruits, called parthenocarpic, develop without fertilisation and are seedless, a trait selected in cultivated bananas and some grapes.

Seed dispersal

Fruits protect developing seeds and aid their dispersal. Fleshy fruits are eaten by animals, which deposit the seeds in dung away from the parent plant; dry fruits may be carried by wind (as in the winged samaras of maples), float on water (as coconuts do), or attach to animal fur by hooks and barbs.

Nutrition

Fresh fruit is generally high in fibre, vitamin C, and water, and regular consumption is associated with reduced risks of several chronic diseases. Commercial cultivation relies on a relatively small number of species, with bananas, apples, grapes, oranges, and mangoes among the most produced worldwide.
