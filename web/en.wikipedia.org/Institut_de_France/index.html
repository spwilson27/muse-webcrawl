The Institut de France is a French learned institution that groups five academies: the Académie française, the Académie des inscriptions et belles-lettres, the Académie des sciences, the Académie des beaux-arts, and the Académie des sciences morales et politiques. Founded in 1795, it is housed in the Palais de l'Institut on the Left Bank of the Seine in Paris.

History

The Institut was created during the French Revolution to replace the royal academies abolished in 1793, reorganising the nation's learned life under a single umbrella. Napoleon Bonaparte, himself a member, installed it in the former Collège des Quatre-Nations, whose domed chapel-facing facade remains one of the landmarks of Paris. The five-academy structure was settled in the 1830s.

Functions

The Institut and its academies elect members to fill vacant seats, award prizes, advise the state, and administer numerous foundations, museums, and properties bequeathed to them, including the Château de Chantilly. The Académie française is charged with stewardship of the French language; the Académie des sciences is one of France's principal scientific bodies.

The Palais

The building, designed by Louis Le Vau and completed in the 1680s, also contains the Bibliothèque Mazarine, the oldest public library in France. The academies meet beneath its dome, the famous Coupole, for ceremonial sessions including the reception of new members.
