George Washington (February 22, 1732 – December 14, 1799) was an American Founding Father, military officer, and statesman who served as the first president of the United States from 1789 to 1797. Appointed by the Second Continental Congress as commander of the Continental Army in 1775, Washington led the Patriots to victory in the American Revolutionary War.

Early life

Washington was born into a Virginia planter family and worked as a surveyor before serving as an officer in the Virginia militia during the French and Indian War, an experience that included the skirmish at Jumonville Glen in 1754 and the defeat at Fort Necessity. He resigned his commission in 1758, married the wealthy widow Martha Dandridge Custis in 1759, and managed his Mount Vernon estate, where enslaved people performed the labor. He was elected to the Virginia House of Burgesses and became a leading opponent of British colonial policies.

Revolutionary War

As commander-in-chief, Washington held together a poorly supplied army through defeats around New York in 1776, struck back with victories at Trenton and Princeton, endured the winter at Valley Forge, and, with French alliance and support, forced the British surrender at Yorktown in 1781. In 1783 he resigned his commission to Congress at Annapolis, an act of republican self-restraint widely admired.

Presidency

Unanimously elected by the Electoral College in both 1789 and 1792, Washington set enduring precedents: forming a cabinet, delivering inaugural addresses, maintaining neutrality in the wars of the French Revolution (the Neutrality Proclamation of 1793), suppressing the Whiskey Rebellion, and retiring after two terms. His Farewell Address (1796) warned against permanent foreign alliances and the spirit of party.

Legacy

Washington is commonly called the "Father of His Country". His image appears on the U.S. one-dollar bill and quarter, and he is commemorated by the capital city, the state of Washington, and the Washington Monument. Historians rank him among the greatest presidents, while also examining his record as a slaveholder who, in his will, ordered the emancipation of the people he enslaved after Martha's death.
