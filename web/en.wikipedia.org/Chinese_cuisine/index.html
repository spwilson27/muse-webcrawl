Chinese cuisine

Chinese cuisine comprises cuisines originating from China, as well as from Chinese communities in the global Chinese diaspora. Because of the diaspora and the historical power of the country, Chinese cuisine has profoundly influenced many other cuisines in Asia and beyond, with modifications made to cater to local palates. Chinese food staples such as rice, soy sauce, noodles, tea, chili oil, and tofu, and utensils such as chopsticks and the wok, can now be found worldwide.

The preferences for seasoning and cooking techniques of Chinese provinces depend on differences in social class, religion, historical background, and ethnic groups. Geographic features such as mountains, rivers, forests, and deserts also have a strong effect on the local available ingredients, considering that the climate of China varies from tropical in the south to subarctic in the northeast.

Regional cuisines

The most widely recognized classification divides Chinese cooking into the "Eight Great Traditions": Shandong (Lu), Sichuan (Chuan), Cantonese (Yue), Jiangsu (Su), Fujian (Min), Zhejiang (Zhe), Hunan (Xiang), and Anhui (Hui). Cantonese cuisine, from Guangdong, is known for dim sum, fresh seafood, and delicate seasoning; Sichuan cuisine for the numbing pungency of Sichuan pepper and chilli; Shandong cuisine, the oldest of the great traditions, for seafood and refined techniques; and Jiangsu cuisine for elegant presentation and sweet, mild flavours. Hunan food is famously fiery, while Fujian cooks excel at soups and seafood.

Staples and cooking methods

A Chinese meal typically pairs fan—grain foods such as rice in the south or wheat-based noodles and breads in the north—with shared dishes of vegetables, meat, or tofu. Steaming, stir-frying in a wok over high heat (wok hei), braising, red cooking in soy sauce, deep-frying, and simmering are characteristic techniques. Flavour balance is fundamental: sweet, sour, bitter, salty, and pungent tastes are harmonised, and colour, aroma, and taste are the classic criteria for judging a dish.

History and diaspora

Chinese culinary culture developed over millennia, documented in texts from the Zhou dynasty onward and shaped by imperial court cooking, regional traditions, and religious practices including Buddhist vegetarian cooking. From the 19th century, Chinese emigrants carried their cuisines worldwide, giving rise to adapted traditions such as American Chinese cuisine, with dishes like chop suey and fortune cookies, and to vibrant Chinatowns in cities across the globe.
