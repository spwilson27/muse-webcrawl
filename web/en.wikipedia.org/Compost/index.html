Compost is a mixture of ingredients used as plant fertilizer and to improve soil's physical, chemical, and biological properties. It is commonly prepared by decomposing plant and food waste, recycling organic materials, and manure. The resulting mixture is rich in plant nutrients and beneficial organisms such as bacteria, protozoa, nematodes, and fungi, and is used in gardens, landscaping, horticulture, organic farming, and agriculture.

The composting process

Decomposition is driven by microorganisms whose activity depends on the balance of carbon-rich "browns" (straw, dry leaves, wood chips) and nitrogen-rich "greens" (grass clippings, kitchen scraps, manure), together with moisture and oxygen. In managed "hot" composting, piles are turned to maintain aeration and pass through mesophilic and thermophilic phases, reaching temperatures that kill most pathogens and weed seeds. "Cold" composting is slower and less thorough. Vermicomposting uses worms — typically red wigglers — to process organic waste at ambient temperatures, and bokashi pre-ferments scraps anaerobically before burial.

Benefits and uses

Compost improves soil structure, water retention, and drainage, supplies nutrients in slow-release form, supports soil food webs, and can suppress some plant diseases. At scale, composting diverts organic waste from landfills, where its anaerobic decomposition would generate methane, a potent greenhouse gas; many cities now run curbside organics collection and industrial composting facilities.

History

Composting is ancient — compost heaps are described in Roman agricultural writings and were long central to farming in China, Korea, and Japan — but was systematized in the modern organic movement by Sir Albert Howard, whose Indore method, developed in India in the early twentieth century, influenced organic agriculture worldwide.
