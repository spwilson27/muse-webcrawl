Power station

A power station, also referred to as a power plant and sometimes generating station or generating plant, is an industrial facility for the generation of electric power. Power stations are generally connected to an electrical grid. Many power stations contain one or more generators, rotating machines that convert mechanical power into three-phase electric power. The relative motion between a magnetic field and a conductor creates an electric current.

Types

The energy source harnessed to turn the generator varies widely. Most power stations in the world burn fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas to generate electricity; nuclear power plants use controlled fission reactions; and renewable sources include hydroelectric, wind, solar, geothermal, and tidal power. Thermal power stations—coal, gas, nuclear, and geothermal—boil water to make steam that drives a steam turbine, whereas hydroelectric plants drive turbines directly with falling water and wind farms convert wind kinetic energy with wind turbines.

Power stations are typically classified by heat source (thermal, nuclear, renewable) and by function in the grid: baseload plants run near continuously, peaking plants operate only during high demand, and load-following plants adjust output between the two. The largest power station in the world is the Three Gorges Dam in China, a hydroelectric facility with an installed capacity of 22,500 megawatts.

History

The world's first public electricity supply was provided in late 1881 at Godalming, England, powered by waterwheels. In 1882, Thomas Edison's Pearl Street Station in New York began generating electricity for customers, and Nikola Tesla's alternating current systems soon enabled efficient long-distance transmission, inaugurating large central stations. Station sizes grew steadily through the twentieth century, and efficiency improved with higher steam temperatures and pressures, reheat cycles, and combined-cycle gas turbines.

Operation and environmental impact

Power stations convert primary energy into electricity with varying efficiency—typically around 33–40 percent for coal and nuclear steam plants and up to about 60 percent for combined-cycle gas plants. Fossil-fuel stations are major sources of carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and particulate emissions, and their reduction is a central element of climate policy, driving growth in renewables and the retirement of coal plants in many countries. Waste heat is often rejected through cooling towers or into bodies of water.
