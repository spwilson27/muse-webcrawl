Maker culture

A community makerspace, a typical hub of maker culture.

Maker culture (also known as the maker movement) is a contemporary subculture representing a technology-based extension of DIY culture that intersects with hacker culture and the maker ethos of learning through doing. Typical interests include electronics, robotics, 3D printing, CNC machining, woodworking, metalworking, and other hands-on fabrication, pursued for enjoyment, learning, entrepreneurship, and sharing.

History

The modern movement coalesced in the mid-2000s. Make magazine, launched in 2005 by Dale Dougherty at O'Reilly Media, gave the community a name and a flagship publication; the first Maker Faire was held in San Mateo, California in 2006 and grew to attract over one hundred thousand visitors, with licensed events worldwide. Affordable platforms such as Arduino (from 2005), the Raspberry Pi (2012), home 3D printers following the open-source RepRap project, and laser cutters lowered the barriers to fabrication.

Makerspaces

Shared workshops known as makerspaces, hackerspaces, or fab labs provide members with tools, workspace, and community. The Fab Lab network began at MIT's Center for Bits and Atoms under Neil Gershenfeld. Public libraries and schools have increasingly established maker areas, linking the movement to STEM education.

Philosophy and practices

Maker culture values open sharing of designs and code, iterative tinkering, repair and reuse, and learning by making. Designs are commonly published under open licenses on platforms such as Thingiverse, GitHub, and Instructables. The movement overlaps with open-source hardware, the right-to-repair movement, and startup hardware entrepreneurship.

Criticism

Commentators have noted the movement's demographic skews, its occasional hype around consumer 3D printing, and tensions between hobbyist sharing and commercialisation.
