Self-fulfilling prophecy

Self-fulfilling prophecy
FieldSociology; social psychology
Defined byRobert K. Merton (1948)
Related conceptsPygmalion effect, Thomas theorem, bank run, labelling theory

A self-fulfilling prophecy is a prediction that comes true at least in part as a result of the prediction itself: a belief or expectation about a person or event influences behaviour in ways that cause the belief to be realized. The sociologist Robert K. Merton coined the term in 1948, building on the Thomas theorem — "if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences."

Merton's formulation

Merton defined the self-fulfilling prophecy as "a false definition of the situation evoking a new behavior which makes the originally false conception come true." His classic example was a bank run: a rumour that a solvent bank is failing prompts depositors to withdraw their money, and the withdrawals themselves cause the bank to fail. He also applied the idea to racial prejudice, where false beliefs about a group produce treatment that generates the very outcomes the prejudice predicted.

Examples and research

In education, the Pygmalion effect — demonstrated in Rosenthal and Jacobson's 1968 classroom study — found that students randomly labelled as likely to "bloom" intellectually gained more than peers, apparently through changed teacher expectations. Self-fulfilling prophecies also operate in economics (inflation expectations, market panics), in interpersonal perception, and in stereotyping. The opposite process, in which a prediction defeats itself, is called a self-defeating or self-denying prophecy.

Criticism

Some later studies have found expectancy effects to be smaller and less robust than early reports suggested, and replication of the original classroom findings has been debated; effects appear strongest where expectations shape many small interactions over time. See also Pygmalion effect and Thomas theorem.
