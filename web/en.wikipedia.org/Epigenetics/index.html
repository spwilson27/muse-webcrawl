Epigenetics

Epigenetics is the study of heritable traits, or stable changes in gene expression, that do not involve alterations to the underlying DNA sequence. The word epigenetics is of Greek origin and literally means "over and above" (epi-) genetics. Epigenetic mechanisms control which genes are switched on or off in a given cell, allowing genetically identical cells to differentiate into the many cell types of the body.

Mechanisms

The best-understood epigenetic mechanisms are:

  • DNA methylation — the addition of a methyl group to cytosine bases (typically at CpG sites), usually repressing gene transcription.
  • Histone modification — chemical changes (acetylation, methylation, phosphorylation and others) to the histone proteins around which DNA is wound, loosening or compacting chromatin.
  • Non-coding RNA — RNA molecules that do not code for protein but regulate gene expression, including microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs.

Examples

Classic examples include the differentiation of stem cells into specialised cell types, X-chromosome inactivation in female mammals, and genomic imprinting, in which a gene's expression depends on whether it was inherited from the mother or the father. In plants, epigenetic silencing of transposable elements is essential to genome stability.

Environmental influence and inheritance

Epigenetic marks can be influenced by environmental factors such as diet, stress, and toxins. Some studies — for example of the Dutch Hunger Winter cohort — suggest that prenatal exposures can leave lasting epigenetic signatures. Whether such acquired marks are commonly transmitted across multiple generations in humans remains debated, since most epigenetic marks are erased during embryonic development.

Medicine

Aberrant epigenetic patterns are implicated in cancer, imprinting disorders such as Prader–Willi and Angelman syndromes, and other diseases. Drugs that reverse epigenetic modifications, including DNA methyltransferase inhibitors and histone deacetylase inhibitors, are used in some cancer therapies.
