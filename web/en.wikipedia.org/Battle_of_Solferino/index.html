Battle of Solferino

Date24 June 1859
LocationSolferino, Lombardy (present-day Italy)
ResultFranco-Sardinian victory
BelligerentsFrench Empire and Kingdom of Sardinia vs. Austrian Empire
CommandersNapoleon III; Victor Emmanuel II vs. Franz Joseph I

The Battle of Solferino was fought on 24 June 1859 between the allied French and Piedmont-Sardinian armies under Napoleon III and Victor Emmanuel II and the Austrian army under Emperor Franz Joseph I. It was the decisive battle of the Second Italian War of Independence and one of the largest battles in Europe between Waterloo and the First World War, involving some 300,000 soldiers. The horrific suffering of the wounded, witnessed by the Swiss businessman Henry Dunant, led directly to the founding of the Red Cross and the Geneva Conventions.

The struggle for the heights of Solferino, the key to the Austrian position.

Background

France and Piedmont-Sardinia had allied against Austria in pursuit of Italian unification and the expulsion of Austrian power from northern Italy. After the allied victory at Magenta on 4 June, the Austrian army retreated eastward behind the Mincio river, then unexpectedly turned to fight around the villages of Solferino, San Martino and Medole.

The battle

The armies blundered into each other on the morning of 24 June. Fierce fighting raged for some fifteen hours in extreme heat. The French eventually stormed the Solferino heights, held by the Austrian centre, while the Piedmontese engaged the Austrian right around San Martino. A violent thunderstorm late in the day helped cover the Austrian withdrawal across the Mincio. Combined casualties were roughly 40,000 killed and wounded, and medical services were wholly inadequate to care for them.

Consequences

Shaken by the carnage and wary of Prussian intervention, Napoleon III concluded the armistice of Villafranca, by which Austria ceded Lombardy. Henry Dunant, who had arrived on business, organised local civilians to care for wounded of all sides and published A Memory of Solferino (1862), proposing national relief societies and an international convention for the protection of the wounded. His ideas produced the International Committee of the Red Cross in 1863 and the first Geneva Convention in 1864.
