The Great Heathen Army, also known as the Great Viking Army, was a coalition of Scandinavian warriors who invaded England from 865. Unlike earlier Viking raids, which were small seasonal expeditions, the army intended conquest and settlement. According to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle it was a micel hæðen here—a "great heathen army"—and tradition links its leadership to the sons of Ragnar Lodbrok, said to be avenging their father's death at the hands of King Ælla of Northumbria.

Campaigns

The army landed in East Anglia in 865, secured horses, and in 866 captured York, capital of Northumbria. In 869 it returned to East Anglia, defeating and killing King Edmund (later Saint Edmund). It overwintered at Repton in Mercia in 873–74, driving King Burgred into exile, and took London and Cambridge in subsequent campaigns. In 876 Halfdan "shared out the lands of Northumbria" among his men, marking a shift from conquest to settlement—the origin of the Danelaw.

Defeat in Wessex

In 878 a renewed invasion under Guthrum nearly destroyed the kingdom of Wessex; King Alfred the Great, reduced to the marshes of Athelney, rallied his forces and defeated the army at the Battle of Edington. Guthrum accepted baptism and withdrew under the Treaty of Wedmore, and he later ruled East Anglia as a Christian king. The army's campaigns permanently reshaped England: three of its four kingdoms fell, and Scandinavian settlement left lasting marks on place names, law, and language in the north and east.

Archaeology

The winter camps at Torksey (872–73) and Repton, with their mass graves and thousands of metal-detected finds, have confirmed the army's scale, while historians debate its exact size—estimates range from hundreds to several thousand warriors.
