Brittleness

Brittleness describes the property of a material that fractures when subjected to stress but has little tendency to deform before fracture. Brittle materials absorb relatively little energy prior to fracturing, even those of high strength. Breaking is often accompanied by a snapping sound. Brittle materials include most ceramics and glasses (which do not deform plastically at room temperature), and some polymers, such as poly(methyl methacrylate) and polystyrene. Many steels become brittle at low temperatures, depending on their composition and microstructure.

When used in materials science, brittleness is generally applied to materials that fail in tension rather than yielding. The opposite property is ductility: a ductile material deforms plastically and absorbs considerable energy before fracture. A material's stress–strain curve reveals the distinction directly — a brittle material shows a nearly linear rise to failure with little or no plastic strain, while a ductile material exhibits a yield point followed by extended plastic deformation.

Causes

Brittleness arises when dislocations — the mobile defects that allow plastic deformation — are impeded or scarce. In ceramics and glasses, strong directional covalent or ionic bonds resist atomic slip. In metals, brittleness can be induced by:

  • Low temperature — many body-centred cubic steels undergo a ductile-to-brittle transition as temperature falls; this contributed to failures such as the Liberty ship fractures of the Second World War.
  • Hydrogen embrittlement — atomic hydrogen diffusing into steel reduces its ductility.
  • Grain boundary weakening — impurities such as sulfur or phosphorus segregating to grain boundaries, as in temper embrittlement of alloy steels.
  • Irradiation — neutron damage in reactor pressure-vessel steels raises the transition temperature.

Toughening

Brittle materials can be toughened by introducing mechanisms that absorb energy or deflect cracks: composite materials combine brittle fibres with a matrix that arrests crack growth; tempered glass places the surfaces in compression so that cracks cannot easily open; and transformation toughening in zirconia ceramics uses a stress-induced phase change to shield crack tips.
