Altarpiece

An altarpiece is a work of art — a painting, sculpture, or relief — placed above and behind the altar in a Christian church, where it serves as the visual focus of worship. Altarpieces range from simple single panels to vast multi-panel constructions with movable wings.

A polyptych altarpiece with hinged wings, open to display its painted panels.

Types

A single-panel altarpiece is simply an altar painting; two panels form a diptych, three a triptych, and more a polyptych. Many Northern European altarpieces were carved wooden retables with painted shutters that could be closed during Lent and opened on feast days to reveal gilded sculpture inside. In Spain the retablo grew to fill the entire east wall of a church. The Italian pala typically combined its figures in a single unified painted field framed by classical architecture.

History

Altarpieces became common from the thirteenth century as the celebrant's position moved and lay devotion intensified. The late medieval and Renaissance periods produced masterpieces such as the Ghent Altarpiece by the Van Eyck brothers (1432), Matthias Grünewald's Isenheim Altarpiece (1512–16), and Rogier van der Weyden's Descent from the Cross. The Counter-Reformation favoured large, emotionally direct single scenes; later centuries saw many altarpieces removed to museums, where they are now studied as paintings detached from their liturgical settings.
