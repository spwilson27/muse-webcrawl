Method of indivisibles
DeveloperBonaventura Cavalieri
Key workGeometria indivisibilibus (1635)
FieldGeometry; precursor of integral calculus

In geometry, the method of indivisibles is a technique to compute areas and volumes by regarding a region as composed of an infinite number of indivisible, infinitesimally thin slices. The method was developed by the Italian mathematician Bonaventura Cavalieri, a student of Galileo, and published in his Geometria indivisibilibus continuorum nova quadam ratione promota (1635). It is related to the ancient method of exhaustion but dispenses with its rigour in favour of a more powerful heuristic.

Cavalieri's principle

The best-known result of the method is Cavalieri's principle: if two solids are included between parallel planes and every plane parallel to them intersects both solids in cross-sections of equal area, then the solids have equal volume. Using indivisibles, Cavalieri computed areas under curves corresponding to integrals of powers of x, and Evangelista Torricelli and others extended the technique.

Influence

Though criticised by contemporaries such as Paul Guldin for its shaky foundations, the method influenced John Wallis's Arithmetica Infinitorum and the later development of integral calculus by Newton and Leibniz. In China, an equivalent principle was stated by Zu Geng in the 5th century.
