Unconventional superconductor

ExamplesCuprates, iron pnictides, heavy-fermion compounds, Sr2RuO4
Pairing symmetryOften d-wave or p-wave
ContrastConventional (BCS) superconductors

Unconventional superconductors are materials in which superconductivity cannot be adequately described by the standard Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer (BCS) theory or its extensions. In these materials the Cooper pairs typically form with an order parameter that has a more complex symmetry than the isotropic s-wave of conventional superconductors — most commonly d-wave symmetry — and the pairing "glue" is thought to be something other than phonons, often magnetic fluctuations.

Classes

The largest family is the high-temperature superconducting cuprates, discovered in 1986 by Georg Bednorz and K. Alex Müller, in which superconductivity emerges when a Mott-insulating copper oxide parent compound is doped with holes or electrons. Other major families include the iron-based superconductors discovered in 2008, heavy-fermion superconductors such as CeCu2Si2 and UPt3, organic superconductors, and the strontium ruthenate Sr2RuO4, long studied as a candidate spin-triplet superconductor. Superfluid helium-3 is often cited as the prototype unconventional paired state.

Signatures

Evidence for unconventional pairing includes nodes in the superconducting gap detectable by angle-resolved photoemission and thermal transport, a linear (rather than exponential) temperature dependence of the magnetic penetration depth, sign changes of the order parameter measured in phase-sensitive experiments, and superconductivity arising near a magnetic quantum critical point. Despite decades of research, the microscopic pairing mechanism of the cuprates remains one of the central unsolved problems of condensed matter physics.
