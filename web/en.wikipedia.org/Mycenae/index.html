Mycenae

LocationArgolis, Peloponnese, Greece
PeriodBronze Age, flourished c. 1600–1100 BCE
CivilisationMycenaean Greece
Key featuresLion Gate, Cyclopean walls, Grave Circles A and B, Treasury of Atreus
DesignationUNESCO World Heritage Site (1999, with Tiryns)

Mycenae is an archaeological site near the village of Mykines in Argolis, in the north-eastern Peloponnese of Greece. In the second millennium BCE it was one of the major centres of Greek civilisation — a fortified palace citadel that dominated much of southern Greece. The Bronze Age culture of mainland Greece is named Mycenaean after it. In Greek legend Mycenae was the kingdom of Agamemnon, leader of the Greek expedition against Troy in Homer's Iliad, which calls it "rich in gold."

The Lion Gate (c. 1250 BCE), the main entrance to the citadel.

History

The hill was occupied from Neolithic times, but Mycenae rose to prominence in the Late Bronze Age. The shaft graves of Grave Circles B (c. 1650 BCE) and A (c. 1600–1500 BCE) buried an early warrior elite with gold masks, weapons, and jewellery. Around 1350 BCE a palace was built on the summit and the citadel was enclosed by massive "Cyclopean" walls — so named because later Greeks believed only the Cyclopes could have lifted such stones. The Lion Gate and the great tholos tombs, including the so-called Treasury of Atreus (c. 1250 BCE) with its vast corbelled dome, date from the citadel's height. Mycenae's palaces administered a bureaucratic kingdom recorded in Linear B tablets; the citadel was destroyed by fire amid the general Late Bronze Age collapse around 1200–1100 BCE, after which the site dwindled.

Excavations

Heinrich Schliemann excavated Grave Circle A in 1876, convinced he had found the tombs of Agamemnon and his companions; the gold "Mask of Agamemnon" he recovered actually predates any plausible date for the Trojan War by centuries. Later work by Greek and British archaeologists, including Christos Tsountas and Alan Wace, uncovered the palace, houses outside the walls, and further tombs.

Mythology

According to myth, Mycenae was founded by Perseus. The house of Atreus — Atreus, Agamemnon, and Clytemnestra — anchors the tragedies of Aeschylus's Oresteia: Agamemnon sacrifices his daughter Iphigenia, is murdered on his return from Troy by his wife, and is avenged by his son Orestes.
