Bose–Einstein condensate

Bose–Einstein condensate
State of matterQuantum degenerate gas

Typical temperatureBelow ~170 nK for Rb-87

A Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC) is a state of matter that is typically formed when a gas of bosons at very low density is cooled to temperatures very close to absolute zero. Under such conditions, a large fraction of the bosons occupy the lowest quantum state, at which microscopic quantum-mechanical phenomena, particularly wavefunction interference, become apparent macroscopically.

History

Bose and Einstein predicted the phenomenon in 1924–25 from the new statistics of light quanta and massive particles. Seventy years later, on 5 June 1995, Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman's group at JILA produced the first gaseous condensate, in rubidium-87 cooled to 170 nanokelvin; Wolfgang Ketterle's MIT group followed with sodium. The three shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Properties

BECs display superfluid-like behaviour, quantised vortices, and atom coherence exploited in atom lasers and precision interferometry. The Gross–Pitaevskii equation describes their macroscopic wavefunction. Condensates have since been made with many atomic species, molecules, and even quasiparticles such as exciton-polaritons.
