Gruyère cheese

Gruyère (French: gruyère) is a hard Swiss cheese named after the town of Gruyères in the canton of Fribourg, Switzerland. It is made from raw cow's milk and aged for a minimum of five months, with some varieties matured for up to two years. Gruyère has held protected designation of origin status (AOP) in Switzerland since 2001.

Production

Raw milk is heated in copper vats, curdled with liquid rennet, and the curd is cut into small pieces, stirred, and cooked. The curd is pressed into round moulds forming wheels of 25 to 40 kilograms, salted in brine, and ripened in cellars on spruce boards, where the wheels are regularly turned and brushed with salt water. Maturation develops the cheese's characteristic smooth, slightly salty and nutty flavour, which becomes more assertive with age. Unlike Emmental, Gruyère has few and small holes, or none.

Varieties and uses

Classifications by age include doux (mild, five months), mi-salé, salé, and the highly regarded alpage Gruyère made in alpine dairies during the summer. Gruyère is a key ingredient in French onion soup, croque-monsieur, quiche, and gratin dishes, and is one of the classic cheeses for fondue, often blended with Vacherin and white wine. It melts smoothly, making it prized in cooking.

Name disputes

The name has been the subject of long-running disputes between Switzerland, France (which has its own Gruyère production), and producers elsewhere. Swiss Gruyère is protected in the European Union through bilateral agreements, while courts in the United States have ruled "gruyère" generic there.
