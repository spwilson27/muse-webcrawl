Phenetics

Also calledNumerical taxonomy
Key figuresRobert Sokal, Peter Sneath
Key textPrinciples of Numerical Taxonomy (1963)

In biology, phenetics — also called numerical taxonomy — is an approach to classification that groups organisms strictly by overall similarity in observable characteristics, without regard to their evolutionary relationships. Pheneticists score many characters for each operational taxonomic unit, compute pairwise similarity or distance values, and use clustering algorithms to produce a branching diagram called a phenogram.

A phenogram: taxa clustered by overall similarity

The school emerged in the late 1950s and 1960s, promoted by Charles Michener, Robert Sokal and Peter Sneath, whose Principles of Numerical Taxonomy (1963) was its manifesto. Its advocates argued that classification should be objective, repeatable and free of untestable assumptions about phylogeny, and that all characters should be weighted equally. Phenetic methods were among the first to bring computers into taxonomy.

Decline and legacy

From the 1970s, phenetics lost ground to cladistics, which insists that classification reflect evolutionary branching, and later to molecular phylogenetics. Critics objected that overall similarity confuses shared ancestry with convergence. Nevertheless, phenetic techniques — distance matrices, cluster analysis such as UPGMA, and ordination — remain in routine use in ecology, microbiology and morphometrics, and distance-based methods such as neighbour joining remain standard in molecular systematics.
