Choro

Choro (Portuguese for "cry" or "lament"), also known as chorinho ("little cry"), is a Brazilian popular music genre that originated in 19th-century Rio de Janeiro. Despite its name, choro is typically fast, virtuosic, and cheerful, characterised by intricate melodies, improvisation, and sophisticated harmonies and counterpoint.

History

Choro emerged in the 1870s from Rio ensembles that fused European dance forms — the polka, waltz, and schottische — with African rhythms and Brazilian sensibilities. Early groups (ternos) combined flute, guitars, and cavaquinho; the pandeiro was added later. Composers and performers such as Joaquim Callado, Chiquinha Gonzaga, Ernesto Nazareth, and later Pixinguinha defined the genre.

Instrumentation and style

A traditional choro ensemble (regional) features a soloist — flute, clarinet, saxophone, mandolin, or trumpet — accompanied by six- and seven-string guitars, cavaquinho, and pandeiro. Pieces are usually in a rondo-like three-part form (AABBACCA), with each section modulating and the soloist improvising ornamented variations. Choro is considered a precursor and foundation of samba and much of Brazilian instrumental music.
