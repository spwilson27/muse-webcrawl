Cosmopolitan distribution

In biogeography, a cosmopolitan distribution is the range of a taxon that extends across all or most of the world, in appropriate habitats. Such a taxon is said to exhibit cosmopolitanism. True cosmopolitanism is rare, because few organisms tolerate the full range of global climates; many supposedly cosmopolitan "species" have turned out, on genetic study, to be complexes of regionally distinct species.

Examples

Classic examples include the peregrine falcon, found on all continents except Antarctica; the osprey; the barn owl; the orca (killer whale), the most widely distributed mammal after humans; and many marine organisms such as the blue shark. Among plants, the bracken fern (Pteridium aquilinum) and the common reed (Phragmites australis) are nearly worldwide. Extremely widespread microorganisms, lichens and some human commensals — the housefly, brown rat, and the human species itself — are also cosmopolitan. Weeds and invasive species such as the dandelion and the cane toad owe much of their modern range to human transport.

Causes

Cosmopolitan ranges arise through high dispersal ability (wind-borne spores, ocean currents, migratory flight), broad ecological tolerance, association with humans, or an ancient origin predating continental drift for slowly dispersing groups. The opposite of a cosmopolitan distribution is an endemic distribution, where a taxon is confined to a single small region. A distribution spanning both northern and southern temperate zones but absent from the tropics is termed bipolar.
