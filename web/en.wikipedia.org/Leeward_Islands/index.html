Leeward Islands

The Leeward Islands are a group of islands situated where the northeastern Caribbean Sea meets the Atlantic Ocean. They form the northern part of the Lesser Antilles chain, starting with the Virgin Islands east of Puerto Rico and extending southeast to Guadeloupe and its dependencies.

Geography

The group includes the U.S. and British Virgin Islands, Anguilla, Saint Martin/Sint Maarten, Saint Barthélemy, Saba, Sint Eustatius, Saint Kitts and Nevis, Antigua and Barbuda, Montserrat, and Guadeloupe. The name refers to their position relative to the prevailing northeasterly trade winds: a ship sailing from Europe arrived "leeward" at these islands compared with the more southerly Windward Islands.

History

The islands were originally inhabited by Arawak and later Carib peoples. From the 17th century they were colonised by England, France, the Netherlands, Denmark, and Spain, and developed plantation economies based on enslaved labour. The British Leeward Islands existed as a federal colony from 1671 to 1958, with interruptions, before its territories became separate colonies and later independent states or overseas territories.

Usage of the term

In French and Dutch usage the division differs: French îles Sous-le-Vent and Dutch Benedenwindse Eilanden refer to other island groups near South America, while the Dutch call the Leeward Antilles of this article the Bovenwindse Eilanden ("Windward Islands").
