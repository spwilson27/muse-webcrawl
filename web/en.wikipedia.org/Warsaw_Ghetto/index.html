The Warsaw Ghetto was the largest of the Nazi ghettos during the Second World War. It was established by the German authorities in October 1940 in the Muranów district of occupied Warsaw and sealed off from the rest of the city on 16 November 1940. At its peak, roughly 445,000–460,000 Jews were imprisoned within an area of about 3.4 km² — around 30 per cent of Warsaw's population crowded into some 2.4 per cent of its area.

Life in the ghetto

Conditions were deliberately lethal: official rations amounted to starvation levels, and disease — especially typhus — and hunger killed tens of thousands. Smuggling food across the wall was a major form of everyday resistance. Despite the conditions, the ghetto sustained an intense clandestine cultural and educational life, and the historian Emanuel Ringelblum organised the secret Oneg Shabbat archive, whose documents were buried in milk churns and metal boxes and recovered after the war.

Deportations

Between July and September 1942, in the Grossaktion Warsaw, approximately 265,000–300,000 Jews were deported from the ghetto's Umschlagplatz to the Treblinka extermination camp and murdered. Thousands more were killed in the ghetto during the round-ups. Adam Czerniaków, head of the Judenrat (Jewish Council), took his own life on 23 July 1942 rather than sign deportation orders for children.

The uprising

On 19 April 1943, when German forces entered to complete the liquidation, the Jewish Combat Organization (ŻOB) and the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW) rose in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising — the largest act of Jewish resistance during the Holocaust. A few hundred poorly armed fighters held out for nearly a month. SS commander Jürgen Stroop's forces burned the ghetto block by block; the uprising ended symbolically with the destruction of the Great Synagogue on 16 May 1943. Some 13,000 Jews died in the fighting and fires; most of the survivors were deported to extermination and labour camps.

Legacy

The ghetto was razed; after the war the district was rebuilt as a residential area, in places on top of the rubble. Memorials, including Nathan Rapoport's Monument to the Ghetto Heroes (1948) and the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews (opened 2013 on the site of the former ghetto), commemorate its history.
