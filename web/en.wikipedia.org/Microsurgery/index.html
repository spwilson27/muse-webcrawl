Microsurgery

Microsurgery is a general term for surgery requiring an operating microscope and specialized precision instruments. The surgeon uses magnification to operate on extremely small structures — blood vessels and nerves often less than a millimetre in diameter — most famously in the reattachment of severed limbs and the transfer of tissue between parts of the body.

History

The Swedish otolaryngologist Carl-Olof Nylén first used a microscope in surgery in 1921, and ear surgeons adopted it in the 1950s. In the 1960s Jacobson and Suarez described microscope-assisted blood-vessel repair, and in 1962–1963 teams in the United States and China performed the first successful replantations of amputated arms. Harry Buncke's laboratory work and the 1968 thumb replantation by Komatsu and Tamai in Japan established modern microvascular technique; in 1972 Buncke performed the first free tissue transfer in the United States.

Techniques and applications

Using microsutures finer than a human hair (9-0 to 12-0), surgeons anastomose tiny vessels and nerves. Applications include replantation of digits and limbs, free flap reconstruction after cancer or trauma, lymphoedema surgery (lymphaticovenular bypass), nerve repair and grafting, vasectomy reversal and tubal reanastomosis, corneal transplantation and cataract surgery, and neurosurgical procedures. Robotic microsurgery systems are an emerging development.
