Lulav

A lulav with its accompanying species and an etrog

Type: Ritual object

Religion: Judaism

Holiday: Sukkot

Lulav ([luˈlav]; Hebrew: לולב) is a closed frond of the date palm tree. It is one of the Four Species used during the Jewish holiday of Sukkot. The other species are the hadass (myrtle), the aravah (willow), and the etrog (citron). When bound together, the myrtle, willow, and lulav are often referred to collectively as "the lulav".

Religious practice

The Torah commands in Leviticus 23:40: "And you shall take on the first day the fruit of splendid trees, branches of palm trees and boughs of leafy trees and willows of the brook, and you shall rejoice before the LORD your God for seven days." During Sukkot prayers, the Four Species are held and waved in six directions — east, south, west, north, up, and down — during the recitation of Hallel, symbolising God's presence everywhere and prayers for rain and agricultural abundance.

Requirements

Rabbinic law specifies detailed criteria for a kosher lulav: it should generally be at least four handbreadths long, its central spine should be intact, and its leaves should remain mostly closed and naturally joined. The lulav is bound with strips of palm leaf in a woven holder (koishelekh) together with three myrtle twigs on its right and two willow branches on its left.

Interpretations of the Four Species liken them to parts of the body — the lulav to the spine — or to classes of Jews united in worship despite differences in learning and observance.
