Sourdough is bread made by fermenting dough with a starter culture of wild yeasts and lactobacilli naturally present in flour. The bacteria produce lactic and acetic acids, giving the bread its characteristic tangy flavour, chewy texture and keeping quality. Sourdough was the standard form of leavening from antiquity until commercial baker's yeast displaced it in the 19th–20th centuries.

The starter

A starter (also called a levain, mother or chef) is a mixture of flour and water colonised by wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria, maintained by regular "feedings" of fresh flour and water. Starters can live for decades — some bakeries claim century-old lineages — and their microbial communities vary by place; San Francisco's famous sourdough is associated with the bacterium Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis. A 2021 study of 500 starters found geography mattered less than flour type and maintenance regime.

Process and properties

Typical sourdough bread involves mixing, bulk fermentation for several hours, shaping, a slow proof (often refrigerated overnight) and baking in a hot oven, traditionally with steam. Fermentation partially breaks down gluten and phytic acid, which can improve digestibility and mineral availability, and sourdough breads tend to have a lower glycaemic response than yeasted equivalents — claims of probiotic benefit are limited because baking kills the microbes.

History

Leavened bread arose in ancient Egypt and spread through Greece and Rome. In the California and Klondike gold rushes, miners carried starters so prized that "Sourdough" became a nickname for a Yukon/Alaska old-timer. Artisan and home baking revived the craft from the 1980s, with a notable surge during the COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns of 2020.
