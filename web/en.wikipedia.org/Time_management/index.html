Time management

Time management is the process of planning and exercising conscious control of time spent on specific activities — especially to increase effectiveness, efficiency and productivity. It involves juggling the demands of work, study, social life, family and personal interests within a finite amount of time, using skills, tools and techniques such as goal setting, prioritisation, scheduling, to-do lists and delegation.

Approaches

Common methods include the Eisenhower matrix, which sorts tasks by urgency and importance; David Allen's Getting Things Done system, based on capturing all tasks into a trusted external system and reviewing them regularly; the Pomodoro Technique, which works in focused 25-minute intervals separated by short breaks; timeblocking, in which the calendar is reserved for specific activities; and the Pareto principle (the "80/20 rule"), which holds that a minority of efforts produce the majority of results. Effective time management typically begins with setting clear goals, breaking large projects into concrete next actions, estimating durations realistically (guarding against the planning fallacy), and reviewing progress.

Obstacles and research

Major obstacles include procrastination, interruptions, multitasking (which research shows usually degrades performance through task-switching costs), perfectionism and poor estimation. Empirical studies find that perceived control of time is associated with job satisfaction and reduced stress, though the strength of formal time-management training effects on objective performance is debated. Cultural attitudes to punctuality, deadlines and the separation of work and leisure also shape time-management practices.
