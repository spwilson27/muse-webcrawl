The Kaw people, or Kansa (in their own language, Káⁿza), are a federally recognised Native American tribe in the United States, organised as the Kaw Nation with headquarters at Kaw City, Oklahoma. The state of Kansas takes its name from the tribe, as does the Kansas River (the Kaw). A Siouan-speaking people of the Dhegiha branch, the Kaw are closely related to the Osage, Omaha, Ponca and Quapaw.

History

The Dhegiha peoples are believed to have migrated westward from the Ohio Valley, with the Kaw settling along the Kansas and Missouri rivers. When Lewis and Clark passed in 1804, the principal Kansa village stood near the mouth of the Kansas River. Through treaties in 1825 and 1846 the Kaw ceded most of their land, retaining a diminished reservation near Council Grove, Kansas, and in 1873 they were removed to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), where they received allotments in what became Kay County.

By the early 20th century the tribal population had fallen to a few hundred. Charles Curtis, of part-Kaw descent, became Vice President of the United States under Herbert Hoover (1929–1933). The last fluent native speaker of the Kansa language, Walter Kekahbah, died in 2002, but the Kaw Nation operates a language programme using archival recordings and documents to teach and revitalise Kansa.

Government and culture

The Kaw Nation is governed by an elected council under a constitution adopted in the 20th century, and operates enterprises including casinos and the Kanza Health Clinic. Annual cultural events include a powwow at Washunga Days in Council Grove, named for the Kaw chief Washunga, whose remains were repatriated and reinterred in 2000. The Allegawaho Memorial Heritage Park near Council Grove preserves a Kaw agency site and the tribe's Kansas homeland connection.
