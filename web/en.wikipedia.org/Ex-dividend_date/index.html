Ex-dividend date

The ex-dividend date, also known as the ex-date, is the day on and after which a security trades without the right to receive the next declared dividend. An investor who buys a stock on or after its ex-dividend date will not receive the upcoming dividend payment; the seller retains it. Conversely, an investor who buys before the ex-date and is a holder on the record date is entitled to the dividend.

How it works

When a company declares a dividend it announces a record date — the date by which an investor must be on the company's books to receive payment. Because stock trades settle after the trade date, exchanges set the ex-dividend date based on settlement rules. Under the T+1 settlement cycle adopted in the United States in May 2024, the ex-dividend date is generally the same business day as the record date; previously, under T+2, it fell one business day before the record date.

On the ex-date, the opening price of a stock is typically reduced by approximately the amount of the dividend, all else equal, because new buyers no longer acquire the right to the payment. Special rules apply to very large dividends (those exceeding 25 percent of the stock's value), for which the ex-date is deferred until after the payment date.

Related terms

  • Declaration date — the date the board of directors announces the dividend.
  • Record date — the date used to determine eligible shareholders.
  • Payment date (payable date) — the date dividend checks are issued or accounts credited.
  • Cum dividend — trading with the right to the dividend, before the ex-date.
