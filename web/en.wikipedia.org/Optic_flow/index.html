Optic flow

Optic flow (or optical flow) is the pattern of apparent motion of objects, surfaces, and edges in a visual scene caused by the relative motion between an observer and the scene. As an observer moves forward, objects appear to stream outward from a point called the focus of expansion; during rotation, the whole field shifts uniformly. The concept was developed by psychologist James J. Gibson in the 1940s as part of his ecological approach to perception.

Uses in perception

Animals use optic flow to guide locomotion, estimate heading, judge time to contact with obstacles, maintain balance, and control landing in flying insects. Bees, for example, regulate flight speed and center themselves in tunnels by monitoring the flow in each eye. In humans, flow is processed prominently in areas MT and MST of the visual cortex.

Computation

In computer vision, optical flow estimation refers to algorithms that compute the motion of image pixels between frames, typically assuming brightness constancy. Classical methods include the Lucas–Kanade and Horn–Schunck algorithms; modern approaches use deep learning. Applications include video compression, motion segmentation, object tracking, structure from motion, and visual odometry for robots and autonomous vehicles.

Limitations

The aperture problem — the ambiguity of motion seen through a small window — and the absence of texture in uniform regions make flow estimation ill-posed, so algorithms must impose smoothness or other constraints. Biological systems face the same difficulties and solve them by integrating flow over large regions of the visual field.
