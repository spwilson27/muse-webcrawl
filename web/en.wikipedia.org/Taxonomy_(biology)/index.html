Taxonomy (biology)

In biology, taxonomy is the science of naming, defining and classifying groups of organisms on the basis of shared characteristics. Organisms are grouped into taxa (singular: taxon), which are arranged in a hierarchical classification and given scientific names governed by international codes of nomenclature.

The Linnaean system

The modern system descends from the work of the Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus, whose Systema Naturae (1735) and Species Plantarum (1753) established binomial nomenclature — a two-part Latin name for each species consisting of its genus and a specific epithet, as in Homo sapiens. The principal ranks, from broadest to most specific, are domain, kingdom, phylum (or division in botany), class, order, family, genus and species, with many intermediate ranks in use.

Modern taxonomy

Since Darwin, classifications have aimed to reflect evolutionary relationships (phylogeny). The cladistic approach of Willi Hennig groups organisms by shared derived characters, and molecular data — DNA and protein sequences — now dominate phylogenetic reconstruction. Codes such as the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature and the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants regulate naming, while proposals such as the PhyloCode define taxon names by ancestry. The three-domain system of Woese divides cellular life into Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya.

Applications

Taxonomy underpins biodiversity science, conservation, agriculture and medicine: identifying pests, pathogens and invasive species, cataloguing life in collections and databases such as GBIF and the Catalogue of Life, and estimating the total number of species on Earth — around 1.6–2 million described of an estimated several million.
