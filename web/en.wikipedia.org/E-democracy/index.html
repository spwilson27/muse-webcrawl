E-democracy

E-democracy (a portmanteau of electronic and democracy), also known as digital democracy or Internet democracy, is the use of information and communication technology (ICT) in political and governance processes. The term is understood to cover a wide range of practices, from online voting and electronic referendums to government use of social media, online deliberation forums, and digital tools for civic participation, petitions and consultation.

Definitions and scope

Scholars distinguish between e-democracy directed at electoral processes (such as e-voting) and broader efforts to strengthen democratic participation between elections. Steven Clift, who popularised the term in the 1990s, described e-democracy as the use of information and communication technologies and strategies by democratic actors within political and governance processes of local communities, nations and international organisations. The concept builds on the assumption that digital networks lower the cost of participation and make it easier for citizens to access information, deliberate and organise.

Forms

  • E-voting — casting ballots electronically, including internet voting used in Estonia since 2005.
  • E-petitions — online petition systems, such as the UK Parliament's petitions website, where petitions passing a threshold receive an official response or parliamentary debate.
  • Online deliberation — structured discussion platforms where citizens debate policy proposals, for example Decidim in Barcelona or the vTaiwan process in Taiwan.
  • Participatory budgeting — digital platforms that let residents propose and vote on how a portion of a public budget is spent.
  • E-government — online delivery of public services and publication of government data (open government data).

Criticisms

Critics raise concerns about the digital divide, the security and verifiability of electronic voting, the vulnerability of online political discourse to manipulation and disinformation, and the risk that digital participation reproduces existing inequalities of voice. Others argue that online tools are frequently used for consultation rather than genuine decision-making power, a pattern sometimes described as "participation washing".
