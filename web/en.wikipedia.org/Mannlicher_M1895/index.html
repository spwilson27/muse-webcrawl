The Mannlicher M1895 (German: Infanterie Repetiergewehr M.95) is a straight-pull bolt-action rifle designed by Ferdinand Mannlicher and adopted in 1895 as the standard service rifle of the Austro-Hungarian Army. It was manufactured chiefly by Österreichische Waffenfabriks-Gesellschaft (Steyr) in Austria and at FÉG in Budapest, Hungary. Nicknamed the "Ruck-Zuck" ("right now" or "very quick") by Austro-Hungarian troops for its fast-cycling action, it served through both world wars in the hands of many armies.

Design

The M1895's defining feature is its straight-pull bolt: instead of lifting, pulling, pushing, and closing a conventional bolt handle, the shooter simply pulls the bolt handle straight back and pushes it forward. An internal cam mechanism rotates the bolt head's locking lugs in and out of engagement, giving the rifle an exceptionally rapid rate of fire for a manually operated arm.

The rifle is fed from a fixed box magazine using Mannlicher's five-round en-bloc clip: the clip, loaded with cartridges, is inserted whole into the magazine, and when empty it falls out through an opening in the magazine's base. When the bolt is open the clip can be ejected by a button at the front of the trigger guard. The design has a characteristic drawback: without a clip, the rifle is effectively a single-shot weapon.

The original chambering was the 8×50mmR Mannlicher cartridge (M93 scharfe Patrone). The sights were graduated in Schritt (paces), later in metres, to long ranges typical of the period.

Variants and service

The M1895 was produced in three main configurations: the long infantry rifle, a shorter carbine for cavalry and artillery, and the intermediate Stutzen (short rifle). It was the principal rifle of Austria-Hungary in the First World War, fighting on the Eastern, Italian, and Balkan fronts. After 1918 it passed to the successor states and others, including Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Italy (as war reparations and captured stocks), Greece, Bulgaria, and Romania.

In the 1930s many rifles were converted to the more powerful spitzer 8×56mmR cartridge (designated M95/30 in Austria and 31M in Hungary, marked with an "S" on the barrel), and Yugoslavia converted others to 7.92×57mm Mauser as the M95M. Second World War service followed with Austria (within the German Wehrmacht), Hungary, Bulgaria, Italy, and various partisan and reserve formations.
