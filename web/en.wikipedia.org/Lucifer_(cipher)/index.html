In cryptography, Lucifer was a series of block ciphers developed by Horst Feistel and his colleagues at IBM. Lucifer was one of the first civilian block ciphers, freely available for commercial use. It is the direct ancestor of the Data Encryption Standard (DES).

History

Feistel began work on Lucifer in the late 1960s at IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center. Several variants were produced under the name; the best known operates on 128-bit blocks with a 128-bit key, using a Feistel network with substitution boxes (S-boxes) and permutation boxes (P-boxes). A paper describing the design and its rationale was published by Feistel in Scientific American in 1973.

Relation to DES

When the U.S. National Bureau of Standards solicited a cipher for a national encryption standard, IBM submitted a descendant of Lucifer. The result, adopted as DES in 1977, reduced the block size to 64 bits and the effective key size to 56 bits, changes that generated controversy about whether the standard had been deliberately weakened.

Security

One version of Lucifer, with 48-bit blocks and keys, was shown to be vulnerable to differential cryptanalysis; nevertheless, the design introduced key innovations of modern block cipher design: the Feistel structure, non-linear S-boxes, and product-cipher rounds combining substitution and permutation.
