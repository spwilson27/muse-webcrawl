Chinese dragon

The Chinese dragon, also known as the loong, long, or lung (simplified Chinese: 龙; traditional Chinese: 龍; pinyin: lóng), is a legendary creature in Chinese mythology and folklore. Unlike the malevolent, fire-breathing dragons of Western tradition, Chinese dragons are benevolent beings associated with water, rainfall, rivers, and imperial authority. The dragon is the fifth animal of the Chinese zodiac and one of the most potent symbols in Chinese culture.

Description

Classical descriptions give the dragon a composite anatomy: the head of a camel, horns of a deer, eyes of a demon or rabbit, ears of a cow, neck of a snake, belly of a clam, scales of a carp, claws of an eagle, and paws of a tiger. Dragons are usually depicted with four legs and a long, scaly, serpentine body, often clutching or chasing a flaming pearl, interpreted as a symbol of wisdom or the moon. Imperial dragons bore five claws; four-clawed dragons were assigned to lesser princes and nobles.

Dragons and water

Chinese dragons are rulers of the waters. Four Dragon Kings (Lóngwáng) govern the seas of the four directions, while lesser dragons inhabit rivers, lakes, and wells. In times of drought, communities prayed and made offerings at dragon temples to induce rain, and dragon dances remain a feature of festivals including Chinese New Year.

The dragon and the emperor

From early times the dragon symbolized the emperor: the throne was the "dragon throne," the imperial robe the "dragon robe," and the emperor himself the "true dragon, Son of Heaven." Legend held that the mythical Yellow Emperor transformed into a dragon, and emperors were said to be born with dragon features.

Types of dragons

  • Tianlong — celestial dragons, guardians of the heavens.
  • Shenlong — spiritual dragons controlling wind and rain.
  • Fucanglong — underworld dragons guarding buried treasure.
  • Yinglong — winged dragons, the eldest type.
  • Jiaolong — hornless or flood dragons.
