Dissonance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dissonance is a concept with meanings in several fields. Most prominently, in music theory, dissonance is the quality of a sound that seems unstable, tense or "clashing", and which traditionally demands resolution to a consonance. The article summarises the main senses of the term.

Music

In Western music theory, intervals such as the minor second, major seventh and tritone are classed as dissonant, while octaves, perfect fifths and major and minor thirds and sixths are consonant. What counts as dissonant has changed over history: thirds were treated as dissonances in medieval music, and the emancipation of dissonance in the early 20th century, associated with composers such as Arnold Schoenberg, freed dissonant sonorities from the obligation to resolve. Psychoacoustic research attributes perceived dissonance partly to roughness caused by beating between nearby partials within a critical band.

Psychology

In psychology, cognitive dissonance is the mental discomfort experienced when a person's beliefs, values or attitudes conflict with one another or with their behaviour. Proposed by Leon Festinger in 1957, the theory holds that people are motivated to reduce this discomfort by changing beliefs, changing behaviour, or rationalising the conflict.

Other uses

In poetry and literature, dissonance denotes a deliberate jarring combination of sounds or images. In physics and acoustics the term may refer to harsh or inharmonic sound in general.
