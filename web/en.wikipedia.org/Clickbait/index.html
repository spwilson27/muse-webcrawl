Clickbait

Clickbait is sensationalised or misleading text or thumbnail imagery used on websites, especially in headlines, designed to attract attention and encourage visitors to click on a link to a particular web page. Clickbait headlines typically exploit the "curiosity gap", providing just enough information to make readers curious but not enough to satisfy that curiosity without clicking. The term is generally pejorative, implying that the content behind the link disappoints relative to the headline's promise.

Characteristics

Common clickbait techniques include withholding the key fact of a story ("You won't believe what happened next"), listicles with provocative titles, exaggerated claims, emotionally charged wording, and images cropped or chosen to mislead. The style was popularised in the early 2010s by viral-content websites such as Upworthy and BuzzFeed and by content recommendation widgets appearing at the foot of news pages.

Responses

Social media platforms have repeatedly adjusted their algorithms to demote clickbait: Facebook announced measures in 2014 and 2016 to reduce the reach of posts with clickbait headlines, and other platforms followed. Browser extensions and satirical projects, such as "Clickbait spoilers" accounts, emerged to deflate the technique. Critics of the term note that it is sometimes applied loosely to any engaging headline, blurring the line between legitimate audience-building and deception.

Background

Clickbait is widely seen as a descendant of yellow journalism and tabloid headline writing, adapted to an online economy in which advertising revenue follows page views and engagement metrics reward curiosity and outrage.
