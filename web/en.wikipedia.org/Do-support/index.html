Do-support

Do-support (or do-insertion) is the use of the auxiliary verb do (in its forms do, does, and did) as a dummy auxiliary in English clauses where no other auxiliary is present. It occurs most characteristically in questions ("Do you agree?"), negated statements ("She does not agree"), and emphatic assertions ("I do agree").

Distribution

In Modern English, lexical verbs cannot normally be negated directly or inverted with the subject: forms such as *"Agrees she not?" are ungrammatical. Where a clause lacks a modal or perfect/progressive auxiliary, a form of do is inserted to carry tense and agreement, leaving the main verb in its bare infinitive form. Do-support is also triggered in elliptical constructions ("John likes tea, and Mary does too") and in tag questions.

Emphatic do

In affirmative declarative clauses, stressed do can be used for emphasis or contrast: "I do want to come." This emphatic use is sometimes treated as a separate phenomenon from the grammatically required do of questions and negation.

History

Do-support emerged in Middle English and became regularised during the Early Modern English period, roughly between 1500 and 1700. Earlier stages of English allowed direct negation and inversion of lexical verbs, as modern German and French still do. Scholars have debated whether the change was internally motivated or influenced by contact with Celtic languages, whose periphrastic constructions show some similarities.

Analysis

In generative grammar, do-support is classically analysed as a last-resort operation: when tense features cannot attach to the main verb — for example because negation intervenes — they are spelled out on the dummy verb do. The phenomenon has been a central case study in theories of auxiliary syntax and affix lowering.
