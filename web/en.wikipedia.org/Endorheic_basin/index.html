Endorheic basin

An endorheic basin (from Greek endo, "within", and rhein, "to flow") is a drainage basin that normally retains water and allows no outflow to other external bodies of water, such as rivers and oceans. Instead, water converges into lakes or swamps, permanent or seasonal, that equilibrate through evaporation. Such basins may also be called closed or terminal basins, in contrast to exorheic basins whose waters reach the sea.

Characteristics

Endorheic regions are usually found in the arid interiors of continents, far from oceanic moisture, or where mountain ranges block drainage to the sea. Because water can leave only by evaporation or seepage, dissolved minerals concentrate in terminal lakes, which are often saline or alkaline. Examples include the Caspian Sea (the world's largest inland body of water), the Aral Sea basin, the Dead Sea in the Jordan Rift Valley, the Great Salt Lake in Utah's Great Basin, Lake Chad, and Australia's Lake Eyre basin, which covers about a sixth of that continent.

Formation

Endorheic basins arise through tectonic subsidence, rifting, volcanic damming, or the drying of formerly connected drainage networks as climates became more arid. Some basins are only conditionally endorheic: in unusually wet years, terminal lakes can overflow into neighbouring catchments, as Lake Eyre's spillways occasionally suggest happened in the distant past. Fossil drainage patterns and old shorelines record such changes.

Environmental issues

Because terminal lakes integrate the whole basin, they are sensitive indicators of climate and of upstream water use. Diversion of feeder rivers for irrigation shrank the Aral Sea catastrophically in the late twentieth century, and similar pressures affect the Dead Sea and the Salton Sea. Salinisation, dust from exposed lakebeds, and loss of fisheries are common consequences.
