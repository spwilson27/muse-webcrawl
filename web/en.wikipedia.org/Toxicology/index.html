Toxicology is a scientific discipline, overlapping with biology, chemistry, pharmacology, and medicine, that involves the study of the adverse effects of chemical substances on living organisms and the practice of diagnosing and treating exposures to toxins and toxicants. The relationship between dose and its effects is of central importance; the field is summarized by the maxim attributed to Paracelsus: "the dose makes the poison" (sola dosis facit venenum).

History

Knowledge of poisons is ancient — the Ebers Papyrus records Egyptian toxicology, and Socrates was executed with hemlock in 399 BC. The Swiss physician Paracelsus (1493–1541) founded the discipline's modern approach, insisting that a substance's toxicity is dose-dependent. In the 19th century, Mathieu Orfila systematized forensic toxicology, and industrialization brought occupational toxicology as workers were exposed to lead, mercury, and chemical fumes.

Basic principles

Toxic effects may be local or systemic, reversible or irreversible, immediate or delayed. Key measures include the median lethal dose (LD₅₀) and threshold limit values for occupational exposure. Toxicologists study toxicokinetics — how a substance is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted — and toxicodynamics, its mechanisms of action at cellular and molecular levels.

Subdisciplines

  • Medical/clinical toxicology — diagnosis and treatment of poisoning.
  • Forensic toxicology — detection of drugs and poisons in legal investigations.
  • Environmental toxicology (ecotoxicology) — effects of pollutants on ecosystems.
  • Regulatory toxicology — risk assessment supporting safety standards for drugs, food additives, and chemicals.
  • Occupational toxicology — workplace chemical hazards.

Testing methods

Traditionally reliant on animal studies, toxicology increasingly uses in vitro cell cultures, computational (in silico) models, and high-throughput screening under frameworks such as the "Toxicity Testing in the 21st Century" vision, aiming to reduce animal use while improving prediction of human risk. Risk is generally characterized as the product of hazard and exposure.
