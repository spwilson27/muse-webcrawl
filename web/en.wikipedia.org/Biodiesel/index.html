Biodiesel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biodiesel is a renewable, biodegradable fuel manufactured from vegetable oils, animal fats, or recycled restaurant grease. Chemically, it consists of long-chain fatty acid esters, typically produced by transesterification: reacting triglycerides with methanol or ethanol in the presence of a catalyst such as sodium hydroxide, yielding methyl esters and glycerol as a by-product.

Production and use

Feedstocks include soybean oil (United States), rapeseed oil (Europe), palm oil (Southeast Asia), and used cooking oil. Biodiesel can be burned in most diesel engines, often blended with petroleum diesel — designations such as B20 (20% biodiesel) and B100 (pure biodiesel) are common. Rudolf Diesel himself ran an early engine on peanut oil at the 1900 Paris Exposition.

Properties

Biodiesel has higher lubricity and cetane than most petroleum diesel and contains virtually no sulfur, reducing particulate and carbon-monoxide emissions, though nitrogen-oxide emissions may increase slightly. It gels at higher temperatures than conventional diesel, which can limit cold-weather use.

Environmental debate

Because the carbon released on combustion was recently captured by the feedstock crop, biodiesel is often described as near carbon-neutral over its life cycle. Critics point to land-use change — particularly deforestation for palm plantations — fertiliser use, and food-versus-fuel competition, which can erode or reverse the climate benefit.
