Apixaban, sold under the brand name Eliquis, is an anticoagulant medication used to treat and prevent blood clots and to prevent stroke in people with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation through directly inhibiting factor Xa. It is a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), taken by mouth, and was developed jointly by Bristol-Myers Squibb and Pfizer.

Medical uses

Apixaban is used to prevent stroke and systemic embolism in atrial fibrillation — in the ARISTOTLE trial it was superior to warfarin for stroke prevention with less major bleeding — to treat deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism and prevent their recurrence, and to prevent venous thromboembolism after hip or knee replacement surgery. The usual dose is 5 mg twice daily, reduced to 2.5 mg twice daily in patients meeting at least two of: age ≥80 years, body weight ≤60 kg, or serum creatinine ≥1.5 mg/dL.

Adverse effects and reversal

The principal risk is bleeding, including gastrointestinal and intracranial haemorrhage, though rates are lower than with warfarin. Unlike warfarin, routine coagulation monitoring is not required. The specific reversal agent andexanet alfa can be used for life-threatening bleeding. Apixaban is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is among the most widely prescribed anticoagulants; generic versions have entered several markets.
