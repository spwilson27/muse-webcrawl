Chirp


Spectrogram of a linear frequency chirp

A chirp is a signal in which the frequency increases (an up-chirp) or decreases (a down-chirp) with time. The name derives from the sound made by chirping birds and insects, whose calls often sweep rapidly in pitch. In a linear chirp, the instantaneous frequency varies linearly with time; in an exponential or geometric chirp it varies as a geometric progression. Chirps occur naturally in bat and dolphin echolocation and in the gravitational-wave signals of inspiralling compact binaries, and are widely engineered in radar, sonar and communications.

Applications

In radar and sonar, transmitting a long chirped pulse and compressing it on reception — pulse compression, implemented by matched filtering — yields high range resolution and good detection performance without requiring high peak power. This chirp spread spectrum principle is also used in wireless communications, notably in the LoRa modulation scheme for low-power wide-area networks, where data is encoded in chirps whose starting frequency is shifted. Chirped mirrors and chirped pulse amplification (CPA) use analogous ideas in optics: in CPA, an ultrashort laser pulse is stretched (chirped), amplified, and recompressed, a technique recognised by the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to Gérard Mourou and Donna Strickland.

Gravitational waves

The signal from two orbiting neutron stars or black holes sweeping upward in frequency and amplitude as they inspiral is called a chirp; its exact form encodes the chirp mass of the system. Such signals were first directly detected by LIGO in 2015 (event GW150914).
