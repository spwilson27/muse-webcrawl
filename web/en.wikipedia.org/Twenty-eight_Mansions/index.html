Twenty-eight Mansions

The Twenty-eight Mansions (Chinese: 二十八宿, èrshíbā xiù) are part of the Chinese constellations system. Similar in function to the zodiac of Western astronomy, they divide the ecliptic into twenty-eight segments through which the Moon passes in the course of one sidereal month, each segment corresponding to one night of the Moon's journey. The mansions are grouped into the Four Palaces, each associated with a cardinal direction, a season, and a mythical animal: the Azure Dragon of the East, the Vermilion Bird of the South, the White Tiger of the West, and the Black Tortoise of the North.

History

The system is among the oldest elements of Chinese astronomy. A lacquer chest from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng, dated to about 433 BCE, bears the names of all twenty-eight mansions arranged around a depiction of the Northern Dipper, providing the earliest complete surviving list. The mansions were used to track the motions of the Moon, Jupiter, and other celestial bodies, and they played an important role in calendrical calculation and astrology.

Structure

Unlike the twelve equal signs of the Western zodiac, the mansions are of unequal width, ranging from less than one degree to more than thirty degrees. Each mansion is defined by a determinative star, and its extent runs from that star to the determinative star of the next mansion. The seven mansions of each palace are traditionally listed beginning with Horn (角, Jiǎo) in the Azure Dragon.

Indian and other parallels

A strikingly similar system of twenty-seven or twenty-eight lunar stations, the nakṣatra, exists in Indian astronomy, and comparable schemes appear elsewhere in Asia. Scholars have long debated whether the Chinese and Indian systems share a common origin or developed independently; the mansions were transmitted along with Buddhism into Japan, where they are known as the nijūhachi shuku.
