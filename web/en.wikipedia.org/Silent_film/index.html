Silent film

A silent film is a film without synchronized recorded sound (or more generally, no audible dialogue). Though silent films convey narrative and emotion visually, various plot elements (such as a setting or era) or key lines of dialogue may, when necessary, be conveyed through the use of intertitle cards.

The term "silent film" is something of a misnomer, as these films were almost always accompanied by live sound—typically music played by a pianist, organist, or theatre orchestra, and often sound effects. From cinema's beginnings in the 1890s until the late 1920s, virtually all films were silent; the era is sometimes called the "Age of the Silver Screen."

History

Motion pictures emerged in the 1890s with short films by pioneers such as Thomas Edison, the Lumière brothers, and Georges Méliès. By the 1910s and 1920s silent cinema had matured into a sophisticated art form with stars such as Charlie Chaplin, Buster Keaton, and Mary Pickford, and masterworks such as Metropolis, Battleship Potemkin, and The Birth of a Nation. The commercial success of The Jazz Singer (1927) inaugurated synchronized sound, and within a few years silent production had virtually ceased in the West, though it lingered slightly longer in Japan.

Performance and preservation

Silent films were often projected at variable speeds (16–24 frames per second) and were tinted or toned for effect. An estimated majority of silent-era features—figures around 75% for American films—are now lost, making film preservation of surviving prints a major archival concern. Notable modern silent films include The Artist (2011), which won the Academy Award for Best Picture.
