Surface weather analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Surface weather analysis is a special type of weather map that provides a view of weather elements over a geographical area at a specified time based on information from ground-based weather stations. Weather maps are created by plotting or tracing the values of relevant quantities such as sea level pressure, temperature, and cloud cover onto a geographical map to help find synoptic scale features such as weather fronts.

The first weather maps in the 19th century were drawn well after the fact to help devise a theory on storm systems. With the advent of the telegraph, it became possible to base observations of surface weather on simultaneous measurements, and by the late 19th century surface analyses were being performed on a routine basis. The use of the front concept and the classification of air masses came into widespread use in the 1920s under the influence of the Bergen School of meteorology in Norway.

Elements of a surface analysis

A surface analysis typically displays:

  • Isobars, lines of equal sea-level pressure, spaced according to national convention;
  • High and low pressure centres, marked H and L, with their central pressures;
  • Fronts: cold fronts (blue lines with triangles), warm fronts (red lines with semicircles), stationary fronts (alternating symbols), and occluded fronts (purple);
  • Station plots at observation sites showing temperature, dew point, wind direction and speed, pressure and its tendency, cloud cover, and present weather; and
  • Troughs, squall lines, dry lines, and areas of precipitation, often shaded.

Uses

Surface analyses are a basic tool of operational weather forecasting. They identify the current position and intensity of cyclones and anticyclones, indicate zones of temperature advection and moisture transport, and provide the starting point for short-term forecasts. In the United States, the Weather Prediction Center issues North American surface analyses; other national meteorological services and ocean prediction centres produce corresponding analyses for their regions and for the world's oceans.

History

The first attempt to construct a synoptic weather map is credited to the 1820s work of Heinrich Wilhelm Brandes, who plotted data from 1783. After the invention of the telegraph in 1845, simultaneous observations became practical: the Smithsonian Institution collected telegraphic reports in the 1850s, and the United States Weather Bureau, the British Meteorological Office, and other services began regular chart production. The Norwegian cyclone model, developed by Vilhelm Bjerknes and his collaborators after the First World War, added fronts and air-mass concepts and remains the visual language of surface charts today.
