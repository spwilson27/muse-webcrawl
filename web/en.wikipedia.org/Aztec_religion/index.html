Aztec religion

Aztec religion was a Mesoamerican religion of the Aztec civilization. Like other Mesoamerican religions, it had elements of human sacrifice in connection with a large number of religious festivals which were held according to patterns of the Aztec calendar. It possessed a complex polytheistic pantheon, a cosmology in which the present world is the fifth in a succession of creations ("suns"), and an elaborate priesthood centred on the temple precincts of the imperial capital, Tenochtitlan.

Cosmology

Aztec cosmology taught that the universe passes through cycles of creation and destruction. Four previous suns had each ended in catastrophe — by jaguars, wind, fiery rain and flood — and the current Fifth Sun, created at Teotihuacan by the self-sacrifice of the gods, would one day end in earthquakes. The world was ordered into thirteen heavens above, the earth in the middle, and nine underworlds (Mictlan) below; direction, colour and deity correspondences tied every part of daily life to this cosmic order.

Principal deities

  • Huitzilopochtli — solar deity and god of war, tribal patron of the Mexica, whose shrine stood atop the Great Temple.
  • Tlaloc — ancient god of rain, storms and fertility, sharing the Great Temple's summit.
  • Quetzalcoatl — the feathered serpent, god of wind, learning and creation.
  • Tezcatlipoca — the "Smoking Mirror", god of night, sorcery and destiny.
  • Tonatiuh — the sun god, depicted at the centre of the Aztec calendar stone.

Ritual and sacrifice

The 365-day solar calendar (xiuhpohualli) interlocked with the 260-day ritual calendar (tonalpohualli); each of the eighteen monthly festivals honoured particular gods with processions, dances, offerings and, in many cases, human sacrifice, usually of war captives. Aztec theology held that the gods had sacrificed themselves to set the sun in motion, and that human beings owed a debt of blood and hearts to keep the sun moving and stave off the end of the Fifth Sun. Autosacrifice — ritual bloodletting by rulers and commoners alike — was also widespread.

After the conquest

The Spanish conquest and Catholic evangelisation destroyed the state cult and its temples, but many festivals, beliefs and agricultural rites were adapted within a syncretic folk Catholicism that persists in Nahua communities today.
