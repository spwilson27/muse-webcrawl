Unification (computer science)

In logic and computer science, unification is an algorithmic process of solving equations between symbolic expressions. Each expression is a term built from constants, variables and function symbols; a solution — a unifier — is a substitution assigning terms to variables that makes the two expressions identical. When a solution exists, there is a unique (up to renaming) most general unifier (MGU) from which every other solution is obtained by further substitution.

Example

The terms f(a, x) and f(y, b) unify with the substitution {y → a, x → b}. The terms f(x, x) and f(a, b) do not unify, since x cannot equal both a and b. A variable also cannot be bound to a term containing itself (the occurs check): x and f(x) have no finite unifier.

Algorithms and applications

Unification was given an influential near-linear algorithm by Martelli and Montanari (1982), presented as rewriting rules on sets of equations; earlier exponential procedures appeared in J. Alan Robinson's 1965 resolution proof method for automated theorem proving. Unification is the core operation of logic programming languages such as Prolog, the basis of pattern matching in functional languages, and the mechanism behind Hindley–Milner type inference in ML and Haskell. Extensions include higher-order unification (undecidable in general), E-unification modulo equational theories, and nominal unification for terms with binders.

History

The modern concept grew out of work by Jacques Herbrand (1930) on solving equations over term algebras, and was made central to computing by Robinson's resolution principle. Unification has since become one of the fundamental operations of symbolic computation.
