Electroless nickel plating

Electroless nickel plating (ENP) is an auto-catalytic chemical process used to deposit a coating of nickel–phosphorus or nickel–boron alloy on a substrate without passing an electric current through the bath. Because no current distribution is involved, the coating has highly uniform thickness even on complex shapes.

Electroless nickel coatings are valued for corrosion resistance, hardness, and uniform coverage.

Process

The part is immersed in a hot aqueous solution of a nickel salt and a reducing agent, commonly sodium hypophosphite. The reducing agent deposits nickel metal chemically at the surface, and phosphorus from the reducing agent is co-deposited, producing an alloy with typically 2–14% phosphorus. The reaction is catalytic and continues as long as the part remains in the bath.

Properties and uses

Higher phosphorus content improves corrosion resistance; heat treatment can greatly increase hardness, approaching that of hard chrome. Applications include valves and pumps, aerospace and automotive parts, electronic components, and the metallization of plastics. The process was developed commercially in the 1940s following work by Abner Brenner and Grace Riddell at the U.S. National Bureau of Standards.
