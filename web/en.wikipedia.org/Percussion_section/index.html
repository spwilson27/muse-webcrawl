Percussion section

The percussion section is one of the principal sections of the modern orchestra, together with the strings, woodwinds and brass. Positioned conventionally at the back of the ensemble, it comprises percussion instruments — instruments sounded by being struck, shaken or scraped — played by a small number of versatile percussionists, with the timpanist often treated as a distinct role.

Instruments

The core of the section is the timpani (kettledrums), the only percussion instruments routinely tuned to definite pitches. Other instruments regularly required include the bass drum, snare drum, cymbals (crash and suspended), triangle, tambourine, tam-tam (gong), glockenspiel, xylophone, vibraphone, tubular bells (chimes), marimba, castanets, whip and a wide range of "effects". Composers of the 20th and 21st centuries have greatly expanded the battery, sometimes requiring dozens of instruments and found objects.

Role and history

Percussion joined the orchestra gradually. Timpani and "Turkish" instruments (bass drum, cymbals, triangle) entered in the Classical era, often to evoke military or Janissary colour, as in Haydn's Military Symphony and Beethoven's Ninth Symphony. Romantic composers enlarged the section, and modern scores by Stravinsky, Bartók, Varèse and others made it central to rhythm and timbre. Orchestral percussionists are expected to master hundreds of instruments and techniques, reading parts that may switch instruments many times within a movement. A principal percussionist coordinates the section, assigning parts and instruments.

In concert bands and wind ensembles the percussion section is generally larger, and in marching bands its front ensemble ("pit") and drumline form distinct subsections.
