Military logistics

Military logistics is the discipline of planning and carrying out the movement, supply and maintenance of military forces. In its broadest sense it covers the design, development, acquisition, storage, distribution, maintenance and evacuation of materiel; the transport of personnel; the acquisition and furnishing of facilities; and the provision of services such as food, medical support and communications. As the aphorism attributed to various generals puts it, "amateurs talk strategy, professionals talk logistics."

Convoys and supply chains keep deployed forces fed, fuelled and armed.

History

Pre-modern era

Armies historically lived off the land or followed supply trains of wagons and pack animals. Alexander the Great organised supply depots along his line of march, and Roman legions built roads and fortified granaries to sustain campaigns. The size and reach of armies remained limited by the carrying capacity of horses and the availability of fodder.

Napoleonic and industrial eras

Napoleon's armies marched faster by requisitioning supplies, but the system broke down catastrophically in the 1812 invasion of Russia. The railway and steamship transformed logistics in the nineteenth century: the American Civil War and the Franco-Prussian War were the first conflicts in which railways moved mass armies and their supplies. The term "logistics" itself entered military vocabulary through the writings of Antoine-Henri Jomini.

World wars

The First World War's static fronts consumed artillery shells in unprecedented quantities, driving the "shell crisis" of 1915 and the creation of ministries of munitions. In the Second World War, logistics decided campaigns: the Allied Red Ball Express trucked supplies across France in 1944, Liberty ships and the Persian Corridor sustained the Soviet Union, and in the Pacific the US Navy's fleet train allowed carrier forces to operate far from fixed bases. The Allied effort was supported by the Lend-Lease programme.

Modern era

Containerisation, pioneered commercially by Malcom McLean, revolutionised military supply; palletised loads, the 463L airlift system and pre-positioned stocks characterise modern practice. The 1990–91 Gulf War required a six-month build-up (Operation Desert Shield) before a 100-hour ground war. Contemporary forces use just-in-time concepts adapted from commercial supply chain management, along with contractor support on a large scale.

Functions

  • Supply — ammunition, fuel, food, water and spare parts, classified in NATO into five classes of supply.
  • Transport — strategic airlift and sealift, theatre distribution by road, rail and pipeline.
  • Maintenance — repair and recovery of equipment at unit, field and depot levels.
  • Medical support — treatment and evacuation of casualties.
  • Infrastructure — ports, airfields, roads and bridging.

Key concepts

Logisticians measure the "tooth-to-tail ratio" between combat and support forces, calculate days of supply, and plan for throughput at ports and airfields of debarkation. Failures in logistics have famously constrained operations, from the German advance on Moscow in 1941 to the Allied pause after the breakout from Normandy in 1944.
