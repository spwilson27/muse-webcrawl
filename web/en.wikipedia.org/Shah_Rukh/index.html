Shah Rukh (Persian: شاهرخ, Shāhrukh; 20 August 1377 – 13 March 1447) was the youngest son of the Central Asian conqueror Timur and the ruler of the eastern portions of the Timurid Empire from 1405 to 1447. Unlike his father, Shah Rukh was a statesman and patron of culture rather than a conqueror: his long reign brought stability and prosperity, and his court at Herat became the centre of the flourishing of Persian art, architecture and learning known as the Timurid Renaissance.

Rise and reign

Shah Rukh governed Khurasan from Herat from 1397. After Timur's death in 1405, a succession struggle ensued; by 1409 Shah Rukh had defeated his nephew Khalil Sultan, taken Samarkand, and consolidated control over Transoxiana, Khurasan and much of Persia, although western Iran and Azerbaijan were contested with the Qara Qoyunlu Turkomans. He confirmed his son Ulugh Beg as governor of Transoxiana, where Ulugh Beg built his famous observatory in Samarkand. Shah Rukh maintained diplomatic and trade relations with Ming China, exchanging embassies recorded by the painter-diplomat Ghiyath al-Din Naqqash, and with the Mamluks of Egypt and the early Ottoman sultans.

Patronage

Shah Rukh and his formidable wife Gawhar Shad sponsored major building works, including the Gawhar Shad Mosque in Mashhad and the Musalla complex in Herat, and supported poets, historians (such as Hafiz-i Abru), calligraphers and the Herat school of Persian miniature painting. Their son Baysunghur's library-atelier in Herat set the standard for the Persian arts of the book. The stability of Shah Rukh's reign allowed cities devastated by Timur's campaigns to recover.

Death and succession

Shah Rukh died in 1447 while campaigning in western Persia. His death triggered another succession war among his descendants; his son Ulugh Beg briefly succeeded but was overthrown and killed by his own son in 1449, fragmenting the empire.
