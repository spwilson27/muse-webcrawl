Informal economy


Street trading is a visible part of the informal economy in many cities.

An informal economy (or informal sector) is the part of economic activity that is neither taxed nor monitored by government and is not included in official measures such as gross national product. It encompasses street vending, unregistered small workshops, casual day labour, domestic work, subsistence production for sale, and unlicensed services, as well as illegal activities in the underground economy. The International Labour Organization estimates that around two billion workers — roughly 60 per cent of the world's employed population — work informally, with the share far higher in developing countries.

Characteristics

Informal work typically lacks contracts, social protection, minimum-wage coverage and workplace safety regulation. Entry barriers are low, enterprises are small and family-based, and skills are often acquired outside formal education. Workers in the informal economy are disproportionately women and young people.

Causes and debate

Economists attribute informality to burdensome regulation and taxation, weak state capacity, rapid urbanisation and the inability of the formal sector to generate enough jobs. The Peruvian economist Hernando de Soto argued that excessive bureaucracy forces entrepreneurs into informality and that granting them formal property rights would unlock capital. Critics respond that informal work is often a survival strategy marked by low productivity and insecurity, and that formalisation policies must extend social protection rather than merely registration. Policy approaches range from simplified tax and registration regimes to microfinance, cash transfers and extending labour rights to informal workers.
