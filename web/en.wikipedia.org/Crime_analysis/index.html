Crime analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crime analysis is a law enforcement function that involves systematic analysis for identifying and analyzing patterns and trends in crime and disorder. Information on patterns can help law enforcement agencies deploy resources in a more effective manner and assist detectives in identifying and apprehending suspects. Crime analysis also plays a role in crime prevention strategies, problem solving, and evaluation of police performance.

Although the term is often used broadly, crime analysis in its modern professional sense developed largely in the United States during the second half of the twentieth century, drawing on earlier practices such as the pin maps used by police departments to chart reported offenses by hand.

History

Early forms of crime analysis included the mapping of crime by nineteenth-century social researchers such as André-Michel Guerry and Joseph Fletcher, who used official statistics to study the geography of offending. Modern police crime analysis grew out of community policing and problem-oriented policing movements of the 1970s and 1980s, and was institutionalized through professional bodies such as the International Association of Crime Analysts (IACA), founded in 1990.

Types

Crime analysis is commonly divided into tactical, strategic, administrative, and intelligence or investigative analysis. Tactical analysis deals with immediate criminal offenses, series, and patterns to support patrol and investigations. Strategic analysis concerns longer-term trends and emerging problems. Administrative analysis supports management decisions about staffing, budgeting, and resource allocation, while intelligence analysis examines criminal networks, organized crime, and offender groups.

Methods

Analysts use a variety of qualitative and quantitative techniques, including crime mapping with geographic information systems (GIS), statistical analysis, temporal analysis, link analysis, and social network analysis. Common products include bulletins, pattern reports, hot spot maps, and suspect profiles derived from reported incidents, calls for service, arrests, and field interview data.
