Wabi-sabi

Wabi-sabi (侘び寂び) is a Japanese aesthetic worldview centered on the acceptance of transience and imperfection. Rooted in Zen Buddhism and the tea ceremony, it finds beauty in things that are modest, rustic, aged, and incomplete — sometimes summarized by the phrase "nothing lasts, nothing is finished, and nothing is perfect."

Origins

The two terms were originally distinct. Wabi referred to the loneliness of living in nature, remote from society, and came to connote rustic simplicity, quietness, and understated elegance. Sabi meant "chill", "lean", or "withered", and developed into an appreciation of the patina and wear that come with age. The tea master Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591) was central to refining wabi aesthetics in the tea ceremony, favoring rough, locally made bowls over flawless imported wares.

Characteristics

Qualities associated with wabi-sabi include asymmetry, roughness, economy, austerity, modesty, intimacy, and an appreciation of the ingenuous integrity of natural objects and processes. Kintsugi — the repair of broken pottery with gold-dusted lacquer — is often cited as a related practice that honors damage rather than concealing it. The aesthetic influences Japanese gardens, ceramics, flower arrangement, architecture, and, increasingly, design worldwide.

Influence

Leonard Koren's 1994 book Wabi-Sabi for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers introduced the concept widely to Western audiences, where it has been applied to interior design, craft movements, and minimalist lifestyles.
