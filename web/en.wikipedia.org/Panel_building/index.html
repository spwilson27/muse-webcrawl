Panel building

A panel building is a type of prefabricated structure constructed from large, factory-made concrete panels — walls and floor slabs — that are transported to the site and assembled. The system was developed after the Second World War to provide mass housing quickly and cheaply, and became the dominant form of urban residential construction across the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc, as well as a significant element in Western European housing.

History

Large-panel systems were pioneered in the 1940s and 1950s in France (the Camus system), Denmark (Larsen & Nielsen), and elsewhere. In the USSR, industrialised panel housing was promoted from the late 1950s under Nikita Khrushchev, producing the five-storey khrushchyovka blocks and later taller series of nine to sixteen storeys. Similar programmes built districts in East Germany (the Plattenbau), Poland (wielka płyta), Czechoslovakia (panelák), Hungary (panelház), and Yugoslavia.

Characteristics

Standardised designs allowed entire housing estates (microrayons) to be erected rapidly, with identical layouts repeated across many cities. Advantages included speed, low cost, and the provision of self-contained flats with central heating and indoor plumbing to millions of families. Criticisms focus on monotonous appearance, poor sound and thermal insulation, deteriorating joints, and limited apartment layouts.

Legacy

Tens of millions of people still live in panel buildings across Europe and the former Soviet Union. Since 1990 many estates have been renovated with insulation and new façades, while others have been demolished. The buildings remain a defining feature of post-socialist cityscapes and a subject of renewed architectural and social interest.
