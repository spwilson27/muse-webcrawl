EF hand

The EF hand is a helix–loop–helix structural motif found in a large family of calcium-binding proteins. It consists of two alpha helices, conventionally labelled E and F, joined by a loop of about twelve residues whose oxygen atoms coordinate a Ca2+ ion. The motif takes its name from the E and F helices of parvalbumin, the carp muscle protein in whose crystal structure the motif was first described by Robert Kretsinger in 1973; the geometry was likened to the thumb and forefinger of a right hand.

Structure and binding

The calcium ion is coordinated in a pentagonal bipyramidal geometry by residues at positions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 of the loop, with the residue at position 12 (usually glutamate) providing a bidentate ligand. EF hands almost always occur in pairs, forming a stable domain in which the two motifs pack together through short antiparallel beta-sheet interactions between their loops; proteins may contain two, four, six or more EF hands.

EF-hand proteins

The superfamily includes calmodulin (four EF hands; the ubiquitous eukaryotic calcium sensor), troponin C (regulating muscle contraction), S100 proteins, calbindin, parvalbumin, and neuronal calcium sensor proteins such as recoverin. In many of these, calcium binding exposes hydrophobic surfaces that then engage target enzymes — as when calmodulin activates kinases and phosphatases — making the EF hand a central device of calcium signalling in cells.
