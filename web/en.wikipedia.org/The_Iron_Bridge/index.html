The Iron Bridge is a cast iron arch bridge spanning the River Severn in Shropshire, England. Opened on New Year's Day 1781, it was the first major bridge in the world constructed of cast iron, and a celebrated monument of the Industrial Revolution — so influential that it gave its name to the nearby town of Ironbridge. The bridge crosses the Ironbridge Gorge, the cradle of coke smelting pioneered by Abraham Darby I in 1709, and it is now a Grade I listed building, a scheduled monument, and part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Construction

The steep gorge needed a single-span crossing that would not obstruct river traffic, and the local ironmasters promoted iron as the material. Architect Thomas Farnolls Pritchard produced the design, and ironmaster Abraham Darby III cast the components — some 384 tons of iron — at his Coalbrookdale works, erecting the arch in the summer of 1779. Because cast iron could not be joined like timber or masonry, the bridge used carpentry-style joints — mortise and tenon, dovetails, and wedges — translated into iron, as revealed in conservation work. The structure cost about £6,000, over its estimate, a shortfall borne by Darby.

Later history

The bridge demonstrated the potential of iron in construction and was widely imitated. Movement in the gorge sides caused early cracking; road traffic was restricted from the 1930s and the bridge became pedestrian-only in 1950. A major conservation project by English Heritage, completed in 2018, repaired and repainted the structure. It remains the icon of the Ironbridge Gorge Museums.
