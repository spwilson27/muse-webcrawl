Congress derives its power to grant copyrights from the Copyright Clause of the U.S. Constitution.

The copyright law of the United States grants authors and creators a set of exclusive rights in their original works of authorship, including literary, musical, dramatic, artistic, and certain other intellectual works. Its stated purpose, drawn from the Constitution, is "to promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts."

History

The Copyright Clause of the 1787 Constitution empowered Congress to secure exclusive rights "for limited Times." The first federal statute, the Copyright Act of 1790, protected books, maps, and charts for fourteen years, renewable once. Successive acts extended the subject matter and duration. The Copyright Act of 1909 governed much of the twentieth century before being comprehensively replaced by the Copyright Act of 1976, effective 1 January 1978. The Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998 lengthened terms by twenty years, and the United States joined the Berne Convention in 1989, dropping most formalities.

Scope and rights

Copyright subsists automatically in original works fixed in a tangible medium of expression. The owner holds exclusive rights to reproduce the work, prepare derivative works, distribute copies, and perform or display the work publicly. Protection does not extend to ideas, facts, procedures, or systems. Standard terms are the life of the author plus seventy years; works made for hire generally endure 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter.

Limitations

Rights are subject to limitations including the first-sale doctrine, exceptions for libraries and archives, and the doctrine of fair use, which permits limited use of protected material for purposes such as criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, and research, judged by four statutory factors. The Digital Millennium Copyright Act of 1998 added anti-circumvention rules and safe harbours for online service providers.
