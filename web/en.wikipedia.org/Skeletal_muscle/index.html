Skeletal muscle (also called voluntary or striated muscle) is one of the three major types of muscle tissue in vertebrates, the others being cardiac muscle and smooth muscle. Skeletal muscles attach to bones by tendons and work in pairs to produce movement of the skeleton. They are under voluntary control through the somatic nervous system, although some actions, such as reflexes and posture, are involuntary.

Structure

A skeletal muscle is made of bundles (fascicles) of long, cylindrical muscle fibers, each fiber a giant multinucleated cell formed by fused myoblasts. Connective tissue layers — the epimysium around the whole muscle, perimysium around fascicles, and endomysium around each fiber — provide structure and transmit force. Within each fiber, myofibrils of repeating sarcomeres give the tissue its striated appearance; the sarcomere, bounded by Z-discs, is the basic contractile unit, containing interdigitating actin and myosin filaments.

Contraction

Contraction is triggered when a motor neuron releases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction, generating an action potential that travels along the fiber and down the T-tubules, causing calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds troponin, exposing myosin-binding sites on actin; myosin heads then cycle through cross-bridge formation, powered by ATP, in the sliding filament model. Relaxation occurs when calcium is pumped back into storage.

Fiber types

Fibers are commonly classed as slow-twitch (type I, oxidative, fatigue-resistant, suited to endurance) and fast-twitch (type II, glycolytic, powerful but fatigable). Training can shift fiber characteristics; endurance training increases oxidative capacity, while resistance training mainly causes hypertrophy of fibers.
