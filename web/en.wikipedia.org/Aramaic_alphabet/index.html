The ancient Aramaic alphabet was used to write the Aramaic language beginning in the early first millennium BC. It is a descendant of the Phoenician alphabet and was widely adopted across the Near East because Imperial Aramaic served as the administrative lingua franca of the Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, and Achaemenid Persian empires. Like its parent, it is an abjad of 22 consonantal letters, written right to left, with certain letters (matres lectionis) secondarily used to indicate vowels.

History

The earliest Aramaic inscriptions, from the 9th–8th centuries BC (such as those from Sam'al and the Tell Fekheriye statue), are nearly indistinguishable from Phoenician. As Imperial Aramaic spread, regional cursive forms emerged. After the fall of the Achaemenid Empire, the script diversified into national alphabets: the Jewish "square script" (the Hebrew alphabet used to this day), Syriac scripts of Christian communities, Nabataean (the ancestor of the Arabic alphabet), Palmyrene, Hatran, Mandaic, and — via Pahlavi and Sogdian — arguably the scripts of Central Asia, including possibly Brahmi-derived writing in India, though that link remains debated.

Letters

The letters are: ʾālap, bēth, gāmal, dālath, hē, waw, zain, ḥēth, ṭēth, yūdh, kap, lāmadh, mīm, nūn, semkath, ʿē, pē, ṣādhē, qōp, rēsh, shīn, taw. Several letters developed distinct final forms when written at the end of a word, a feature inherited by Hebrew and Arabic.

Legacy

Because Aramaic writing rode the administrative machinery of successive empires, it is an ancestor — direct or indirect — of most modern alphabets of the Middle East and many of Asia, making it one of the most influential scripts in history.
