Shaft mining

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shaft mining or shaft sinking is a form of underground mining in which access to a deep orebody is gained through a vertical (or near-vertical) excavation from the surface. It is the method used for the world's deepest mines, including the gold mines of South Africa's Witwatersrand — Mponeng extends nearly 4 kilometres below the surface — and is contrasted with drift mining and slope (decline) mining, which use horizontal or inclined entries and suit shallower deposits.

Shaft sinking

A shaft is excavated ("sunk") by drilling, blasting, and mucking out broken rock, then lined, usually with concrete, and equipped with guides, pipes, cables, and hoisting plant housed in the headgear (headframe) at surface. Modern sinking uses mechanised jumbos and shaft muckers, and ground freezing or grouting may be needed to pass through water-bearing strata. Shafts may be circular — favoured for deep shafts because of pressure distribution — or rectangular in older, shallower mines.

Layout and operation

From the shaft, horizontal levels are driven at intervals, stations are cut at each level, and stopes or longwall faces extract the ore, which is hauled to the shaft and hoisted to the surface in skips. Personnel travel in cages operated by winders. Mines typically have at least two shafts or connections to surface for ventilation and emergency egress; ventilation shafts, often fitted with large fans, are integral to the system, and deep hot mines require refrigeration plants to keep workings survivable.
