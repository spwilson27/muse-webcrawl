Saga

LanguageOld Norse / Old Icelandic
PeriodChiefly 13th–14th centuries
RegionIceland; Norway
Notable examplesNjáls saga; Egils saga; Laxdæla saga; Völsunga saga

A saga is a prose narrative written in Old Norse, mostly in Iceland in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, recounting the history, feuds, voyages and heroes of the Norse-speaking world. The word derives from the Old Norse verb segja, "to say, to tell". Sagas range from sober-seeming chronicle to romance and myth; many preserve oral tradition about events of the Viking Age centuries before they were written down.

Genres

Scholars divide the sagas into groups: kings' sagas (konungasögur), biographies of Norwegian kings such as Snorri Sturluson's Heimskringla; family sagas or sagas of Icelanders (Íslendingasögur), the most renowned, dealing with settlers and their descendants in the generations after Iceland's settlement around 930; legendary sagas (fornaldarsögur), set in a remote, heroic past, including the Völsunga saga; contemporary sagas (samtíðarsögur), such as the Sturlunga compilation; and bishops' and saints' sagas, many of them translated or adapted from Latin.

Style and themes

Family sagas are famous for their laconic, understated prose, extensive genealogies, and focus on honour, feud, law and fate. Dialogue is sparse and pointed, understatement is pervasive, and the narrative voice is seemingly objective even when recounting killings and lawsuits. Great examples include Njáls saga (the longest and most admired), Egils saga (attributed to Snorri), Laxdæla saga and Gísla saga.

Legacy

Preserved in vellum manuscripts and later paper copies, the sagas became foundational to Icelandic national identity and influenced writers from William Morris and W. H. Auden to J. R. R. Tolkien and modern Scandinavian crime novelists. The term "saga" has since been extended in English to any long, multi-generational story.
