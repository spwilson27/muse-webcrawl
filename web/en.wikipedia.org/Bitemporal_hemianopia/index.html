Bitemporal hemianopia

Bitemporal hemianopia is the medical description of a type of partial blindness where vision is missing in the outer half of both the right and left visual field. It is usually associated with lesions of the optic chiasm, the area just below the brain where the two optic nerves cross. Because fibers from the nasal (inner) retina of each eye cross at the chiasm, damage there destroys the temporal (outer) visual fields of both eyes, producing a kind of "tunnel vision".

Causes

The most common cause is a pituitary adenoma compressing the optic chiasm from below. Other causes include craniopharyngioma, meningioma of the tuberculum sellae, aneurysm of the anterior communicating artery, and demyelinating or inflammatory disease. The defect typically develops gradually and may first be noticed as difficulty with peripheral vision or bumping into objects on both sides.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by visual field testing (perimetry), which shows loss of the temporal hemifields respecting the vertical midline. Magnetic resonance imaging of the sella and optic pathways is used to identify the causative lesion, and endocrine assessment is performed when a pituitary tumor is suspected.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause, most often surgical removal of the compressing tumor, sometimes combined with radiotherapy or medication. Visual field recovery can occur after decompression, especially when treated early.
