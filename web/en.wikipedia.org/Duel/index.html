Duel

A duel is an arranged engagement in combat between two people with matched weapons, in accordance with agreed-upon rules. During the 17th and 18th centuries (and earlier), duels were mostly single combats fought with swords (the rapier and later the small sword), but beginning in the late 18th century in England, duels were more commonly fought using pistols. Fencing and pistol duels continued to co-exist throughout the 19th century.

Codes and procedure

Duels were fought not to kill the opponent but to gain "satisfaction" for an insult — to restore the insulted party's honour by demonstrating willingness to risk one's life for it. Formal codes such as the Irish Code Duello (1777) governed the ritual: an apology was first sought; if refused, the parties exchanged challenges through trusted friends called seconds, who negotiated the time, place and weapons, measured distances, and attempted reconciliation at every stage. A surgeon often attended.

History

The duel grew out of the medieval judicial duel and the Renaissance culture of honour among gentlemen. It spread across Europe and the Americas despite repeated legal prohibitions and church condemnation. Famous fatal duels include Alexander Pushkin's death in 1837, Évariste Galois's in 1832, and the 1804 Burr–Hamilton duel in the United States, in which sitting Vice President Aaron Burr killed former Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton. The practice declined after the First World War as aristocratic honour culture faded and enforcement hardened; the last well-documented European pistol duels occurred in the mid-20th century, and a ceremonial sword duel was fought in France as late as 1967.
