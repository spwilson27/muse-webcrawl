Gelug

FounderJe Tsongkhapa (1357–1419)
FoundedEarly 15th century, Tibet
HeadThe Ganden Tripa; the Dalai Lamas are its best-known figures
Nickname"Yellow Hat" school

The Gelug (Tibetan: དགེ་ལུགས་, "virtuous order") is the newest and, historically, the dominant of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism. It was founded by Je Tsongkhapa (1357–1419), a renowned scholar and reformer, and its leading lineage holders include the Dalai Lamas. Gelug monks are recognisable by their yellow pandit hats, worn on ceremonial occasions.

Teachings and practice

The Gelug school emphasises rigorous scholastic study based on the great Indian treatises of Nālandā, combined with monastic discipline (vinaya) and the graded path to enlightenment (lamrim), as set out in Tsongkhapa's Lamrim Chenmo (Great Treatise on the Stages of the Path). Tsongkhapa's commentaries present a distinctive interpretation of Madhyamaka philosophy. Monks at the great monastic universities — Ganden, Sera and Drepung near Lhasa — pursue the Geshe degree through years of philosophical debate.

History

Tsongkhapa founded Ganden Monastery in 1409. In the 17th century the school attained political supremacy in Tibet when the Fifth Dalai Lama, Lozang Gyatso, with Mongol military support, unified the country in 1642; the Gelug establishment thereafter governed Tibet from the Potala Palace. Following the events of 1959 the school's leadership went into exile in India, where its monasteries were re-established in Karnataka, and Gelug centres later spread worldwide.
