Pond

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pond with emergent vegetation at its margins

A pond is a small, still body of water, natural or artificial, that is smaller than a lake. There is no universally agreed size limit: ponds are often defined by being shallow enough for light to reach the bottom across their whole area, allowing rooted plants to grow throughout, whereas in lakes a deep central zone receives no light. The distinction is ecological rather than legal, and usage varies by country and tradition.

Formation and types

Natural ponds form in depressions left by glaciers (kettle ponds), in floodplains as cut-off meanders, in volcanic craters, and as temporary pools after rain. Beaver activity creates ponds by damming streams. Humans dig ponds for livestock watering, irrigation, fish farming, fire protection, ornament (garden ponds), wildlife conservation and stormwater retention. Mill ponds and ice ponds were historically important in rural economies.

Ecology

Ponds support a rich food web: algae and macrophytes at the margins, invertebrates such as dragonfly larvae and water beetles, amphibians (frogs, newts), small fish, and visiting birds and mammals. Because they are shallow and warm quickly, ponds are highly productive but also prone to nutrient enrichment (eutrophication) and oxygen depletion. Small water bodies collectively make a disproportionately large contribution to regional biodiversity, and networks of ponds ("pondscapes") are important for species dispersal. Many temporary ponds dry out seasonally, which excludes fish and favours specialised invertebrates and amphibians.

Management

Over time, ponds fill with sediment and vegetation in a process of succession and may become marsh or wet woodland unless managed. Conservation work often involves periodically clearing vegetation, controlling nutrient inputs and maintaining a range of pond ages and depths across a landscape.
