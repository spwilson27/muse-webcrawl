Ultralight backpacking

An ultralight setup weighs every item to minimise base weight.

Ultralight backpacking is a style of lightweight backpacking that emphasizes carrying the lightest and simplest gear safely possible for a given trip. Practitioners quantify their kit by "base weight" — the weight of the pack and everything in it except consumables such as food, water, and fuel. A base weight under 10 pounds (4.5 kg) is conventionally "ultralight"; under 5 pounds is sometimes called "super ultralight".

Philosophy and methods

The approach was popularized by Ray Jardine's 1992 book PCT Hiker's Handbook (later Beyond Backpacking). Techniques include weighing every item, choosing multipurpose gear, using frameless packs, quilts instead of sleeping bags, tarp shelters, trail-running shoes instead of boots, and making or modifying equipment from lightweight materials such as Dyneema composite fabrics, titanium, and silnylon. The "big three" — pack, shelter, and sleep system — account for most weight savings.

Criticism

Critics note that ultralight gear can be expensive and less durable, and that extreme weight cutting must be balanced against safety margins appropriate to the conditions; advocates respond that lighter loads reduce fatigue and injury and allow longer daily distances.
