Pozzolana, also known as pozzolanic ash, is a natural siliceous or siliceous-and-aluminous material of volcanic origin. Finely divided and in the presence of water, it reacts with calcium hydroxide at ordinary temperatures to form compounds with cementitious properties. The name derives from Pozzuoli, near Naples, where deposits of volcanic ash were quarried in antiquity.

Roman use

The Romans mixed pozzolana with lime to produce a hydraulic concrete, opus caementicium, that could set underwater. This material was used in harbour works and monumental structures, most famously the dome of the Pantheon in Rome, still the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome.

Chemistry

The pozzolanic reaction consumes calcium hydroxide released by hydrating Portland cement and produces additional calcium silicate hydrate, the principal binding phase in concrete. This improves long-term strength and durability and reduces permeability.

Modern pozzolans

Modern pozzolans include natural volcanic ashes and industrial by-products such as fly ash, silica fume and ground granulated blast-furnace slag. They are widely used as supplementary cementitious materials to reduce the carbon footprint of cement production and improve concrete performance.
