Nicolaus Copernicus (Polish: Mikołaj Kopernik; 19 February 1473 – 24 May 1543) was a Renaissance polymath who formulated a model of the universe that placed the Sun rather than Earth at its center. His book De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres), published just before his death in 1543, is regarded as the starting point of modern astronomy and a defining episode in the Scientific Revolution.

Life

Copernicus was born in Toruń (Thorn), in the province of Royal Prussia in the Kingdom of Poland, into a wealthy merchant family. After his father's death he was raised by his uncle, Lucas Watzenrode the Younger, a prince-bishop of Warmia. He studied mathematics and astronomy at the University of Kraków, then law and medicine in Italy at Bologna, Padua and Ferrara, where he received a doctorate in canon law in 1503.

Returning to Warmia, Copernicus served as a canon of Frombork (Frauenburg) Cathedral while working as a physician, administrator, diplomat and economist. He formulated an early quantity theory of money and a version of what later became known as Gresham's law.

Heliocentric model

Working privately over several decades, Copernicus developed a heliocentric model in which the Earth and planets revolve around a stationary Sun near the center of the universe. An early summary, the Commentariolus, circulated in manuscript by about 1514. His great work retained circular orbits and epicycles but greatly simplified the explanation of retrograde motion. See also quantum mechanics for a much later revolution in physics, and the mathematics of his era.

Legacy

The "Copernican Revolution" shifted humanity's picture of its place in the cosmos. The chemical element copernicium (Cn, 112), the lunar crater Copernicus and the Martian crater Copernicus are named in his honor.
