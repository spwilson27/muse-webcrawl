Walter Bendix Schönflies Benjamin (July 15, 1892 – September 26, 1940) was a German Jewish philosopher, cultural critic, media theorist, and essayist. Associated with the Frankfurt School, he combined ideas from historical materialism, German idealism, Romanticism, and Jewish mysticism.

Life

Born into a prosperous assimilated Jewish family in Berlin, Benjamin studied philosophy at the universities of Berlin, Freiburg, Munich, and Bern, where he completed his dissertation on early German Romantic criticism in 1919. His habilitation thesis, The Origin of German Tragic Drama, was rejected by the University of Frankfurt, ending his hopes of an academic career. He worked as a freelance writer, critic, and translator, translating Baudelaire and Proust into German, and became close to Theodor W. Adorno, Max Horkheimer, Bertolt Brecht, and Gershom Scholem.

Exile and death

When the Nazis came to power in 1933, Benjamin fled to Paris. After the fall of France in 1940 he attempted to escape over the Pyrenees to Spain. Fearing capture and delivery to the Gestapo at Portbou, he died on the night of September 26, 1940, in circumstances generally understood to be suicide by morphine overdose.

Works

Benjamin's best-known essay, "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935), argues that technologies of reproduction dissolve the "aura" of the unique artwork, with profound political consequences. His unfinished magnum opus, The Arcades Project (Passagenwerk), is a sprawling study of nineteenth-century Paris. His final work, "On the Concept of History" (the "Theses"), contains the famous image of the Angel of History, blown backward into the future by the storm of progress.
