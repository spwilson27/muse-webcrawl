The history of Sardinia is shaped by the Mediterranean's second-largest island position between Italy, Iberia, North Africa and France. Colonised and fought over by Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Romans, Vandals, Byzantines, Pisans, Genoese, Aragonese and Savoyards, Sardinia nevertheless preserved distinctive languages and traditions, from its prehistoric nuraghi to the medieval giudicati.

Prehistory and antiquity

Neolithic cultures such as Ozieri were followed by the Nuragic civilisation, whose people raised some 7,000 surviving nuraghi — truncated-cone stone towers — and produced the bronze figurines (bronzetti) for which Sardinia is famous. Phoenician trading posts became Carthaginian cities after the 6th century BCE. Rome seized the island from Carthage in 238 BCE; it became a grain-supplying province, though the mountainous interior (the Barbagia) long resisted full control.

Medieval giudicati

After Vandal and Byzantine interludes, Arab raids cut Sardinia off from Constantinople, and by the 9th–10th centuries four independent kingdoms — the giudicati of Cagliari, Arborea, Torres and Gallura — governed the island under native judges. Pisan and Genoese influence grew; Arborea under Mariano IV and his daughter Eleanor (author of the law code Carta de Logu, 1392) nearly expelled the newcomers before Aragon completed its conquest by 1420.

Spanish, Savoyard and Italian Sardinia

Spanish rule lasted three centuries. By the treaties of Utrecht and London the island passed via Austria to the House of Savoy in 1720, whose mainland state took the name Kingdom of Sardinia; in 1861 that kingdom became the Kingdom of Italy. The "perfect fusion" of 1847 had already ended Sardinia's separate institutions. In 1948 the island became an autonomous region of the Italian Republic with a special statute, recognising its distinct identity and the Sardinian language.
