Government shutdowns in the United States


A closed national park entrance during the 2013 federal government shutdown.

In the United States, government shutdowns occur when funding legislation required to finance the federal government is not enacted into law before the next fiscal year begins. In a shutdown, the federal government curtails agency activities and services, ceases non-essential operations, furloughs non-essential workers, and retains only essential employees in departments covering the protection of life and property. Shutdowns have also occurred at the state, territorial, and local levels of government.

Since 1976, when the current budget and appropriations process was enacted, there have been 22 gaps in budget funding, eight of which led to federal employees being furloughed. Before 1980, funding gaps did not lead to government shutdowns, until Attorney General Benjamin Civiletti issued a legal opinion that a funding gap was a violation of the Antideficiency Act. This opinion was not consistently applied until the shutdown of 1981, the first to involve furloughs.

Mechanism

Under the separation of powers in the U.S. Constitution, Congress controls appropriations. If Congress fails to pass the twelve annual appropriations bills (or a continuing resolution extending funding) and no funding has been enacted for an agency, that agency must suspend normal operations — an interpretation grounded in the Antideficiency Act, which prohibits federal agencies from spending or obligating funds in advance of appropriations. Employees performing "excepted" functions (such as law enforcement, emergency medicine, air traffic control, and national security) continue working without immediate pay, while "non-excepted" employees are furloughed; under the Government Employee Fair Treatment Act of 2019, federal employees receive retroactive pay when the shutdown ends. Mandatory spending programmes (Social Security, Medicare, interest on the debt) continue because they do not depend on annual appropriations.

Notable shutdowns

The longest shutdowns include: the 1995–1996 shutdowns under President Bill Clinton (a total of 26 days, arising from disputes with the Republican Congress over the budget); the 16-day shutdown of October 2013, when efforts to defund or delay the Affordable Care Act stalled funding legislation; and the 35-day shutdown of 2018–2019 — the longest in U.S. history — over funding for a U.S.–Mexico border wall, which ended with a temporary funding bill without the wall appropriation after disruptions including staffing shortages at airports. Earlier, brief shutdowns occurred repeatedly in the late 1970s and 1980s.

Effects

Shutdowns close national parks and museums, delay passport and visa processing, loan approvals, and regulatory activity, and reduce economic output; the Congressional Budget Office estimated that the 2018–2019 shutdown cost the U.S. economy about $11 billion in lost GDP, $3 billion of it permanently. Shutdowns also shake public and market confidence and damage the morale of the federal workforce. State and local governments experience their own shutdowns, such as Minnesota (2005, 2011) and New Jersey (2006, 2017), when state budgets are not enacted on time.
