Walrasian auction

A Walrasian auction, introduced by Léon Walras, is a type of simultaneous auction where each agent calculates their demand for a good at every possible price and submits this demand schedule to an auctioneer. The auctioneer then iteratively adjusts prices — raising the price of goods in excess demand and lowering the price of goods in excess supply — until a set of prices is found at which the market clears. Only then are trades executed, so that no exchanges take place at non-equilibrium prices. The price-adjustment process is known as tâtonnement (French for "groping").

The tâtonnement process

The auctioneer calls out a vector of prices for all goods. Each agent responds with the quantities they wish to buy or sell at those prices. If, for some good, aggregate demand exceeds aggregate supply, the auctioneer raises its price; if supply exceeds demand, the price is lowered. The process repeats until demand equals supply for every good simultaneously, at which point the announced allocations are carried out. In Walras's original formulation, no "false trading" occurs during the adjustment process.

Role in economic theory

The Walrasian auction provides the standard narrative mechanism behind Walras's law and the general equilibrium theory developed later by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu. Questions about the stability and uniqueness of the equilibrium reached by tâtonnement motivated extensive research: the Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu results showed that aggregate excess demand functions can take almost any form consistent with Walras's law, meaning that convergence of the simple price-adjustment process cannot be guaranteed in general. Variants of the mechanism appear in modern market design, including double auctions and some electricity and spectrum markets where an auctioneer-like coordinator computes clearing prices.
