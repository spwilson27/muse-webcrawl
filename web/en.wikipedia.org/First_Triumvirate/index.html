The First Triumvirate was an informal political alliance among three prominent politicians in the late Roman Republic: Gaius Julius Caesar, Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus (Pompey the Great) and Marcus Licinius Crassus. Formed around 60 BC, it had no official legal status — unlike the later Second Triumvirate — but for several years the combined wealth, armies and popularity of the three men dominated Roman politics against the resistance of the senatorial optimates.

Formation

Each partner had been frustrated by the Senate: Pompey's veterans awaited land grants and his eastern settlement awaited ratification; Crassus's equestrian allies sought relief on their Asian tax contracts; Caesar, returning from Spain, wanted a triumph and the consulship. Caesar reconciled the long-feuding Pompey and Crassus and sealed the bargain by marrying his daughter Julia to Pompey in 59 BC.

Domination and renewal

The alliance carried Caesar's consulship of 59 BC, in which he passed the partners' measures amid obstruction and violence, and secured himself the five-year proconsular command of Gaul from which he conquered the whole region (58–50 BC). The triumvirs renewed their compact at the Conference of Luca in 56 BC, arranging consulships for Pompey and Crassus (55 BC) and great commands: Spain for Pompey, Syria for Crassus.

Collapse

Julia's death in 54 BC severed the family bond; Crassus's death at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC destroyed the balance. Pompey drifted toward the Senate, and by 50 BC the rivalry between Pompey and Caesar had become irreconcilable, leading directly to Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon in January 49 BC and the civil war that destroyed the Republic.
