Rubidium is a chemical element; it has symbol Rb and atomic number 37. It is a very soft, whitish-grey solid in the alkali metal group, similar to potassium and caesium. Rubidium is the first alkali metal in the group to have a density higher than water, and it reacts violently with water.

Discovery

Rubidium was discovered in 1861 by Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff in Heidelberg, Germany, using their newly developed technique of flame spectroscopy. Its name comes from the Latin rubidus, meaning deep red, the colour of its emission spectrum lines.

Properties

Rubidium metal is soft enough to be cut with a knife and melts just above body temperature. Like other alkali metals it ignites spontaneously in air and must be stored under mineral oil or an inert atmosphere. Natural rubidium consists of two isotopes, stable 85Rb and the slightly radioactive 87Rb, which has a half-life of about 49 billion years and is used in rubidium–strontium dating of rocks.

Applications

Rubidium compounds are used in research and development, particularly in atomic clocks: rubidium frequency standards are widely used in telecommunications and satellite navigation. Rubidium vapour cells feature in magnetometers, lasers and studies of Bose–Einstein condensates.
