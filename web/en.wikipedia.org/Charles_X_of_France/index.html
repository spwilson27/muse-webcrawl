Charles X (born Charles Philippe, Count of Artois; 9 October 1757 – 6 November 1836) was King of France from 1824 until his abdication in the July Revolution of 1830. The younger brother of Louis XVI and Louis XVIII, he led the ultra-royalist faction in exile and on the throne pursued a deeply conservative, clerical program that sought to roll back the legacy of the French Revolution, ultimately provoking his overthrow.

Early life and exile

As Count of Artois, Charles was notorious for his extravagance and reactionary politics. He fled France days after the storming of the Bastille in 1789, becoming a leader of the émigrés, intriguing from Turin, Koblenz, and Edinburgh for foreign intervention to restore the old order. He returned only with the Bourbon Restoration of 1814–1815, heading the ultras who wanted "to learn nothing and forget nothing."

Reign

Succeeding Louis XVIII in 1824, Charles was crowned at Reims with full medieval ceremony. His ministry under Villèle compensated former émigrés for confiscated lands (the "milliard des émigrés"), passed an Anti-Sacrilege Act prescribing death for profanation of consecrated hosts, and increased Church influence. The conquest of Algiers in 1830 was meant to bolster his popularity. In March 1830, after the Chamber of Deputies declared no confidence in his new Polignac ministry, Charles dissolved it; when fresh elections strengthened the opposition, he issued the July Ordinances on 25 July 1830, suspending press freedom, dissolving the newly elected chamber, and altering the electoral law.

July Revolution and death

Paris rose in the Trois Glorieuses (27–29 July). Charles, at Saint-Cloud, abdicated on 2 August in favor of his grandson the Count of Chambord, but the Chamber instead proclaimed Louis Philippe king of the French. Charles went into exile at Holyrood, then Prague and Gorizia, where he died of cholera in 1836 — the last Bourbon of the elder line to reign in France.
