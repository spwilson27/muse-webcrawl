Consumer Price Index

The CPI tracks changes in the price of a basket of consumer goods and services.

A consumer price index (CPI) measures the overall change in consumer prices based on a representative basket of goods and services over time. It is one of several price indices calculated by most national statistical agencies and is the most widely used measure of inflation. The annual percentage change in a CPI is used as a measure of the inflation rate, and CPIs are used to index wages, pensions, social security payments and regulated prices.

Calculation

The index is computed by collecting prices for a fixed basket of items — food, housing, clothing, transport, healthcare, recreation and others — weighted according to their share of typical household expenditure, as determined by consumer expenditure surveys. In the United States, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the CPI-U (for all urban consumers) and the CPI-W; a chained variant, the C-CPI-U, accounts for consumer substitution between goods.

Uses and limitations

Critics note substitution bias, quality changes, and the arrival of new goods as sources of error in the cost-of-living measurement; the 1996 Boskin Commission estimated that the US CPI overstated inflation by about 1.1 percentage points per year at that time. Housing treatment also differs between countries: the US uses owners' equivalent rent, while some other indices exclude owner-occupied housing costs.
