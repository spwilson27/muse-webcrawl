Assortative mating

Assortative mating (also referred to as positive assortative mating or homogamy) is a mating pattern and a form of sexual selection in which individuals with similar phenotypes or genotypes mate with one another more frequently than would be expected under a random mating pattern. The opposite is disassortative mating.

In evolutionary biology

Assortative mating increases genetic relatedness within families and can increase homozygosity, altering genotype frequencies without changing allele frequencies. When assortment is based on a heritable trait, it can strengthen trait variance in a population and, combined with reproductive isolation, contribute to sympatric speciation. Examples occur in many animals, including size-assortative mating in some fish and amphibians.

In humans

Human mating is strongly assortative for traits such as educational attainment, height, intelligence test scores, religion, and political attitudes. Educational assortative mating has been studied for its effects on household income inequality. Assortment also matters in medical genetics, where mating patterns can affect the distribution of heritable disorders.
