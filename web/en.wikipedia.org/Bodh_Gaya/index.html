Bodh Gaya is a religious site and place of pilgrimage associated with the Mahabodhi Temple complex, situated in the Gaya district in the Indian state of Bihar. It is famous for being the place where Gautama Buddha is said to have attained enlightenment (Bodhi) under what became known as the Bodhi Tree. For Buddhists, Bodh Gaya is the most important of the four main pilgrimage sites related to the life of Gautama Buddha, the other three being Kushinagar, Lumbini, and Sarnath.

History

According to tradition, Siddhartha Gautama, after years of ascetic practice, sat in meditation beneath a sacred fig tree at Uruvela on the banks of the Phalgu (Lilajan) River and resolved not to rise until he had attained awakening, which occurred during the night of the full moon. In the 3rd century BCE the emperor Ashoka visited the site and is credited with founding the first temple and the Vajrasana (Diamond Throne) marking the exact spot. The present Mahabodhi Temple, a soaring pyramidal stone structure about 55 metres high, dates mainly to the Gupta period (5th–6th century CE), with later restorations, including extensive 19th-century work in which Alexander Cunningham and the Burmese missions participated.

The site today

A descendant of the original Bodhi Tree grows beside the temple, and monasteries built by Buddhist communities from Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan, Thailand, Japan, Sri Lanka, Vietnam, and elsewhere surround the complex, each in its national style. The town hosts the annual Buddha Mahotsav and draws pilgrims throughout the winter season. In July 2013 a series of small bombs exploded at the complex, injuring several people; security was subsequently tightened.
