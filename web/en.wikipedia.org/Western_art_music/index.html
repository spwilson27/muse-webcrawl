Western art music (classical music)

Western art music, generally referred to as classical music, is the tradition of composed music originating in Western culture, rooted in the liturgical and secular music of Europe from roughly the 9th century onward. The term "classical music" first appeared in the early 19th century, in an attempt to canonize the period from Johann Sebastian Bach to Ludwig van Beethoven as a golden age.

Characteristics

Western art music is distinguished by its use of staff notation, developed from the 11th century, which allows complex works to be composed, preserved, and reproduced by performers. It encompasses a wide range of forms — symphony, concerto, sonata, opera, string quartet, and song — and places emphasis on the individual composer's authored work, performed by trained musicians on instruments such as the piano, violin, and the modern orchestra.

Periods

Histories conventionally divide the tradition into the Medieval period (to c. 1400), the Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), the Baroque (c. 1600–1750), the Classical period (c. 1750–1820), the Romantic era (c. 1800–1910), and the modern and contemporary periods of the 20th and 21st centuries. Major figures include Hildegard von Bingen, Guillaume de Machaut, Josquin des Prez, Claudio Monteverdi, Bach, Handel, Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Chopin, Wagner, Brahms, Mahler, Stravinsky, and Schoenberg.

Terminology

Scholars sometimes prefer "Western art music" to "classical music" to describe the entire tradition, reserving "Classical" for the period of Haydn and Mozart, and to avoid confusion with the classical traditions of other cultures, such as Indian or Chinese classical music.
