Backdoor (computing)

A backdoor is a method, often secret, of bypassing normal authentication or encryption in a computer system, product or embedded device, in order to gain access without going through the usual security checks.

Origins and purposes

Backdoors may be built in by developers for legitimate purposes — maintenance access, troubleshooting or password recovery — but they become vulnerabilities if discovered by others. Ken Thompson's famous 1984 Turing Award lecture, "Reflections on Trusting Trust", showed how a compiler could be subverted to insert a backdoor into a login program in a way invisible in the source code, illustrating how hard planted backdoors can be to detect.

Malicious and state-sponsored backdoors

Malware frequently installs backdoors (a class of trojan known as a backdoor trojan or, with remote control, a RAT) to give attackers persistent access. Hardware and software backdoors allegedly planted or demanded by intelligence agencies are a recurring controversy: examples debated in public include the Clipper chip proposal of the 1990s and weaknesses in the Dual_EC_DRBG random number generator standard. Because any built-in access mechanism can be abused by whoever learns of it, security experts generally argue that deliberately weakened systems cannot be kept safe "for the good guys only".
