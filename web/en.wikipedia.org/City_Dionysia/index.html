City Dionysia

The Theatre of Dionysus in Athens, home of the City Dionysia's dramatic contests.

The City Dionysia (Greek: Διονύσια τὰ ἐν ἄστει, Dionysia ta en astei), also called the Great Dionysia, was an ancient Athenian festival held in honour of Dionysus, the god of wine and theatre. Instituted by the tyrant Pisistratus in the sixth century BC, it became the principal civic festival of democratic Athens, at which tragedy, satyr play and comedy were performed in competition.

Held each spring in the month of Elaphebolion (March/April), the festival opened with a procession carrying the wooden cult image of Dionysus Eleuthereus from the temple outside the city into the Theatre of Dionysus. The days that followed were devoted to dramatic performances: three tragedians each presented a tetralogy of three tragedies and a satyr play, competing for the prize of a wreath; five comedies competed separately. The city's generals presided, and the chorus and costumes were funded by wealthy citizens performing the liturgy of the choregia.

Historical significance

The surviving plays of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides were premiered at the City Dionysia, making the festival the cradle of Western drama. A famous episode occurred in 415 BC, when Aristophanes won first prize with The Birds and Athenian attention was riveted on preparations for the disastrous Sicilian expedition.
