History of the Jews in Poland

The history of the Jews in Poland dates back more than a thousand years. For centuries Poland, and later the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, was home to the largest and most vibrant Jewish community in the world. Poland became a principal centre of Ashkenazi Jewish culture, learning and religious life, and Polish Jewry produced major movements including Hasidism. The community was nearly annihilated in the Holocaust: of roughly 3.3 million Polish Jews in 1939, about 90 per cent were murdered.

Early settlement and privileges

Jewish merchants and travellers reached Polish lands by the 10th–11th centuries, and settlement grew as Jews fled persecution in Western Europe during the Crusades. In 1264 Bolesław the Pious issued the Statute of Kalisz, granting Jews legal protection, freedom of worship and commercial rights; Casimir the Great extended and confirmed these privileges in the 14th century. Poland's rulers valued Jewish skills in trade, finance and estate management.

The Commonwealth: golden age

From the 16th to the mid-17th century the Jewish community of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth flourished. The Council of Four Lands (c. 1580–1764) coordinated Jewish self-government across the Commonwealth. Great yeshivas and scholars such as Moses Isserles of Kraków made Poland the intellectual heart of Ashkenazi Jewry. Jews were prominent as merchants, artisans and leaseholders (arendators) on noble estates. Estimates suggest that by the mid-17th century the Commonwealth held one of the largest Jewish populations in the world.

Decline and partitions

The Khmelnytsky Uprising of 1648–49 brought massacres of Jews in Ukraine and began a period of crisis, followed by wars that devastated the Commonwealth. In the 18th century Hasidism arose in the south-east under the Baal Shem Tov, while the Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment) later spread from the west. The partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795) divided Polish Jewry among Russia, Prussia and Austria; most fell under the Russian Pale of Settlement, subject to discrimination and restrictions.

The 19th century

Under partition, Jews faced conscription, residence restrictions and periodic violence, though opportunities differed: Galicia under Austria and Congress Poland saw growing urbanisation and industry, with Warsaw, Łódź and Kraków developing large communities. Jews played key roles in textiles, banking and the professions. Political movements flourished: Zionism, the socialist Bund (founded 1897) and diaspora nationalism. Emigration, mainly to the United States, drew away over a million Jews by 1914.

Interwar Poland

The reborn Polish Republic of 1918 contained about three million Jews — roughly 10 per cent of the population, and more than a third of Warsaw's residents. Jewish life was extraordinarily diverse: Yiddish and Hebrew schools and press, theatres, political parties from Agudath Israel to the Bund, and major centres of Orthodox and secular learning such as the Chachmei Lublin Yeshiva. Jews were central to commerce and industry but suffered from the Depression, discriminatory policies and rising antisemitism in the 1930s.

The Holocaust

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939. Jews were herded into ghettos — Warsaw's held over 400,000 people — and from 1941–42 murdered in mass shootings and extermination camps, nearly all of which (Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Bełżec, Sobibór, Chełmno, Majdanek) were located on occupied Polish soil. The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943 was the largest Jewish revolt of the war. About three million Polish Jews were killed; only a few hundred thousand survived, many in the Soviet interior.

Postwar and today

Survivors returning home sometimes met violence, as in the Kielce pogrom of 1946. Most surviving Jews emigrated, in waves after 1945, 1956 and the antisemitic campaign of 1968, which drove out most of those who remained. After 1989, Jewish life in Poland revived modestly, with festivals in Kraków's Kazimierz district and the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews, opened in Warsaw in 2013, which commemorates the thousand-year story of the community.
