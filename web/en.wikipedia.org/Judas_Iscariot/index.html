Judas Iscariot

Judas Iscariot (died c. 30–33 AD) was, according to Christian tradition and the canonical Gospels, one of the original Twelve Apostles of Jesus Christ. Judas betrayed Jesus to the Sanhedrin in the Garden of Gethsemane by kissing him and addressing him as "rabbi" to reveal his identity to the crowd who came to arrest him.

Betrayal and death

The Gospels relate that Judas agreed to hand Jesus over for thirty pieces of silver. At the Last Supper Jesus predicted his betrayal by one of the apostles present. After the arrest, Judas was remorseful: Matthew's Gospel says he returned the silver and hanged himself; the Acts of the Apostles instead describes him falling headlong in a field he bought with the reward. His place among the Twelve was filled by Matthias.

Legacy

Judas's name became synonymous with betrayal (a "Judas") and a traitor's kiss a "Judas kiss." His motives have been debated for centuries — greed, disillusionment, or a misguided attempt to force Jesus's hand are among the explanations offered. The 2nd-century Gnostic Gospel of Judas, published in 2006, portrays Judas uniquely as Jesus's closest disciple, acting at Jesus's own request. In Dante's Inferno, Judas is chewed eternally in the mouth of Satan at the bottom of Hell, alongside Brutus and Cassius.
