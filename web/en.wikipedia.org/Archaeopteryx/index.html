Archaeopteryx (from Ancient Greek ἀρχαῖος arkhaîos, "ancient", and πτέρυξ pteryx, "feather, wing"), sometimes referred to by its German name Urvogel ("original bird"), is a genus of bird-like dinosaurs. The name derives from the ancient Greek for "ancient wing". Between the late 19th century and the early 21st century, Archaeopteryx had been generally accepted by palaeontologists and popular reference books as the oldest known bird (member of the group Avialae). Older potential avialans, such as Anchiornis from China, have since been identified, but Archaeopteryx remains the iconic transitional fossil between non-avian dinosaurs and birds.

Discovery

All specimens come from the Solnhofen limestone of Bavaria, Germany, deposited about 150 million years ago in a tropical lagoon. A single feather was described in 1861, followed the same year by the "London specimen" — the first skeleton, announced just two years after Darwin's On the Origin of Species and immediately recognised as a link between reptiles and birds. The celebrated "Berlin specimen" (1877) preserves a complete skeleton with feather impressions. Around a dozen skeletal specimens are now known, variously assigned to A. lithographica, A. siemensii, and A. albersdoerferi.

Description

Archaeopteryx was roughly the size of a raven, up to about 0.5 m long. It had fully developed flight feathers forming wings, but retained unmistakably dinosaurian features: jaws with sharp teeth, three clawed fingers on each hand, a long bony tail, and a flat sternum without the deep keel of modern birds. Studies of its feathers, brain, and inner ear suggest it was capable of some form of flight, though probably weaker and different in style from that of modern birds; whether it flew actively, glided, or combined both, and whether flight evolved "from the ground up" or "from the trees down", remain debated.

Significance

For more than a century Archaeopteryx was the principal fossil evidence for the dinosaurian origin of birds, a hypothesis championed by Thomas Henry Huxley. Discoveries of feathered dinosaurs in China from the 1990s onwards placed it within a rich spectrum of paravian dinosaurs, refining rather than removing its importance: it is among the earliest and most completely known avialans and a cornerstone taxon in analyses of early bird evolution.
