Curve fitting

Curve fitting is the process of constructing a curve, or mathematical function, that has the best fit to a series of data points, possibly subject to constraints. Curve fitting can involve either interpolation, where an exact fit to the data is required, or smoothing, in which a "smooth" function is constructed that approximately fits the data. A related topic is regression analysis, which focuses more on questions of statistical inference such as how much uncertainty is present in a curve that is fit to data observed with random errors.

Background

Fitted curves can be used as an aid for data visualization, to infer values of a function where no data are available, and to summarize the relationships among two or more variables. Extrapolation refers to the use of a fitted curve beyond the range of the observed data, and is subject to a greater degree of uncertainty since it may reflect the method used to construct the curve as much as it reflects the observed data.

Least squares

The most common method of fitting is the method of least squares, in which the parameters of the model are chosen to minimize the sum of the squares of the residuals — the differences between observed and fitted values. For a straight line this problem has a closed-form solution; for nonlinear models, iterative numerical optimization is typically required.

Fitting types

Algebraic curves such as circles, ellipses, and parabolas can be fitted to data, as can parametric curves and surfaces. Polynomial fitting uses polynomials of increasing degree, though high-degree polynomials can oscillate wildly between points (Runge's phenomenon). Spline fitting uses piecewise low-degree polynomials joined smoothly at knots.
