Landscape ecology

Landscape ecology is the science of studying and improving the relationships between ecological processes and the spatial pattern of the environment across large areas and multiple scales. It examines how the composition and arrangement of landscape elements — patches, corridors, and the surrounding matrix — influence organisms, the flow of energy and materials, and disturbances such as fire and disease.

History

The term "landscape ecology" (German: Landschaftsökologie) was coined by geographer Carl Troll in 1939, inspired by aerial photography. The field grew rapidly in the 1980s with the formation of the International Association for Landscape Ecology (1982), the journal Landscape Ecology (1987), and the influential patch–corridor–matrix model of Richard Forman and Michel Godron. Advances in remote sensing and geographic information systems made quantitative analysis of large landscapes routine.

Core concepts

Key ideas include scale (pattern depends on the grain and extent of observation), habitat fragmentation and connectivity, edge effects where two habitats meet, metapopulations linked by dispersal, and landscape metrics that quantify pattern. Landscape ecology informs conservation planning (for example, designing wildlife corridors), land-use planning, restoration, and the management of fire and invasive species. Unlike traditional ecology's focus on homogeneous study plots, it treats spatial heterogeneity itself as a driver of ecological dynamics.
