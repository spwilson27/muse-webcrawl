Microfossil

Microfossil
SizeTypically less than 1 mm
Studied byMicropaleontology
ExamplesForaminifera, radiolaria, diatoms, conodonts, pollen

A microfossil is a fossil that is generally between 0.001 mm and 1 mm in size, whose study requires the use of light or electron microscopy. Microfossils are abundant and widespread in sedimentary rocks and are among the most important tools for dating and correlating strata, a field known as micropaleontology.

Types

Calcareous microfossils include foraminifera and coccolithophores; siliceous ones include radiolaria and diatoms; phosphatic microfossils include conodonts, once-mysterious tooth-like elements now attributed to early vertebrates. Organic-walled microfossils, or palynomorphs, include pollen, spores and dinoflagellate cysts. Microfossils also encompass fragments of larger organisms, bacteria, and the tests and shells of microscopic animals.

Applications

Because microfossils are small, numerous and evolve rapidly, they are ideal index fossils. The petroleum industry uses them extensively in biostratigraphy to date and correlate rock layers encountered in drilling. Palynology — the study of fossil pollen and spores — reconstructs past climates and vegetation, while assemblages of foraminifera record ancient ocean temperatures and chemistry, often via oxygen isotopes in their shells.

Distribution

Microfossils occur from the Precambrian to the present. Cherts preserve ancient microbial communities, and microfossil evidence features in debates about the earliest life on Earth, including putative filamentous fossils in rocks over 3.4 billion years old.
