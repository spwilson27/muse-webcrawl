Heat index

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The heat index (also called the apparent temperature, or humidex in Canada) is a measure of how hot it really feels when relative humidity is factored in with the actual air temperature. The human body cools itself by sweating; evaporation of sweat becomes less effective as humidity rises, so humid conditions feel hotter than the thermometer reading alone suggests.

History

The concept was developed by Robert G. Steadman in a 1979 paper that produced a table of apparent temperatures based on a model of human physiology and clothing. The United States National Weather Service adopted a regression formula, derived from Steadman's table by Lans P. Rothfusz, to compute the heat index from temperature and dew point or relative humidity.

Limitations

The heat index assumes shade and light wind; full sunshine can raise the apparent temperature substantially. It also does not directly account for individual differences in age, health or acclimatisation. Related measures include the wet-bulb temperature, the humidex used in Canada, and the Australian apparent temperature.
