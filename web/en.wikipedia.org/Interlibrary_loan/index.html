Interlibrary loan

Interlibrary loan (abbreviated ILL, and sometimes called interloan, document delivery, or resource sharing) is a service that enables patrons of one library to borrow materials owned by another library. A user submits a request at their home library, which, acting on behalf of the requester, sends a message to other libraries to locate and supply the item, either physically or as a scanned copy.

Operation

Modern ILL relies on union catalogues such as WorldCat to locate holdings, and on request-management systems and protocols (such as ISO 10160/10161 and NCIP) to route and track requests. Consortia often run courier networks that move large volumes of material regionally; digital delivery of journal articles is now the fastest-growing part of the service. Copyright guidelines restrict how much may be copied and how many articles a library may borrow from a single title per year.

History

Lending between libraries dates to the early twentieth century in the United States, where the American Library Association adopted the first interlibrary loan code in 1917. The practice expanded greatly with union catalogues and photocopying, and it remains essential for research collections that cannot hold everything locally.
