Four arts

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The four arts (Chinese: 四藝, sìyì), also called the four accomplishments, were the four scholarly pursuits that the cultivated Chinese scholar-gentleman of imperial China was expected to master: qín (the guqin, music), qí (the strategy board game weiqi, known in the West as Go), shū (Chinese calligraphy), and huà (Chinese painting). Together they are often summarized as qínqíshūhuà (琴棋書畫).

The four arts

Qín (琴) — music

The qin here is the guqin, a seven-stringed zither of great antiquity, associated with Confucius and the literati. Its quiet, refined playing was considered a discipline of self-cultivation rather than public performance.

Qí (棋) — the board game

Qi refers to weiqi (圍棋), the encirclement game played with black and white stones on a 19×19 board, known in Japanese as Go (碁) and in Korean as baduk. It was prized as a training in strategy and temperament.

Shū (書) — calligraphy

Calligraphy, the art of writing Chinese characters with brush and ink, was ranked among the highest arts; a person's handwriting was taken to reveal character and cultivation.

Huà (畫) — painting

Painting refers chiefly to ink wash painting (shuimo), especially landscape (shanshui) painting executed with the same brush techniques as calligraphy.

Cultural significance

Mastery of the four arts defined the ideal of the scholar-official class from at least the Song dynasty onward, and depictions of the four arts — or of their instruments — became a standard motif in Chinese art, porcelain and garden design. The concept parallels other sets of literati accomplishments and remains a common shorthand for traditional Chinese high culture.
