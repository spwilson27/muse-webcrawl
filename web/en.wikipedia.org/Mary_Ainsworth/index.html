Mary Ainsworth

Mary Dinsmore Salter Ainsworth (December 1, 1913 – March 21, 1999) was an American-Canadian developmental psychologist known for her work in the theory of attachment, and for devising the Strange Situation procedure to study attachment security in children. Working with John Bowlby in London in the 1950s and later at Johns Hopkins University, she provided the empirical foundations of attachment theory.

Life and research

Ainsworth was born in Glendale, Ohio, raised in Toronto, and earned her doctorate at the University of Toronto in 1939, influenced by William Blatz's security theory. After wartime service in the Canadian Women's Army Corps, she joined Bowlby's research unit at the Tavistock Clinic in 1950, studying maternal separation. She then conducted naturalistic observations of mother–infant interaction in Uganda (1954–55), published as Infancy in Uganda (1967), and a long-term longitudinal study in Baltimore.

The Strange Situation

In the Strange Situation (developed in the 1960s–70s and reported in Patterns of Attachment, 1978, with colleagues), a child aged about 12–18 months is observed through episodes of separation from and reunion with its caregiver in an unfamiliar room. Ainsworth identified three attachment patterns — secure, insecure-avoidant and insecure-ambivalent/resistant — linked to the caregiver's sensitive responsiveness; Mary Main later added a fourth, disorganized pattern. The procedure became one of the most widely used and debated methods in developmental psychology.
