The Académie Française is France's pre-eminent learned body for matters of the French language. Founded in 1635 by Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister to Louis XIII, it comprises forty members elected for life and known as les immortels (the Immortals), each occupying a numbered seat (fauteuil).

Role

The Academy's statutory mission is to give "definite rules to our language" — to compile the official Dictionnaire de l'Académie française, first published in 1694 and now in its ninth edition, and to rule on grammar, orthography and neologisms. Its pronouncements are advisory rather than legally binding, but they carry great cultural weight in France and the francophone world.

Membership and ceremonies

New members are elected by the existing body when a seat falls vacant; candidates traditionally refrain from openly campaigning. Immortals wear the habit vert, a green embroidered uniform with a ceremonial sword, at formal sessions under the dome of the Institut de France. Members have included Racine, Voltaire, Victor Hugo, Louis Pasteur and, as the first woman elected (1980), Marguerite Yourcenar.
