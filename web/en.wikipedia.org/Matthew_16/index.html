Matthew 16

Matthew 16 is the sixteenth chapter of the Gospel of Matthew in the New Testament of the Christian Bible. It contains some of the most theologically consequential passages in the Gospel, including Peter's confession of Jesus as the Christ at Caesarea Philippi, the saying about the rock and the keys of the kingdom, and the first explicit prediction of Jesus's suffering, death, and resurrection.

Contents

The demand for a sign (verses 1–4). Pharisees and Sadducees ask Jesus for a sign from heaven; he refuses, offering only "the sign of Jonah," and remarks on their ability to read the weather but not "the signs of the times."

The leaven of the Pharisees (verses 5–12). Jesus warns the disciples against the "leaven" of the Pharisees and Sadducees, which they eventually understand to mean their teaching.

Peter's confession (verses 13–20). At Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks who people say he is, then who the disciples say he is. Simon Peter answers, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God." Jesus blesses Peter, declaring: "You are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not overcome it. I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven; whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven."

Prediction of the Passion and rebuke of Peter (verses 21–23). Jesus foretells his suffering in Jerusalem; Peter rebukes him and is himself rebuked with the words "Get behind me, Satan!"

The cost of discipleship (verses 24–28). Jesus teaches that whoever would follow him must deny himself and take up his cross, asking, "What will it profit a man if he gains the whole world and forfeits his soul?" The chapter closes with a promise that some standing there will not taste death before seeing the Son of Man coming in his kingdom — often linked to the Transfiguration narrated in the next chapter.

Interpretation

Verse 18 is among the most debated texts in Christian history: Roman Catholic teaching understands the "rock" as Peter himself and grounds the papacy in this passage, while many Protestant interpreters take the rock to be Peter's confession of faith. The "keys of the kingdom" (v. 19) underpin Catholic doctrines of the sacrament of penance and papal authority, and Peter is traditionally depicted in Christian art holding keys.
