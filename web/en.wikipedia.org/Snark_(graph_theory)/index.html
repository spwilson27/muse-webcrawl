Snark (graph theory)


The Petersen graph, the smallest snark

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a snark is an undirected graph with three vertices per node-connected structure in which the graph is connected, bridgeless, and cubic (every vertex has exactly three edges), and whose edges cannot be properly coloured with only three colours. Equivalently, by the four colour theorem, a snark is a bridgeless cubic graph that is not planar in a way that would permit a 3-edge-colouring; snarks form the minimal counterexamples in several conjectures.

Definition and naming

Definitions vary slightly among authors but generally require the graph to be connected, bridgeless, cubic, and class II (chromatic index 4), often with additional conditions excluding trivial cases, such as girth at least 5 and non-trivial connectivity. The name was introduced by Martin Gardner in 1976, after the elusive creature in Lewis Carroll's poem The Hunting of the Snark.

Examples and history

The smallest snark is the Petersen graph (10 vertices), discovered in connection with Julius Petersen's work in 1898. Others include the Blanuša snarks (18 vertices), the Descartes snark, the Szekeres snark (50 vertices), and the flower snarks. P. G. Tait showed in 1880 that the four colour theorem is equivalent to the statement that no snark is planar. Snarks are central to conjectures such as the cycle double cover conjecture and the Petersen colouring conjecture; the Tutte 5-flow conjecture would imply that no snark admits a nowhere-zero 5-flow.
