The Roman–Persian wars, also called the Roman–Iranian wars, were a series of conflicts between states of the Greco-Roman world and two successive Iranian empires, the Parthian and the Sasanian, lasting from 54 BC to 628 AD. Fought mainly over the contested borderlands of Armenia, Mesopotamia, and the Caucasus, they form one of the longest-running rivalries in ancient history, spanning nearly seven centuries.

Roman–Parthian wars

Hostilities began when Marcus Licinius Crassus invaded Mesopotamia and was decisively defeated and killed by the Parthian general Surena at the Battle of Carrhae in 53 BC, losing the legions' eagle standards. Mark Antony's invasion of Media in 36 BC also failed. Under Augustus, diplomacy recovered the captured standards in 20 BC. Thereafter the two empires clashed repeatedly over the throne of Armenia. Trajan's campaigns (114–117 AD) briefly annexed Mesopotamia and captured the Parthian capital Ctesiphon, but his successor Hadrian abandoned the conquests. Later wars under Lucius Verus and Septimius Severus again saw Ctesiphon sacked, and northern Mesopotamia was annexed by Rome.

Roman–Sasanian wars

In 224 AD the Parthian Empire was overthrown by Ardashir I, founder of the more centralized and aggressive Sasanian Empire. The Sasanians launched repeated invasions of Roman Syria and Anatolia; Shapur I defeated and captured the emperor Valerian at Edessa in 260 AD — depicted triumphantly in Persian rock reliefs at Naqsh-e Rostam. Rome recovered under the soldier-emperors and Diocletian, whose general Galerius won a decisive victory in 298, securing favourable borders by the Peace of Nisibis.

In late antiquity the rivalry continued between the Sasanian Empire and the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire. Julian's great Persian expedition of 363 ended in disaster and his death; the ensuing treaty ceded Nisibis and much of Roman Mesopotamia. The fifth century was comparatively peaceful, but major wars erupted again under Anastasius, Justinian (whose general Belisarius fought at Dara and Callinicum), and Khosrow I, who sacked Antioch in 540. The final and most destructive conflict, the Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628, saw Persian armies conquer Syria, Egypt, and much of Anatolia and besiege Constantinople, before the emperor Heraclius counterattacked deep into Mesopotamia and forced a peace restoring the old borders.

Consequences

The last war left both empires militarily and fiscally exhausted. Within a decade, the armies of the Rashidun Caliphate overran the Sasanian Empire entirely and stripped Byzantium of Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, ending the ancient Roman–Persian rivalry forever.
