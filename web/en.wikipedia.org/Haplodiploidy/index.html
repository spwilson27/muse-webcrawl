Haplodiploidy

Haplodiploidy is a sex-determination system in which males develop from unfertilised eggs and are haploid, while females develop from fertilised eggs and are diploid. It is characteristic of the insect order Hymenoptera (ants, bees, and wasps), and also occurs in thrips, some beetles, rotifers, and certain mites and scale insects.

Genetics

A female is related to her full sisters by, on average, three-quarters of their genes, because sisters share their father's entire haploid genome. This asymmetry underlies W. D. Hamilton's kin-selection explanation for the repeated evolution of eusociality in Hymenoptera: a worker may pass on more of her genes by helping her mother raise sisters than by reproducing herself.

Complementary sex determination

In honey bees and many other species, sex is actually determined at a single highly variable locus (csd): heterozygotes become female, hemizygotes become male, and rare homozygous diploids become sterile or inviable diploid males. Inbreeding increases the frequency of diploid males, imposing a significant genetic load.

Consequences

Haplodiploidy exposes recessive alleles to selection in males, lets queens control offspring sex ratio by choosing whether to fertilise an egg, and shapes colony conflicts over sex allocation studied in evolutionary biology.
