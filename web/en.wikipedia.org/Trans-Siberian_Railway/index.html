The Trans-Siberian Railway is a network of railways connecting Moscow with the Russian Far East. Its main route, from Moscow's Yaroslavsky station to Vladivostok on the Pacific, is 9,289 kilometres long — the longest railway line in the world — crossing eight time zones and linking European Russia with Siberia and the Pacific coast. Branch routes connect to Mongolia, China and North Korea (the Trans-Mongolian and Trans-Manchurian lines).

History

The line was championed by finance minister Sergei Witte as a means of developing Siberia and projecting Russian power in the Far East. Construction began in 1891, with Tsarevich Nicholas (later Nicholas II) ceremonially inaugurating work at Vladivostok. Tens of thousands of labourers — soldiers, convicts and hired workers — built the line in sections across taiga, steppe and permafrost. A shortcut through Manchuria (the Chinese Eastern Railway) opened in 1903, but the Russo-Japanese War exposed its vulnerability; the all-Russian Amur route was completed in 1916. Ferries and later the Circum-Baikal line overcame the obstacle of Lake Baikal, the line's most difficult section.

The route

Trains pass through cities including Yaroslavl, Perm, Yekaterinburg (crossing the Europe–Asia boundary in the Urals), Omsk, Novosibirsk, Krasnoyarsk, Irkutsk, Ulan-Ude, Chita and Khabarovsk before reaching Vladivostok. The flagship Rossiya train (No. 1/2) takes about six days; local trains serve hundreds of smaller stations. The line was fully electrified in 2002 and remains a freight artery for coal, timber, oil and container traffic between Asia and Europe — the "Eurasian land bridge".
