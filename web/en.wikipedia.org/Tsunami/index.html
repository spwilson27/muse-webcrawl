Tsunami

A tsunami (from Japanese tsu, "harbour", and nami, "wave") is a series of waves in a water body caused by the displacement of a large volume of water, generally in an ocean or a large lake. Unlike normal wind waves, which involve only the surface, a tsunami involves the entire water column and has a very long wavelength, often hundreds of kilometres.

Causes

Tsunamis are most commonly generated by undersea earthquakes at subduction zones, where a sudden vertical displacement of the seafloor displaces the water above. They can also be caused by submarine landslides, volcanic eruptions (such as Krakatoa in 1883), caldera collapses, and rarely by meteorite impacts.

Behaviour

In the deep ocean a tsunami may be only tens of centimetres high but travel at speeds of 500–800 km/h. As it enters shallow water, the wave slows and grows dramatically in height — a process called shoaling — sometimes exceeding 30 metres. The sea often recedes noticeably before the first crest arrives, a natural warning sign. The first wave is not always the largest; a tsunami is a series of waves that may continue for hours.

Notable tsunamis

The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, triggered by a magnitude 9.1–9.3 earthquake off Sumatra, killed an estimated 227,898 people in 14 countries. The 2011 Tōhoku tsunami in Japan caused more than 18,000 deaths and the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster. Earlier events include the 1755 Lisbon tsunami and the 365 Crete tsunami.

Warning and mitigation

Systems such as the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center and networks of deep-ocean DART buoys detect tsunamis and issue warnings. Seawalls, evacuation planning, vertical-evacuation structures and public education on natural warning signs reduce loss of life.
