Mahram

In Islam, a mahram (Arabic: محرم) is a family member with whom marriage is permanently unlawful, and with whom the rules of seclusion and modest dress (hijab) are consequently relaxed. The concept derives from the Quran — notably verses 4:23 and 24:31 — and from hadith, and it shapes rules about travel, privacy, and social interaction in traditional Islamic jurisprudence.

Categories

Mahrams are of three kinds: by blood (for example, a father, brother, son, or paternal and maternal uncles), by marriage (a father-in-law, a son-in-law, or a stepson), and by milk kinship (rada'a), established when a woman breastfeeds an infant who is not her own. A woman's husband is also counted among her mahrams. A person outside these relations is a non-mahram (ajnabi), toward whom the stricter rules apply.

Practical implications

Classical scholars generally required a woman travelling beyond a certain distance to be accompanied by a mahram, a rule most famously applied to the Hajj pilgrimage; modern authorities differ over its scope, and Saudi Arabia since 2019 has permitted women pilgrims to travel in groups without a mahram under specified conditions. Practices vary considerably among Muslim communities and legal schools.
