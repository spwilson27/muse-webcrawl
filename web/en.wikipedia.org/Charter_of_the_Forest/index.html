Charter of the Forest

Charter of the Forest
Issued: 6 November 1217
Issued by: Henry III of England (regency of William Marshal)
Latin name: Carta de Foresta

The Charter of the Forest (Carta de Foresta) is a charter issued on 6 November 1217 by the regency government of the young Henry III of England. A companion to Magna Carta, it restored and protected the traditional rights of free men to use the royal forest for grazing, fuel, and food, and it rolled back the harsh forest law imposed by the Norman and Angevin kings.

Background

Under William the Conqueror and his successors, "forest" — a legal category that could include farmland and villages, not only woodland — covered perhaps a third of England. Forest law imposed savage penalties for killing deer or cutting timber, and large sums were extracted through fines. Grievances against the forest system were a major cause of the baronial unrest that produced Magna Carta in 1215.

Provisions

The charter disafforested lands enclosed by Henry II, abolished the death penalty and mutilation for taking venison (replaced by fines or imprisonment), confirmed rights of pannage (pasturing pigs), estovers (gathering wood), and agistment (grazing livestock), and regulated the forest courts and officials (foresters, verderers) who enforced the law. Reissued with Magna Carta in 1225, it remained in force for centuries; parts survived in law until the 1970s, and some common rights it protected persist in the New Forest and Forest of Dean.

Legacy

Historians regard the charter as an early statement of the rights of commoners to subsistence resources — the "commons" — and it is increasingly cited in discussions of land rights and environmental justice.
