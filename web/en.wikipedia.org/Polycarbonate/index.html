Polycarbonate

TypeThermoplastic polymer
Invented1953 (Bayer; General Electric)
Trade namesLexan, Makrolon
Key propertiesTransparent, impact-resistant, heat-resistant

Polycarbonates (PC) are a group of thermoplastic polymers containing carbonate groups in their chemical structures. The most common commercial material is derived from bisphenol A (BPA). Polycarbonate is a tough, transparent, dimensionally stable plastic with exceptional impact strength, used in applications ranging from eyeglass lenses and compact discs to bullet-resistant glazing and aircraft canopies.

Production and history

Polycarbonate based on bisphenol A was developed nearly simultaneously in 1953 by Hermann Schnell at Bayer in Germany and by Daniel Fox at General Electric in the United States; it was marketed as Makrolon and Lexan respectively. It is produced industrially either by the interfacial reaction of BPA with phosgene or, increasingly, by melt transesterification with diphenyl carbonate.

Properties and uses

The material transmits visible light almost as well as glass, withstands temperatures up to about 147 °C (glass transition), and is virtually unbreakable in sheet form. It is used for optical discs (CDs, DVDs, Blu-ray), safety glasses, riot shields, greenhouses and roofing sheets, automotive headlamp lenses, water bottles, medical devices and electronic housings. Its use in food and drink containers has been controversial because of leaching of BPA, an endocrine disruptor, which has led to restrictions in baby bottles in many jurisdictions and a shift to BPA-free alternatives.
