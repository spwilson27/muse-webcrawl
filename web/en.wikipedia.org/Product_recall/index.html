Product recall

A recall notice informs consumers of a defective product.

A product recall is a request to return a product after the discovery of safety issues or product defects that might endanger the consumer or put the maker or seller at risk of legal action. Recalls may be initiated voluntarily by a manufacturer or ordered by a government regulator such as the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission, the Food and Drug Administration, or the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration for vehicles.

Process

A recall typically involves identifying the affected production lots, notifying distributors and retailers to remove the product from sale, publicizing the hazard to consumers, and offering a remedy such as repair, replacement, or refund. Food recalls are commonly triggered by contamination with pathogens such as Salmonella or Listeria, undeclared allergens, or foreign objects; automotive recalls address defects in airbags, brakes, software, and other safety-critical systems.

Costs and consequences

Recalls can cost manufacturers hundreds of millions of dollars in logistics, repairs, and settlements, and can seriously damage brand reputation. Well-known large-scale recalls include the Takata airbag inflator recall, the largest automotive recall in history, and the 1982 Tylenol tampering crisis, which led to tamper-evident packaging across the industry.
