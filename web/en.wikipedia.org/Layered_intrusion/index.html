Layered intrusion

Modal and cryptic layering in a mafic intrusion; alternating bands reflect successive crystal accumulation.
Layered intrusion
Rock typeIgneous (mafic–ultramafic)
CompositionGabbro, norite, anorthosite, peridotite, pyroxenite
FormationFractional crystallisation and crystal settling in a large magma chamber
Famous examplesBushveld, Stillwater, Skaergaard, Rum, Great Dyke
Economic importancePlatinum-group elements, chromium, vanadium, nickel, copper

A layered intrusion is a large sill-like body of mafic or ultramafic igneous rock that displays striking internal layering — vertical variations in mineral proportions (modal layering), mineral chemistry (cryptic layering), and texture, produced as crystals accumulated on the floor of a large, slowly cooling magma chamber. Layered intrusions range in age from Archaean to Eocene and are typically associated with continental rifting and large igneous provinces.

Formation

As a basaltic magma cools, minerals crystallise in a predictable sequence (governed by Bowen's reaction series) and, being denser than the melt, many sink to form cumulate layers — dunite and harzburgite at the base, through pyroxenites and norites, to gabbros and anorthosites near the top. The composition of successive layers shifts as the residual magma evolves (cryptic layering, e.g. declining magnesium number in pyroxenes and falling anorthite content in plagioclase). Rhythmic repetitions of layering are thought to reflect new influxes of primitive magma, convection, and episodic changes in crystallisation conditions.

Major examples

  • The Bushveld Igneous Complex in South Africa (c. 2.06 billion years old), the largest layered intrusion on Earth, host to the Merensky Reef and UG2 chromitite — the world's principal source of platinum-group elements — as well as vast chromium and vanadium resources.
  • The Stillwater Complex in Montana, USA, known for its J-M Reef platinum–palladium mineralisation.
  • The Skaergaard intrusion in East Greenland, the type example of extreme fractional crystallisation producing iron-rich late-stage rocks, much studied by L. R. Wager and colleagues.
  • The Rum intrusion in Scotland, the Great Dyke of Zimbabwe, the Muskox intrusion in Canada, and the Kiglapait intrusion in Labrador.

Economic significance

Layered intrusions are among the most important ore repositories on Earth. Thin chromitite seams can be mined for chromium; sulphide-rich layers concentrate nickel and copper; and "reef"-type deposits such as the Merensky Reef supply most of the world's platinum and palladium. Magnetite layers are sources of vanadium and iron.
