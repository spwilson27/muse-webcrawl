The Proto-Elamite script is an undeciphered writing system used in ancient Elam (present-day southwestern Iran) during the early Bronze Age, roughly from 3100 to 2900 BC. It is the earliest writing system known from Iran and one of the oldest in the world, roughly contemporary with the earliest cuneiform of neighbouring Mesopotamia. The script is named for the Elamites, although the language it recorded—if it recorded a spoken language at all—is unknown.

Corpus and discovery

Around 1,600 Proto-Elamite tablets have been found, most of them in excavations at Susa, with smaller numbers from sites including Tepe Yahya, Tepe Sialk, and Shahr-e Sukhteh. The tablets are clay, inscribed with a stylus, and appear overwhelmingly administrative: records of rations, livestock, labour, and land. More than a thousand distinct signs have been catalogued, many of them pictographic or abstract "object" signs, used together with numerical signs adapted in part from Mesopotamian systems.

Decipherment status

Proto-Elamite remains undeciphered. Progress has been made on the numerical systems, which are now fairly well understood and confirm the tablets' accounting function; some signs appear to denote commodities such as grain, sheep and goats, or jars of oil. In 2020, researchers including François Desset announced progress on the related but later Linear Elamite script, proposing phonetic values for many of its signs, though the decipherment claims remain debated. Proto-Elamite shows both similarities to and independence from Mesopotamian proto-cuneiform, and whether it was inspired by it or developed separately is unresolved.

Related scripts

Proto-Elamite was succeeded, after a gap, by Linear Elamite (c. 2300–1880 BC) and later by Elamite cuneiform, adapted from Mesopotamian writing, in which the Elamite language is well attested. The sign repertoires of Proto-Elamite and Linear Elamite overlap only partially, and no direct line of descent between them has been proven.
