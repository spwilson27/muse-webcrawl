Cecil was a well-known lion studied by Oxford University researchers at Hwange.

The killing of Cecil the lion in July 2015 sparked worldwide outrage and intense debate about trophy hunting. Cecil (c. 2002 – 1 July 2015) was a male African lion who lived primarily in Hwange National Park in Matabeleland North, Zimbabwe. He was being studied and tracked by the Wildlife Conservation Research Unit (WildCRU) of the University of Oxford as part of a long-running research project, and was recognisable by his distinctive black-fringed mane and GPS collar.

The hunt

On the night of 1 July 2015, Cecil was lured out of the protected area of the park onto adjacent private land, where American recreational big-game hunter Walter Palmer, a dentist from Minnesota, wounded him with an arrow from a compound bow. The lion was tracked for many hours afterward and finished off with a second arrow; Palmer's party then removed his collar and skinned and beheaded the body. Palmer said he had paid about US$50,000 for the hunt and believed it to be legal and properly permitted.

Reaction

When the story broke internationally at the end of July 2015, it went viral: Palmer's dental practice was flooded with negative reviews and protests and temporarily closed, he received death threats and went into hiding, more than a million people signed petitions, and celebrities and politicians condemned the killing. Zimbabwe's environment minister initially called Palmer a "foreign poacher", and authorities charged the professional hunter Theo Bronkhorst and the landowner with poaching offences; Palmer himself was not charged, as Zimbabwean officials later confirmed he held proper papers. Palmer's guides' prosecutions were eventually dropped.

Aftermath

The incident — sometimes called the "Cecil effect" — led several airlines to ban carriage of hunting trophies, increased donations to lion conservation (WildCRU received millions of dollars), and prompted policy reviews: in December 2015 the United States listed African lions under the Endangered Species Act, restricting trophy imports. Cecil's brother Jericho survived the controversy; one of Cecil's sons, Xanda, was legally trophy-hunted in 2017, renewing the debate.
