Chapati

Chapati (alternatively spelled chapatti, chappati, chapathi, or chappathi; pronounced [tʃəˈpaːti]), also known as roti, rotli, safati, shabaati, phulka and (in the Maldives) roshi, is an unleavened flatbread originating from the Indian subcontinent and a staple in India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, East Africa, the Arabian Peninsula and the Caribbean. Chapatis are made from whole-wheat flour known as atta, mixed into a dough with water, oil (optional) and salt (optional) in a mixing bowl called a parat, and are cooked on a tava (flat skillet).

Preparation

The dough is kneaded until soft, rested, divided into balls and rolled into thin discs about 15–20 cm across. The disc is first cooked on a hot tava until small bubbles appear and the underside has light brown spots, then flipped; finally it is often held with tongs directly over an open flame (or pressed on the tava), where trapped steam puffs it into a balloon — the form called phulka ("puffed"). Finished chapatis may be brushed with ghee or oil and kept wrapped in cloth to stay soft. Skill in rolling perfectly round, evenly thin chapatis is traditionally prized.

History and spread

Unleavened wheat flatbreads are ancient in the Indus region; the word derives from Hindi chapat ("slap, flat"), referring to the hand-slapping used to shape the dough before rolling pins became standard. Chapatis travelled with Indian labour and trade to East Africa (where they are a breakfast and street-food staple, sometimes layered with oil in the paratha-like style), to Fiji, Mauritius, the Caribbean and Malaysia, and became a global diaspora food. In 1857, stories that chapati distribution heralded revolt — the mysterious "chapati movement", in which flatbreads passed rapidly from village to village across northern India — unsettled British officials on the eve of the Indian Rebellion.

Nutrition and use

Made from whole-wheat atta, chapatis are a significant source of complex carbohydrate, fibre and B vitamins in vegetarian diets. They are torn by hand and used to scoop curries, dals and vegetables, and form the basis of wraps and of dishes such as chapati rolls and leftover-chapati snacks like khakhra (crisped) or vaghareli rotli.
