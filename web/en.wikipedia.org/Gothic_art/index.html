Gothic art

Gothic art was a style of medieval art that developed in Northern France out of Romanesque art in the 12th century AD, led by the concurrent development of Gothic architecture. It spread to all of Western Europe, and much of Northern, Southern and Central Europe, never quite effacing more classical styles in Italy. In the late 14th century, the sophisticated court style of International Gothic developed, which continued to evolve until the late 15th century.

Origins and characteristics

The style is closely associated with Abbot Suger's rebuilding of the Basilica of Saint-Denis near Paris in the 1140s. In architecture, its hallmarks are the pointed arch, the ribbed vault, and the flying buttress, which together allowed taller buildings with larger windows filled with stained glass. Gothic sculpture became more naturalistic than the stiff forms of the Romanesque, and panel painting, illuminated manuscripts, and fresco all developed distinctive Gothic forms.

Periods

Art historians divide the style into Early Gothic (12th century), High Gothic (c. 1200–1280), Rayonnant (c. 1240–1350), and Flamboyant or Late Gothic (15th century), with regional variants such as English Perpendicular Gothic. In painting, the International Gothic style of around 1400 united the courts of Europe in an elegant, detailed manner.

Legacy

The term "Gothic" was applied pejoratively by Renaissance critics who associated the style with the Goths and barbarism. The Gothic Revival of the 18th and 19th centuries restored its reputation and made it a model for churches, universities, and public buildings worldwide.
