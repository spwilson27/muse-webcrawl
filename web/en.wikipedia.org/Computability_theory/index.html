Computability theory

Computability theory, also known as recursion theory, is a branch of mathematical logic, computer science, and the theory of computation that originated in the 1930s with the study of computable functions and Turing degrees. It asks which mathematical problems can in principle be solved by an effective procedure (an algorithm), and classifies those that cannot by their degree of unsolvability.

The Turing machine: the standard model of effective computation

Origins

David Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem (1928) asked for an algorithm to decide the truth of any mathematical statement. Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed limits to formal provability, and in 1936 Alonzo Church (with the lambda calculus and recursive functions) and Alan Turing (with his abstract machines) independently proved that no such algorithm exists. The agreement of their definitions underlies the Church–Turing thesis: the effectively calculable functions are exactly those computable by a Turing machine — equivalently, the general recursive (μ-recursive) functions.

Core concepts

  • Computable and computably enumerable sets — sets decidable by an algorithm, and sets whose members can be listed by one.
  • The halting problem — Turing's proof that no algorithm decides whether arbitrary programs halt is the paradigmatic undecidable problem.
  • Reductions and Turing degrees — Post's program of classifying unsolvable problems by relative computability, with results such as the Friedberg–Muchnik theorem solving Post's problem.
  • The arithmetical hierarchy — stratification of definable sets by quantifier complexity.

Extensions

The field has generalized in many directions: higher recursion theory, computable analysis and computable structures, reverse mathematics (calibrating the set-existence axioms needed to prove theorems), and algorithmic randomness. Its concerns complement computational complexity theory, which classifies the solvable by the resources required rather than by solvability itself.
