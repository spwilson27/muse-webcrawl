MEDEVAC

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medical evacuation, often shortened to medevac or medivac, is the timely and efficient movement and en route care provided by medical personnel to wounded personnel being evacuated from a battlefield, to injured patients being transferred from a smaller facility to a trauma centre, or to patients in remote areas who cannot be reached by ground ambulance. The term usually applies to transport by air ambulance (helicopter or fixed-wing aircraft), but ground vehicles and ships are also used.

In modern US military usage, a distinction is drawn between MEDEVAC — movement of patients aboard dedicated, marked medical platforms staffed by medical personnel, protected under the Geneva Conventions — and CASEVAC (casualty evacuation), the opportunistic movement of casualties aboard non-medical assets, which may be armed and are not protected.

History

Origins

Organised casualty evacuation dates to Dominique Jean Larrey's ambulances volantes ("flying ambulances") in the French Revolutionary Wars. The first aerial medical evacuation took place during the First World War, when aircraft of several nations carried stretcher cases from front-line strips; the Serbian campaign of 1915 and British operations in the Middle East were early examples. The US Army Air Corps experimented with air ambulances in the 1920s and 1930s.

Korea and Vietnam

The helicopter transformed medevac. In the Korean War, the US Army's Helicopter Detachments and MASH hospitals used Bell H-13 Sioux helicopters with external stretcher pods, evacuating some 20,000 casualties and cutting the time from wounding to surgery dramatically; died-of-wounds rates fell markedly compared with the Second World War. In Vietnam, dedicated aeromedical units — famously call-signed "Dustoff" — flying the UH-1 Huey evacuated roughly 900,000 patients; the average time from wounding to hospital fell to about an hour, and the call sign became a generic synonym for helicopter medevac.

The golden hour

Military experience underpinned the civilian concept of the golden hour: that survival of the severely injured depends on definitive care within about sixty minutes. In 2009 the US Secretary of Defense mandated a 60-minute standard for medevac response in Afghanistan, a policy credited in studies with substantial reductions in mortality. Civilian helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS) and fixed-wing air ambulances apply the same model worldwide.

Organisation and procedure

In US and NATO practice, a medevac request uses the standardised 9-line MEDEVAC request, transmitting the pickup location, radio frequency and call sign, number of patients by precedence (urgent, urgent-surgical, priority, routine, convenience), special equipment required, patient type (litter or ambulatory), security of the pickup zone, marking method, patient nationality and status, and terrain or contamination details. En route care is provided by flight medics or flight paramedics trained in critical care, airway management, blood transfusion and trauma resuscitation.

Dedicated platforms include the UH-60 Black Hawk (HH-60) in US service, the NH90 and H145M in European forces, and fixed-wing aircraft such as the C-17 Globemaster III and C-130 Hercules configured as flying hospitals for strategic aeromedical evacuation. The International Committee of the Red Cross and civilian operators conduct analogous evacuations in disaster and humanitarian settings.

Legal protection

Under the Geneva Conventions of 1949, medical aircraft and vehicles exclusively engaged in medical duties and displaying the red cross, red crescent or red crystal emblem must be respected and protected and may not be attacked. This protection is forfeited if the platform is used to commit acts harmful to the enemy, which is why dedicated medevac aircraft are typically unarmed except for crew personal weapons.
