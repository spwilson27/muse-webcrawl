Nonviolence is the personal practice of not causing harm to others under any condition, and a method of social and political action that seeks change without physical violence. It may arise from a moral, religious, or spiritual commitment — as in the Jain, Hindu, and Buddhist principle of ahimsa — or from a strategic calculation that nonviolent methods are the most effective means of achieving political goals. Nonviolent action includes protests, strikes, boycotts, sit-ins, and civil disobedience, and it has driven many of the most consequential movements of the modern era.

History

Religious traditions long taught non-harm: ahimsa is a cardinal virtue in Jainism, Hinduism, and Buddhism, and early Christian pacifism later influenced figures such as Leo Tolstoy, whose The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894) inspired Mohandas Gandhi. Gandhi developed satyagraha ("truth force") in South Africa and India, leading campaigns — including the 1930 Salt March — that helped end British colonial rule. His methods shaped the American civil rights movement under Martin Luther King Jr., the anti-apartheid struggle, and pro-democracy movements worldwide.

Theory

Theorists distinguish principled nonviolence, embraced as an ethical commitment, from pragmatic nonviolence, adopted for effectiveness. Gene Sharp catalogued 198 methods of nonviolent action and argued that political power rests on the consent and cooperation of the governed, which nonviolent movements can withdraw. Empirical studies by Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan found that major nonviolent campaigns between 1900 and 2006 succeeded roughly twice as often as violent insurgencies, in part because they mobilize broader participation.

Practice and criticism

Nonviolent resistance has toppled governments in the Philippines (People Power, 1986), Eastern Europe (1989), Serbia (2000), and elsewhere. Critics argue that nonviolence may fail against ruthless opponents, depends on media access and opponent restraint, and can be slow; defenders respond that violent victories more often lead to authoritarian outcomes, while nonviolent movements more often produce durable democracies.
