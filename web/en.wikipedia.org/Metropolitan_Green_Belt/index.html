Metropolitan Green Belt

LocationAround London, England
AreaApprox. 486,000 hectares (1,860 sq mi)
Established1938 (legislative basis); formalized 1955
Governing frameworkNational Planning Policy Framework; London Plan

The Metropolitan Green Belt is a statutory green belt around London, England. It covers an area of roughly 486,000 hectares — about three times the size of Greater London itself — spanning parts of London and the surrounding counties of Berkshire, Buckinghamshire, Essex, Hertfordshire, Kent, Oxfordshire, and Surrey. Within it, development is strictly controlled in order to check the unrestricted sprawl of the capital's built-up area.

History

The idea of a green girdle around London was promoted from the 1930s; the Green Belt (London and Home Counties) Act 1938 allowed local authorities to buy land for protection. The Greater London Plan of 1944 by Patrick Abercrombie proposed a continuous belt seven to ten miles wide. In 1955, Housing Minister Duncan Sandys issued a circular inviting local authorities to formally designate green belts, and the Metropolitan Green Belt was progressively defined in development plans thereafter.

Policy

National planning policy sets five purposes for green belt land: to check unrestricted sprawl of large built-up areas, to prevent neighbouring towns merging, to safeguard the countryside from encroachment, to preserve the setting of historic towns, and to assist urban regeneration by encouraging recycling of derelict urban land. Development is presumed "inappropriate" unless it falls within defined exceptions, such as agriculture, forestry, outdoor sport, and limited infilling in villages.

Debate

The Green Belt is politically popular for protecting countryside and preventing sprawl, but economists and housing advocates argue it constrains housing supply around London, inflates land and house prices, and pushes development to leapfrog into more distant commuter settlements. Reviews of its boundaries, and proposals to release lower-quality land near railway stations, recur in London planning debates.
