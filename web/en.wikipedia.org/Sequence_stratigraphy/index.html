Sequence stratigraphy

Sequence stratigraphy is a branch of geology, specifically a branch of stratigraphy, that attempts to link genetically related sedimentary deposits into unconformity-bounded units called sequences. It studies rock relationships within a chronostratigraphic framework of repetitive, genetically related strata bounded by surfaces of erosion or deposition, or their correlative conformities. It is widely used in the petroleum industry to predict the distribution of reservoir, source, and seal rocks.

Key concepts

The framework rests on the interplay between accommodation (space made available by subsidence and sea-level change) and sediment supply. Deposits are organized into systems tracts: lowstand, transgressive, highstand, and falling-stage systems tracts, separated by key surfaces such as sequence boundaries, transgressive surfaces, and maximum flooding surfaces. Stacking patterns of parasequences — progradation, retrogradation, and aggradation — record changes in this balance through time.

History

The method grew out of seismic stratigraphy developed at Exxon in the 1970s by Peter Vail and colleagues, who recognized that seismic reflections follow depositional surfaces and that global cycles of sea-level change leave a correlatable record. The landmark 1977 AAPG memoir and the 1988 SEPM volume edited by Wilgus and others codified its terminology, which has since been refined and debated extensively.

Applications

Sequence stratigraphy is applied in hydrocarbon exploration, groundwater studies, and academic research into basin evolution and past sea-level and climate change, using outcrop, well-log, core, and seismic data.
