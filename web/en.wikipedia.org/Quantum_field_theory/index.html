In theoretical physics, quantum field theory (QFT) is a theoretical framework that combines classical field theory, special relativity and quantum mechanics, used to construct physical models of subatomic particles and their interactions. QFT treats particles as excited states — quanta — of underlying quantum fields that pervade space and time, so that particle creation and annihilation, impossible in ordinary quantum mechanics, occur naturally.

Principles

Each type of particle corresponds to a field: electrons to the electron (Dirac) field, photons to the electromagnetic field, quarks and gluons to their respective fields. Interactions are described by terms in a Lagrangian density, expanded perturbatively into Feynman diagrams, whose lines represent particles propagating and interacting at vertices. The procedure of renormalisation absorbs infinities in these expansions into a finite set of measured parameters, leaving predictions of extraordinary accuracy — the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron agrees with experiment to more than ten significant figures.

History

QFT arose in the late 1920s with the quantisation of the electromagnetic field by Dirac, and the work of Heisenberg, Pauli, Jordan and others on field operators and antiparticles. Quantum electrodynamics (QED) was completed in the late 1940s by Feynman, Schwinger, Tomonaga and Dyson. Yang–Mills gauge theories then underlay the electroweak unification (Glashow, Weinberg, Salam) and quantum chromodynamics (QCD) in the 1960s–70s, together forming the Standard Model of particle physics.

Applications and extensions

  • The Standard Model of particle physics
  • Condensed matter physics: superconductivity, quantum Hall effects, critical phenomena
  • Renormalisation group methods and effective field theories
  • Quantum field theory in curved spacetime (Hawking radiation, Unruh effect)
