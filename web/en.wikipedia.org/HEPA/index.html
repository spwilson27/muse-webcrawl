HEPA

HEPA (high-efficiency particulate air) is a standard of air filtration. A filter meeting the HEPA standard must remove at least 99.97% of particles with a diameter of 0.3 micrometres from the air passing through it (under the US Department of Energy standard); the European standard EN 1822 defines grades H13 and H14 with efficiencies of 99.95% and 99.995% respectively at the most penetrating particle size.

How it works

HEPA filters consist of a mat of randomly arranged fibres, usually fibreglass, pleated to increase surface area. Particles are captured by a combination of mechanisms: interception, impaction and diffusion. Contrary to intuition, filters are least efficient at about 0.3 µm — smaller particles are captured effectively by diffusion (Brownian motion), and larger ones by impaction and interception.

History and applications

The technology was developed during the Second World War, in connection with the Manhattan Project, to remove radioactive contaminants from air. HEPA filters are now standard in hospital operating theatres, cleanrooms for semiconductor and pharmaceutical manufacture, aircraft cabin air systems, vacuum cleaners and domestic air purifiers. They trap pollen, dust mite allergens, mould spores, bacteria and many virus-laden aerosols, though they do not remove gases or odours, for which activated carbon is used.

Marketing terms such as "HEPA-type" or "99% HEPA" do not denote certified HEPA performance.
