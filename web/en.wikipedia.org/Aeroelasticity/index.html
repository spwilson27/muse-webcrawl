Aeroelasticity

Branch ofAeronautical engineering, fluid–structure interaction
PhenomenaFlutter, divergence, buffeting, control reversal, limit-cycle oscillation
Key toolWind-tunnel testing, computational simulation, flight flutter testing

Aeroelasticity is the branch of physics and engineering that studies the interactions between the inertial, elastic and aerodynamic forces occurring while an elastic body is exposed to a fluid flow. Because structures — especially aircraft wings — are not completely rigid, aerodynamic loads deflect them, and the deflections change the aerodynamic forces, which may lead to further deflection. Aeroelasticity draws on structural dynamics, aerodynamics and control theory.

The field's central hazards are dynamic instabilities. Flutter is a self-excited oscillation in which the structure extracts energy from the airflow; it can destroy an aircraft within seconds and famously contributed (via torsional oscillation) to the 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge. Divergence is a static instability in which aerodynamic twisting overwhelms a wing's stiffness. Other phenomena include buffeting (unsteady loading from separated flow), control-surface buzz, and the reversal of control effectiveness when a deflected surface twists the wing it is mounted on.

History and practice

Aeroelastic effects appeared early in aviation — the Wright brothers exploited wing warping for control, and tail flutter afflicted First World War aircraft; the systematic theory was built in the 1920s–30s by figures such as Theodore Theodorsen. The disasters of early jet aircraft, including the Lockheed Electra accidents of 1959–60 traced to whirl flutter of propeller nacelles, made aeroelastic clearance compulsory: modern aircraft must be shown by analysis, wind-tunnel models and flight flutter testing to be free of flutter throughout (and 15% beyond) their flight envelope. Modern practice emphasises aeroelastic tailoring — designing composite structures so that deformation improves performance — and active flutter suppression using control systems.
