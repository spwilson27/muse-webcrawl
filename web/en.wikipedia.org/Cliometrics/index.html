Cliometrics

Cliometrics, sometimes called the new economic history or econometric history, is the systematic application of economic theory, econometric techniques and other formal quantitative methods to the study of history — especially social and economic history. The term combines Clio, the muse of history, with -metrics, the science of measurement.

Development

The field arose in the United States in the late 1950s and 1960s with works such as Alfred Conrad and John Meyer's 1958 study of the profitability of slavery and Robert Fogel's Railroads and American Economic Growth (1964), which used a counterfactual model to estimate the railways' true contribution to the economy. Fogel and Douglass North were jointly awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1993 "for having renewed research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods".

Methods and debates

Cliometricians build explicit models and test them against large assembled datasets of prices, wages, trade, demography and production. Controversial landmarks include Fogel and Stanley Engerman's Time on the Cross (1974) on American slavery, which provoked fierce methodological and ethical debate, and studies of living standards during industrialisation. Critics among narrative historians charged the approach with reductionism, while its practitioners argued it replaced speculation with testable claims. The field institutionalised through the Cliometric Society (founded 1983) and journals such as Explorations in Economic History, and its methods have since been absorbed into mainstream economic history.
