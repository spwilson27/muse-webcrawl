Piccolo

The piccolo (Italian for "small", pronounced [ˈpikkolo]) is a half-size flute and a member of the woodwind family of musical instruments. Sometimes called a "baby flute" or flauto piccolo, the modern piccolo has most of the same fingerings as a standard transverse flute, but the sound it produces is an octave higher. It is the highest-pitched instrument of the orchestra.

History

Small transverse flutes were used in military music and early orchestras; by the early 19th century, composers such as Beethoven began scoring for the piccolo in works like the Fifth and Sixth Symphonies. Theobald Böhm's key system was applied to the piccolo in the later 19th century, and the instrument became a regular, if specialty, orchestral voice.

Construction and use

Modern piccolos are made of wood (often grenadilla), resin, or metal, with a conical or cylindrical bore. In the orchestra the piccolo typically reinforces the flute section at the octave and adds brilliance and sparkle; famous solos include Sousa's march "The Stars and Stripes Forever". It is also standard in wind bands, military bands, and drum and bugle corps, and it appears in folk traditions such as the Irish flute band repertoire.
