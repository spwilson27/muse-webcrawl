The C-ration (officially Field Ration, Type C) was a United States military ration consisting of prepared, canned wet foods, intended to be issued to US military personnel when fresh or packaged unprepared food (A-ration and B-ration) was impractical or unavailable. It was developed in the late 1930s and served as the standard American individual combat ration through the Second World War, the Korean War, and — in modified form as the Meal, Combat, Individual (MCI) — the Vietnam War era, before being replaced by the Meal, Ready-to-Eat (MRE) in the early 1980s.

Composition

A day's C-ration consisted of three meals, each containing an "M-unit" of canned meat (entries such as meat and beans, meat and vegetable stew, or chicken and noodles) and a "B-unit" of biscuits or bread, plus an accessory pack containing instant coffee, sugar, salt, toilet paper, and cigarettes, and items such as candy, jam, or canned fruit. Cans were opened with the famous P-38 can opener. The ration provided roughly 3,700 kilocalories per day.

History

The Type C ration was adopted in 1938 and first issued in quantity during the Second World War. Troops frequently criticised its monotony and weight, and traded or discarded components. Post-war revisions could not keep pace with complaints, and in 1958 the ration was redesignated the Meal, Combat, Individual (MCI), which remained in service — widely still called "C-rats" by troops — until the MRE was fielded beginning in 1981.
