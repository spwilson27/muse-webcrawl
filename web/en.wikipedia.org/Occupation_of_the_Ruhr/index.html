Occupation of the Ruhr

The occupation of the Ruhr (German: Ruhrbesetzung) was the occupation of the Ruhr industrial area of Germany by troops of the French Third Republic and Belgium from 11 January 1923 to 25 August 1925, undertaken in response to the Weimar Republic's failure to meet its reparations obligations under the Treaty of Versailles.

Background

In late 1922 the Allied Reparations Commission declared Germany in default on deliveries of timber and coal. French premier Raymond Poincaré ordered troops into the Ruhr to seize its coal mines, steel works and factories as "productive pledges" guaranteeing payment, despite British misgivings.

Passive resistance

The German government under Chancellor Wilhelm Cuno responded with a policy of passive resistance: officials refused orders from the occupiers, workers struck, and the state paid their wages by printing money. The policy, combined with the loss of Ruhr output, fuelled catastrophic hyperinflation in 1923. Clashes between occupiers and inhabitants caused over a hundred German deaths, and France supported Rhenish separatist ventures that briefly proclaimed a Rhenish Republic.

Resolution

Chancellor Gustav Stresemann called off passive resistance in September 1923, a humiliating but necessary step that paved the way for currency stabilisation and the Dawes Plan of 1924, which restructured reparations and brought American loans. French and Belgian troops withdrew from the Ruhr in the summer of 1925.
