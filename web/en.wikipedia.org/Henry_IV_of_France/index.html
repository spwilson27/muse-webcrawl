Henry IV, founder of the Bourbon dynasty in France

Henry IV (13 December 1553 – 14 May 1610), also known by the epithets Good King Henry and Henry the Great, was King of Navarre (as Henry III) from 1572 and King of France from 1589 to 1610. He was the first monarch of France from the House of Bourbon, a cadet branch of the Capetian dynasty.

Wars of Religion

Raised a Calvinist by his mother, Jeanne d'Albret, Henry became the leading Huguenot prince during the French Wars of Religion. His marriage to Margaret of Valois in 1572 was followed days later by the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre, which he survived by briefly converting to Catholicism. He later escaped Paris, rejoined the Protestant cause, and, as heir presumptive, won the battles of Arques (1589) and Ivry (1590). Concluding that "Paris is well worth a Mass," he abjured Protestantism in 1593 and was crowned at Chartres in 1594.

Reign

In 1598 Henry issued the Edict of Nantes, granting substantial rights to Huguenots and effectively ending the wars of religion. With his minister Sully he restored royal finances, promoted agriculture and infrastructure, lowered taxes on peasants, and founded the first French colonial ventures in Canada. He is remembered for the wish that every labourer might have "a chicken in the pot" on Sundays. His first marriage was annulled and he married Marie de' Medici in 1600.

Assassination

Henry was assassinated in Paris on 14 May 1610 by François Ravaillac, a Catholic zealot, and was succeeded by his son Louis XIII under Marie's regency. He remains one of France's most popular kings.
