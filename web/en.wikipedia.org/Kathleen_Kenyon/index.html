Dame Kathleen Mary Kenyon (5 January 1906 – 24 August 1978) was a British archaeologist of Neolithic culture in the Fertile Crescent. She led excavations at Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) from 1952 to 1958 and at Jerusalem from 1961 to 1967, and was one of the most influential archaeologists of the 20th century.

Career

Kenyon read history at Somerville College, Oxford, and was drawn into archaeology through work with Gertrude Caton-Thompson in Zimbabwe (1929) and with Mortimer Wheeler at Verulamium (St Albans). With John Crowfoot she excavated at Samaria in Palestine in the 1930s, refining stratigraphic technique. During the Second World War she served with the Red Cross; afterward she taught at the Institute of Archaeology in London, where she became a leading figure in training a generation of archaeologists.

Jericho and Jerusalem

At Jericho, Kenyon excavated deep soundings that revealed one of the earliest fortified towns in the world, with a massive stone tower dating to the Neolithic (around 8000 BCE), and the famous plastered human skulls. Her dating showed the celebrated walls were largely Bronze Age but much later than the biblical account of Joshua suggested. In Jerusalem she uncovered parts of the ancient city on the southeastern hill, including remains associated with the City of David. She was principal of St Hugh's College, Oxford, from 1962 to 1973, and founded the British School of Archaeology in Jerusalem.

Method

Kenyon adapted and taught the Wheeler–Kenyon method of excavation: digging in a grid of squares separated by standing baulks, with meticulous recording of stratigraphic sections — a technique that became standard in Levantine archaeology.
