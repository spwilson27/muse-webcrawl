Volga Bulgars

The Volga Bulgars were a Turkic people who settled in the Volga region of Eastern Europe during the 7th century CE and founded the state of Volga Bulgaria. Descended from the broader Bulgar confederation that had earlier formed Old Great Bulgaria under Kubrat in the Pontic-Caspian steppe, the Volga branch migrated north after the collapse of that polity under Khazar pressure, establishing themselves at the confluence of the Volga and Kama rivers.

History

After the death of Kubrat around 665, his sons divided the Bulgar tribes. One group, led according to tradition by Kotrag, moved north to the middle Volga. By the 9th century the Volga Bulgars had formed a loose confederation that controlled important trade routes between the Islamic world, the Rus', and the forest peoples of the north. They traded in furs, slaves, wax, honey, and amber, importing silver dirhams, textiles, and metalwork from the Middle East.

In 922 the ruler Almish (Almış) formally adopted Islam, an event recorded by the Arab envoy Ibn Fadlan, whose travel account is a key source for the region's history. Islam spread gradually among the ruling elite and urban population, while elements of the earlier Tengrist religion persisted in the countryside.

Volga Bulgaria flourished through the 10th to 13th centuries as a major commercial and agricultural power. Its cities, Bolgar and Bilär, were noted for stone and brick architecture, baths, and crafts production. The state survived pressure from the Khazars and later the Rus' principalities, but was devastated by the Mongol invasion: Bolgar was destroyed in 1236 and the territory was absorbed into the Golden Horde.

Culture and legacy

The Volga Bulgars left behind Arabic-inscribed tombstones, jewellery, ceramics, and fortifications. Their linguistic legacy is debated: the modern Chuvash language, the only surviving member of the Oghur branch of Turkic, is generally regarded as descended from a language closely related to Volga Bulgar. The Volga Tatars also claim the Bulgars as ancestors, and the heritage of Volga Bulgaria remains an important element of regional identity in Tatarstan.
