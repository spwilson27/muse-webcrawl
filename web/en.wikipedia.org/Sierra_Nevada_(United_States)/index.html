Sierra Nevada (United States)

The Sierra Nevada (/siˌɛrə nᵻˈvɑːdə, -ˈvædə/) is a mountain range in the Western United States, between the Central Valley of California and the Great Basin. The vast majority of the range lies in the state of California, although the Carson Range spur lies primarily in Nevada. The Sierra Nevada is part of the American Cordillera, an almost continuous sequence of mountain ranges that forms the western "backbone" of the Americas.

The Sierra runs 400 miles (640 km) north-south and is approximately 70 miles (110 km) across east-west. Notable features include General Grant Grove, home of giant sequoia trees; Yosemite Valley; Lake Tahoe, the largest alpine lake in North America; and Mount Whitney, at 14,505 feet (4,421 m) the highest point in the contiguous United States.

Geography

The Sierra Nevada stretches from the Susan River and Fredonyer Pass in the north to Tehachapi Pass in the south. To the west, it is bounded by California's Central Valley; to the east, by the Basin and Range Province. The range is asymmetrical: its crest and eastern escarpment are much steeper than its gradual western slope, which is dissected by deep river canyons cut by glaciers, including those of the Kings, Kern, and San Joaquin rivers.

Geology

The Sierra Nevada began to form in the Jurassic period, when a volcanic island arc collided with the west coast of North America; the associated granitic plutons cooled to form the Sierra Nevada batholith. Uplift of the range began around 25 million years ago, tilting the batholith westward and exposing the granitic core. During the Pleistocene, extensive glaciers carved the range's famous U-shaped valleys, domes, and cirques.

Ecology

The range contains several distinct biotic zones stacked by elevation, from foothill woodlands and chaparral through montane and subalpine forests of ponderosa pine, lodgepole pine, red fir, and western white pine, to alpine tundra above the tree line. The giant sequoia groves of the western slope grow in a narrow elevational band and include the largest trees on Earth by volume.

History

Native peoples, including the Paiute, Miwok, and Yokuts, inhabited the range for millennia. The first recorded crossing by a European-American was by Jedediah Smith in 1827. The California Gold Rush beginning in 1848 transformed the western foothills. John Muir's writings helped inspire the creation of Yosemite and Sequoia national parks and the modern conservation movement.
