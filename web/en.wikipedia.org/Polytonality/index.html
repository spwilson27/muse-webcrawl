Polytonality

Polytonality (also called polyharmony) is the musical use of more than one key simultaneously. In the strictest sense, polytonality requires the simultaneous presentation of chords or melodic lines in two or more different keys, so that the listener perceives the parts as belonging to separate tonal systems rather than to a single extended chord.

History and examples

Brief polytonal effects appear in earlier music — for example, in Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber's Battalia (1673), where a folk tune is deliberately played in clashing keys to depict drunken soldiers. Polytonality became a significant modernist device in the early twentieth century. Famous examples include the "Petrushka chord" in Igor Stravinsky's ballet Petrushka (1911), which superimposes C major and F♯ major triads; passages of The Rite of Spring (1913); Darius Milhaud's chamber works and Brazilian-inspired dances; Charles Ives's layering of independent bands in different keys; and some works of Béla Bartók and Alfred Schnittke, who coined the related term "polystylism".

Bitonality and polychords

The term bitonality is used for the use of exactly two keys at once. A polychord — two chords stacked together — produces a polytonal effect only if the chords are heard as belonging to different keys; in many cases analysts instead interpret the combination as an extended tertian chord in one key, a debate exemplified by the Petrushka chord. Composers in jazz, including pianists who use upper-structure triads from different keys, also exploit polytonal sonorities.
