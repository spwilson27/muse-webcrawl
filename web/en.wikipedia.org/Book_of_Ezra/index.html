The Book of Ezra is a book of the Hebrew Bible which, together with the Book of Nehemiah, forms the composite work Ezra–Nehemiah. It recounts the return of the Jewish exiles from Babylon to Jerusalem after the decree of Cyrus the Great in 538 BC, the rebuilding of the Second Temple, and the reforms of Ezra the scribe.

Summary

The first six chapters cover the period from Cyrus's decree permitting the exiles to return, through the laying of the Temple's foundations under Zerubbabel, opposition from neighbouring peoples, and the eventual completion and dedication of the Second Temple in 516 BC, aided by the prophets Haggai and Zechariah and confirmed by a decree of Darius I.

Chapters 7–10 introduce Ezra, a priest and scribe "skilled in the Law of Moses", who arrives in Jerusalem in the reign of Artaxerxes (traditionally 458 BC) with a royal commission to teach and enforce the Law. The book ends with Ezra's campaign against intermarriage between the returned exiles and the peoples of the land, which he regarded as a threat to the community's covenant identity.

Composition and sources

The book incorporates a number of documents presented as official correspondence — decrees of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes, and letters exchanged with the Persian court, several of them in Aramaic (Ezra 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26 are in Aramaic rather than Hebrew). Scholars debate the authenticity and dating of these documents and the book's final editing, which is often placed in the 4th century BC or later. In the Septuagint and Latin traditions the material is divided differently, giving rise to the apocryphal 1 Esdras.
