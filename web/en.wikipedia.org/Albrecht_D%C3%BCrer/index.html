Albrecht Dürer

Albrecht Dürer (21 May 1471 – 6 April 1528), sometimes spelled in English as Durer, was a German painter, printmaker, and theorist of the German Renaissance. Born in Nuremberg, he established his reputation and influence throughout Europe in his twenties due to his high-quality woodcut prints.

Dürer's vast body of work includes engravings, his preferred technique in his later prints, altarpieces, portraits and self-portraits, watercolours, and books. His woodcuts series include Apocalypse (1498) and two series on the Passion of Christ. His best-known engravings are the three Meisterstiche ("master prints"): Knight, Death and the Devil (1513), Saint Jerome in his Study (1514), and Melencolia I (1514).

Life and career

Dürer was apprenticed to his father, a goldsmith, and then to the painter Michael Wolgemut, in whose workshop he learned woodcut illustration. After his apprenticeship he travelled as a journeyman through Germany and twice to Italy, where the art of the Italian Renaissance — including the perspective theory of Leon Battista Alberti and the work of Leonardo da Vinci — profoundly influenced him. He opened his own workshop in Nuremberg in 1495 and later worked for the Emperor Maximilian I.

Works and theory

Beyond his art, Dürer wrote treatises on measurement, fortification, and human proportion, including the Four Books on Measurement (Underweysung der Messung, 1525). His watercolours such as the Young Hare (1502) and his nature studies are landmarks of observational art. His famous monogram "AD" became one of the earliest artist's trademarks, and he pursued legal protection against copyists.

Legacy

Dürer is regarded as the greatest artist of the Northern Renaissance, bridging the Gothic world of the late Middle Ages and the humanism of the Italian Renaissance. His prints were copied throughout Europe and shaped generations of artists.
