Nubia is a region along the Nile River encompassing the area between the first cataract at Aswan in southern Egypt and the confluence of the Blue and White Niles at Khartoum in Sudan. In antiquity it was home to a succession of rich African civilisations — the Kerma culture, the Kingdom of Kush, and the city of Meroë — and the source of much of Egypt's gold; its name may derive from the Egyptian nub, "gold."

History

Nubian kingdoms traded and warred with pharaonic Egypt for millennia. Egypt conquered Kush during the New Kingdom, but as Egypt weakened, Kushite kings rose to rule Egypt itself as the 25th Dynasty (c. 744–656 BC), the era of the "Black Pharaohs" such as Piye and Taharqa. The Kushite capital later moved south to Meroë, famous for its iron production, its still-undeciphered Meroitic script, and fields of steep-sided pyramids — Sudan has more pyramids than Egypt. In the medieval period Nubia held the Christian kingdoms of Nobatia, Makuria, and Alodia, later succeeded by Islamic states such as the Funj Sultanate of Sennar.

Modern Nubia

In the 1960s, the flooding of Lower Nubia by Lake Nasser behind the Aswan High Dam displaced over 100,000 Nubians in Egypt and Sudan and prompted a famous UNESCO campaign that rescued monuments including Abu Simbel and Philae. Nubian languages, part of the Nilo-Saharan family, are still spoken today.
