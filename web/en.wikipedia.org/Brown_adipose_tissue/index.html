Brown adipose tissue

TypeAdipose tissue
FunctionNon-shivering thermogenesis
Key proteinUncoupling protein 1 (UCP1, thermogenin)

Brown adipose tissue (BAT) or brown fat makes up the adipose organ together with white adipose tissue. It is found in almost all mammals and is especially abundant in newborns and hibernating animals. Brown adipocytes are packed with mitochondria, whose iron-rich contents give the tissue its brown colour, and contain many small lipid droplets, in contrast to the single large droplet of white adipocytes.

Function

The primary function of brown fat is non-shivering thermogenesis: generating heat without muscular activity. This is achieved by uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1) in the inner mitochondrial membrane, which dissipates the proton gradient as heat rather than allowing it to drive ATP synthesis. The process is activated by noradrenaline released from sympathetic nerves, typically in response to cold exposure.

In humans

Human infants have significant deposits of brown fat around the neck, shoulders, and along the spine, protecting against hypothermia. Adults were long thought to retain little functional BAT, but imaging studies in 2009 confirmed metabolically active brown fat in adult humans, mainly in the supraclavicular and neck regions. Activity declines with age and is lower in people with obesity.

Research

Because activated brown fat burns glucose and fatty acids, stimulating BAT or converting white adipocytes into energy-burning "beige" adipocytes is an active area of research for potential treatments of obesity and type 2 diabetes.
