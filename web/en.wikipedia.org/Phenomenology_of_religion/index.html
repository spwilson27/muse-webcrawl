Phenomenology of religion

The phenomenology of religion is a comparative, descriptive approach to the study of religion that classifies and compares religious phenomena — myths, rituals, sacred times and places, symbols, and experiences — while suspending judgement about their truth or ultimate origin (the method of epoché). It seeks to understand religion from the standpoint of the believer and to uncover the structures and meanings of the sacred as it appears in human experience.

History

The term was first used in this sense by the Dutch scholar Pierre Daniël Chantepie de la Saussaye in his Lehrbuch der Religionsgeschichte (1887). The approach was systematized by Gerardus van der Leeuw in Religion in Essence and Manifestation (1933) and popularized by Mircea Eliade, whose works The Sacred and the Profane (1957) and Patterns in Comparative Religion drew on concepts such as the hierophany (manifestation of the sacred) and the axis mundi. Rudolf Otto's The Idea of the Holy (1917), with its analysis of the numinous — the mysterium tremendum et fascinans — is a key precursor.

Criticism

Critics have charged the approach with essentialism, with smuggling theological commitments into ostensibly neutral description, and with decontextualizing phenomena from history and society. Nevertheless it shaped religious studies as an autonomous discipline distinct from theology and from purely sociological or psychological reductionism, and its categories still inform comparative work. It borrows its name and bracketing method from Husserlian phenomenology (philosophy), though it developed partly independently.
