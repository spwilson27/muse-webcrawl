Cultural assimilation of Native Americans

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The cultural assimilation of Native Americans was a series of efforts by the United States to assimilate Native Americans into mainstream European-American culture between the years of 1790 and the 1920s, though policies of forced assimilation continued well into the twentieth century. The belief behind these efforts was that Native peoples should abandon their tribal cultures, languages and religions in favour of the norms of the settler society.

Early policy

George Washington and Secretary of War Henry Knox formulated a policy of "civilizing" Native nations through agriculture, education and Christianity, formalised in the Civilization Fund Act of 1819, which funded missionary schools. Assimilation proceeded alongside removal: the Indian Removal Act of 1830 displaced the southeastern nations to Indian Territory.

Allotment and boarding schools

The Dawes Act of 1887 broke up communal tribal landholdings into individual allotments, with "surplus" land sold to settlers, costing Native nations some 90 million acres. From 1879, when Richard Henry Pratt founded the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, off-reservation boarding schools removed children from their families, cut their hair, forbade their languages and imposed military discipline — Pratt's stated philosophy was to "kill the Indian... and save the man". Thousands of children passed through the system, and many died of disease and neglect.

Termination and reversal

The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 ended allotment and encouraged tribal self-government, but the termination policy of the 1950s dissolved the federal status of more than 100 tribes. The Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 marked a decisive turn toward tribal sovereignty; language revitalisation programmes, repatriation under NAGPRA (1990), and official apologies and investigations into the boarding school system have followed.
