The figure of the Earth is the size and shape used to model the Earth in geodesy and astronomy. Although the Earth's surface is topographically irregular, it can be approximated at increasing levels of precision: first as a sphere, then as an oblate spheroid (an ellipsoid of revolution flattened at the poles), and more exactly by the geoid, the equipotential surface of Earth's gravity field that coincides with mean sea level. Determining the figure of the Earth has been a central problem of geodesy since antiquity.

History

The sphericity of the Earth was established in Greek antiquity, and Eratosthenes (c. 240 BC) measured its circumference with remarkable accuracy. Isaac Newton predicted from theory that a rotating Earth should bulge at the equator and flatten at the poles; 18th-century French geodesic expeditions to Peru and Lapland under Maupertuis and others confirmed the oblate form against the rival Cassinian elongated model. In the 19th century, arc measurements by Bessel, Clarke and others produced reference ellipsoids used for national surveys.

Modern determination

Satellite geodesy, very-long-baseline interferometry, satellite laser ranging and the GNSS have refined the figure to centimetre precision. The widely used WGS 84 ellipsoid has a semi-major axis of 6,378,137 m and flattening of 1/298.257. The geoid's undulations relative to the ellipsoid reach about ±100 m, mapped by gravity missions such as GRACE and GOCE. Departures from the ellipsoid matter for precise positioning, orthometric heights, oceanography and satellite orbits.

Modelling choices

For most purposes a sphere suffices for small-scale maps; regional mapping uses local reference ellipsoids fitted to a nation's geoid; precise heights use geoid models; and studies of the Earth's interior use the gravity field itself. The Earth's figure also changes slowly through glacial isostatic adjustment, plate tectonics and mass redistribution, monitored by modern geodetic observatories.
