The corvus (Latin for "crow" or "raven") was a Roman naval boarding device used in the First Punic War against Carthage. It was a hinged boarding bridge fitted with a heavy spike, which was dropped onto the deck of an enemy ship to lock the two vessels together so that Roman marines could board and fight a land-style battle at sea.

Description

According to the historian Polybius, the corvus consisted of a wooden gangway about 11 metres (36 feet) long and 1.2 metres (4 feet) wide, mounted on a vertical pole at the bow and raised or swung by ropes. At its far end, on the underside, was a pointed iron beak — the resemblance to a bird's beak gave the device its name. When a Roman ship came alongside an enemy, the bridge was dropped; the spike pierced and anchored into the enemy's deck planking, and legionaries crossed two abreast, shields raised, to overwhelm the opposing crew.

Use and effectiveness

Rome, a land power with an inexperienced navy, built its first large fleet in 261–260 BC and adopted the corvus to neutralise the superior seamanship of the Carthaginians. The device contributed to striking Roman victories at Mylae (260 BC), Sulci, Tyndaris, and Ecnomus (256 BC). However, the bridge's weight raised the ships' centre of gravity and apparently impaired their seaworthiness; Roman fleets suffered catastrophic storm losses in 255 and 249 BC, and the corvus is not mentioned in connection with the war's later battles. By the final Roman victory at the Aegates Islands (241 BC), the Romans relied instead on fast ships and trained crews.

Legacy

The corvus exemplifies Rome's pragmatic adaptation of its strengths — infantry discipline — to an unfamiliar element. Modern reconstructions differ in detail because Polybius's description is the only full account and some scholars doubt whether the spike could have held ships together in a seaway.
