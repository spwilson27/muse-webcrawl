Diffuse reflection

Diffuse reflection is the reflection of light or other waves or particles from a surface such that a ray incident on the surface is scattered at many angles rather than at just one angle as in the case of specular reflection. An ideal diffuse reflecting surface is said to exhibit Lambertian reflection, meaning that there is equal luminance when viewed from all directions lying in the half-space adjacent to the surface.

Mechanism

A surface built from a non-absorbing powder such as plaster, or from fibres such as paper, or from a polycrystalline material such as white marble, reflects light diffusely with great efficiency. Many common materials exhibit a mixture of specular and diffuse reflection. In general, diffuse reflection arises because the surface is rough at the scale of the wavelength of the light, or because the light penetrates slightly into the material and is scattered internally before re-emerging—a process known as subsurface scattering. The visibility of objects, excluding light-emitting ones, is primarily caused by diffuse reflection of light: it is diffusely-scattered light that forms the image of the object in the observer's eye.

Lambertian reflection

When a surface is roughened or textured so that it scatters light equally in all directions according to Lambert's cosine law, its apparent brightness is the same regardless of the observer's angle of view. The radiant intensity observed is proportional to the cosine of the angle between the direction of the incident light and the surface normal. Many computer-graphics shading models use Lambertian diffuse reflection as a basic approximation.

Coloured surfaces

Objects that show diffuse reflection but no specular reflection appear matte and take on colour because the material absorbs certain wavelengths within the volume of the material before scattering the remainder back out. This is in contrast to glossy surfaces, where the specularly reflected light retains the colour of the illumination.
