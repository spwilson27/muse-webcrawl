Aristophanes (c. 446 – c. 386 BC) was an Athenian playwright of Old Comedy, the only one of its exponents whose plays survive in complete form. His comedies — fantastical, satirical and famously scurrilous — lampooned the politicians, generals, intellectuals and war policy of Athens during the Peloponnesian War, and provide an unrivalled comic portrait of Athenian public life.

Life and career

Little is securely known of Aristophanes's life: he was an Athenian citizen of the deme Cydathenaeum, began producing plays as a young man in the 420s BC — initially through producers such as Callistratus — and won first prize at the Dionysia and Lenaia festivals on several occasions. He wrote about forty plays; eleven survive complete, selected for study in later antiquity.

The surviving plays

The Acharnians (425 BC) pleads for peace; The Knights (424) attacks the demagogue Cleon; The Clouds (423, later revised) caricatures Socrates and the new education; The Wasps (422) mocks the jury system; The Birds (414) imagines a city in the sky; Lysistrata (411) stages a women's sex strike to end the war; The Frogs (405) pits Aeschylus against Euripides in the underworld; and Assemblywomen and Wealth (388) show the transition toward Middle Comedy. The comedies feature a hero with an ingenious scheme, a chorus in fantastic costume, direct address to the audience (the parabasis) and exuberant invective and obscenity licensed by the festival context.

Influence

Aristophanes shaped the Western comic tradition from Roman comedy and Renaissance drama to modern political satire. His portrait of Socrates, however unfair, contributed — in Plato's account — to the prejudice that led to the philosopher's trial in 399 BC.
