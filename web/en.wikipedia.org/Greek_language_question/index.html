Greek language question

Period: 19th–20th centuries (resolved 1976)

Forms in dispute: Katharevousa vs. Demotic Greek

The Greek language question (Greek: το γλωσσικό ζήτημα) was a long-running dispute over the official and literary language of Greece: whether it should be Katharevousa, an archaizing, "purified" form modelled on Ancient Greek and used by the state, or Demotic, the vernacular language actually spoken by the Greek people. The controversy shaped Greek education, literature and politics for over 150 years and was not finally settled until 1976, when Demotic was made the official language.

Origins

After Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire, the new state adopted Katharevousa — developed by scholars such as Adamantios Korais as a compromise between ancient and spoken Greek — for administration, law and education. The spoken language, however, continued to evolve independently, producing a situation of diglossia. Writers and intellectuals divided: the "demoticists", inspired by works such as Ioannis Psycharis's My Journey (1888), argued that literature and the state should use the people's language, while archaists defended Katharevousa as a link to classical and Byzantine heritage.

Conflict

The question repeatedly turned violent or political. Riots in Athens in 1901 (the Evangelika) protested a Demotic translation of the Gospels, and the 1903 Oresteiaka riots opposed a Demotic translation of Aeschylus. Constitutions of 1911 and later entrenched Katharevousa as the official language, though reformers such as Eleftherios Venizelos introduced Demotic into primary schools in 1917. Language became entangled with party politics, conservatism and nationalism; the military junta of 1967–1974 imposed Katharevousa aggressively, discrediting it by association.

Resolution

After the restoration of democracy, Law 309 of 1976 established Demotic as the language of education and administration, and in 1982 the polytonic accent system was replaced by the simpler monotonic system in official use. Modern Greek as written today is essentially standardised Demotic, enriched by many learned Katharevousa words.
