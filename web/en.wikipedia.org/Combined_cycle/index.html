Combined cycle

A combined cycle power plant is an assembly of heat engines that work in tandem from the same source of heat, converting it into mechanical energy. In a typical electric generating plant, a gas turbine (operating on the Brayton cycle) generates electricity, and its hot exhaust is used to boil water for a steam turbine (operating on the Rankine cycle), generating additional electricity from heat that would otherwise be wasted.

Principles

The thermodynamic advantage of the combined cycle comes from exploiting a wider range of temperatures: the gas turbine operates at very high inlet temperatures, while the steam cycle recovers energy at lower temperatures. In a combined-cycle gas turbine (CCGT) plant, exhaust gases leaving the gas turbine at roughly 550–650 °C pass through a heat recovery steam generator (HRSG), which produces steam to drive a steam turbine. The steam turbine typically adds about half as much power again as the gas turbine alone.

Efficiency

Combined cycle plants are among the most efficient means of converting fuel into electricity. Modern CCGT plants burning natural gas achieve net thermal efficiencies of around 60 per cent on a lower heating value basis, and the most advanced designs exceed 62–64 per cent. This compares with roughly 33–40 per cent for a simple-cycle gas turbine or a conventional steam plant alone.

Applications

Combined cycles are widely used for electricity generation, especially with natural gas, and variants are used in cogeneration (combined heat and power), marine propulsion, and integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) plants that gasify coal or other feedstocks. Their relatively low capital cost, quick construction, and fast start-up capability make them attractive for both baseload and load-following duty, although the steam cycle slows response compared with a simple-cycle turbine.
