Gondwana

Gondwana was a large landmass (a supercontinent) that formed from the breakup of the earlier supercontinent Pannotia in the late Neoproterozoic and existed for hundreds of millions of years before fragmenting during the Mesozoic Era. At its greatest extent it included most of the land of today's Southern Hemisphere: South America, Africa, Antarctica, Australia, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Indian subcontinent. It was named by Eduard Suess after the Gondwana region of central India, where its characteristic glacial and Glossopteris fossil sequences were first recognised.

Formation and breakup

Gondwana assembled around 600–530 million years ago through collisions that closed ancient oceans, creating mountain belts such as the East African Orogen. During the late Paleozoic it merged with Laurentia and other northern continents to form Pangaea. After Pangaea split, Gondwana itself broke apart from about 180 million years ago: Africa separated from India and Antarctica, the South Atlantic opened as South America rifted from Africa, India raced north to collide with Asia, and Australia finally detached from Antarctica about 45 million years ago.

Climate and life

Gondwana experienced a major glaciation in the late Paleozoic (the Karoo Ice Age) and supported the distinctive Glossopteris flora, whose distribution across the southern continents became key evidence for continental drift and plate tectonics. Its long isolation shaped the evolution of marsupials, ratite birds, southern beeches (Nothofagus), and many other lineages.
