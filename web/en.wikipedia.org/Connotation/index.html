Connotation

A connotation is a commonly understood cultural or emotional association that a word or phrase carries, in addition to its explicit or literal meaning, its denotation. Connotations may be positive, negative or neutral, and they vary between cultures, communities and periods.

Examples

The words "slim", "thin" and "skinny" share a similar denotation but differ in connotation: slim is complimentary, thin neutral and skinny often unflattering. Similarly, "home" connotes warmth and security beyond its denotation of a dwelling, while "residence" sounds formal. Writers and speakers exploit connotation to shape tone and persuade audiences.

In logic and semantics

In logic, the connotation of a term — a usage developed by John Stuart Mill — is the set of attributes it implies, corresponding to the term intension, as opposed to its denotation or extension, the things it applies to. In semiotics, connotation describes second-order meanings built on literal signs, as analysed by Roland Barthes in his account of myth.

Change over time

Connotations shift as words acquire new associations: terms once neutral may become approving or pejorative through use. Because connotations depend on shared cultural knowledge, they are a frequent source of misunderstanding across languages and in translation.
