Carl Ortwin Sauer (1889–1975) was an American geographer, widely regarded as one of the founders of modern cultural geography. A professor at the University of California, Berkeley, from 1923 until his retirement, he led the "Berkeley School" of geography and shaped the discipline's study of landscapes, agriculture and human impacts on the environment.

Career and ideas

Sauer studied at Central Wesleyan College and earned his doctorate at the University of Chicago, where he was influenced by geology and ecology. In his landmark 1925 paper "The Morphology of Landscape", he defined the cultural landscape as the natural landscape reshaped by a culture group — "culture is the agent, the natural area is the medium, the cultural landscape the result" — in explicit opposition to the environmental determinism then dominant in geography. He emphasised fieldwork, historical depth and the study of how human societies transform the earth over time.

Research interests

Sauer conducted extensive research in Mexico and Latin America, on the origins and diffusion of agriculture and domesticated plants, on early Spanish exploration, and on indigenous peoples. His later work, including the essay "The Agency of Man on the Earth", anticipated themes of the Anthropocene and historical ecology. He served as president of the Association of American Geographers and influenced generations of students at Berkeley.
