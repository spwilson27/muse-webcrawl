The Nobel Prize in Chemistry is awarded annually by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences to scientists in the field of chemistry. It is one of the five Nobel Prizes established by the 1895 will of Alfred Nobel, the Swedish inventor of dynamite, and is awarded for "the most important chemical discovery or improvement." The prize is presented in Stockholm on December 10, the anniversary of Nobel's death, together with a gold medal and diploma; the monetary award is shared among at most three laureates.

History

The first Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded in 1901 to the Dutch chemist Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff for his work on chemical dynamics and osmotic pressure. Marie Curie received the 1911 prize for the discovery of radium and polonium, having shared the 1903 Physics prize, making her the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. Frederick Sanger and Barry Sharpless are the only laureates to have won the Chemistry prize twice.

Selection process

Nominations are solicited from invited experts worldwide and evaluated by the Nobel Committee for Chemistry of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, with investigations by specialist institutes. The Academy votes in October, and the prize is announced shortly thereafter; nominations remain sealed for 50 years.

Notable laureates and discoveries

Honored achievements include the Haber process for ammonia synthesis, the structure of the chemical bond (Linus Pauling), the elucidation of protein structures, polymerase chain reaction (Kary Mullis), green fluorescent protein, cryo-electron microscopy, lithium-ion batteries (2019), and the CRISPR-Cas9 genome-editing method, for which Emmanuelle Charpentier and Jennifer Doudna shared the 2020 prize. The prize has occasionally been controversial, as with Fritz Haber's award amid his role in chemical warfare.
