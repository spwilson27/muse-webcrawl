Land value tax

A land value tax or location value tax (LVT) is a levy on the value of unimproved land. Unlike property taxes, it disregards the value of buildings, personal property and other improvements to real estate. Land value taxes are generally favoured by economists, as they do not cause economic inefficiency, and reduce inequality.

A land value tax is an example of a Pigovian tax: because the supply of land is essentially fixed, the tax is borne by landowners and does not discourage production, making it one of the least distortionary revenue sources in public finance theory.

History

The idea was advanced by the Physiocrats in eighteenth-century France and by classical economists including Adam Smith and David Ricardo, who analysed land rent extensively. It was popularised by the American reformer Henry George in Progress and Poverty (1879), which proposed a "single tax" on land values to replace other taxes; Georgism inspired political movements and model communities, and influenced early Sun Yat-sen and others.

Where it applies

Elements of land value taxation operate in Denmark, Estonia, parts of Australia, Taiwan, Singapore and Hong Kong (through land lease premiums), and a split-rate form in some Pennsylvania municipalities, most famously Pittsburgh from 1913 to 2001. Proposals for broader adoption are periodically debated in the United Kingdom and elsewhere.

Arguments

Advocates argue LVT captures publicly created location value, discourages land speculation and vacant lots, and encourages density and efficient land use. Critics raise difficulties in valuing land separately from improvements, transition hardship for asset-rich but income-poor owners, and the political unpopularity of taxing a highly visible asset.
