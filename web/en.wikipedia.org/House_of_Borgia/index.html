The House of Borgia (Spanish: Borja) was an Italo-Spanish noble family that rose to prominence during the Italian Renaissance. Originating from Borja in Aragon, the family produced two popes — Callixtus III and Alexander VI — and became notorious for corruption, nepotism and political ruthlessness, as well as for notable patronage of the arts.

Rise

Alfons de Borja became Pope Callixtus III in 1455. His nephew Rodrigo Borgia, elected Alexander VI in 1492, advanced his children openly: Cesare Borgia was made cardinal, then resigned to pursue a military career carving out a state in the Romagna; Lucrezia Borgia's marriages served family strategy in Naples, Milan and Ferrara; Juan Borgia became Duke of Gandía, a title the Spanish branch retained for centuries.

Downfall and later history

Alexander's death in 1503 and the election of the family's enemy Julius II destroyed Cesare's position; he died in Navarre in 1507. The Spanish line continued as dukes of Gandía and produced Saint Francis Borgia, third Superior General of the Jesuits. The family's black legend — of poisonings and scandal — was amplified by enemies and later literature, and modern historians treat many tales cautiously, though the core record of ambition and violence is well documented.
