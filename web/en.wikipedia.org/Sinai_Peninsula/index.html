The Sinai Peninsula, or simply Sinai (Arabic: سيناء), is a peninsula in Egypt, the only part of the country located in Asia. It is between the Mediterranean Sea to the north and the Red Sea to the south, and is a land bridge between the continents of Africa and Asia. Sinai has a land area of about 60,000 km² (23,000 sq mi).

The peninsula is bounded by the Gulf of Suez to the west and the Gulf of Aqaba to the east. Its southern mountain region includes Mount Catherine, the highest point in Egypt, and the traditional site of Mount Sinai near Saint Catherine's Monastery. A total of around 600,000 inhabitants live in the two governorates of North Sinai and South Sinai.

History

Sinai was mined for turquoise and copper in ancient Egyptian times and was the route of invasions and trade between Egypt and the Levant. In biblical tradition it is associated with the Exodus and the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai.

During the 1967 Six-Day War, Israel captured the entire peninsula from Egypt. Israel returned Sinai to Egypt in stages following the 1978 Camp David Accords, completing the withdrawal in 1982 under the Egypt–Israel peace treaty of 1979. A Multinational Force and Observers mission has monitored the border since then.

The Red Sea coast of South Sinai, including Sharm el-Sheikh, Dahab and the Ras Mohammed National Park, became a major international resort area. From around 2011 the peninsula faced an insurgency in the north, with attacks by militant groups affiliated with the Islamic State, against which Egyptian forces conducted long-running campaigns.

Geography

The north is a low sandy plain, giving way to limestone plateaus in the centre and rugged granite mountains in the south. Scattered oases and wadis support a small Bedouin population traditionally engaged in herding, together with coastal settlements on both gulfs.
