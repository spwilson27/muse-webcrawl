Expected utility hypothesis

The expected utility hypothesis is a foundational concept in decision theory and economics, holding that a rational agent facing choices under uncertainty should select the option whose expected value of utility is the highest. Utility is a measure of the satisfaction or value an agent assigns to outcomes, and the expected utility of a gamble is the probability-weighted average of the utilities of its possible outcomes:

EU = Σ pi · u(xi)

History

The idea originated with Daniel Bernoulli in 1738, in his resolution of the St. Petersburg paradox. Bernoulli proposed that the moral expectation of wealth, measured by its logarithm, explained why people reject apparently favourable gambles. The modern axiomatic formulation was given by John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern in their 1944 book Theory of Games and Economic Behavior.

von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms

The von Neumann–Morgenstern utility theorem shows that if an agent's preferences over lotteries satisfy four axioms — completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence — then there exists a utility function such that the agent behaves as if maximising its expected value.

Risk aversion

The shape of the utility function describes attitudes to risk: a concave function implies risk aversion, a convex function risk seeking, and a linear function risk neutrality. Expected utility theory underlies much of modern finance, insurance theory and game theory.

Criticism

Behavioural economists, notably through the Allais paradox and the work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky on prospect theory, have shown that real human choices systematically violate the independence axiom, motivating descriptive alternatives to the theory.
