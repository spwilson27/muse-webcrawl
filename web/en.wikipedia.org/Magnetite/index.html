Magnetite is a mineral and one of the main iron ores, with the chemical formula Fe3O4. It is one of the oxides of iron and is ferrimagnetic: it is attracted to a magnet and can itself be magnetised to become a permanent magnet. Naturally magnetised pieces of magnetite, called lodestone, were humanity's first magnets and the basis of early compasses. Magnetite is the most magnetic of all naturally occurring minerals on Earth.

Properties and occurrence

Magnetite is black or brownish-black with a metallic lustre, a hardness of 5.5–6.5, and a black streak. It crystallises in the cubic system, commonly forming octahedra, and belongs to the spinel group, with an inverse spinel structure containing both Fe2+ and Fe3+. It occurs in igneous and metamorphic rocks worldwide, in banded iron formations, and as black sand in beach deposits. Major deposits are mined in Sweden (Kiruna), Australia, and elsewhere as a high-grade iron ore.

Biological occurrences

Magnetite crystals occur in living organisms: magnetotactic bacteria grow chains of magnetite (magnetosomes) that orient them in the Earth's magnetic field, and magnetite has been found in the beaks of birds and in other animal tissues, where it may contribute to magnetoreception — the sense some animals use to navigate by the geomagnetic field.
