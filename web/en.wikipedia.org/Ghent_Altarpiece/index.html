The Ghent Altarpiece, also called the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb (Dutch: Het Lam Gods), is a very large and complex 15th-century polyptych altarpiece in St Bavo's Cathedral, Ghent, Belgium. It was begun around the mid-1420s and completed by 1432, and it is attributed to the Early Netherlandish painters and brothers Hubert and Jan van Eyck. It is considered a masterpiece of European art and one of the world's first major oil paintings.

Description

The altarpiece consists of twelve panels in two registers, eight of which are hinged shutters that close to form an exterior view. When closed, the exterior shows the Annunciation across the top panels, with the donors Jodocus Vijd and his wife Lysbette Borluut kneeling before statues of St John the Baptist and St John the Evangelist in painted niches below.

When open — its festal state — the interior is overwhelming in scale and colour. The upper register shows God the Father (or Christ the King) enthroned in majesty at the centre, flanked by the Virgin Mary and John the Baptist (a Deësis), and by singing and music-making angels, with Adam and Eve at the far extremes. The lower register presents the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb: the Lamb of God stands on an altar bleeding into a chalice, surrounded by angels, while processions of saints, martyrs, prophets, and Church Fathers converge across a lush paradisal landscape toward the Fountain of Life.

Attribution

A quatrain on the frame, discovered during cleaning in 1823, states that Hubert van Eyck, "greater than all," began the work and that his brother Jan, "second in art," completed it in 1432. Since almost nothing else is securely known of Hubert, the division of labour between the brothers has been debated for two centuries; most scholars now credit Jan with the majority of the painting as it survives.

Thefts and restoration

The altarpiece is sometimes called the most frequently stolen artwork in history. Panels were nearly burned by Calvinist iconoclasts, seized by French Revolutionary troops for the Louvre, sold (in part) to the King of Prussia, and looted by Nazi Germany and stored in the Altaussee salt mines, whence the Monuments Men recovered them in 1945. In 1934 two panels were stolen; one, The Just Judges, was never recovered and is still missing — it is represented in the cathedral by a copy painted by Jef Van der Veken. A major conservation campaign begun in 2012 removed discoloured varnish and overpaint, revealing, among other things, the Lamb's original, strikingly humanoid face, whose restoration attracted worldwide attention in 2020.
