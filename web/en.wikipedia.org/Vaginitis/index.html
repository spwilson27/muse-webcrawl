Vaginitis, also known as vulvovaginitis, is inflammation of the vagina and vulva. Symptoms may include itching, burning, pain, discharge, and a bad smell. Certain types of vaginitis may result in complications during pregnancy. It is one of the most common reasons women seek medical care.

Causes

The three main infectious causes are bacterial vaginosis (an imbalance of the normal vaginal flora, the most common cause), vaginal yeast infection (usually Candida albicans), and trichomoniasis, a sexually transmitted infection caused by the parasite Trichomonas vaginalis. Non-infectious causes include irritants and allergens (soaps, detergents, spermicides), foreign bodies, and atrophic vaginitis from low estrogen after menopause.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on history, pelvic examination, measurement of vaginal pH, saline and KOH microscopy ("wet mount"), the amine ("whiff") test, and where needed culture or nucleic acid amplification tests. Bacterial vaginosis is often diagnosed clinically using the Amsel criteria. Because symptoms overlap considerably, self-diagnosis is frequently inaccurate.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause: bacterial vaginosis and trichomoniasis are treated with antibiotics such as metronidazole (with partner treatment recommended for trichomoniasis), yeast infections with antifungal agents such as fluconazole or topical azoles, and irritant or atrophic vaginitis by removing the irritant or using topical estrogen. Vaginitis caused by sexually transmitted infections requires evaluation for other infections.

Epidemiology

Vaginal infections affect tens of millions of women annually. About 75% of women will have at least one episode of vulvovaginal candidiasis in their lifetime, and bacterial vaginosis is the most prevalent vaginal condition in women of reproductive age.
