The Elamite language, also known as Hatamtite, is an extinct language spoken in Elam, in what is now southwest Iran. It was used from roughly 2600 BC into the early centuries AD, making it one of the longest-attested languages of the ancient Near East. Elamite is generally regarded as a language isolate, unrelated to the neighboring Semitic, Sumerian, or Indo-European languages, although a proposed Elamo-Dravidian connection with the Dravidian languages of India remains debated.

History and corpora

Elamite texts survive in a cuneiform script adapted from Akkadian, used for royal inscriptions, administrative documents, and legal records. Elamite was one of the official languages of the Achaemenid Persian Empire, and many Old Persian royal inscriptions — including the great trilingual inscription of Darius I at Behistun — were accompanied by Elamite versions, which greatly aided decipherment. Large archives from Persepolis record the empire's administration in Elamite.

Grammar

Elamite is an agglutinative language with a system of noun classes distinguishing animate and inanimate nouns. The verb agrees with its subject through suffixes, and definiteness and plurality are marked by suffixes derived from pronouns. Word order is generally subject–object–verb.

Other scripts

Two earlier scripts associated with the region remain only partly understood: Proto-Elamite (c. 3100–2900 BC), among the earliest writing systems in the world, and Linear Elamite (c. 2300–1880 BC), for which partial decipherments have been proposed in recent years.
