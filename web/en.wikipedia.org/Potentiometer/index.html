Potentiometer

Type: Variable resistor (passive component)

Terminals: 3

Symbol: Resistor with arrow (IEC) or zigzag with arrow (ANSI)

Uses: Volume controls, position sensing, calibration

A potentiometer is a three-terminal resistor with a sliding or rotating contact that forms an adjustable voltage divider. If only two terminals are used (the wiper and one end), it acts as a variable resistor or rheostat. Potentiometers are ubiquitous in electronic equipment, where they serve as user controls — most familiarly as the volume and tone knobs of audio equipment — and as position sensors.

Construction

The resistive element may be carbon composition, carbon film, cermet, conductive plastic or a coil of resistance wire. Rotary potentiometers are turned by a shaft; linear (slide) potentiometers by a lever. Multi-turn "trimmer" potentiometers, adjusted with a screwdriver, are used for calibration inside equipment. The resistance may vary linearly with angle ("linear taper") or logarithmically ("audio taper"), matching the ear's response for volume controls.

Applications

Beyond audio controls, potentiometers measure displacement in joysticks, servomechanisms and automotive throttle sensors. Historically, the potentiometer was also a precision laboratory instrument — a null-balance device for measuring electromotive force and calibrating voltmeters, refined by Poggendorff and used in the standardisation of the volt.

Digital successors

Digital potentiometers are integrated circuits that emulate the function with digitally selectable resistance taps, allowing microcontroller adjustment, though analogue types remain common for direct user controls.
