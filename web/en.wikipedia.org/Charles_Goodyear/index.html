Charles Goodyear

Charles Goodyear (December 29, 1800 – July 1, 1860) was an American self-taught chemist and manufacturing engineer who developed vulcanisation, the process of heating rubber with sulphur to make it durable, elastic and resistant to heat and cold. His discovery laid the foundation of the modern rubber industry, though he died in debt.

Search for durable rubber

Goodyear, a failed hardware merchant from New Haven, Connecticut, became obsessed with the problem that natural rubber melted in summer heat and cracked in winter cold. For years he experimented, at times in debtors' prison, trying mixtures of rubber with substances such as magnesia and nitric acid. In 1839, at Woburn, Massachusetts, he accidentally dropped rubber mixed with sulphur onto a hot stove and found that instead of melting it formed a tough, elastic material.

Vulcanisation patents

After further experiments to perfect the process, Goodyear received a United States patent for vulcanised rubber in 1844. In England, Thomas Hancock obtained a patent shortly before Goodyear's application arrived, and prolonged litigation followed on both sides of the Atlantic. Despite the enormous commercial value of vulcanised rubber—used for tyres, hoses, footwear and insulation—Goodyear earned little, selling licences cheaply and spending his means on lawsuits.

Legacy

Goodyear died in New York in 1860 owing some $200,000. The Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company, founded in Akron, Ohio in 1898, was named in his honour but had no connection with him or his family.
