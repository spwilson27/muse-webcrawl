Gregorian chant

Gregorian chant is the central tradition of Western plainchant, a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song in Latin of the Roman Catholic Church. Gregorian chant developed mainly in western and central Europe during the 9th and 10th centuries, with later additions and redactions. Although popular legend credits Pope Gregory I (r. 590–604) with inventing or collecting the chant repertoire, scholars believe it arose from a synthesis of Roman and Gallican chant in the Carolingian era. It is sung in free rhythm, organized into eight church modes, and notated with neumes.

History

As the Frankish kings Pepin and Charlemagne sought to unify their realm, they promoted the Roman liturgy and its chant in place of local Gallican practice; the resulting Romano-Frankish synthesis became "Gregorian" chant. Neumatic notation — first unheighted memory aids, then staff notation credited to Guido d'Arezzo in the 11th century — allowed the huge repertoire (thousands of melodies) to be standardized and transmitted. Regional dialects (Ambrosian in Milan, Mozarabic in Spain, Beneventan, Old Roman) mostly yielded to it.

Musical characteristics

Gregorian melodies are built from formulas appropriate to eight modes (four finals: D, E, F, G, each with authentic and plagal ranges). Chant genres differ in style: syllabic chants (one note per syllable, e.g., antiphons), neumatic, and highly melismatic pieces (many notes on one syllable, as in the Alleluia and gradual) sung by soloists. Rhythm is free, following the prose text rather than a meter — a matter of continuing scholarly and practical debate.

Liturgical functions

Chants fill the Mass (the Proper — Introit, Gradual, Alleluia, Offertory, Communion — and the sung Ordinary: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) and the Divine Office, where psalms are sung to tones with antiphons. Famous individual chants include the Requiem's Dies Irae, the Easter sequence Victimae paschali laudes, and the hymn Veni Creator Spiritus. The standard books are the Graduale Romanum and the Liber Usualis.

Revival and modern use

In the 19th century, monks of Solesmes Abbey (notably Dom Prosper Guéranger, Dom Joseph Pothier and Dom André Mocquereau) restored what they argued were authentic rhythms and readings from medieval manuscripts, producing the editions adopted by the Vatican after Pius X's 1903 motu proprio made chant the supreme model of sacred music. The Second Vatican Council affirmed chant's "pride of place" even as vernacular music displaced it in most parishes. Chant has since reached mass audiences through recordings — the Benedictine Monks of Santo Domingo de Silos' album Chant (1994) became an unlikely international bestseller — and influenced composers from the Notre Dame organum masters (its historical descendants) to Arvo Pärt.
