The Quiet American is a 1955 novel by the English author Graham Greene. Narrated by Thomas Fowler, a jaded middle-aged British war correspondent in Saigon during the First Indochina War, it tells of his rivalry — professional, political, and romantic — with Alden Pyle, a young, idealistic American aid worker who is secretly supporting a "Third Force" against both the French colonialists and the Viet Minh. The novel is celebrated for its prescient criticism of American intervention in Vietnam.

Plot

Fowler, who professes detachment ("one has to take sides if one is to remain human" is a maxim he resists), shares with Pyle an interest in Phuong, a young Vietnamese woman who has been Fowler's mistress. Pyle, steeped in the theories of the fictional writer York Harding, helps the warlord General Thé stage bombings that kill civilians in Saigon in order to build an anti-communist third force and blame the communists. Horrified by the carnage — and motivated in part by jealousy — Fowler assists the communists in arranging Pyle's assassination. The novel opens with Pyle already dead and proceeds in flashback.

Themes and context

Greene drew on his own reporting in Indochina in 1951–52. The novel opposes Fowler's weary European experience to Pyle's dangerous innocence: Pyle is "quiet" in demeanor yet implicated in violence he cannot comprehend. Published a decade before major American escalation in Vietnam, the book came to be read as prophetic; it was attacked in the United States on publication as anti-American, and a 1958 Hollywood adaptation inverted its politics, while the faithful 2002 film with Michael Caine as Fowler restored Greene's intentions.
