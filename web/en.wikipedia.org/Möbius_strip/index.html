The Möbius strip, also called the Möbius band, is a surface that can be formed by attaching the ends of a strip of paper together with a half-twist. As a mathematical object it was discovered independently by the German mathematicians Johann Benedict Listing and August Ferdinand Möbius in 1858. It is the classic example of a non-orientable surface: it has only one side and one boundary component.

Properties

An ant walking along the strip returns to its starting point mirrored, having traversed "both" sides without crossing an edge. Cutting a Möbius strip along its centre line yields not two strips but one longer strip with two full twists; cutting about a third of the way from the edge yields two linked strips, one of them a thinner Möbius strip. The strip can be parametrised and embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space, though it cannot be realised as a complete smooth surface there without self-intersection in some variants (paper models have non-zero Gaussian curvature, whereas a "flat" Möbius strip exists as a developable surface).

Applications and culture

Möbius geometry appears in conveyor-belt designs (doubling wear life), electronic resonators, and molecular chemistry — Möbius aromaticity and synthesised Möbius molecules. The recycling symbol's twisted arrows evoke the strip, and M. C. Escher's Möbius Strip II famously depicts ants crawling on one.
