Clostridium botulinum is a gram-positive, rod-shaped, anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium that produces botulinum toxin, one of the most potent neurotoxins known. The toxin causes botulism, a serious paralytic illness in humans and other animals. The organism and its spores are widely distributed in soils and in the sediments of lakes and coastal waters.

Characteristics

C. botulinum is an obligate anaerobe, growing only in the absence of oxygen. Its spores are highly resistant to heat and can survive ordinary cooking; they germinate and produce toxin in improperly preserved low-acid foods, particularly home-canned goods kept without oxygen. Strains are divided into groups based on their physiology, and they produce toxin types designated A through G, of which A, B, E, and F most commonly affect humans.

Botulinum toxin

The toxin blocks the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at neuromuscular junctions, causing flaccid paralysis that typically begins with the cranial nerves—producing blurred vision, drooping eyelids, and difficulty swallowing—and can progress to respiratory failure. Despite its extreme toxicity, purified toxin is used medically and cosmetically in minute doses, for example to treat muscle spasticity and to reduce facial wrinkles.

Prevention

Botulism is prevented by proper canning techniques, adequate heating of preserved foods, and refrigeration. Heating food to boiling for several minutes destroys the toxin itself, although the spores require pressure canning at higher temperatures to be eliminated.
