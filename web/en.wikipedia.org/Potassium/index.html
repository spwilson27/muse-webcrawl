Potassium is a chemical element with the symbol K and atomic number 19. It is a silvery white alkali metal that is soft enough to be cut with a knife and tarnishes rapidly in air. Potassium reacts violently with water, producing hydrogen gas that may ignite, and burns with a lilac-colored flame.

History

Potassium compounds such as potash were known since antiquity, but the element itself was first isolated by Humphry Davy in 1807 by electrolysis of molten caustic potash (KOH) — the first metal isolated by electrolysis. Davy named it from the English word "potash"; the symbol K derives from the neo-Latin kalium.

Properties

Potassium is the seventh most abundant element in Earth's crust (about 2.1% by weight) and has electron configuration [Ar] 4s1. It has low density — it floats on water while reacting — and low melting point. Naturally occurring isotopes are 39K, 40K (slightly radioactive, with a half-life of about 1.25 billion years, useful in potassium-argon dating), and 41K.

Occurrence and production

Because of its reactivity, potassium never occurs as the free metal in nature. It is found in minerals such as sylvite, carnallite, and feldspar, and dissolved in seawater. The metal is produced commercially by reduction of potassium chloride with sodium vapor at high temperature.

Compounds and applications

Potassium chloride and other salts are used overwhelmingly as fertilizers. Potassium hydroxide is an important industrial base; potassium nitrate is a component of gunpowder; potassium permanganate is an oxidizer and disinfectant; and potassium cyanide, though highly toxic, is used in gold extraction. The metal itself has niche uses, including the sodium-potassium alloy (NaK) as a coolant.

Biological role

Potassium ions are essential to all living cells. The sodium-potassium pump maintains the electrochemical gradient across cell membranes that underlies nerve impulses and muscle contraction. Adequate dietary potassium supports normal blood pressure; both deficiency (hypokalemia) and excess (hyperkalemia) can disturb heart rhythm and be life-threatening.
