Emigration from Europe

European emigration is the successive waves of migration from the European continent to other parts of the world, above all across the Atlantic. From the beginning of the colonial era to the middle of the 20th century, tens of millions of Europeans left for the Americas, Australia and New Zealand, Africa, and Asia, profoundly reshaping the demography and cultures of both the sending and receiving societies.

Colonial era

From the 16th century onward, Spaniards, Portuguese, French, English, and Dutch established settler colonies overseas. Early emigration was driven by trade, missionary activity, penal transportation, indentured labour, and the search for land and religious freedom.

The great transatlantic migration

Between roughly 1815 and 1914, some 50–60 million Europeans emigrated, the largest voluntary mass migration in history to that date. Successive waves came from different regions: first the British Isles and Germany; then Scandinavia; and finally southern and eastern Europe, including Italians, Poles, and Jews fleeing pogroms in the Russian Empire. Major destinations included the United States (the largest single recipient), Canada, Brazil, Argentina, and Australia. Push factors included population growth, agricultural change and land pressure, famine — notably the Irish Potato Famine — political upheaval, and religious persecution; pull factors included cheap land, industrial jobs, and chain migration networks.

After 1914

The First World War, restrictive immigration laws such as the United States' Immigration Act of 1924, and the Great Depression sharply reduced the flow. After the Second World War, emigration continued on a smaller scale (including assisted passages to Australia, such as the "Ten Pound Poms"), while Europe itself increasingly became a destination of immigration, reversing the historic pattern. In the 21st century, intra-European mobility under EU freedom of movement has become the dominant form of migration by Europeans.
