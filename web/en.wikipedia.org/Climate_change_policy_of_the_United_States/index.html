The climate change policy of the United States comprises the federal, state and local measures taken to mitigate greenhouse gas emissions and adapt to the impacts of climate change. The United States is historically the largest cumulative emitter of carbon dioxide, and its domestic policy has oscillated sharply between administrations, with regulation under the Clean Air Act, executive action, and — from 2022 — major clean-energy legislation alternating with periods of deregulation.

Early history

American climate science policy developed from the 1970s, and the United States ratified the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change in 1992. The Clinton administration signed the Kyoto Protocol in 1997, but the Senate's Byrd–Hagel Resolution signalled opposition and the protocol was never ratified; the George W. Bush administration formally rejected it in 2001. In Massachusetts v. EPA (2007) the Supreme Court held that greenhouse gases are air pollutants under the Clean Air Act, obliging the EPA to regulate them if found to endanger public health — a finding the agency made in 2009.

Obama and Trump administrations

The Obama administration pursued regulation through executive action after cap-and-trade legislation (the Waxman–Markey bill) passed the House in 2009 but stalled in the Senate. Its centrepiece, the Clean Power Plan (2015), set state targets for power-sector emissions; it was stayed by the courts and later repealed. The United States joined the Paris Agreement in 2016. The Trump administration announced withdrawal from Paris in 2017 (effective 2020) and rolled back numerous environmental rules.

Biden administration and the Inflation Reduction Act

President Biden rejoined the Paris Agreement in 2021 and pledged to cut emissions 50–52% below 2005 levels by 2030. The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 committed roughly $369 billion to clean energy tax credits, electric vehicles, and emissions reduction — the largest climate legislation in US history. The Supreme Court's decision in West Virginia v. EPA (2022) nevertheless limited the EPA's authority to restructure the power system through regulation.

State policy

States have often led where federal policy stalled: California's vehicle standards and cap-and-trade programme, the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative in the Northeast, and renewable portfolio standards in many states have driven substantial emissions cuts in the power sector.
