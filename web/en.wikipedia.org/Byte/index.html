Byte

Byte
SymbolB
Composition8 bits (in modern usage)
Values0–255 (unsigned, for an 8-bit byte)
Coined1956, by Werner Buchholz

The byte is a unit of digital information that most commonly consists of eight bits. Historically, the byte was the number of bits used to encode a single character of text in a computer, and for this reason it is the smallest addressable unit of memory in many computer architectures. The term was coined by Werner Buchholz in 1956 during the design of the IBM Stretch computer; the spelling "byte" was chosen to avoid accidental mutation to "bit".

Size and history

Early computers used bytes of various sizes — six, seven or nine bits were common — but the eight-bit byte became dominant with the IBM System/360 architecture of the 1960s and the rise of ASCII text encoding and microprocessors in the 1970s. Because the word "byte" was not originally guaranteed to mean eight bits, the term octet is used in standards (especially in networking and in French) for an unambiguous eight-bit unit. Half of a byte — four bits — is a nibble.

Multiples

Larger quantities are expressed with SI and binary prefixes. The kilobyte (kB) is 1,000 bytes in SI usage, while the kibibyte (KiB) is 1,024 bytes; similarly the megabyte (MB) and mebibyte (MiB), gigabyte and gibibyte, and so on. Confusion between the two conventions has historically caused discrepancies in the stated capacities of storage devices and operating systems, and the IEC binary prefixes were introduced to resolve it.
