Type II supernova

The expanding remnant of a massive star's core-collapse explosion
ClassCore-collapse supernova
ProgenitorMassive star, > 8 solar masses
Spectral signatureHydrogen lines present
RemnantNeutron star or black hole

A Type II supernova is a type of supernova that occurs when a massive star (at least 8–10 times the mass of the Sun) exhausts its nuclear fuel and its core collapses under gravity. Type II supernovae are distinguished by the presence of hydrogen in their spectra, reflecting the star's retained hydrogen envelope.

As fusion in the core proceeds through progressively heavier elements, an inert iron core accumulates. Once the core exceeds the effective Chandrasekhar mass, electron degeneracy pressure can no longer support it and it collapses in less than a second, heating to billions of degrees. The collapse halts when the core reaches nuclear density, producing a proto-neutron star and a shock wave; the explosion is powered largely by the enormous flux of neutrinos released, which revives the shock and ejects the outer layers.

Classification

Type II supernovae are subdivided by their light curves: Type II-P supernovae show a plateau of roughly constant brightness lasting about 100 days, while Type II-L decline more linearly. Type IIn supernovae show narrow emission lines from interaction with circumstellar material, and Type IIb events (such as SN 1993J) lose most of their hydrogen envelope and evolve toward a Type Ib appearance. The famous SN 1987A in the Large Magellanic Cloud was an unusual Type II supernova whose neutrinos were detected on Earth.
