Ground source heat pump

A ground source heat pump (also geothermal heat pump, or GSHP) is a heating and cooling system that transfers heat to or from the ground. It uses the earth as a heat source in winter and a heat sink in summer, taking advantage of the moderate, stable temperatures below the surface. Ground source heat pumps are among the most energy-efficient technologies for heating and cooling buildings.

How it works

A few metres below the surface, ground temperature remains roughly constant year-round (about 7–13 °C in temperate climates). A heat pump circulates a water–antifreeze mixture through buried pipes (the ground loop), exchanging heat with the soil or groundwater. In winter the fluid absorbs heat from the ground and the heat pump concentrates it to warm the building; in summer the cycle reverses, rejecting heat into the cooler ground. Because the pump moves heat rather than generating it by combustion or resistance, it typically delivers three to five units of heat for each unit of electricity consumed (a coefficient of performance of 3–5).

Ground loop configurations

Closed loops may be installed horizontally in trenches about 1–2 m deep, vertically in boreholes 50–150 m deep, or submerged in a pond or lake. Open-loop systems draw groundwater from a well, pass it through a heat exchanger, and discharge it to a second well or surface water. Vertical boreholes are common where land area is limited.

Advantages and drawbacks

GSHPs have low running costs and emissions, long service lives (indoor components often 20–25 years, ground loops 50 years or more), and provide both heating and cooling. Their main drawback is high installation cost, especially drilling, though operating savings and government incentives can offset this over time. Modern systems are often combined with underfloor heating or low-temperature radiators for best efficiency.
