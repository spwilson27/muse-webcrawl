Reinforced concrete (RC) is a composite material in which concrete's relatively low tensile strength and ductility are compensated for by the inclusion of reinforcement having higher tensile strength or ductility. The reinforcement is usually steel reinforcing bars (rebar) embedded passively before the concrete sets. Reinforced concrete is the most widely used construction material in the world.

Principle

Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension — its tensile strength is roughly a tenth of its compressive strength. Embedding steel bars where tensile stresses occur lets the two materials act together: the concrete carries compression, the steel carries tension, and bond between them (improved by deformations rolled into the bars) transfers the load. The materials are compatible because steel and concrete have nearly equal coefficients of thermal expansion, and the alkaline environment of fresh concrete passivates the steel against corrosion.

History

Experiments with embedding iron in concrete began in the 19th century: Joseph-Louis Lambot built a reinforced boat in 1848, and Joseph Monier patented iron-reinforced flowerpots and structural elements from 1867. François Hennebique developed an integrated system of reinforced concrete construction in the 1890s, and by the early 20th century reinforced concrete had become a principal material for high-rise buildings, bridges, and industrial structures, championed by architects such as Auguste Perret and Le Corbusier.

Design and construction

Reinforcement is designed from structural analysis of bending, shear, and axial loads, and detailed in codes such as Eurocode 2 and ACI 318. Steel is placed in cages within formwork, with a specified depth of concrete "cover" to protect it from corrosion and fire; the concrete is then poured, compacted, and cured. Variants include prestressed concrete, in which the steel is tensioned to pre-compress the concrete, and fibre-reinforced concrete, which uses dispersed short fibres.

Durability

The chief durability threats are carbonation of the cover concrete and chloride ingress (from de-icing salts or seawater), which can depassivate the steel and allow it to rust; the expansive corrosion products crack and spall the cover. Alkali–silica reaction, freeze–thaw cycles, and sulfate attack also degrade the material. Proper mix design, adequate cover, and curing are the main defences.
