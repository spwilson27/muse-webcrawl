Thalamus

The thalamus (from Greek thalamos, "chamber") is a large mass of grey matter located in the dorsal part of the diencephalon of the brain, on either side of the third ventricle. Its best-known function is as a relay and processing station: with the exception of the sense of smell, every sensory pathway passes through the thalamus on its way to the cerebral cortex. The thalamus also participates in motor control, consciousness, sleep, alertness, and memory.

Anatomy

The two thalami are egg-shaped structures about 3 cm long, joined in most people by the interthalamic adhesion. Each is divided into groups of nuclei — anterior, medial, lateral, posterior, and the laminated structures of the internal medullary lamina — including the lateral and medial geniculate nuclei (relaying vision and hearing respectively), the ventral posterolateral and posteromedial nuclei (somatosensation), and the mediodorsal and pulvinar nuclei (association functions).

Function

Thalamic relay neurons transmit sensory information to the cortex but are heavily modulated by feedback from the cortex itself and by the thalamic reticular nucleus; the thalamus thus "gates" sensory flow according to attention and arousal state. Intralaminar and midline nuclei contribute to consciousness, and thalamic damage can produce sensory loss, pain syndromes (thalamic pain syndrome), disorders of consciousness, or memory deficits resembling Korsakoff syndrome.

Clinical significance

Thalamic stroke, usually from occlusion of thalamogeniculate or paramedian arteries, produces characteristic syndromes combining sensory disturbance, weakness, and cognitive changes. Deep brain stimulation of thalamic targets is used to treat essential tremor and some seizure disorders.
