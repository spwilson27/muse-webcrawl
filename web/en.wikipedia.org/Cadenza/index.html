Cadenza

In music, a cadenza is, generically, an improvised or written-out ornamental passage played or sung by a soloist, usually in a "free" rhythmic style, allowing virtuosic display. Cadenzas are most familiar from concertos, where the orchestra pauses on a cadential chord while the soloist performs alone.

History

In the Baroque and Classical eras, cadenzas were improvised by the performer at a marked point, typically near the end of the first movement, over a held dominant or tonic six-four chord. Mozart and Beethoven began writing cadenzas out; Beethoven's written cadenza for his Piano Concerto No. 4 and the cadenza integrated into his Violin Concerto mark a shift toward composed cadenzas. Later composers such as Mendelssohn, Tchaikovsky and Rachmaninoff supplied their own, though performers sometimes substitute famous alternatives, such as Kreisler's or Joachim's cadenzas for standard violin concertos.

Characteristics

A cadenza typically develops themes from the movement, extends into brilliant passagework, trills and arpeggios, and ends with a conventional signal — often a long trill — cueing the orchestra's return.
