Structural film

Structural film is an avant-garde film movement prominent in the United States in the 1960s and early 1970s. The term was coined by the critic P. Adams Sitney in his 1969 essay "Structural Film", published in Film Culture. Structural films foreground the material properties of the film medium itself — grain, flicker, loops, rephotography off a screen, and fixed camera positions — rather than narrative or representational content.

Sitney's definition

Sitney identified four formal characteristics, rarely all present in a single film: (1) a fixed camera position; (2) the flicker effect; (3) loop printing; and (4) rephotography off a screen. The overall shape or structure of the film — often simple, predetermined and even monotonous — takes precedence over any content, which may be minimal or incidental. Sitney's approach drew criticism for formalism, and he later expanded the discussion in his book Visionary Film (1974).

Key works

  • Wavelength (1967), Michael Snow — a 45-minute slow zoom across a loft, often considered the movement's masterpiece.
  • Tom, Tom, the Piper's Son (1969), Ken Jacobs — an extended rephotography study of a 1905 film.
  • T,O,U,C,H,I,N,G (1968) and Nostalgia (1971), Paul Sharits and Hollis Frampton respectively.
  • Serene Velocity (1970), Ernie Gehr — fixed camera in a corridor, alternating focal lengths.
  • Zorns Lemma (1970), Hollis Frampton — a structural alphabet of images and text.

Structural/materialist film

In Britain, filmmakers associated with the London Film-Makers' Co-operative, notably Peter Gidal and Malcolm Le Grice, developed a politicised variant called structural/materialist film, which insisted on the anti-illusionist materiality of film and attacked narrative cinema ideologically. In Austria, Peter Kubelka's metric films of the 1950s–60s were important precursors.
