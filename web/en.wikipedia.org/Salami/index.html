Salami

Sliced salami with visible fat marbling.

Salami is a type of cured sausage consisting of fermented and air-dried meat, typically pork. Historically, salami was popular among Southern, Eastern, and Central European peasants because it can be stored at room temperature for up to 45 days once cut, supplementing a potentially meagre or inconsistent supply of fresh meat. The name comes from the Italian salame, from sale ("salt"), with the suffix -ame indicating a collective noun.

Production

Traditional salami is made from a mixture of ground meat (usually pork, sometimes beef or game), salt, and spices such as garlic, black pepper, fennel, and wine. The mixture is stuffed into a casing, fermented with lactic acid bacteria, and hung to dry for weeks or months. During fermentation the pH drops, inhibiting pathogens, and a white mould coat of Penicillium species often forms on the casing, protecting the sausage.

Varieties

There are many regional varieties. Italian types include Genoa salami, soppressata (often spicy, from Calabria), finocchiona (flavoured with fennel seed, from Tuscany), and felino. Hungary produces the famous téliszalámi ("winter salami") of Szeged and Budapest. France makes saucisson sec, Germany various Dauerwurst, and Spain the related salchichón. In the United States, salami features in Italian-American delicatessens and sandwiches.

Nutrition

Salami is energy-dense and high in fat and sodium; like other processed meats, it is classified by the World Health Organization's IARC as a Group 1 carcinogen with respect to colorectal cancer when consumed in quantity.
