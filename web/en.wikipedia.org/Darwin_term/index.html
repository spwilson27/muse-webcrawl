Darwin term

In quantum electrodynamics, the Darwin term is a correction to the effective interaction potential of an electron with an atomic nucleus, first derived by Charles Galton Darwin in 1928 using the Dirac equation. Together with the relativistic kinetic-energy correction and spin–orbit coupling, it is one of the three leading contributions to the fine structure of hydrogen-like atoms.

Form

For an electron of mass m and charge −e in a potential V(r), the Darwin term takes the form:

HDarwin = (ħ²/8m²c²) ∇²V = −(ħ²/8m²c²) 4π ρ(r)

where ρ is the charge density of the source. For a point nucleus of charge Ze, ∇²V is proportional to a Dirac delta function δ³(r), so the term contributes only for states with nonzero probability density at the nucleus — that is, s-orbitals (ℓ = 0).

Physical interpretation

The term is commonly interpreted as arising from Zitterbewegung, the rapid trembling motion of the relativistic electron over a region of size comparable to the Compton wavelength. Because of this jitter, the electron effectively "smears" the Coulomb potential over a small volume; for l = 0 states, which penetrate to the nucleus, the averaged potential differs from the point value, shifting the energy level. The effect raises the energy of s-states.

Role in fine structure

Summing the three order-(Zα)⁴ corrections — the relativistic correction to the kinetic energy, spin–orbit coupling, and the Darwin term — reproduces the fine-structure formula obtained directly from the Dirac equation for hydrogenic atoms. The Darwin term contributes only to states with ℓ = 0, exactly compensating the corresponding absence of spin–orbit energy for those states.
