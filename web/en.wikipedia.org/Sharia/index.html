Sharia (Arabic: شَرِيعَة, sharīʿa) is the body of religious law forming part of the Islamic tradition, derived primarily from the Quran and the Sunnah — the teachings and practices of the Prophet Muhammad preserved in Hadith — and elaborated by jurists through consensus (ijmaʿ) and analogical reasoning (qiyas). It covers ritual worship, family law, commerce, crime, and ethics, and remains influential in the legal systems of many Muslim-majority countries, in forms that range from personal-status law to full constitutional frameworks.

Sources and method

Classical legal theory (usul al-fiqh) ranks the Quran and Sunnah as primary sources, supplemented by scholarly consensus and analogy, with some schools also accepting juristic preference or public interest. Jurists distinguished sharia — God's ideal law — from fiqh, the fallible human understanding of it. Judgments classify acts into five categories: obligatory, recommended, neutral, discouraged, and forbidden.

Fields of law

Fiqh manuals treat ritual purity and prayer, fasting, almsgiving (zakat) and pilgrimage (hajj); marriage, divorce and inheritance — where fixed Quranic shares apply; contracts, property, and prohibitions such as riba (usury) that shape Islamic finance; and criminal law, including hudud punishments fixed by the texts, qisas (retaliation for homicide and injury), and discretionary taʿzir penalties.

Modern application

Colonial-era reforms displaced sharia courts in many places, confining them to family matters. Since the late 20th century some states — among them Saudi Arabia, Iran, and Pakistan — have expanded or reasserted sharia-based law, while others, such as Turkey, maintain secular codes. Application varies widely by country and school, and sharia is a frequent subject of political and human-rights debate in both Muslim and Western societies.
