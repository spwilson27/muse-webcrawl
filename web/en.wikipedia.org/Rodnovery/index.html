Rodnovery

Rodnovery, also known as the Slavic Native Faith, is a modern Pagan religion that reconstructs and revives the pre-Christian beliefs and practices of the Slavic peoples. The name derives from the Slavic words rodnaya ("native") and vera ("faith"). Adherents — Rodnovers — worship the traditional Slavic gods such as Perun, Veles, Svarog, Mokosh, and Rod, and celebrate seasonal festivals tied to the agricultural and solar calendar, including Kupala Night and Maslenitsa.

History

Slavic native-faith ideas emerged in the 19th and early 20th centuries within Romantic nationalism and folklore studies in Eastern Europe. Organised movements formed in Poland and Ukraine in the interwar period and in the Soviet Union's later decades through dissident and nationalist circles. After the fall of communism in 1989–1991, Rodnovery grew substantially in Russia, Ukraine, Poland, and other Slavic countries, and many organisations obtained official registration as religious bodies.

Beliefs and practices

Rodnovery is generally polytheistic and nature-oriented, emphasising reverence for ancestors, community, and the land. Practice varies widely between groups: some reconstruct rituals from historical and ethnographic sources, while others incorporate folk magic, runes-like symbols (such as the kolovrat), and invented traditions. The controversial "Book of Veles", purportedly an ancient text, is accepted as scripture by some groups and regarded as a forgery by scholars and other Rodnovers.

Demographics and debates

Estimates of adherents vary widely; censuses and surveys suggest tens of thousands of practitioners in Russia, with smaller communities elsewhere and in the Slavic diaspora. Scholars have debated the movement's relationship to nationalism: while many groups focus on heritage and ecology, some factions have been associated with far-right ideology, and observers distinguish between these strands.
