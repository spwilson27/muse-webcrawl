The Uruguay Round was the eighth round of multilateral trade negotiations conducted within the framework of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT).

The Uruguay Round was the eighth round of multilateral trade negotiations (MTN) conducted within the framework of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), spanning from 1986 to 1993 and embracing 123 countries as "contracting parties". The Round led to the creation of the World Trade Organization (WTO), with GATT remaining as an integral part of WTO agreements. The broad mandate of the Round had been to extend GATT trade rules to areas previously exempted as too difficult to liberalize (agriculture, textiles) and increasingly important new areas previously not included (trade in services, intellectual property, investment policy trade distortions).

The Round came into effect in 1995 with deadlines ending in 2000 (2004 in the case of developing country contracting parties) under the administrative direction of the newly created WTO.

Goals

The aims of the Uruguay Round, as stated in its objectives, were:

  • to reduce agricultural subsidies;
  • to lift restrictions on foreign investment;
  • to begin the process of opening trade in services — like banking and insurance;
  • to include a code to deal with the violation of intellectual property.

History

Launching

The Round was launched at a meeting of the GATT contracting parties in Punta del Este, Uruguay, in September 1986. The resulting Ministerial Declaration set out a negotiating mandate covering trade in goods, agriculture, services, and intellectual property — subjects well beyond the traditional GATT focus on tariffs on manufactured goods.

Negotiations and deadlock

Negotiations were expected to conclude within four years, but they repeatedly stalled, above all over agriculture, where the European Community and the United States took sharply opposed positions on farm subsidies and export support. A ministerial meeting in Montreal in December 1988 deadlocked, and a further attempt to conclude the Round at Brussels in December 1990 collapsed over the same issue. In April 1989, at a mid-term review in Geneva, negotiators agreed the framework of an agreement on agriculture, textiles, safeguards and services.

In December 1991, GATT Director-General Arthur Dunkel tabled a comprehensive draft — the "Dunkel Draft" — proposing compromises across all negotiating areas. Agriculture remained the most divisive area, and progress was slow through 1992. A breakthrough between the United States and the European Union on agriculture, reached at Blair House in Washington in November 1992, cleared the way for final negotiations.

Conclusion

The Round was finally completed on 15 December 1993. The Final Act was signed by ministers of most of the 123 participating governments on 15 April 1994 at a meeting in Marrakesh, Morocco. The agreements entered into force on 1 January 1995 with the establishment of the WTO.

Achievements

The Uruguay Round agreements included:

  • the creation of the World Trade Organization as a permanent institution with a strengthened dispute-settlement system;
  • GATT 1994, incorporating the original GATT with the Round's tariff reductions — average tariffs on manufactured goods fell to about 3.8 percent;
  • the Agreement on Agriculture, beginning the process of converting quotas and other non-tariff barriers into tariffs and reducing export subsidies;
  • the phasing out of the Multifibre Arrangement quotas on textiles and clothing over ten years;
  • the General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS), the first multilateral framework for services trade;
  • the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS), setting minimum standards for patents, copyrights and trademarks;
  • the Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures (TRIMs).

Criticism

Opponents of economic globalization charged that the Round's agreements, particularly TRIPS, benefited multinational corporations and wealthy countries at the expense of developing nations, for example by raising the price of patented medicines. Others argued that special and differential treatment provisions for developing countries were too weak, and that promised gains in agriculture and textiles were slow to materialize.
