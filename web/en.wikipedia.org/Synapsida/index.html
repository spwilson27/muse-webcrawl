Synapsida is a group of animals that includes mammals and every animal more closely related to mammals than to the other living amniotes (reptiles and birds). Synapsids are distinguished by a single opening, the temporal fenestra, low in the skull behind each eye, which anchored powerful jaw muscles. They were the dominant large land animals of the Permian period and include the only tetrapods to evolve hair, lactation, and warm-blooded metabolism.

Evolutionary history

Synapsids diverged from sauropsids in the Late Carboniferous, about 318 million years ago; among the earliest known is Archaeothyris from the coal forests of Nova Scotia. The early "pelycosaur" grade included the sail-backed herbivore Edaphosaurus and the apex predator Dimetrodon — popularly mistaken for dinosaurs, though they died out tens of millions of years before dinosaurs appeared. In the middle Permian the therapsids replaced pelycosaurs as the dominant fauna: dicynodont herbivores, gorgonopsian predators, therocephalians, and the small, increasingly mammal-like cynodonts.

Survival and mammals

The end-Permian mass extinction, the largest in Earth's history, devastated synapsid diversity; the dicynodont Lystrosaurus famously dominated Early Triassic landscapes. During the Mesozoic, surviving cynodont descendants gave rise to true mammals by the Late Triassic or Jurassic, while dinosaurs dominated large-animal niches. Synapsids retook dominance of terrestrial ecosystems only after the end-Cretaceous extinction 66 million years ago, and today the roughly 6,400 living mammal species are the sole surviving synapsids.
