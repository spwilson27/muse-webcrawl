Te Deum

The Te Deum (from the Latin Te Deum laudamus, "Thee, O God, we praise") is a Latin Christian hymn traditionally ascribed to Saints Ambrose and Augustine, composed on the occasion of Augustine's baptism in 387 AD. It is a hymn of praise to God the Father and Christ, sung in the Roman Catholic Church at the end of the Office of Readings on major feasts, on occasions of public thanksgiving, and by other Western churches including the Anglican Communion, where it forms part of Morning Prayer.

Text and structure

The hymn falls into three parts: a hymn to God the Father, joining the praise of angels, apostles, prophets, and martyrs; a hymn to Christ the Redeemer; and a series of supplications drawn partly from the Psalms. Its opening verses echo the Sanctus of the Mass. In the traditional legend, Ambrose and Augustine improvised the hymn antiphonally at the font; modern scholarship generally dates the text to the early 5th century and associates it with Niceta of Remesiana.

Musical settings

The Te Deum has been set to music by many composers, for coronations, victories, and state thanksgivings. Famous settings include those by Handel (the Utrecht and Dettingen Te Deums), Charpentier (whose prelude serves as the Eurovision theme), Haydn, Mozart, Berlioz, Verdi, Bruckner, Kodály, and Arvo Pärt. In England, Te Deums were composed for national thanksgiving services by Purcell, Handel, Stanford, and Walton, among others.

Use

Beyond the liturgy, "Te Deum" became shorthand for a service of public thanksgiving; Te Deums were sung to celebrate military victories and treaties across early modern Europe, and the hymn is still sung at the close of conclave celebrations and on New Year's Eve at the Vatican, when a plenary indulgence is traditionally attached to its public recitation.
