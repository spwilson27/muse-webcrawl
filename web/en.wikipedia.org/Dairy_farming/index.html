Dairy farming

Dairy farming is a class of agriculture for the long-term production of milk, which is processed (either on the farm or at a dairy plant, both of which may be called a dairy) for the eventual sale of a dairy product. Dairy farming has a history that goes back to the early Neolithic era, around the seventh millennium BC, in many regions including Europe and Africa. Before the 20th century, milking was done by hand on small farms.

History

Humans have kept animals for milk since the domestication of cattle, sheep, and goats. Large-scale dairying became possible with rail transport, refrigeration, and mechanized milking in the late 19th and 20th centuries. The milking machine, patented in various forms from the 19th century and widely adopted by the mid-20th, transformed herd sizes and labor requirements. Twentieth-century advances in artificial insemination, feed science, and veterinary medicine raised milk yields dramatically.

Production

Most modern dairy farms keep Holstein Friesian cows for their high milk yields, though breeds such as Jersey, Ayrshire, and Brown Swiss are also common. Cows typically begin producing milk after their first calving at about two years of age and are milked two or three times daily, sometimes by robotic milking systems. A high-producing cow may yield over 10,000 litres per lactation. India is the world's largest milk producer, followed by the United States, with the European Union collectively a major producer and exporter.

Processing and products

Raw milk is cooled, transported to processing plants, pasteurized, and made into fluid milk, cheese, butter, yogurt, ice cream, and milk powders. Quality is monitored for bacterial counts and somatic cell counts, and in many countries the use of growth hormones such as rBST is restricted or banned.

Environmental and welfare issues

Dairy farming is a significant source of greenhouse gas emissions, particularly methane from enteric fermentation and manure, and of water pollution from nutrient runoff. Animal welfare concerns include lameness, mastitis, early separation of calves, and the fate of male calves. These concerns have driven growth in pasture-based systems, welfare certification schemes, and plant-based milk alternatives.
