Thyroid hormone

FunctionRegulation of metabolism, growth and development

The thyroid hormones are two hormones produced and released by the thyroid gland, namely triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4). They are tyrosine-based hormones that are primarily responsible for regulation of metabolism. T3 and T4 are partially composed of iodine, and a deficiency of iodine leads to decreased production and enlargement of the thyroid (goitre).

Production and release

The thyroid gland produces mainly T4, which is relatively inactive, under stimulation by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from the anterior pituitary, itself controlled by thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) from the hypothalamus. Iodide is taken up by follicular cells and incorporated into the protein thyroglobulin, whose tyrosine residues are iodinated and coupled to form the hormones, stored in the follicular colloid until release.

Conversion and transport

Most T4 is converted in peripheral tissues by deiodinase enzymes to the active T3, which is several times more potent. In the blood, the hormones circulate bound mainly to thyroxine-binding globulin, transthyretin, and albumin; only a small free fraction is biologically active.

Effects

Thyroid hormones increase basal metabolic rate, stimulate protein synthesis and the breakdown of carbohydrates and fats, promote growth and maturation of the nervous system in infancy, and sensitize tissues to catecholamines, raising heart rate and contractility. They act largely through nuclear thyroid hormone receptors that regulate gene transcription.

Clinical significance

Overproduction causes hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis), while underproduction causes hypothyroidism, treated with synthetic levothyroxine. Hormone levels, together with TSH, are routinely measured to assess thyroid function.
