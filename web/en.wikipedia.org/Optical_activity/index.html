Optical activity

Optical activity is the rotation of the plane of linear polarisation of light as it travels through certain materials. It occurs in chiral substances whose molecules are not superposable on their mirror images, including many sugars, amino acids and other biomolecules, as well as in chiral crystals such as quartz. Substances that rotate light clockwise are called dextrorotatory (+), and those that rotate it anticlockwise levorotatory (−).

Principles

Plane-polarised light can be viewed as a superposition of left- and right-circularly polarised components. A chiral medium refracts the two components at different speeds (circular birefringence), so their phase relationship — and hence the polarisation plane — changes along the path. The observed rotation depends on the path length, concentration, wavelength and temperature, and is quantified as specific rotation, [α].

History and applications

Jean-Baptiste Biot established the phenomenon's laws in the 1810s. In 1848 Louis Pasteur separated tartaric acid crystals by hand into left- and right-handed forms, founding stereochemistry and demonstrating the link between molecular asymmetry and optical rotation. Today polarimetry is used to measure sugar concentration in industry, to characterise pharmaceuticals (where one enantiomer may be therapeutic and the other inactive or harmful), and in fundamental physics; optical rotation also underlies phenomena such as the Faraday effect's non-reciprocal cousin in chiral media.
