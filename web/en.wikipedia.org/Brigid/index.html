Brigid

Brigid or Brigit (Old Irish: Brigit [ˈbʲɾʲiʝidʲ]; Modern Irish: Bríd) is a figure of Irish mythology and Christianity. As a goddess of pre-Christian Ireland, Brigid was a member of the Tuatha Dé Danann, associated with spring, fertility, healing, poetry and smithcraft, with wisdom, and with the dawn. Through syncretism she is widely identified with Saint Brigid of Kildare (c. 451–525), one of Ireland's patron saints, whose feast on 1 February corresponds to the Gaelic festival of Imbolc. Her cult spread to Scotland, Wales (as Saint Ffraid) and continental Europe, and she is venerated today by Christians and honoured in modern Paganism (Neopaganism) alike.

The goddess

In medieval Irish texts such as the Lebor Gabála Érenn and the glossary Sanas Cormaic, Brigid is the daughter of the Dagda and a poet herself; Cormac's Glossary describes her as "a goddess whom poets worshipped" and assigns her two sisters also named Brigid — associated with healing and smithcraft — leading scholars to see a triple goddess. Her name derives from Proto-Celtic *Brigantī, "the Exalted One", cognate with the British goddess Brigantia and the Sanskrit bṛhatī; sanctuaries and place names across Celtic Europe (Bregenz, Brigantium) bear related names. Brigid is credited in legend with inventing keening, and her festival Imbolc (1 February) marked the beginning of spring and the lactation of ewes.

Saint Brigid of Kildare

The saint's traditional biography, in Cogitosus's 7th-century Vita and later lives, makes her the daughter of a pagan chieftain and a Christian slave, born at Faughart, County Louth. She founded the monastery of Kildare — a double monastery of men and women — where a sacred perpetual flame was tended by nuns, echoing pre-Christian fire cults; Gerald of Wales described the flame still burning in the 12th century, and a flame was relit in Kildare in 1993 and is kept by the Brigidine Sisters at Solas Bhríde. Her recorded miracles include multiplying food for the poor, healing, and the miraculous expansion of her cloak to claim land for her monastery. With Patrick and Columba, she is one of the three patron saints of Ireland, and her feast is marked by weaving Brigid's crosses of rushes and visiting holy wells. In 2022 the Irish government made St Brigid's Day (the Monday nearest 1 February) a public holiday — the first Irish public holiday named after a woman.

Historicity and legacy

Scholars debate whether Saint Brigid was a historical person whose cult absorbed the goddess's attributes, or the goddess herself Christianised wholesale; the near-total overlap of names, feast and associations makes some absorption certain either way. Brigid remains a potent cultural figure: patroness of poets, healers, midwives, blacksmiths, fugitives and dairymaids, and a symbol claimed by Irish nationalism, feminism and modern Druidry and Wicca, in which she is one of the most widely honoured deities of the Wheel of the Year.
