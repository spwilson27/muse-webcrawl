Air-to-air missile

An air-to-air missile (AAM) is a guided missile fired from an aircraft for the purpose of destroying another aircraft or missile. Air-to-air missiles are typically powered by rocket motors, fly at supersonic speeds, and home on their targets using infrared, radar, or laser guidance. They are the primary weapon of modern air combat.

Guidance types

Infrared-homing ("heat-seeking") missiles track the heat of an engine's exhaust and are usually short-ranged; examples include the AIM-9 Sidewinder. Radar-guided missiles may be semi-active, following reflections from the launch aircraft's radar, or active, carrying their own radar seeker — as in the AIM-120 AMRAAM — enabling "fire-and-forget" engagements beyond visual range.

History

The first guided air-to-air missiles appeared at the end of the Second World War in German projects such as the Ruhrstahl X-4. The United States fielded the AIM-4 Falcon and AIM-9 Sidewinder in the 1950s; early combat use over Vietnam in the 1960s exposed reliability problems that drove major improvements in seekers and pilot training. Subsequent generations added all-aspect infrared seekers, helmet-mounted cueing, and imaging seekers resistant to flares.

Performance

Modern beyond-visual-range missiles can engage targets more than 100 km away and manoeuvre at tens of g, while the "no-escape zone" describes the region in which a target cannot outrun the missile. Countermeasures include chaff, flares, towed decoys, and electronic jamming.
