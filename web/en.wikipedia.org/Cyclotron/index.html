Cyclotron

A cyclotron is a type of particle accelerator invented by Ernest Lawrence in 1929–1930 at the University of California, Berkeley, and patented in 1934. A cyclotron accelerates charged particles outwards from the center of a flat cylindrical vacuum chamber along a spiral path. The particles are held to a spiral trajectory by a static magnetic field and accelerated by a rapidly varying electric field.

Principle of operation

Particles are injected at the center of the chamber, where two hollow semicircular electrodes (called "dees" because of their shape) enclose them. An alternating voltage applied between the dees accelerates the particles each time they cross the gap. The magnetic field, perpendicular to the plane of motion, bends the particles into a circular path; because the revolution frequency of a non-relativistic particle in a uniform magnetic field is independent of its radius, the particles stay in step with the fixed-frequency accelerating field as they spiral outward.

History

Lawrence conceived the cyclotron in 1929 after reading a paper by Rolf Widerøe on linear acceleration. With graduate student M. Stanley Livingston, he built the first working device in 1930–1931; early machines were small enough to hold in one hand. Larger cyclotrons at Berkeley's Radiation Laboratory produced beams used in nuclear physics and in the discovery of several transuranium elements. Lawrence received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1939 for the invention.

Limitations and successors

As particles approach relativistic speeds, their effective mass increases and they fall out of step with the accelerating field. Modifications such as the synchrocyclotron and the isochronous cyclotron addressed this, while the synchrotron became the design of choice for the highest energies.

Uses

Cyclotrons are used in nuclear physics research, materials science, and the production of radioisotopes for medical imaging and proton therapy.
