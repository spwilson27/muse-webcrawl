Microcirculation

The microcirculation is the circulation of blood in the smallest blood vessels, the microvessels of the microvasculature present within organ tissues. It comprises the terminal arterioles, capillaries and venules, and is where the exchange of gases, nutrients and waste products between blood and tissue takes place.

Structure

Blood enters the microcirculation through arterioles, which regulate flow by vasoconstriction and vasodilation, and passes through metarterioles into networks of capillaries. Capillary walls are a single endothelial cell thick, allowing diffusion of oxygen, carbon dioxide, water, ions and small molecules. Precapillary sphincters can open or close capillary beds according to local metabolic demand. Blood then drains into venules and back into veins.

Function and regulation

Besides exchange, the microcirculation regulates tissue perfusion and contributes to blood pressure control, thermoregulation (especially in the skin), inflammation — white blood cells leave the circulation through venule walls at inflamed sites — and the formation of tissue fluid and lymph. Starling forces, the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures across the capillary wall, govern fluid movement; disturbance of this balance causes oedema.

Clinical significance

Microcirculatory dysfunction is central to shock, sepsis, diabetes complications and peripheral vascular disease. Techniques such as capillaroscopy and laser Doppler flowmetry allow the microcirculation to be assessed clinically and in research.
