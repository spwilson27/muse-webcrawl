Publius Quinctilius Varus (c. 46 BC – September AD 9) was a Roman general and politician under the first emperor, Augustus. A member of the patrician gens Quinctilia, he held the consulship in 13 BC alongside the future emperor Tiberius, governed Africa and then Syria — where his harsh suppression of revolts after the death of Herod the Great earned him a reputation for severity and greed — and in AD 7 was appointed to govern the newly organised territory of Germania east of the Rhine. He is remembered above all for the catastrophic defeat of his army in the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest in AD 9.

Teutoburg Forest

In September AD 9, Varus was marching three legions (XVII, XVIII, and XIX), six cohorts of auxiliaries, and three squadrons of cavalry — perhaps 20,000 men — back to winter quarters when he was drawn into an ambush prepared by Arminius, a Cheruscan prince and Roman auxiliary commander he trusted. Over several days of fighting in wooded, rain-soaked terrain near modern Kalkriese, the Roman force was annihilated. Varus, seeing the battle lost, killed himself on his own sword, as did several of his senior officers. The lost eagles of the three legions were only partly recovered in later campaigns.

Aftermath and reputation

The disaster halted Rome's expansion into Germania; Augustus is said by Suetonius to have cried out, "Quinctilius Varus, give me back my legions!" (Quintili Vare, legiones redde!), and to have let his hair and beard grow in mourning. Roman historians such as Velleius Paterculus and Cassius Dio portrayed Varus as negligent and complacent — a civilian administrator unsuited to command, who had provoked the Germans by taxing and Romanising them too abruptly — though modern scholarship notes that hostile tradition and the recovery of the Kalkriese battlefield have complicated that verdict. The defeat, the clades Variana ("Varian disaster"), remains one of the most famous in Roman history and shaped the cultural boundary between Latin and Germanic Europe.
