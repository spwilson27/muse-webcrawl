Economics

Economics is a social science that studies the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services. Economics focuses on the behaviour and interactions of economic agents and how economies work. Microeconomics analyses how agents—households and firms—make decisions to allocate limited resources; macroeconomics analyses the economy as a system, including inflation, economic growth, unemployment, and monetary and fiscal policy. A standard definition is Lionel Robbins's: "the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses."

Scope and method

Economists formulate theories and test them against data using statistical methods (econometrics). Economic reasoning emphasises scarcity, opportunity cost, incentives, and marginal analysis. Research distinguishes positive economics (what is) from normative economics (what ought to be), and uses models—mathematical simplifications—to predict and explain phenomena such as price formation in markets.

Microeconomics

Microeconomics studies supply and demand, market structures (perfect competition, monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition), consumer choice, production and cost, and market failures such as externalities, public goods, and information asymmetry. Key concepts include elasticity, consumer and producer surplus, and general equilibrium theory, formalised by Léon Walras and Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu.

Macroeconomics

Macroeconomics emerged from the Great Depression and John Maynard Keynes's The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936). It studies aggregate output (GDP), business cycles, inflation, unemployment, money and banking, exchange rates, and long-run economic growth. Schools include Keynesianism, monetarism (Milton Friedman), new classical economics, and new Keynesian economics; policy tools include monetary policy (central-bank interest rates and quantitative easing) and fiscal policy (government spending and taxation).

History of economic thought

Systematic economic analysis began with the physiocrats and Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776), followed by David Ricardo, Thomas Malthus, and John Stuart Mill (classical economics), Karl Marx's critique of capitalism, the marginal revolution of the 1870s (William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, Walras), Alfred Marshall's neoclassical synthesis, Keynes's macroeconomics, the post-war Keynesian consensus, and the monetarist and rational-expectations counter-revolutions. Behavioural economics, game theory, and development economics have reshaped the field since the mid-20th century.

Fields

Specialised fields include labour economics, international economics, public economics, health economics, environmental economics, financial economics, industrial organisation, economic history, econometrics, and game theory. Economists work in academia, government, central banks, international institutions such as the IMF and World Bank, and the private sector.
