Détournement

Détournement
OriginLetterist / Situationist International, 1950s
Key figuresGuy Debord, Gil J. Wolman
MeaningRe-routing or hijacking of pre-existing cultural material
RelatedCulture jamming, subvertising, appropriation art

Détournement (French for "rerouting" or "hijacking") is a technique developed by the Letterist International and refined by the Situationist International in the 1950s and 1960s, in which pre-existing artistic or media elements — advertisements, comic strips, films, famous paintings, photographs — are recombined or altered to create a new work with a different, often subversive or satirical, message. Guy Debord and Gil J. Wolman set out the method in their 1956 text "A User's Guide to Détournement".

Theory

For the Situationists, détournement was a weapon against the "spectacle" — the commodified flow of images in capitalist society. By tearing cultural fragments out of their original context and reassembling them, the artist could expose and undermine their ideological function. Debord distinguished "minor détournement", using elements with no importance of their own, from "deceptive détournement", which turns significant elements such as famous artworks or slogans against themselves. The Situationists opposed détournement to plagiarism as mere copying: détournement was openly acknowledged reuse in the service of critique, and they rejected copyright in their own publications.

Legacy

Détournement strongly influenced the punk movement's cut-and-paste graphics, culture jamming and subvertising by groups such as Adbusters, the Billboard Liberation Front, and the Yes Men, and later remix and mashup culture on the internet. Situationists complained of "recuperation" — the absorption of détournement techniques by advertising itself, which adopted subversive-looking imagery to sell products.
