Horizontal integration

Horizontal integration: expansion at the same stage of the supply chain

Horizontal integration is the process of a company increasing production of goods or services at the same part of the supply chain, typically by acquiring or merging with competitors in the same industry. A company that grows in this way is called horizontally integrated. The strategy contrasts with vertical integration, in which a firm expands into stages upstream or downstream of its own.

Advantages

Horizontal integration can yield economies of scale, increase market share and market power, reduce competition, and give access to new customers, products and distribution channels. Classic examples include John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, which bought out rival refiners in the nineteenth century, and modern mergers of airlines, banks and media companies.

Antitrust concerns

Because horizontal mergers directly remove competitors, they attract scrutiny under competition law. Regulators assess whether a proposed combination is likely to create or strengthen a monopoly or oligopoly; many jurisdictions apply concentration measures such as the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index when reviewing horizontal mergers.
