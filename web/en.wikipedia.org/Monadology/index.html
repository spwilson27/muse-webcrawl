Monadology


Author: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
Written: 1714
Published: 1720 (German), 1840 (French original)
Propositions: 90

The Monadology (French: La Monadologie) is one of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz's best-known works of his later philosophy, written in 1714. It consists of 90 short paragraphs presenting his metaphysical system of monads — simple, indivisible, "windowless" substances that are the ultimate constituents of reality.

Doctrine

Leibniz argues that because composites exist, there must be simple substances without parts: the monads. Monads have no extension, shape, or divisibility, and they cannot interact causally with one another — they "have no windows" — but each monad internally represents, or "mirrors", the entire universe from its own point of view, with varying degrees of clarity. The apparent harmony among them is explained by a pre-established harmony ordained by God, the supreme monad, who chose the "best of all possible worlds" from among infinitely many possibilities. Souls are monads with distinct perception and memory; rational minds can apprehend eternal truths.

Publication and influence

Leibniz wrote the work in French in 1714, traditionally said to be for Prince Eugene of Savoy. It was not published in his lifetime; a German translation appeared in 1720 and the French original only in 1840. The Monadology, together with the Discourse on Metaphysics and the Principles of Nature and Grace, remains a central text of rationalist metaphysics, and its claim that this is the best of all possible worlds was famously satirized by Voltaire in Candide.
