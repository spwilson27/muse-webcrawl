Thirty Years' War

Thirty Years' War

The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) was one of the most destructive conflicts in European history, fought primarily in Central Europe. It began as a war between Protestant and Catholic states within the fragmented Holy Roman Empire, but gradually developed into a more general conflict involving most of the European great powers, driven as much by dynastic rivalry — above all between Habsburg Austria and Spain and Bourbon France — as by religion.

Origins

The war was triggered by the Defenestration of Prague on 23 May 1618, when Protestant Bohemian nobles threw two imperial governors from a window of Prague Castle, and by the Bohemian estates' election of the Calvinist Frederick V of the Palatinate as king in place of the Catholic Ferdinand II. Imperial forces crushed the Bohemian revolt at the Battle of White Mountain in 1620.

Phases

Historians commonly divide the war into Bohemian (1618–1620), Danish (1625–1629), Swedish (1630–1635), and Franco-Swedish (1635–1648) phases. Denmark's intervention ended with the Treaty of Lübeck; Sweden under Gustavus Adolphus landed in Pomerania in 1630, won a great victory at Breitenfeld in 1631, and lost its king at Lützen in 1632. The imperial general Albrecht von Wallenstein was assassinated in 1634. After the Peace of Prague (1635), France under Cardinal Richelieu entered openly against the Habsburgs, and fighting continued until the exhaustion of all parties brought negotiations.

Peace of Westphalia

The war was ended by the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, a series of treaties signed at Münster and Osnabrück. The settlement reaffirmed the principle of cuius regio, eius religio from the Peace of Augsburg (1555), extended recognition to Calvinism, confirmed the independence of the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederacy, and granted the Empire's princes greater sovereignty. It is often seen as a foundational moment in the modern system of sovereign states.

Human cost

Famine, disease, and marauding armies devastated the German lands; some regions lost a third or more of their population. Modern estimates put total deaths at between 4.5 and 8 million, making the war one of the deadliest religious conflicts in history.
