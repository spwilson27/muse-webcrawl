English country house

An English country house is a large house or mansion in the English countryside. Such houses were often owned by individuals who also owned a town house, allowing them to spend time in the country surrounded by their estate, and to exercise local political and social influence as landowners, justices of the peace, and members of Parliament. Country houses range from fortified medieval manor houses to the great classical palaces of the eighteenth century, such as Blenheim Palace, Castle Howard, and Holkham Hall.

Development

After the dissolution of the monasteries and the decline of feudal warfare, defensible castles gave way to comfortable prodigy houses of the Elizabethan and Jacobean eras (Hardwick Hall, Hatfield House). The seventeenth century brought classical designs influenced by Inigo Jones; the eighteenth century saw the Palladian and later neoclassical country house set in landscaped parkland by designers such as Capability Brown, with Robert Adam's interiors inside. The nineteenth century added Gothic Revival and eclectic styles and vast numbers of smaller Victorian houses.

Life in the country house

At their height, great houses were run by large staffs of servants organised in a strict hierarchy under a housekeeper and butler, and functioned as centres of hospitality, hunting, shooting, and estate management. The "season" of country-house parties structured elite social life.

Decline

From the late nineteenth century, agricultural depression, death duties, and the losses of the First World War undermined the economics of country houses. Hundreds were demolished in the twentieth century, converted to schools, hotels, and institutions, or given to the National Trust. Surviving houses remain cultural icons, widely depicted in literature and film.
