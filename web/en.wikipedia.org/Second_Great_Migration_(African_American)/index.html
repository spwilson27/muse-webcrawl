Second Great Migration (African American)

New arrivals in Chicago, a principal destination of the Second Great Migration.

In the context of the 20th-century history of the United States, the Second Great Migration was the migration of more than five million African Americans from the rural South to the North, Midwest and West between 1940 and 1970. It was much larger than the First Great Migration (1916–1940), in which roughly 1.6 million migrants moved from mostly rural southern areas to northern industrial cities.

Causes

Migrants were driven by the collapse of sharecropping and the mechanisation of southern agriculture, by Jim Crow segregation and racial violence, and drawn by wartime and postwar demand for labour in defence industries and factories. Executive Order 8802 (1941), barring discrimination in defence employment, opened industrial jobs that had previously been closed to Black workers.

Destinations

Whereas the First Migration had concentrated on a few cities such as Chicago, Detroit, New York and Pittsburgh, the Second spread widely: Los Angeles, Oakland and Richmond in California; Seattle and Portland in the Pacific Northwest; and dozens of northern and western industrial centres grew large Black communities. California's Black population rose from about 124,000 in 1940 to over 2.2 million by 1970.

Effects

The migration transformed American cities, politics and culture. Migrants and their children gained greater access to industrial employment and, in northern and western states, the franchise, building the urban political base of the civil rights movement. At the same time they faced housing discrimination, restrictive covenants and redlining, which concentrated Black residents in segregated neighbourhoods. A reverse migration to the "New South" began in the late 20th century as conditions changed.
