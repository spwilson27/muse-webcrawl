Bestiary

A bestiary (from Latin bestiarum vocabulum) is a compendium of beasts — a medieval book describing animals, birds, and even mythical creatures and rocks, in which natural history information is interwoven with moral and religious allegory. Bestiaries were among the most popular illustrated books of the Middle Ages, particularly in England and France in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and they profoundly influenced medieval art and symbolism.

Origins and content

The bestiary tradition descends from the anonymous Greek Physiologus, composed in Alexandria probably in the second century AD, which interpreted the supposed habits of some fifty animals as Christian moral lessons — for example, the pelican wounding its breast to feed its young with its blood as a figure of Christ's sacrifice. Latin bestiaries expanded this core with material from Pliny the Elder, Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae, and other authorities, organizing entries under headings such as beasts, birds, serpents, worms, and fishes.

Famous examples

The Aberdeen Bestiary (c. 1200), now in Aberdeen University Library, and the Ashmole Bestiary are among the best-known surviving manuscripts, richly illuminated with gold leaf. Entries cover real animals (lion, elephant, beaver), creatures of classical legend (unicorn, phoenix, basilisk, dragon), and stones, each accompanied by an image and a moralizing interpretation.

Influence

Bestiaries shaped the iconography of medieval churches, where carved animals embodied virtues and vices, and they preserved — and distorted — knowledge of animals for a largely non-observational culture. In the modern era the term "bestiary" is used for any catalogue of creatures, especially in fantasy literature and games, and for contemporary literary works such as Jorge Luis Borges's Book of Imaginary Beings.
