Hacker culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The hacker culture is a subculture of individuals who enjoy — often in collective effort — the intellectual challenge of creatively overcoming the limitations of software systems or electronic hardware, to achieve novel and clever outcomes. The culture emerged in the 1960s among programmers at institutions such as MIT, and is associated with the development of the internet, the free and open-source software movements, and a distinctive jargon and ethos.

The hacker ethic

Steven Levy's 1984 book Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution codified an informal "hacker ethic": access to computers should be unlimited and total; all information should be free; authority should be mistrusted and decentralisation promoted; hackers should be judged by their hacking, not bogus criteria such as degrees or race; and computers can change the world for the better. Later formulations, such as Eric S. Raymond's writings and the Jargon File, documented the community's vocabulary, humour and values.

Subcultures

Distinct but overlapping hacker subcultures include the academic programmer culture, the hobbyist home computing scene of the 1970s, hardware hackers and makers, and the network-security community. Within the media, "hacker" is often used to mean a malicious intruder — a sense the programming community distinguishes with terms such as "cracker" or the hat colours (white hat, black hat, grey hat).

Institutions

Hackerspaces, conferences such as DEF CON and the Chaos Communication Congress, and events such as hackathons sustain the culture's social life, as do online communities and collaborative projects like Wikipedia itself.
