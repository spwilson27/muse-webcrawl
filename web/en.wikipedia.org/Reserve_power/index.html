Reserve power

For the auxiliary electrical supply, see Operating reserve.

Reserve powers are the discretionary powers of a head of state — typically a monarch, president, or governor-general in a parliamentary system — which may be exercised without, or contrary to, the advice of the executive or legislature in exceptional circumstances. They derive from constitutional conventions, letters patent, or the historical prerogative powers of the Crown.

Nature of the powers

In most constitutional monarchies and parliamentary republics, the head of state normally acts only on the advice of ministers responsible to the legislature. Reserve powers are an exception, retained as a safeguard for situations in which ministerial advice is absent, unconstitutional, or incapable of resolving a crisis. They commonly include the power to appoint a prime minister, to refuse a request for dissolution of parliament, to dismiss a government, and to withhold assent to legislation.

Examples

In the United Kingdom, the monarch's prerogative powers are almost always exercised on ministerial advice, but the personal prerogatives of appointing a prime minister and, in theory, refusing a dissolution remain. In Australia, the reserve powers of the governor-general were at the centre of the 1975 constitutional crisis, when Governor-General Sir John Kerr dismissed the government of Gough Whitlam. In Canada, the King–Byng affair of 1926 concerned the governor general's refusal of a dissolution request.

Controversy

Because reserve powers are usually unwritten and their limits uncertain, their use can be highly controversial, raising questions about democratic legitimacy. Codification in written constitutions — as in Ireland, Germany, and India — has tended to narrow or formalise them.
