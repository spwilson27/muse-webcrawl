Geolocation

Geolocation is the identification or estimation of the real-world geographic location of an object, such as a radar source, mobile phone, or Internet-connected computer terminal. In its broadest sense, geolocation is the set of processes by which a device or a person is positioned on the surface of the Earth, usually expressed as latitude and longitude coordinates.

A location pin marking a position determined by geolocation.

Methods

Modern geolocation relies on several complementary technologies. Satellite navigation systems such as the Global Positioning System (GPS), GLONASS, Galileo, and BeiDou compute position from the timing of signals transmitted by orbiting satellites. Where satellite signals are unavailable, devices may estimate position from nearby Wi-Fi access points, cellular tower triangulation, or IP address databases maintained by internet service providers.

Wi-Fi positioning compares the signal strengths of visible access points against large reference databases of their known locations. IP-based geolocation is coarser, typically resolving a user to a city or region rather than a street address. Hybrid systems combine all of these inputs, weighted by their estimated accuracy, to produce a single position fix.

Applications

Geolocation underpins turn-by-turn navigation, ride-hailing, location-based search, geotagging of photographs, asset tracking, emergency services dispatch, and targeted advertising. Web browsers expose a standard Geolocation API, governed by the W3C, that allows websites to request a visitor's position with permission.

Privacy

Because location data can reveal highly sensitive information about a person's movements, home, and workplace, geolocation has been a focus of privacy regulation. Operating systems generally require explicit user consent before sharing precise location, and laws such as the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation treat location data as personal data.
