Coordinated Universal Time

Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) is the primary time standard globally used to regulate clocks and time. It establishes a reference for the current time, forming the basis for civil time and time zones. UTC is obtained from International Atomic Time (TAI), with leap seconds added at irregular intervals to compensate for the accumulated difference between TAI and time measured by Earth's rotation (UT1).

Definition

UTC is maintained by the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) from a weighted average of hundreds of atomic clocks worldwide. Its seconds are identical to those of TAI, but the two scales differ by an integer number of seconds — the accumulated leap seconds — so that UTC never departs from UT1 by more than 0.9 seconds. The abbreviation UTC is a compromise between English "CUT" and French "TUC".

Leap seconds

Since 1972, leap seconds have been inserted (always on 30 June or 31 December) by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service as Earth's rotation lags. Twenty-seven leap seconds had been added by 2017, and none since. Because leap seconds complicate computing systems, the General Conference on Weights and Measures resolved in 2022 to increase the allowed tolerance by or before 2035, effectively suspending leap seconds for at least a century.

Time zones

Local civil time is expressed as an offset from UTC, such as UTC+01:00 or UTC−05:00. UTC itself is used directly in aviation (as "Zulu time"), weather forecasting, and computing, where unambiguous timestamps are essential. For most everyday purposes UTC is effectively equivalent to Greenwich Mean Time (GMT).

History

UTC was adopted in 1960 and refined to its present leap-second form in 1972. It succeeded GMT as the international reference and underlies standards including ISO 8601.
