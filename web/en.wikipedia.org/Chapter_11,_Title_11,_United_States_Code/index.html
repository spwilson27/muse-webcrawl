Chapter 11 of Title 11 of the United States Code (the Bankruptcy Code) governs the process of reorganization under U.S. bankruptcy law. Unlike Chapter 7 liquidation, Chapter 11 usually leaves the debtor — typically a business, though individuals may also file — in control of its operations as a "debtor in possession," while it proposes a plan to restructure debts and continue operating. It is the mechanism behind most major corporate bankruptcies in the United States, including those of airlines, retailers, automobile manufacturers, and Enron.

Key features

  • Automatic stay (§ 362) — filing immediately halts most collection actions, lawsuits, and foreclosures against the debtor.
  • Debtor in possession (DIP) — existing management usually retains control, acting as a fiduciary for creditors; a trustee is appointed only for cause (e.g., fraud or gross mismanagement).
  • Plan of reorganization — the debtor (initially with exclusivity) proposes a plan classifying claims; creditor classes vote, and the court confirms the plan if statutory requirements are met, including the "cramdown" option over a dissenting class if the plan is fair and equitable.
  • DIP financing and § 363 sales — new lenders may receive priority administrative claims; assets may be sold free and clear of liens, sometimes leading to sale of the whole business.
  • Discharge — confirmation generally discharges pre-confirmation debts as provided in the plan.

Process

A case begins with a voluntary petition (or, rarely, an involuntary one by creditors). Committees of unsecured creditors are commonly appointed. Claims are filed and classified, disclosure statement and plan solicited and voted on, and confirmation sought. Small businesses may use Subchapter V (added by the Small Business Reorganization Act of 2019) for a faster, cheaper process. Cases range from weeks (prepackaged plans) to years for complex reorganizations.

Notable cases

Among the largest Chapter 11 cases are Lehman Brothers (2008, the largest ever, and ultimately a liquidation in substance), Washington Mutual, General Motors and Chrysler (2009, rapid § 363 sales backed by the U.S. government), Enron, WorldCom, Texaco, and many airlines (United, Delta, American) and retailers (Sears, Toys "R" Us). Municipalities use the related Chapter 9 (e.g., Detroit, 2013).

Criticism and reform

Chapter 11 is praised for preserving going-concern value and jobs, and criticized for cost, delay, management entrenchment, and outcomes favouring secured lenders. Reform debates continue around venue shopping, third-party releases (curtailed by the Supreme Court in the Purdue Pharma case, 2024), and protection of employees and retirees.
