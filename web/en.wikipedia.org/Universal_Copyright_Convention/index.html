Universal Copyright Convention

Signed6 September 1952
LocationGeneva, Switzerland
Effective16 September 1955
Administered byUNESCO

The Universal Copyright Convention (UCC) is an international copyright treaty adopted in Geneva, Switzerland, in 1952 under the auspices of UNESCO. It was developed as an alternative to the Berne Convention for those states that disagreed with aspects of Berne but still wished to participate in a multilateral copyright regime.

Background

The Berne Convention, established in 1886, required automatic copyright protection without formalities and imposed minimum standards some countries — notably the United States, which long required registration and notice, and the Soviet Union — were unwilling to accept. The UCC allowed member states to retain certain formalities while still granting reciprocal protection to works from other member states.

The © symbol

The UCC is best known for introducing the © copyright symbol into international practice. Under the convention, a work bearing the symbol ©, the name of the copyright proprietor, and the year of first publication, placed in a conspicuous position, satisfies any formality requirements of member states. This compromise made it possible for countries with registration systems and countries with automatic protection to recognize each other's works.

Decline in importance

The UCC required a minimum term of protection of the life of the author plus 25 years (or 25 years from publication for works protected on that basis). Its significance declined as nearly all major states eventually joined the Berne Convention — the United States did so in 1989 — and as the WTO's TRIPS Agreement (1994) incorporated Berne standards into the global trading system. The UCC remains in force but is largely dormant.
