Eardrum

The eardrum, or tympanic membrane, is a thin, cone-shaped membrane that separates the external ear from the middle ear in humans and other tetrapods. Its function is to transmit sound vibrations from the air to the ossicles inside the middle ear, and from there to the fluid-filled cochlea of the inner ear.

Anatomy

The tympanic membrane is roughly oval, about 8–10 mm in diameter, and consists of three layers: an outer layer of skin continuous with the ear canal, a middle fibrous layer that gives it stiffness, and an inner mucous membrane layer. The handle of the malleus (the first ossicle) is attached to its inner surface, pulling the centre of the membrane inward at a point called the umbo. Most of the membrane is the taut pars tensa; a small, looser upper region is the pars flaccida.

Function

Sound waves striking the eardrum cause it to vibrate. Because the eardrum's effective area is much larger than the footplate of the stapes, and because of the lever action of the ossicles, the vibrations are amplified in pressure — an impedance-matching mechanism that overcomes the resistance of transmitting airborne sound into the fluid of the cochlea.

Clinical significance

The eardrum can be perforated by infection (otitis media), trauma, or sudden pressure changes (barotrauma); most perforations heal spontaneously, though some require surgical repair (myringoplasty). Examination with an otoscope — assessing the membrane's colour, position and the cone of light reflected from it — is a standard part of ear examination. Fluid behind the eardrum, redness and bulging are signs of middle-ear infection.
