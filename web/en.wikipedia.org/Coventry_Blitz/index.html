Coventry Blitz

Date: 14–15 November 1940 (heaviest raid)

Attacker: German Luftwaffe

Casualties: About 568 killed in the main raid

Operation name: Mondscheinsonate ("Moonlight Sonata")

The Coventry Blitz (from the German word Blitzkrieg, "lightning war") was a series of bombing raids by the German Luftwaffe on the English city of Coventry during the Second World War. The most devastating raid occurred on the night of 14–15 November 1940, when some 500 bombers attacked the city for over ten hours.

The November raid

Coventry was a major centre of Britain's engineering, armaments, and motor industries, making it a priority target. Guided by the X-Gerät radio-beam system, the bombers dropped high explosives and thousands of incendiaries, destroying much of the city centre, gutting the medieval St Michael's Cathedral (leaving only its spire and outer walls), and damaging factories, homes, and public buildings. Around 568 people were killed and over 800 seriously injured. The raid was so destructive that Nazi propaganda coined the verb coventrieren — "to Coventrate", meaning to raze a city.

Aftermath and legacy

Further heavy raids followed in April 1941. Coventry's suffering became a symbol of civilian endurance, and the ruined cathedral, preserved beside Basil Spence's new cathedral (consecrated 1962), remains a powerful memorial. Debate has continued over the (discredited) claim that the government sacrificed Coventry to protect the Ultra secret.
