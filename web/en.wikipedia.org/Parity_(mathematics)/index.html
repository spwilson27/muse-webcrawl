Parity (mathematics)

In mathematics, parity is the property of an integer of whether it is even or odd. An integer is even if it is divisible by 2, that is, if it can be written as twice some integer; otherwise it is odd. For example, −4, 0, and 82 are even, while −3, 5, and 41 are odd. Parity applies only to integers; numbers such as 1/2 or 4.57 are neither even nor odd.

Even numbers can be split into two equal groups; odd numbers always leave one element unpaired.

Definition

An even number is an integer of the form

n = 2k,

where k is an integer. An odd number has the form

n = 2k + 1.

Equivalently, parity is the value of the integer modulo 2: even numbers are congruent to 0 (mod 2) and odd numbers to 1 (mod 2). The set of even integers and the set of odd integers partition the integers.

Arithmetic rules

Parity behaves predictably under addition, subtraction, and multiplication:

  • even ± even = even; odd ± odd = even; even ± odd = odd.
  • even × even = even; even × odd = even; odd × odd = odd.

Division does not preserve parity in general, since the quotient of two integers need not be an integer. These rules amount to arithmetic in the field of two elements, Z/2Z.

Applications

Parity arguments are a staple of elementary number theory and combinatorics. A classic example shows that the square root of 2 is irrational by deriving a contradiction from the parity of numerator and denominator. In chess, a bishop always stays on squares of one colour, a parity-style invariant. Parity is also used in error-detecting codes, where a parity bit is appended to binary data so that the total number of 1-bits has a fixed parity, and in the analysis of permutations, where every permutation is either even or odd according to the number of transpositions that produce it.

Generalisation

The notion extends beyond the integers: in algebra, elements of a graded ring may have even or odd degree, and functions are called even or odd according to whether f(−x) equals f(x) or −f(x). In the binary numeral system, an integer's parity is simply its least significant bit.
