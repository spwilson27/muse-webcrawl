Double dissolution

A double dissolution is a procedure permitted under the Australian Constitution to resolve deadlocks in the bicameral Parliament of Australia between the House of Representatives (lower house) and the Senate (upper house). Under section 57 of the Constitution, if the Senate refuses to pass a bill twice that has been passed by the House, with at least three months between the rejections, the Governor-General may dissolve both houses simultaneously, triggering an election for the entire Parliament.

Occurrences

Double dissolutions have occurred seven times: in 1914, 1951, 1974, 1975, 1983, 1987, and 2016. The 1975 dissolution, made by Governor-General Sir John Kerr amid the constitutional crisis that saw the dismissal of the Whitlam government, remains the most controversial.

Aftermath

If, after a double dissolution election, the deadlock persists, the Governor-General may convene a joint sitting of both houses to vote on the disputed bill. This has happened only once, after the 1974 double dissolution, when the Whitlam government's bills were passed at a joint sitting.

Senate terms

Because all senators face election at a double dissolution, quotas for election are lower, which can benefit minor parties. Senators elected at a double dissolution serve terms backdated to the preceding 1 July, preserving the normal rotation.
