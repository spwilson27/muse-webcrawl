Green chemistry

Green chemistry designs chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate hazardous substances.

Green chemistry, also called sustainable chemistry, is an area of chemistry and chemical engineering focused on the design of products and processes that minimise or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. The field was formalised in the 1990s, notably by Paul Anastas and John Warner, who set out the Twelve Principles of Green Chemistry in 1998.

The twelve principles

  1. Prevention — prevent waste rather than treat it afterwards.
  2. Atom economy — maximise incorporation of materials into the final product.
  3. Less hazardous chemical syntheses.
  4. Designing safer chemicals.
  5. Safer solvents and auxiliaries.
  6. Design for energy efficiency.
  7. Use of renewable feedstocks.
  8. Reduce derivatives (avoid unnecessary protecting groups).
  9. Catalysis — catalytic rather than stoichiometric reagents.
  10. Design for degradation.
  11. Real-time analysis for pollution prevention.
  12. Inherently safer chemistry for accident prevention.

Metrics and practice

Common measures include atom economy, the E-factor (kilograms of waste per kilogram of product) and process mass intensity. Industrial examples include catalytic routes to ibuprofen replacing multi-step stoichiometric syntheses, supercritical CO2 as a solvent, and biocatalysis for pharmaceutical intermediates. The U.S. Presidential Green Chemistry Challenge Awards, established in 1995, recognise such innovations annually.
