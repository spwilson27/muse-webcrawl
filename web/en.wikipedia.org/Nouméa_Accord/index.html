The Nouméa Accord (French: Accord de Nouméa) is an agreement signed on 5 May 1998 in Nouméa between the French government under Prime Minister Lionel Jospin, the pro-independence Kanak and Socialist National Liberation Front (FLNKS) and the anti-independence Rally for Caledonia in the Republic (RPCR). It succeeded the Matignon Agreements of 1988 and organised a gradual, irreversible transfer of powers from France to New Caledonia.

Provisions

The Accord recognised the trauma of colonisation for the Kanak people and established a New Caledonian citizenship alongside French citizenship. It provided for a collegial, proportionally composed local government, a Congress with power to pass "country laws" (lois du pays), a Customary Senate representing Kanak customary authorities, and the progressive transfer of competences — most except the sovereign powers of defence, foreign affairs, justice, currency and public order — over a period of fifteen to twenty years. A referendum on the territory's final status was to be held at the end of that period.

Referendums

The Accord was approved by New Caledonian voters on 8 November 1998 by about 72 per cent. Its terms allowed up to three independence referendums. These were held on 4 November 2018 (56.7 per cent against independence), 4 October 2020 (53.3 per cent against) and 12 December 2021 (96.5 per cent against, on a low turnout amid a pro-independence boycott). The results left the territory within the French Republic, and political negotiations over a successor statute followed.
