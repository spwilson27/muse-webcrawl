Mummification

TypePreservation of the dead
Best known fromAncient Egypt

Mummification is the process by which a corpse is preserved, deliberately or by natural conditions, so that soft tissues survive long after death. The term is most associated with ancient Egypt, where artificial mummification was practised for roughly three thousand years.

Ancient Egyptian practice

Embalmers removed the internal organs (storing some in canopic jars), desiccated the body with natron salt for about forty days, then anointed, wrapped it in linen bandages, and placed amulets between the layers. The process reflected beliefs about the afterlife and the need for the body as a home for the soul.

Elsewhere

Mummies are also known from the Chinchorro culture of Chile and Peru (older than Egypt's), from bog bodies in northern Europe, from Inca capacocha sacrifices, and from deliberately preserved monks in parts of Asia. Modern techniques such as plastination and cryonics continue the idea of body preservation.
