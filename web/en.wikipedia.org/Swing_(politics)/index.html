Swing (politics)

Swing, in the politics of countries with two-party or two-bloc competition, is the extent of change in voter support for a party or candidate between two elections, usually expressed as a percentage. It is a standard tool of psephology — the study of elections — for comparing results across time and predicting how many seats would change hands under a uniform shift in support.

Calculation

The conventional (Butler) swing between two parties is calculated as half the sum of one party's gain in vote share and the other party's loss: if Party A rises by 4 points and Party B falls by 6 points, the swing from B to A is 5 percent. An alternative measure, the Steed swing, is used in some analyses. Swing is the basis of the "swingometer," a device popularised in British television election coverage (originally by Peter Milne and later associated with Peter Snow) that displays how a given swing translates into seats.

Uses and limitations

Analysts use swing to identify trends, define marginal or "swing" constituencies and, in the United States, swing states, and to model election outcomes. Its main limitation is the assumption of uniformity: real elections rarely swing by exactly the same amount everywhere, and swing behaves differently in multi-party contests, when a third party's collapse or surge can make simple two-party swing misleading.
