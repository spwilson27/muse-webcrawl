Dalmatia

Dalmatia (Croatian: Dalmacija) is a historical region on the east shore of the Adriatic Sea in modern-day Croatia. It stretches from the island of Rab in the north to the Bay of Kotor in the south, and is one of the four historical regions of Croatia, alongside Croatia proper, Slavonia, and Istria.

Geography

Dalmatia is a narrow belt of land along the eastern Adriatic, backed by the Dinaric Alps, which separate it from the interior of Bosnia and Herzegovina. The region includes a dramatic coastline of rocky shores, peninsulas, and bays, and is fronted by one of the largest archipelagos in the Mediterranean, with major islands including Krk, Brač, Hvar, Korčula, Pag, and Vis. The climate is Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters.

History

The region takes its name from the Delmatae, an Illyrian tribe. It became a Roman province after the Illyrian Wars, and the emperor Diocletian — himself born in Dalmatia — built a vast palace at Split, around which the modern city grew. After the fall of Rome, Dalmatia passed through Byzantine, Croatian, Hungarian, and Venetian hands; the Republic of Venice controlled most of the coast for centuries, leaving a strong Italianate legacy in architecture, while the Republic of Ragusa (Dubrovnik) remained independent. Following the Napoleonic Wars it became a crown land of the Austrian Empire. After the First World War it was incorporated into Yugoslavia, and after 1991 it became part of independent Croatia.

Economy and culture

Tourism is the dominant industry, centred on historic coastal cities such as Dubrovnik, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and Split, Hvar, and Zadar. Traditional industries include fishing, olive oil and wine production, shipbuilding, and salt production. Dalmatian culture is known for klapa multipart singing, inscribed on UNESCO's list of intangible heritage. The Dalmatian dog breed is named after the region.
