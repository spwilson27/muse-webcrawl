Cassoulet

Cassoulet is a rich, slow-cooked stew originating in southern France. It typically contains meat — often pork sausages, goose, duck confit, and sometimes mutton — and white beans (haricots blancs), cooked together in an earthenware pot called a cassole, from which the dish takes its name.

Origins and rivalry

Cassoulet is associated above all with the Languedoc. Three towns claim their own definitive version: Castelnaudary (the self-styled "capital of cassoulet," with beans, duck confit, pork, and sausage), Carcassonne (which may add partridge or mutton), and Toulouse (with Toulouse sausage and duck confit). A famous saying ranks them: Castelnaudary is God the Father, Carcassonne God the Son, and Toulouse the Holy Spirit of cassoulet.

Preparation

The beans are soaked and simmered, then layered with the meats and cooking liquor in the cassole and baked slowly until a dark crust forms; tradition holds that the crust should be broken and pushed into the stew several times during cooking — seven times, by some accounts. The dish developed from medieval bean-and-meat stews and became emblematic of French regional cooking.

Cultural presence

Cassoulet has inspired confraternities dedicated to its defence, festivals in Castelnaudary, and canned commercial versions sold throughout France. A legend links its invention to the siege of Castelnaudary during the Hundred Years' War, when townspeople pooled their remaining provisions into a single great stew.
