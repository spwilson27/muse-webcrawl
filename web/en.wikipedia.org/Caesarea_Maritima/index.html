Caesarea Maritima

LocationNear modern Caesarea, Israel, on the Mediterranean coast
Foundedc. 22–10 BC, by Herod the Great
Named forCaesar Augustus
PeriodsRoman, Byzantine, early Islamic, Crusader
TodayCaesarea National Park archaeological site

Caesarea Maritima, formerly Strato's Tower, is an ancient city and artificial harbour built under Herod the Great about 22–10 BC near the site of a former Phoenician naval station. It lies on the Mediterranean coast of Israel, midway between Tel Aviv and Haifa, close to the modern town of Caesarea.


The Roman theatre and harbour ruins at Caesarea.

Herod dedicated the city to Augustus Caesar — hence its name — and endowed it with a palace, theatre, amphitheatre, hippodrome, temples, and one of the largest artificial harbours of the ancient world, built with hydraulic concrete in open water.

History

Roman period

After Judaea became a Roman province in AD 6, Caesarea became its capital and the residence of Roman prefects and procurators, including Pontius Pilate, whose name was found here on an inscribed stone in 1961. The apostle Paul was imprisoned in Caesarea for two years before being sent to Rome (Acts 23–26). The city was granted colonial status by Vespasian and flourished as a commercial and administrative centre.

Byzantine period

Caesarea was a major centre of early Christian scholarship: Origen founded a famous theological school and library there in the 3rd century, and Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea, wrote his pioneering Ecclesiastical History in the early 4th century. The city remained prosperous into the 6th century, with baths, churches, and an aqueduct system extended from Mount Carmel.

Later history

The city fell to the Persians in 614 and to Muslim armies around 640. It declined under Islamic rule, revived briefly as a fortified Crusader port after its capture in 1101, and was finally destroyed by the Mamluks in 1265 to prevent its reuse by Crusaders. In the 19th century a small Bosniak village was established among the ruins.

Archaeology

Excavations since the 1950s have uncovered the theatre, hippodrome, Herodian and Crusader fortifications, and extensive harbour works, much of them now underwater due to subsidence and sea-level rise. Notable finds include the Pilate Stone, mosaics, and the remains of Herod's palace built on a promontory in the sea. The site is preserved as Caesarea National Park, one of Israel's most visited archaeological attractions.
