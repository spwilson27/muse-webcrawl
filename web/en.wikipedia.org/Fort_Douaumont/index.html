Fort Douaumont

Fort Douaumont (French: Fort de Douaumont) was the largest and highest of the ring of forts defending Verdun in north-eastern France. Built between 1885 and 1913 as part of the Séré de Rivières system, it became the symbolic centrepiece of the Battle of Verdun in 1916, when it was captured almost without a fight by German troops and recaptured by the French eight months later.

Construction

The fort was built on a hill north-east of Verdun and progressively modernised with thick concrete protection, retractable steel gun turrets (including a 155 mm turret and a 75 mm turret), machine-gun turrets and deep underground barracks, kitchens and magazines designed to shelter a garrison of several hundred men.

The Battle of Verdun

Before the German offensive of February 1916, the fort had been partly stripped of guns and left with a small caretaker garrison, its value doubted after the fall of the Belgian forts in 1914. On 25 February 1916 German pioneers entered it almost unopposed. An explosion in a flamethrower-fuel and grenade store on 8 May 1916 killed several hundred German soldiers inside. French forces retook the fort on 24 October 1916, aided by 400 mm railway artillery.

Memorial

Today the fort is preserved as a memorial; its pockmarked superstructure overlooks the Douaumont Ossuary and the vast military cemeteries of the Verdun battlefield.
