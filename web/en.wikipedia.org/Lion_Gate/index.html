Lion Gate

The Lion Gate is the popular name of the main entrance of the Bronze Age citadel of Mycenae in southern Greece. It was erected during the 13th century BC, around 1250 BC, in the northwest side of the acropolis and is named after the relief sculpture of two lionesses or lions in a heraldic pose that stands above the entrance. It is the only monument of Bronze Age Greece to bear an iconographic motif that survived into later times, and the sole surviving monumental sculpture of Mycenaean Greece.

Description

The gate forms part of the massive Cyclopean walls of Mycenae. The doorway is spanned by a great lintel weighing some 20 tons, above which a triangular relief slab — the "relieving triangle" — fills the gap in the wall to relieve pressure on the lintel. On it, two lions flank a central column, their heads (now lost, originally perhaps of separate materials) turned toward approaching visitors. The composition is modelled on Minoan and Near Eastern heraldic art.

The gate was excavated as part of Heinrich Schliemann's and later Greek archaeological work at Mycenae in the 19th century. In later antiquity, Pausanias described the lions and attributed the walls to the Cyclopes, a myth linking the site to Perseus.
