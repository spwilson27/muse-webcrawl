Silencer (genetics)

In genetics, a silencer is a DNA sequence capable of binding transcription regulation factors, called repressors. DNA contains genes and provides the template to produce messenger RNA (mRNA). That mRNA is then translated into proteins. When a repressor protein binds to the silencer region of DNA, RNA polymerase is prevented from transcribing the DNA sequence into RNA. With transcription blocked, the translation of RNA into proteins is impossible. Thus, silencers prevent genes from being expressed as proteins.

Function

Silencers are the functional counterpart of enhancers: where enhancers increase transcription when bound by activators, silencers decrease or completely shut down transcription when bound by repressors. Like enhancers, silencers can be located far upstream or downstream of the genes they regulate and can function in either orientation, because repressor proteins act through DNA looping to bring the silencer into contact with the promoter and the transcriptional machinery.

Mechanisms

Repressors recruited to silencers may act by competing with activators for binding sites, by blocking assembly of the pre-initiation complex, or by recruiting corepressor complexes and histone-modifying enzymes that remodel chromatin into a repressive state. Well-studied examples include the silencer elements controlling the expression of the neuronal gene REST/NRSF targets, yeast mating-type silencing at the HML and HMR loci (though these operate principally through chromatin silencing), and locus control of T-cell receptor genes.
