The First Folio, formally Mr. William Shakespeare's Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies, is a collected edition of William Shakespeare's plays, published in 1623, seven years after his death. It is considered one of the most influential books ever published in the English language.

[Image placeholder: Title page of the First Folio with the Droeshout portrait of Shakespeare]

Compiled by Shakespeare's fellow actors John Heminges and Henry Condell and printed by Isaac Jaggard and Edward Blount, the folio contains 36 plays grouped into comedies, histories, and tragedies. Eighteen of the plays — including Macbeth, The Tempest, Twelfth Night, Julius Caesar, and Antony and Cleopatra — had never been printed before and might have been lost without the Folio.

Publication

The book was entered in the Stationers' Register on 8 November 1623 and sold for one pound — an expensive, prestige format, since plays were normally published in cheap quarto editions and rarely in the large folio format reserved for theology, history, and law. The text was set from a mixture of sources: earlier quartos, theatrical prompt-books, and authorial or scribal manuscripts, with considerable variation in quality. Five compositors set the type, and proofreading was inconsistent, so surviving copies differ in hundreds of small readings because corrections were made while printing continued.

Contents

The volume opens with Ben Jonson's verses "To the memory of my beloved, the Author" and the famous Droeshout engraving of Shakespeare on the title page. Notable omissions include Pericles, Prince of Tyre and The Two Noble Kinsmen. Later folios followed: the Second Folio (1632), Third Folio (1663–1664), and Fourth Folio (1685).

Surviving copies and value

Of the roughly 750 copies printed, 235 are known to survive, most incomplete. The Folger Shakespeare Library in Washington, D.C. holds 82 copies, the largest collection in the world. Complete copies are among the most valuable printed books in English: one sold at auction in 2020 for nearly US$10 million, a record for a work of literature.
