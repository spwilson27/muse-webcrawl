Hannah (Hebrew: חַנָּה, Ḥannāh, "favour" or "grace") is the mother of the prophet Samuel in the Hebrew Bible. Her story is told in the first two chapters of the First Book of Samuel. She is one of the wives of Elkanah, and her prayer and song are among the most celebrated passages of biblical narrative and poetry.

Biblical narrative

Hannah was the beloved wife of Elkanah of Ramathaim, but she was childless, while Elkanah's other wife, Peninnah, had children and taunted her for her barrenness. Each year the family travelled to the sanctuary at Shiloh to worship and sacrifice. On one such visit, Hannah prayed in deep distress at the temple, silently moving her lips as she vowed that if God gave her a son, she would dedicate him to God's service as a Nazirite. The priest Eli, who at first mistook her silent prayer for drunkenness, blessed her. Hannah conceived and bore a son, Samuel, and after weaning him she brought him to Shiloh and presented him to Eli, fulfilling her vow; the boy ministered in the sanctuary and grew up to become Israel's last judge and the anointer of its first kings. According to 1 Samuel 2:21, Hannah afterwards bore three more sons and two daughters.

The Song of Hannah

In 1 Samuel 2:1–10, Hannah utters a hymn of thanksgiving beginning "My heart rejoices in the LORD". The song celebrates God's reversal of fortunes — the bows of the mighty are broken, the hungry are filled, the barren woman bears children — and closes with a reference to God's king and anointed ("messiah"). Scholars regard it as an early piece of Hebrew poetry, possibly older than its narrative setting, and it is widely recognised as a model for the Magnificat of Mary in the Gospel of Luke, which echoes its structure and themes.

Interpretation and legacy

In Jewish tradition Hannah is counted among the seven prophetesses and is a model of prayer: the rabbis derived several laws of prayer from her conduct, including praying silently and with full attention. Her story is read in synagogues as the haftarah for the first day of Rosh Hashanah. In Christian tradition she is honoured as a saint and as a type of the Virgin Mary. Place-name traditions associate her tomb with the village of Nabi Samwil near Jerusalem.
