Big wall climbing

Big wall climbing is a form of rock climbing where a climber ascends a long multi-pitch route, normally requiring more than a single day to complete. Big wall routes are typically on very steep, blank faces of rock, and require a large amount of specialized aid climbing equipment and logistical support, including hauling food, water and shelter (portaledges) up the wall.

A climber bivouacking on a portaledge on a big wall route.

History

The discipline developed in Yosemite Valley, California, in the 1950s and 1960s, where Warren Harding, Royal Robbins and others pioneered multi-day ascents of El Capitan and Half Dome. Harding's 1958 first ascent of The Nose on El Capitan took 47 days of climbing spread over 18 months; Robbins repeated the route in seven days in 1960, and later parties progressively reduced ascent times and dispensed with aid.

Techniques

Big wall ascents combine free climbing and aid climbing. Loads are moved with haul systems using pulleys and mechanical ascenders; climbers sleep on portaledges — hanging tent platforms — anchored to the wall. Parties either fix ropes to the ground to resupply ("siege" tactics) or climb self-contained in a single push from bottom to top ("capsule" style).

Notable walls and ascents

Famous big walls include El Capitan and Half Dome in Yosemite, the Trango Towers in Pakistan, and walls in Greenland, Patagonia and Baffin Island. In 2015 Tommy Caldwell and Kevin Jorgeson completed the first free ascent of El Capitan's Dawn Wall, widely covered as one of the hardest climbs in the world; in 2017 Alex Honnold free-soloed El Capitan's Freerider line.
