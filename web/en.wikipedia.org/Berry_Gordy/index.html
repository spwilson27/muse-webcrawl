Berry Gordy III (born 28 November 1929) is an American retired record executive, songwriter, and producer, best known as the founder of the Motown record label and its subsidiaries. Under his leadership, Motown became the most successful African-American-owned record company of its era and a defining force in popular music.

Early life

Gordy was born in Detroit, the seventh of eight children. He dropped out of high school to pursue boxing, fought professionally as a featherweight, and later served in the U.S. Army during the Korean War. After returning to Detroit, he worked on a Ford assembly line — an experience that later inspired his assembly-line approach to producing hit records.

Songwriting and the founding of Motown

As a songwriter, Gordy co-wrote hits for Jackie Wilson, including "Reet Petite" and "Lonely Teardrops". In 1959, with an $800 loan from his family, he founded Tamla Records, soon followed by Motown Records, headquartered at 2648 West Grand Boulevard in Detroit — "Hitsville U.S.A."

The Motown sound

Gordy built an integrated stable of artists, songwriters, and session musicians (the Funk Brothers), and a rigorous quality-control process modeled on the automobile assembly line. Motown launched the careers of Diana Ross and the Supremes, Marvin Gaye, Stevie Wonder, the Temptations, the Four Tops, Smokey Robinson and the Miracles, and the Jackson 5. In 1988 Gordy sold Motown to MCA Records. He was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame the same year.

Later work

Gordy produced the Broadway musical Motown: The Musical (2013) and received the National Medal of Arts in 2016. His life and the Motown story remain central to histories of American popular music and Black entrepreneurship.
