Yue (Chinese: 粵語) is a branch of the Sinitic languages spoken natively in the provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi in southern China, and in Hong Kong and Macau. Its prestige variety is Cantonese, based on the dialect of Guangzhou (Canton), and in common usage "Cantonese" often refers to Yue as a whole.

Classification and varieties

Yue belongs to the Chinese branch of the Sino-Tibetan family. Internal varieties include the Yuehai group (Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Macau, Zhongshan), the Siyi (Taishanese) dialects of the Pearl River Delta's southwest, and the Gou-Lou, Yong-Xun, and Qin-Lian groups. Many varieties are not mutually intelligible with each other or with Mandarin.

Features

Yue preserves more of the phonology of Middle Chinese than most northern varieties: it retains final stop consonants (-p, -t, -k) and initial ng-, and Cantonese has six contrastive tones (nine counting checked syllables). Yue vocabulary and grammar also preserve classical elements, and written vernacular Cantonese, using characters such as 唔 and 係, is common in Hong Kong media and informal writing.

Diaspora

Centuries of emigration from Guangdong have made Yue varieties prominent in overseas Chinese communities worldwide; Taishanese was historically the dominant Chinese language in North American Chinatowns, and Cantonese remains a major language of the diaspora.
