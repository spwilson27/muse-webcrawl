Treaty of Fort Stanwix

The "Line of Property" of 1768 pushed the colonial boundary west of the Appalachians.

Signed5 November 1768, Fort Stanwix (present-day Rome, New York)
PartiesBritish Crown (Superintendent Sir William Johnson); the Six Nations of the Iroquois Confederacy
PurposeAdjust the boundary between colonial settlement and Native American lands

The Treaty of Fort Stanwix was a treaty finalised between Great Britain and the Six Nations of the Iroquois Confederacy on 5 November 1768 at Fort Stanwix, in what is now Rome, New York. It was attended by Sir William Johnson, British superintendent of Indian affairs, and by more than 3,000 Native American representatives, and it adjusted — far to the west — the boundary line between colonial settlement and indigenous lands that had been proclaimed in the Royal Proclamation of 1763.

Terms

In return for a payment of more than £10,000 in money and goods — the largest yet made in such a negotiation — the Six Nations ceded their claims to a vast territory south and east of a new "Line of Property". The line ran from near Fort Stanwix down the Susquehanna and across Pennsylvania, then down the Ohio River to the mouth of the Tennessee River. In practice the Iroquois ceded lands that were used and claimed by other peoples, including the Shawnee, Delaware and Cherokee — notably the hunting grounds of Kentucky and what is now West Virginia, over which the Iroquois asserted overlordship but did not live.

Consequences

The cession opened Kentucky and the Ohio Valley to a rush of colonial land speculation and settlement, while angering the Shawnee and other nations who had not consented to give up their lands. The resulting tensions fed directly into Lord Dunmore's War of 1774 and into the frontier violence of the American Revolution. A second Treaty of Fort Stanwix, negotiated in 1784 between the United States and the Six Nations, imposed further cessions after the Revolution.
