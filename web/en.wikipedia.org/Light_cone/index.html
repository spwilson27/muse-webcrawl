Light cone

In special and general relativity, a light cone is the path that a flash of light, emanating from a single event and travelling in all directions, would take through spacetime. In a diagram with two spatial dimensions plus time, the paths form a double cone: the future light cone of all events reachable by the light, and the past light cone of all events that could have sent light to the given event.

Causal structure

Because nothing can travel faster than light, the light cone divides spacetime into regions: the causal future (inside the future cone), the causal past (inside the past cone), and the "elsewhere" — spacelike-separated events that can neither influence nor be influenced by the event in question. Separations are classified as timelike, lightlike (null), or spacelike according to whether they lie inside, on, or outside the cone.

Mathematics

For an event at the origin of Minkowski space with coordinates (ct, x, y, z), the cone satisfies x² + y² + z² = c²t². The surface is generated by null geodesics. In general relativity, curved spacetime tilts and distorts light cones; inside a black hole's event horizon, cones tip so completely that all future paths lead toward the singularity. The concept was developed by Hermann Minkowski in his 1908 geometric formulation of Einstein's special relativity.
