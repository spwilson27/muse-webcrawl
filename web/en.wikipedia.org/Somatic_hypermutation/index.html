Somatic hypermutation

Somatic hypermutation (SHM) is a cellular mechanism by which the immune system adapts to foreign elements (antigens) that confront it, as seen during the switch to class-switch recombination. It is a major component of the process of affinity maturation — diversifying B cell receptors used to recognize antigens, allowing the immune system to tailor its response over an individual's lifetime. Somatic hypermutation involves a programmed process of mutation affecting the variable regions of immunoglobulin genes. Unlike germline mutation, SHM affects only an organism's individual immune cells, and the mutations are not transmitted to offspring.

Mechanism

Following activation by antigen and T-cell help, B cells proliferate in germinal centers, where the enzyme activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID) deaminates cytosine to uracil in the DNA of the rearranged V(D)J variable regions. The resulting U:G mismatches are processed by error-prone repair pathways — including uracil-DNA glycosylase, mismatch repair proteins (Msh2/Msh6), and the translesion polymerase Pol η — spreading point mutations at a rate of roughly one mutation per thousand base pairs per cell division, about a million-fold higher than the background somatic mutation rate. Mutations cluster in complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) and at hotspot motifs (RGYW/WRCY).

Selection and affinity maturation

Mutated B cells compete in the germinal-center light zone for antigen displayed on follicular dendritic cells and for T follicular helper cell signals. Clones whose mutated receptors bind antigen with higher affinity are selected to survive, proliferate, and differentiate into plasma cells and memory B cells, while low-affinity or autoreactive clones undergo apoptosis. Iterative rounds of mutation and selection progressively raise antibody affinity — the hallmarks of a maturing secondary immune response. Defects in AID cause hyper-IgM syndrome type 2, characterized by an inability to perform SHM and class switching, while mis-targeted SHM contributes to B-cell lymphomas through mutations in proto-oncogenes such as BCL6 and MYC.
