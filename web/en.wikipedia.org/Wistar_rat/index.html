The Wistar rat is an outbred stock of albino laboratory rats belonging to the species Rattus norvegicus. It was developed at the Wistar Institute in Philadelphia in 1906 for use in biological and medical research, and is notable for being the first standardised laboratory rat stock.

History

The Wistar Institute established its rat colony in 1906 under the guidance of figures including Henry Herbert Donaldson, who standardised the strain's husbandry and growth records. The stock was widely distributed to other laboratories, and most modern laboratory rat strains descend wholly or partly from the Wistar colony; the Sprague Dawley and Long–Evans stocks both have Wistar ancestry.

Characteristics

Wistar rats are characterised by their white coat and pink eyes (albinism), wide head, long ears and a tail shorter than the body length. Males typically weigh 250–450 g and females 200–300 g. Because the stock is maintained as an outbred population, individual animals are genetically heterogeneous, which makes them useful models of a genetically varied population.

Use in research

Wistar rats are used extensively in toxicology, pharmacology, nutrition, ageing and behavioural research, and as a general-purpose model organism in biomedical science. They are also the parent stock of several specialised strains, including the spontaneously hypertensive Wistar Kyoto rat used in cardiovascular research.
