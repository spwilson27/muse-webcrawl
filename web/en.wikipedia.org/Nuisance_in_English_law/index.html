Nuisance in English law

AreaTort law
JurisdictionEngland and Wales
TypesPrivate nuisance, public nuisance, statutory nuisance

Nuisance in English law is an area of tort law concerning unlawful interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or with certain rights over or in connection with land. English law recognises private nuisance, public nuisance, and statutory nuisance under legislation such as the Environmental Protection Act 1990.

Private nuisance

Private nuisance protects a landowner's interest in the use and enjoyment of land. It typically arises from a continuing state of affairs — such as noise, smells, smoke, dust, vibration, or encroaching tree roots and branches — that unreasonably interferes with a neighbour's land. Only a person with a proprietary interest in the affected land may sue. Courts assess reasonableness by reference to locality, duration, sensitivity of the claimant, and the social utility of the defendant's conduct. Leading authorities include St Helen's Smelting Co v Tipping (1865), on material injury to property, and Coventry v Lawrence [2014], on remedies and the relevance of planning permission.

Public nuisance

Public nuisance is an act or omission that materially affects the reasonable comfort and convenience of a class of the public — for example, obstructing the highway or polluting a river. It is both a crime at common law and a tort; a private claimant must show special damage over and above that suffered by the public generally. The Attorney General may bring proceedings on behalf of the public.

Statutory nuisance

Part III of the Environmental Protection Act 1990 obliges local authorities to investigate complaints about matters such as noise, smoke, fumes, accumulation, and premises prejudicial to health, and to serve an abatement notice where a statutory nuisance exists or is likely to occur.

Remedies

Available remedies include damages, injunctions (which remain discretionary), and abatement by the claimant in limited circumstances. Defences include prescription (twenty years of actionable nuisance), statutory authority, and — only partially — the claimant "coming to the nuisance".
