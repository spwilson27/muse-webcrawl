Erich Ludendorff

Erich Friedrich Wilhelm Ludendorff
Born: April 9, 1865, Kruszewnia, Prussia
Died: December 20, 1937, Munich, Germany
Service: Imperial German Army
Rank: General of the Infantry

Erich Friedrich Wilhelm Ludendorff was a German general who achieved fame for his central role in Germany's early victories in World War I, including the Battle of Liège and the Battle of Tannenberg. As First Quartermaster-General under Paul von Hindenburg from 1916, he was the chief policymaker in a de facto military dictatorship that dominated Germany's war effort.

First World War

Ludendorff directed the capture of the Liège forts in August 1914 and, with Hindenburg and Max Hoffmann, the encirclement of the Russian Second Army at Tannenberg. From 1916 he and Hindenburg ran the Supreme Army Command, organising the Hindenburg Programme of total mobilisation, unrestricted submarine warfare, and the 1918 Spring Offensive, whose failure led to Germany's collapse and his resignation in October 1918.

Later life

After the war Ludendorff promoted the "stab-in-the-back" myth, took part in the Kapp Putsch (1920) and Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch (1923), and sat in the Reichstag for the National Socialist Freedom Movement. He later broke with Hitler, devoted himself to conspiracy-laden writings and his wife Mathilde's völkisch religious movement, and died in 1937.
