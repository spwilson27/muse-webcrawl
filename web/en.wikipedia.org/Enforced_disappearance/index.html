Enforced disappearance

An enforced disappearance is the secret abduction or imprisonment of a person by a state or political organization, or by a third party with the authorization, support, or acquiescence of a state, followed by a refusal to acknowledge the person's fate and whereabouts, placing the person outside the protection of the law. Enforced disappearance is a serious violation of international human rights law and, when practiced systematically, can constitute a crime against humanity.

Definition and law

The 2006 International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly, defines the practice and obliges states to criminalize it, investigate cases, and protect victims' families. The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court likewise lists enforced disappearance among crimes against humanity. The International Day of the Victims of Enforced Disappearances is observed on 30 August.

History

The modern practice is often traced to Nazi Germany's Nacht und Nebel ("Night and Fog") decree of 1941, under which suspected resistance members were secretly transferred and vanished. The term desaparecidos ("the disappeared") became widely known during the military dictatorships of Latin America in the 1970s and 1980s, notably in Argentina during the Dirty War, where thousands were abducted by security forces, and in Chile under Pinochet. Similar practices have been documented in many countries, including during the Yugoslav wars, in Syria, and elsewhere.

Impact

Enforced disappearance victimizes not only the disappeared person — who is typically at high risk of torture and extrajudicial killing — but also their families, who are left in uncertainty about the person's fate, sometimes for decades. Organizations of relatives, such as the Madres de Plaza de Mayo in Argentina, have been central to campaigns for truth and justice.
