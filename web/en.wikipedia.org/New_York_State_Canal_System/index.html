The New York State Canal System is a network of four canals in New York State—the Erie Canal, the Champlain Canal, the Oswego Canal, and the Cayuga–Seneca Canal—linking the Hudson River and the Great Lakes with Lake Champlain and the Finger Lakes. The system is the modern successor to the New York State Barge Canal, completed in 1918, which largely canalised natural rivers and lakes to replace the original nineteenth-century Erie Canal alignment. Comprising 524 miles of navigable waterway and 57 locks, it is today used predominantly by recreational boaters, with modest commercial traffic.

History

The original Erie Canal, completed in 1825 under Governor DeWitt Clinton, transformed New York City into the nation's leading port and opened the Midwest to settlement and trade. It was enlarged between 1836 and 1862, and again replaced between 1905 and 1918 by the Barge Canal, which straightened and deepened the route, used dams and movable bridges, and followed the Mohawk River for much of its length. In 1992 the system was renamed and marketed as the New York State Canal System, administered by the New York State Canal Corporation, a subsidiary of the Thruway Authority and later the New York Power Authority.

The canals

The Erie Canal runs 338 miles from the Hudson near Albany to Lake Erie at Buffalo. The Champlain Canal connects the Hudson to Lake Champlain and thus to the St. Lawrence system. The Oswego Canal links the Erie Canal at Three Rivers to Lake Ontario. The Cayuga–Seneca Canal joins the Erie to Cayuga and Seneca lakes in the Finger Lakes region.

Modern use

Although commercial tonnage declined sharply after the opening of the St. Lawrence Seaway in 1959 and competition from rail and road, the canals remain important for tourism, heritage, and recreation. The Erie Canalway National Heritage Corridor was established in 2000, and the canal's 1918 structures are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The navigable season typically runs from May to October or November.
