Potawatomi language

Potawatomi (also spelled Pottawatomie or Pottawatomi; endonym Bodwéwadmimwen) is a Central Algonquian language spoken by the Potawatomi people around the Great Lakes region of North America, historically in what is now Michigan, Wisconsin, Indiana, and Ontario. It is closely related to Ojibwe and belongs to the wider Ojibwe–Potawatomi grouping.

Status and revitalisation

Forced removals in the 1830s, including the Trail of Death, scattered Potawatomi communities to Kansas, Oklahoma, and Ontario, and boarding-school policies suppressed the language. By recent counts only a small number of fluent first-language speakers remain, most of them elderly. Tribal nations including the Citizen Potawatomi Nation, Pokagon Band, and Forest County Potawatomi run language classes, immersion programmes, and online courses to train new speakers.

Features

Like other Algonquian languages, Potawatomi is polysynthetic, with complex verb morphology, animate/inanimate noun gender, and obviation. It has undergone extensive vowel syncope — the loss of unstressed vowels — which distinguishes it sharply from Ojibwe. See also Potawatomi and Algonquian languages.
