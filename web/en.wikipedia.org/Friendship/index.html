Friendship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Two friends walking together]

Friendship is a relationship of mutual affection between people. It is a stronger form of interpersonal bond than an "acquaintance" or an "association", such as a classmate, neighbour, coworker, or colleague. In some cultures, the concept of friendship is restricted to a small number of very deep relationships; in others, a person could have many friends, possibly combined with intense relationships with a smaller number of people.

Although there are many forms of friendship, some of which may vary from place to place, certain characteristics are present in many types of such bonds, including affection, kindness, love, virtue, sympathy, empathy, honesty, altruism, loyalty, generosity, forgiveness, mutual understanding and compassion, enjoyment of each other's company, trust, and the ability to be oneself and make mistakes without fear of judgment.

Philosophical accounts

Aristotle devoted two books of the Nicomachean Ethics to friendship (philia), distinguishing friendships of utility, friendships of pleasure, and the highest form, friendships of virtue, in which each wishes the other well for the other's own sake. Cicero's dialogue Laelius de Amicitia similarly praises friendship as among life's greatest goods.

Psychology and development

Friendships form in childhood through shared play and proximity, deepen in adolescence as intimacy and self-disclosure grow, and continue to shape adult wellbeing. Developmental psychologists describe stages from momentary playmates to stable, loyal bonds. Peer friendships provide social support, buffer stress and predict better physical and mental health outcomes across the lifespan.

The Dunbar number and social networks

Anthropologist Robin Dunbar proposed that humans can maintain only a limited number of stable relationships — about 150, the "Dunbar number" — arranged in layers of intimacy, from a handful of closest friends outward. Modern social media has tested, but not obviously overturned, these cognitive limits.

Cultural variation

Norms of friendship vary: expectations of self-disclosure, physical affection, obligations of hospitality and gift-giving, and the boundary between friendship and kinship differ widely between cultures. Some languages have elaborated vocabularies for grades of friend, and institutions such as blood brotherhood and godparenthood historically formalised friendship ties.

Dissolution

Friendships end by drift, betrayal, relocation or conflict; unlike marriage or employment, friendship usually has no formal process of ending, which can make its dissolution ambiguous. Research suggests most people replace lost friends, with networks turning over substantially across a decade.
