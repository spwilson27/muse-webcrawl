Police procedural

Crime-scene investigation is a hallmark of the police procedural.

The police procedural, or police crime drama, is a subgenre of procedural drama and detective fiction that emphasizes the investigative procedure of police officers as they investigate a crime. Unlike the classical whodunit, which often conceals the criminal's identity until the climax, the police procedural frequently shows the audience the crime at the outset and follows the methodical work — interviews, forensics, surveillance, and paperwork — by which the police solve it.

Characteristics

Police procedurals typically depict an ensemble of officers rather than a lone detective, and follow several cases or a case interwoven with the officers' professional and personal routines. The genre aims for realism in depicting police methods, ranks, and institutions, though it often compresses or dramatizes them.

History

The form developed in American fiction in the 1940s and 1950s, influenced by the semi-documentary radio and television series Dragnet and by Ed McBain's 87th Precinct novels (begun 1956), which established the squad-room ensemble. Lawrence Treat's V as in Victim (1945) is often cited as an early example.

On television the genre became dominant from the late 20th century, with series such as Hill Street Blues, Law & Order and its spin-offs, CSI: Crime Scene Investigation (which foregrounded forensic science), and NCIS. In literature, Sjöwall and Wahlöö's Martin Beck novels pioneered the Scandinavian crime tradition, and writers such as Michael Connelly and Ian Rankin brought the form into the 21st century.
