Distance-transitive graph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a distance-transitive graph is a graph such that, given any two vertices v and w at any distance i, and any other two vertices x and y at the same distance i, there is an automorphism of the graph that maps v to x and w to y. Distance-transitive graphs are among the most symmetric of all graphs.

Examples and classification

Examples include complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs, cycle graphs, the Petersen graph, the Hoffman–Singleton graph, odd graphs and the hypercube graphs. The first systematic treatment was given by Norman Biggs and D. H. Smith in 1971, who showed that there are exactly 12 finite connected cubic (trivalent) distance-transitive graphs, now known as the Biggs–Smith classification. Finite distance-transitive graphs of larger valency were extensively studied in connection with the classification of finite simple groups.

Related concepts

Every distance-transitive graph is distance-regular and vertex-transitive, but the converses do not hold. A weaker notion is distance-transitivity of a permutation group acting on the graph's vertices. Incomplete or partial classification results exist for many families, and infinite distance-transitive graphs have also been studied.
