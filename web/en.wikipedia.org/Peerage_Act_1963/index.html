Peerage Act 1963


The Act reformed membership of the House of Lords.

The Peerage Act 1963 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that permitted women to sit in the House of Lords in their own right, allowed all Scottish peers to sit in the Lords, and — most famously — permitted a person to disclaim an inherited peerage for life.

Background and provisions

The disclaimer provision followed the campaign of Tony Benn, who had been forced out of the House of Commons on inheriting the Viscountcy of Stansgate in 1960. Under the Act, a peer could disclaim within twelve months of succeeding (or, for those who had already succeeded, within a limited window after the Act). Benn disclaimed at once and was re-elected to the Commons. Alec Douglas-Home likewise disclaimed his earldom in 1963 to become Prime Minister from the Commons, and Quintin Hogg disclaimed to stand for the Conservative leadership.

Later changes

The House of Lords Act 1999 removed most hereditary peers' automatic right to sit, largely superseding the Act's Scottish and female-peer provisions, but disclaimer of peerages remains possible under the 1963 Act.
