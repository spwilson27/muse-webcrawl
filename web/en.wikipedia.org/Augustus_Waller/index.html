Augustus Volney Waller, British physiologist.

Augustus Volney Waller FRS (21 December 1816 – 18 September 1870) was a British neurophysiologist. He was the first to describe the degeneration of severed nerve fibres, now known as Wallerian degeneration.

Life

Waller was born at Elverton Farm near Faversham in Kent and spent his youth in Nice, where his father died in 1830. He studied medicine at Edinburgh, graduating MD in 1840, and practised in London before devoting himself increasingly to microscopic research. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1851, and received the Royal Medal in 1860. He later worked in Bonn and Paris; he died in Geneva in 1870 of angina pectoris.

Work

In his celebrated 1850 paper "Experiments on the Section of the Glossopharyngeal and Hypoglossal Nerves of the Frog, and Observations of the Alterations Produced Thereby in the Structure of Their Primitive Fibres", Waller showed that when a nerve is cut, the portion separated from the nerve cell body disintegrates, while the portion still connected to the cell survives. This demonstrated that nerve fibres depend for their nutrition on their cell bodies, and gave experimenters a powerful method — tracing degenerating fibres — for mapping nerve pathways in the central nervous system. The process, in which the axon and its myelin sheath break down and are cleared by Schwann cells and macrophages, remains a central model in the study of nerve injury and regeneration.

Waller also made observations on the diapedesis of white blood cells and on cilia. His son, Augustus Désiré Waller, became a distinguished physiologist in his own right and recorded the first human electrocardiogram.
