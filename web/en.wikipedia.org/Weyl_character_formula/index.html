In mathematics, the Weyl character formula, discovered by Hermann Weyl in work published in 1925 and 1926, describes the character of a finite-dimensional irreducible representation of a complex semisimple Lie group (or, equivalently, of a compact Lie group) in terms of its highest weight. The character — a class function on the group, equal to the trace of each group element in the representation — is expressed as a ratio of two alternating sums over the Weyl group, one of which is the Weyl denominator.

Statement

Let G be a compact connected Lie group with maximal torus T, let ρ be half the sum of the positive roots, and let λ be the highest weight of an irreducible representation V. The character of V is given by

ch(V) = [ Σw ∈ W sgn(w) ew(λ+ρ) ] / [ Σw ∈ W sgn(w) ewρ ]

where W is the Weyl group, sgn(w) is the sign of the permutation w, and the exponentials are understood as characters of the torus. The denominator, known as the Weyl denominator, can be rewritten as a product over the positive roots, and is closely related to the Vandermonde determinant in the classical case of SU(n).

Dimension formula

Evaluating the character at the identity element yields the Weyl dimension formula, which gives the dimension of the representation as a product over the positive roots α:

dim V = Πα > 0 ⟨λ + ρ, α⟩ / ⟨ρ, α⟩

This was known to Weyl and generalises the familiar formulae for the spins of representations of SU(2).

Proof and generalisations

Weyl's original proof used topological methods for compact groups and algebraic methods (the study of Verma modules) for the complex case. The formula admits far-reaching generalisations: the Weyl–Kac character formula extends it to infinite-dimensional Kac–Moody algebras, and related denominator identities of Macdonald connect it with affine Lie algebras and modular forms. The character theory built on the formula is fundamental to the classification of representations of Lie groups and to applications in theoretical physics.
