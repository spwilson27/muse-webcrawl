Aquatic ecology

Aquatic ecology is the study of aquatic ecosystems and the interactions of organisms living in water with one another and with their physical and chemical environment. It encompasses both marine ecosystems (oceans, seas, estuaries, coral reefs, salt marshes) and freshwater ecosystems (lakes, ponds, rivers, streams, wetlands), the latter traditionally studied under limnology. Aquatic ecologists examine energy flow, nutrient cycling, food webs, and biodiversity in water bodies, and apply that knowledge to conservation, fisheries, and water-quality management.

Aquatic ecosystems

Aquatic ecosystems cover roughly three-quarters of the Earth's surface. They are commonly divided into marine ecosystems, characterized by high salt content, and freshwater ecosystems, with low salt content. Within them, ecologists distinguish the sunlit surface layer (euphotic zone), the open water (pelagic zone), and the bottom (benthic zone), each with characteristic communities of plankton, nekton, and benthos.

Key processes

Primary production by phytoplankton and aquatic plants forms the base of most aquatic food webs. Nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus limit production; excess nutrients cause eutrophication and harmful algal blooms. Temperature, dissolved oxygen, light penetration, currents, and salinity structure aquatic communities. Aquatic ecologists also study the effects of pollution, invasive species, overfishing, and climate change, and use indicator organisms to monitor ecosystem health.
