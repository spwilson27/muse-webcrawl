The Old Kingdom is the period in the history of ancient Egypt spanning from the Third Dynasty through the Sixth Dynasty (2686–2181 BC). It is also often called the "Age of the Pyramids" or the "Age of the Pyramid Builders," as it encompasses the reigns of the great pyramid-building pharaohs of the Fourth Dynasty, such as Sneferu, Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure, who are known for constructing the famous Giza pyramid complex.

Third Dynasty

The Old Kingdom began with the Third Dynasty. Its most famous king, Djoser (c. 2670 BC), commissioned the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, designed by his vizier Imhotep — the first large-scale stone building in Egypt and a landmark in architectural history. Imhotep was later deified, a rare honor for a commoner.

Fourth Dynasty

Sneferu, founder of the Fourth Dynasty, built several pyramids, including the Bent Pyramid and the Red Pyramid at Dahshur, experimenting toward the true smooth-sided form. His son Khufu built the Great Pyramid of Giza, the largest Egyptian pyramid and, for more than 3,800 years, the tallest human-made structure in the world. Khafre built the second pyramid and is associated with the Great Sphinx, while Menkaure built the smallest of the three Giza pyramids.

Fifth and Sixth Dynasties

The Fifth Dynasty saw the rise of the sun cult of Ra, with kings building sun temples at Abu Gurab and smaller pyramids at Abusir. The Pyramid Texts, the oldest substantial body of religious writing in the world, first appeared inscribed in the pyramid of Unas at the end of the dynasty. In the Sixth Dynasty, power gradually decentralized as provincial governors (nomarchs) grew wealthier and more independent. Pepi II, one of the longest-reigning monarchs in history, ruled for over 90 years according to tradition, and the kingdom fragmented after his death.

Collapse

A combination of factors — the aging Pepi II, succession disputes, the growing power of the nobility, and possibly a period of low Nile floods linked to the 4.2-kiloyear climate event — led to the collapse of central authority around 2181 BC and the beginning of the First Intermediate Period.

Administration and culture

The Old Kingdom state was highly centralized, administered from Memphis by a bureaucracy of scribes and viziers. The king was regarded as a living god, the embodiment of Horus and son of Ra. Monumental art, sculpture, and tomb painting reached a classical standard of form that later Egyptian art repeatedly emulated.
