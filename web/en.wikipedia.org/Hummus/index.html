Hummus

Hummus (Arabic: حُمُّص, "chickpeas") is a Middle Eastern dip or spread made from cooked, mashed chickpeas blended with tahini, lemon juice and garlic. It is a staple of Levantine cuisine and a popular food worldwide, eaten with pita bread, vegetables or as part of a mezze.

History

Chickpeas have been cultivated in the Middle East for thousands of years. The earliest known recipes resembling hummus appear in Arabic cookbooks from 13th-century Cairo, though the dish's precise origins are debated and claimed by several Levantine communities. Hummus bi tahini became widely established in the Ottoman Levant — today's Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, Israel and Jordan.

Preparation

Dried chickpeas are soaked, sometimes with baking soda, and simmered until very soft, then blended with tahini, lemon juice, garlic and salt, loosening with cooking liquid for a smooth texture. It is traditionally spread in a shallow bowl, swirled with the back of a spoon, and topped with olive oil, whole chickpeas, paprika or cumin.

Nutrition and spread

Hummus provides plant protein, fibre and unsaturated fats, and features in vegetarian and vegan diets globally. Industrial production, led by brands in Israel and the United States, made it a supermarket staple from the 1990s onward; playful rivalries over the "world's largest hummus" between Lebanon and Israel have set Guinness records of several tonnes.
