Book burning


Public burning of books, a recurring act of censorship

Book burning is the deliberate destruction by fire of books or other written materials, usually carried out in public. It is often a component of censorship and typically proceeds from a cultural, religious, or political opposition to the material in question. In some cases the books destroyed are irreplaceable, making the act a severe form of cultural vandalism.

Historical instances

Chinese tradition holds that in 213 BCE, the Qin emperor Qin Shi Huang ordered the burning of books and burying of scholars to suppress dissenting schools of thought. Roman and early Christian authorities burned magical and heretical texts. In 1520 Martin Luther publicly burned the papal bull threatening him with excommunication. The Spanish Inquisition consigned prohibited books to flames under the Index Librorum Prohibitorum.

The most infamous modern episode occurred on 10 May 1933, when Nazi student organizations burned some 25,000 "un-German" volumes in Berlin's Opernplatz and other university towns—an act the poet Heinrich Heine had chillingly foreshadowed a century earlier: "Where they burn books, they will, in the end, burn human beings too." Diego de Landa's 1562 auto-da-fé at Maní destroyed most Maya codices; only a handful survive (see religious texts for the broader context of scripture preservation). Twentieth- and twenty-first-century regimes and movements have continued the practice, from Sarajevo's National Library (1992) to ISIS's destruction of libraries in Mosul (2015).

Responses

Book burning is commemorated as a warning against intolerance; Ray Bradbury's novel Fahrenheit 451 (1953) imagines a society where firemen burn all books. Libraries and archives respond with preservation copying, and Banned Books Week campaigns celebrate the freedom to read.
