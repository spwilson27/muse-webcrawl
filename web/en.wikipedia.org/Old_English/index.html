Old English (Englisċ, pronounced [ˈeŋɡliʃ]), or Anglo-Saxon, is the earliest recorded form of the English language, spoken in England and southern and eastern Scotland in the Early Middle Ages. It developed from the dialects brought to Great Britain by Anglo-Saxon settlers from the mid-5th century, and was spoken and written until the transition to Middle English, which began after the Norman Conquest of 1066 and is usually dated to around 1150.

Characteristics

Old English differs markedly from modern English. It had a complex inflectional system: nouns declined in four cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative) and three genders, adjectives had strong and weak declensions, and verbs conjugated strongly and weakly. Word order was freer than in modern English. Its vocabulary was overwhelmingly Germanic, with loanwords mainly from Latin and, later, Old Norse. It was written first in runes and then in an insular form of the Latin alphabet, using letters such as ash (æ), thorn (þ), and eth (ð).

Dialects and texts

Four main dialects are recognised: Northumbrian, Mercian, Kentish, and West Saxon, the last becoming the dominant literary standard under King Alfred the Great, who sponsored translations of Latin works. The surviving corpus includes the epic poem Beowulf, elegies such as The Wanderer and The Seafarer, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, homilies by Ælfric and Wulfstan, riddles, and glosses. Almost all surviving Old English poetry is preserved in four codices, including the Exeter Book and the Nowell Codex containing Beowulf.

Development

Contact with Norse speakers in the Danelaw simplified inflections and contributed everyday words (sky, egg, they), while the Norman Conquest introduced French as the language of government and began the wholesale borrowing from French that reshaped English into its Middle English form.
