The Kingdom of France was a medieval and early modern monarchy in Western Europe, the predecessor of the modern French Republic. Conventionally dated from the accession of Hugh Capet in 987, it endured, with interruptions during the French Revolution and the Napoleonic era, until 1848.

Origins

The kingdom grew out of West Francia, the western third of the Carolingian Empire partitioned by the Treaty of Verdun in 843. The Capetian dynasty, founded by Hugh Capet, gradually extended royal authority from the Île-de-France over powerful feudal principalities such as Normandy, Aquitaine and Burgundy.

High Middle Ages

Under Philip II Augustus (r. 1180–1223) royal power expanded sharply at the expense of the Angevin kings of England. Louis IX, later canonised, reformed justice and law. The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) against England, and the career of Joan of Arc, forged a stronger sense of French identity and a standing royal army.

Early modern period

The Renaissance kings Francis I and Henry IV strengthened central government, but the French Wars of Religion (1562–1598) split the kingdom between Catholics and Huguenots. Under Louis XIII and his minister Cardinal Richelieu, and then Louis XIV, France became Europe's dominant power and the model of absolute monarchy, centred on the palace of Versailles.

Revolution and end

Fiscal crisis and Enlightenment criticism led to the Revolution of 1789; the monarchy was abolished in 1792 and Louis XVI executed in 1793. Restored in 1814–15, the kingdom fell again in the July Revolution of 1830 and finally in 1848, when the Second Republic was proclaimed.
