Strategic bombing

Strategic bombing is a military strategy used in total war with the goal of defeating the enemy by destroying its military capacity or its will to fight, by attacking infrastructure, industry, transportation, and command centers — and in practice often cities themselves. It contrasts with tactical bombing, which directly supports ground and naval forces.

Development

Theorists such as Giulio Douhet argued in the interwar period that bomber fleets could win wars alone by shattering enemy industry and morale ("the bomber will always get through"). Zeppelin and Gotha raids in the First World War foreshadowed the practice, but it reached its zenith in the Second World War: the Blitz, the RAF's night area-bombing campaign (Hamburg, Dresden), the USAAF's daylight bombing of Germany, and the firebombing of Japan, culminating in the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Assessment and later use

Postwar surveys found strategic bombing's effects mixed: it badly damaged Axis war production late in the war but did not by itself break civilian morale as predicted. In the Cold War, strategic bombing merged with nuclear deterrence, centered on fleets of heavy bombers and later intercontinental missiles. Precision-guided munitions from the Vietnam era onward shifted emphasis toward limited, targeted campaigns, though the legality and ethics of attacks on civilian populations remain governed by the laws of armed conflict.
