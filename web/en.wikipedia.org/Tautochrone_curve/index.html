Tautochrone curve

Objects released from different points on a tautochrone reach the bottom simultaneously.

A tautochrone curve (from Greek tauto, "same", and chronos, "time") is the curve for which the time taken by an object sliding without friction in uniform gravity to its lowest point is independent of its starting point on the curve. The curve is a cycloid, and the time to reach the bottom equals π√(r/g), where r is the radius of the circle generating the cycloid.

History

The tautochrone problem was solved by Christiaan Huygens in 1659, geometrically, and published in his Horologium Oscillatorium (1673). Huygens also proved that the cycloid is the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling circle, and used the tautochrone property to design a pendulum clock whose bob swings along a cycloidal arc — bounded by cycloidal "cheeks" — so that its period is independent of the amplitude of swing.

The tautochrone problem

Determining the curve from the condition of equal descent time can be done using the conservation of energy, converting the condition into an integral equation. Niels Henrik Abel gave the first general analytic treatment in 1823, in what became a foundational example of an integral equation — the problem is sometimes called Abel's mechanical problem. The related brachistochrone problem — the curve of fastest descent — also has the cycloid as its solution.
