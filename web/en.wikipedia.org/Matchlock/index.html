Matchlock

The matchlock was a type of firearm lock mechanism used to ignite the powder charge of early guns. It held a length of slow-burning cord, the "slow match," in a clamp called the serpentine; pulling a trigger or lever lowered the smouldering match into a priming pan, igniting the powder and firing the weapon. The matchlock appeared in Europe in the mid-15th century, spread rapidly through Asia via trade and conquest, and remained the dominant small-arms ignition system for roughly two centuries.

Matchlock
Type: Firearm ignition mechanism
Place of origin: Europe
In service: c. 1475 – c. 1700 (Europe); later in Asia
Predecessor: Hand cannon
Successor: Wheellock, flintlock

History

Earlier hand cannons were fired by touching a hot wire or coal to a touch hole, an awkward process that occupied both hands. The matchlock freed one hand and allowed the shooter to aim. By the 1470s matchlock arquebuses were in use in European warfare, and Portuguese traders carried them to India, Southeast Asia, China, and Japan, where the tanegashima became a decisive battlefield weapon after 1543.

Mechanism

The serpentine, an S-shaped arm, gripped the slow match — hemp or flax cord boiled in saltpetre solution so it smouldered without flame. On firing, the serpentine dipped the match into the pan of fine priming powder beside the barrel; the flash passed through the touch hole to the main charge. Drawbacks included the need to keep the match lit, its glow and smell betraying positions at night, and its unreliability in rain.

Decline

The wheellock, snaplock, and ultimately the flintlock offered ignition without a perpetually burning cord, and by the late 17th century the matchlock had largely vanished from European service, though it survived longer in parts of Asia.
