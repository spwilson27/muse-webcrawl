Micropaleontology

SubjectMicrofossils: foraminifera, radiolaria, diatoms, ostracods, conodonts, pollen and spores, calcareous nannofossils
MethodsMicroscopy, sample washing and sieving, biostratigraphy, isotope geochemistry
ApplicationsPetroleum exploration, paleoclimatology, biostratigraphic dating

Micropaleontology (American spelling; also spelled micropalaeontology) is the branch of paleontology that studies microfossils — fossil organisms whose characteristics require the use of a microscope to be properly examined. Microfossils are abundant, widely distributed, and evolve rapidly, making them among the most useful tools for dating and correlating sedimentary rocks and reconstructing past environments.

Groups studied

Major microfossil groups include foraminifera (single-celled protists with shells, or tests), radiolarians and diatoms with siliceous skeletons, calcareous nannofossils (coccoliths) produced by haptophyte algae, ostracods (small bivalved crustaceans), conodont elements (phosphatic tooth-like remains of early vertebrates), and palynomorphs such as pollen, spores, and dinoflagellate cysts. Samples are typically obtained from outcrops, well cuttings, and deep-sea sediment cores and are prepared by washing, sieving, and picking under a stereomicroscope.

Applications

The petroleum industry adopted micropaleontology in the early 20th century to date and correlate subsurface strata from drill cuttings, and biostratigraphy based on planktonic foraminifera and nannofossils remains central to deep-water exploration. Because microfossil shells record the chemistry of ancient seawater, isotope analysis of foraminifera (notably oxygen isotope stratigraphy) underpins much of paleoclimatology, including reconstructions of past temperatures and ice volume. Microfossil assemblages are also used to infer paleodepth, salinity, ocean productivity, and the timing of mass extinctions and recoveries.
