Han system

CountryJapan (Edo period)
Periodc. 1603–1868
Key figuresDaimyō, Tokugawa shogunate
Abolished1871 (haihan-chiken)

The han system was the feudal administrative structure of Tokugawa Japan, under which the country was divided into domains called han, each governed by a daimyō under the authority of the Tokugawa shogunate in Edo. A han was conventionally defined as a domain assessed at 10,000 koku of rice or more.

Structure

At its height there were roughly 250–300 han, varying greatly in size and wealth. Daimyō were classified by their relation to the Tokugawa house (shinpan, fudai, and tozama) and were subject to shogunal controls including the sankin-kōtai system of alternate attendance in Edo and the Laws for the Military Houses (buke shohatto). Within his domain a daimyō exercised substantial local autonomy in taxation, justice, and administration, centred on a castle town.

Abolition

After the Meiji Restoration, the domains were first returned to the emperor in 1869 (hanseki hōkan) and then abolished outright in 1871 (haihan-chiken), being replaced by prefectures governed by appointed officials — a decisive step in centralising the modern Japanese state.
