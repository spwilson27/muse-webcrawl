Leonidas I (died 11 August 480 BC) was a king of the Greek city-state of Sparta. He was the seventeenth king of the Agiad line and one of the sons of King Anaxandridas II. Leonidas is remembered above all for leading the allied Greek forces at the Battle of Thermopylae in 480 BC, where he died with his force of 300 Spartans and their allies holding a narrow pass against the invading army of the Persian king Xerxes I.

Life

Leonidas was not born to succeed: he was a younger son, and became king around 489 BC only after his half-brother Cleomenes I died without a male heir. He married Cleomenes' daughter Gorgo, renowned in the sources for her intelligence and counsel. Like all Spartan citizens he had passed through the agoge, the rigorous state upbringing.

Thermopylae

In the summer of 480 BC, during the second Persian invasion of Greece, Leonidas led an advance force of about 7,000 Greeks to hold the pass of Thermopylae ("the Hot Gates") between the mountains and the sea. For two days the Greeks repelled frontal Persian assaults. On the third day the Persians, guided over a mountain path by the local man Ephialtes, outflanked the position. Leonidas dismissed most of the allied army and remained with his 300 Spartans, together with 700 Thespians and 400 Thebans, fighting to the death.

Legacy

The stand at Thermopylae became the archetype of heroic last stands. Herodotus preserves the famous epitaph attributed to Simonides: "Go tell the Spartans, stranger passing by, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie." Leonidas's reported reply to Xerxes' demand to surrender his weapons — molōn labe, "come and take them" — remains a byword for defiance. A monument and annual festival, the Leonidea, were held in his honour at Sparta.
