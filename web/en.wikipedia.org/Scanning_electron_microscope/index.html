Scanning electron microscope

A modern scanning electron microscope (SEM).

A scanning electron microscope (SEM) is a type of electron microscope that produces images of a sample by scanning its surface with a focused beam of electrons. The electrons interact with atoms in the sample, producing various signals that contain information about the surface topography and composition of the sample. The electron beam is scanned in a raster scan pattern, and the position of the beam is combined with the intensity of the detected signal to produce an image.

In the most common SEM mode, secondary electrons emitted by atoms excited by the electron beam are detected using a secondary electron detector such as the Everhart–Thornley detector. The number of secondary electrons that can be detected, and thus the signal intensity, depends, among other things, on specimen topography. Some SEMs can achieve resolutions better than 1 nanometer.

History

The first SEM image was obtained by Max Knoll in 1935, showing image contrast from silicon steel. The instrument was further developed by Manfred von Ardenne in 1937. In the 1960s, Charles Oatley and his team at the University of Cambridge developed the instruments that led to the first commercial SEM, marketed by the Cambridge Instrument Company in 1965.

Principles

An electron gun at the top of the microscope emits a beam of electrons, which travels down through a series of magnetic lenses designed to focus the electrons to a very fine spot. Near the bottom, a set of scanning coils moves the focused beam back and forth across the specimen in a grid pattern. When the electron beam hits the sample, secondary electrons, backscattered electrons, and characteristic X-rays are emitted. Detectors collect these signals to form the image.

Sample preparation

Specimens are generally mounted rigidly on a specimen stub using a conductive adhesive. Because the SEM operates under vacuum, samples must be completely dry, and non-conductive samples are usually coated with an ultrathin layer of electrically conducting material, such as gold or platinum, by sputter coating.

Applications

SEM is used in materials science, semiconductor inspection, biological research, forensics, and geology. When combined with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), it can also determine the elemental composition of microscopic regions of a sample.
