Poor Law

The Poor Laws were a system of poor relief in England and Wales that developed from late-medieval and Tudor-era statutes. The system provided material support — "relief" — to people who could not support themselves, financed by a local tax (the poor rate) and administered by individual parishes. It was overhauled by the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 and gradually superseded by the modern welfare state in the 20th century, though elements survived until 1948.

The Old Poor Law

The Elizabethan Poor Relief Act 1601 codified earlier legislation: each parish was responsible for its own poor, overseers collected the poor rate, and relief was given to the "impotent" poor (aged, sick, or orphaned), while the able-bodied were set to work and the "idle" punished. Later refinements included workhouses, the Settlement Laws (1662), which restricted relief to one's parish of settlement, and allowances in aid of wages such as the Speenhamland system (1795).

The New Poor Law

A Royal Commission of 1832–34, influenced by Edwin Chadwick and Nassau Senior, condemned the old system as demoralising and expensive. The 1834 Act created a central Poor Law Commission, grouped parishes into unions with elected Boards of Guardians, and imposed the principles of "less eligibility" and the workhouse test: relief for the able-bodied was to be available only inside workhouses deliberately made less attractive than the lowest-paid employment outside. The system provoked strong resistance, especially in the industrial north.

Decline and abolition

From the late 19th century the Poor Law's functions were progressively transferred elsewhere: public health, education, old-age pensions (1908), and national insurance (1911). Boards of Guardians were abolished in 1929, and the National Assistance Act 1948 formally repealed the Poor Law, closing nearly four centuries of history. Similar but distinct systems operated in Scotland (after 1845) and Ireland (from 1838).
