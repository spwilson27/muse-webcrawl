Sir Joseph John Thomson OM FRS (18 December 1856 – 30 August 1940) was a British physicist credited with the discovery of the electron, the first subatomic particle identified. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906 for his work on the conduction of electricity in gases.

Early life and career

Thomson was born in Cheetham Hill, Manchester, the son of a bookseller. He studied engineering at Owens College (later the University of Manchester) and then mathematics at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he became a fellow. In 1884, aged only 27, he was appointed Cavendish Professor of Experimental Physics at Cambridge, succeeding Lord Rayleigh. He directed the Cavendish Laboratory for 35 years and built it into one of the world's leading research schools; seven of his students later won Nobel Prizes.

Discovery of the electron

In 1897 Thomson's experiments with cathode rays showed that the rays consisted of particles — he called them "corpuscles" — far lighter than any atom, with a charge-to-mass ratio independent of the gas or electrode material used. He concluded that these particles were universal constituents of all atoms, overturning the idea that the atom was indivisible. The corpuscle was soon renamed the electron, after George Stoney's term for the fundamental unit of charge.

Atomic model and other work

In 1904 Thomson proposed the "plum pudding model," in which electrons were embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge. The model was later superseded by Ernest Rutherford's nuclear model — Rutherford had himself been Thomson's student. Thomson also pioneered mass spectrometry: his parabola spectrograph revealed that neon consisted of atoms of two different masses (isotopes), the first evidence for stable isotopes in a non-radioactive element. He studied positive rays (canal rays), for which he won further recognition, and mentored a generation of physicists including Rutherford, Charles Barkla, and his own son George Paget Thomson, who later demonstrated electron diffraction — showing the wave nature of the particle his father had discovered.

Later life

Thomson was knighted in 1908, served as president of the Royal Society from 1915 to 1920, and was Master of Trinity College, Cambridge, from 1918 until his death. He is buried in Westminster Abbey near Isaac Newton.
