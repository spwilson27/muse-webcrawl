Ernest Starling

Ernest Henry Starling CMG FRCP FRS (17 April 1866 – 2 May 1927) was a British physiologist who made lasting contributions to the understanding of body functions. Working mainly at University College London, often with his brother-in-law William Bayliss, he introduced the concept of hormones, formulated the law of the heart that bears his name, and described the forces governing fluid exchange across capillary walls.

Secretin and the hormone concept

In 1902 Starling and Bayliss demonstrated that acid entering the duodenum causes the intestinal mucosa to release a substance into the blood which stimulates the pancreas to secrete — even when all nerves to the organs were cut. They named the substance secretin, the first recognised hormone. In his 1905 Croonian Lectures Starling proposed the word "hormone" (from the Greek hormao, "I excite or arouse") for such chemical messengers, founding modern endocrinology together with the clinical work of contemporaries.

Other contributions

The Frank–Starling law of the heart (based on his 1914 Linacre Lecture, "The Law of the Heart", and complementary work by Otto Frank) states that the stroke volume of the heart increases in response to an increase in the volume of blood filling it, because stretched cardiac muscle contracts more forcefully. The Starling equation (1896) describes the balance of hydrostatic and oncotic pressures that drives filtration and reabsorption of fluid across capillary membranes — the "Starling forces" central to the physiology of oedema. With Bayliss he also studied peristalsis and the electrical activity of the heart.

Later life

Starling served as a medical officer in the First World War, working in Salonika and on food rationing policy. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1899 and appointed CMG in 1920. He died aboard ship in Kingston Harbour, Jamaica, in 1927.
