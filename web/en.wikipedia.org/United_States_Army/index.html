United States Army

The United States Army is the land service branch of the United States Armed Forces and the oldest established branch of the US military, founded on 14 June 1775 as the Continental Army — over a year before the Declaration of Independence. It is the largest of the US armed services by personnel, with roughly 450,000 active-duty soldiers plus large National Guard and Army Reserve components, and is tasked with fighting and winning the nation's land wars.

History

Under George Washington the Continental Army fought the Revolutionary War, disbanding in 1783; the Legion of the United States replaced it in 1792. The Army fought the War of 1812, the Mexican–American War, and expanded enormously for the Civil War, in which some 620,000 Americans died — more than in any other American conflict. It conquered the West, fought the Spanish–American War, and mobilised millions for the two World Wars, fielding over 8 million soldiers at its World War II peak. After 1945 the Army fought in Korea and Vietnam, was rebuilt as an all-volunteer force after the draft ended in 1973, defeated Iraq in the 1991 Gulf War, and then fought long counterinsurgency campaigns in Afghanistan (2001–2021) and Iraq (2003–2011).

Structure

The Army is organised into divisions, brigades (including Brigade Combat Teams), and corps, grouped under Army Service Component Commands for each geographic combatant command. Key branches include Infantry, Armor, Field Artillery, Aviation, Engineers, and the special-operations community under the United States Army Special Operations Command — including the Green Berets (Special Forces), the 75th Ranger Regiment, and Delta Force (1st SFOD-D). Army officers are commissioned through West Point, ROTC and Officer Candidate School.

Culture

The Army's values are encapsulated in the acronym LDRSHIP: Loyalty, Duty, Respect, Selfless Service, Honor, Integrity, Personal Courage. Its birthday, 14 June, is celebrated annually; its song is "The Army Goes Rolling Along."
