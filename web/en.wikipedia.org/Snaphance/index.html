Snaphance

A snaphance lock, with its separate steel and pan cover

A snaphance or snaphaunce is a type of firearm lock in which a flint, held in a cock (hammer), strikes a hinged steel plate to produce sparks that ignite the priming powder, firing the weapon. It was an intermediate development between the earlier wheel lock and the later true flintlock, and was in use from the mid-16th century into the early 18th century, with regional variants persisting longer.

Mechanism

The snaphance differs from the mature flintlock in having a separate steel (frizzen) and pan cover: the pan cover must be pushed open — in some designs it opens automatically via a linkage when the trigger is pulled — and the steel is a plain curved plate that the cock strikes. In the true flintlock, by contrast, the steel and pan cover are a single L-shaped piece that the flint both strikes and pushes open in one motion. The name is thought to derive from the Dutch snaphaan, "snapping hen" or "pecking hen", describing the pecking action of the cock.

Use and variants

Snaphances were cheaper and simpler to maintain than wheel locks, whose clockwork mechanisms were delicate and expensive, and they spread widely in northern and eastern Europe. Regional derivatives include the Scandinavian snaplock, the Spanish miquelet lock and the Dutch and Baltic variants used well into the 1700s. The snaphance era coincides with the spread of the flintlock musket and pistol that would dominate warfare for two centuries.
