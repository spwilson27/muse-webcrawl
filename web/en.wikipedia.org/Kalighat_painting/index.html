Kalighat painting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

MediumWatercolour on paper or cloth
LocationKalighat Temple, Kolkata, India
Period19th – early 20th century

Kalighat painting is a school of Indian painting that flourished in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries around the Kalighat Kali Temple in Kolkata (Calcutta). Produced by hereditary scroll painters known as patuas who had migrated to the city from rural Bengal, the paintings were sold cheaply to the pilgrims and visitors who thronged the temple, and are recognised as one of the earliest forms of popular, mass-produced art in India.

Style

Kalighat paintings are characterised by bold, sweeping brushstrokes, flat areas of bright colour, minimal backgrounds, and shaded modelling that gives figures a sculptural volume. The painters worked rapidly with water-based pigments — later supplemented by cheap imported paints and lithographic tints — on mill-made paper, producing works quickly and in large numbers for a mass market.

Subjects

The earliest and most common subjects were Hindu deities associated with the temple — Kali, Shiva, Durga, Lakshmi, Krishna — together with scenes from the epics and Puranas. From the later nineteenth century the painters also turned to biting social satire: paintings mocked the new urban classes of colonial Calcutta, including the westernised babu, corrupt priests, courtesans, and episodes of celebrated scandals and crimes reported in the press.

Legacy

As chromolithography undercut the market for hand-painted images after about 1900, the Kalighat tradition declined. Its economy of line and bold modernity later attracted artists such as Jamini Roy, who drew on its idiom in developing a modern Indian painting style. Major collections are held by the Victoria and Albert Museum in London and the Victoria Memorial in Kolkata.
