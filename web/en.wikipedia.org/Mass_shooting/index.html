Mass shooting

A mass shooting is a violent crime in which one or more attackers use firearms to kill or injure multiple people in a single incident. There is no universally accepted definition: different governments, law-enforcement agencies, media outlets, and researchers use different thresholds — commonly three or four or more victims shot — and differ on whether to count only deaths, whether to include the perpetrator, and whether to exclude gang-, crime-, or family-related shootings. These differing definitions substantially affect reported statistics.

Definitions of "mass shooting" vary widely, which complicates comparison of statistics between sources and countries.

Definitions

The United States Congress, in the Investigative Assistance for Violent Crimes Act of 2012, defined "mass killing" as three or more killings in a single incident. Research databases such as the Gun Violence Archive count incidents in which four or more people are shot, regardless of outcome, while other definitions restrict the term to public attacks by a lone offender. Criminologists distinguish mass shootings from spree killings, serial killings, and familicide.

Incidence

Mass shootings occur worldwide but receive the greatest attention in the United States, which experiences a disproportionate share of public mass shootings and high-casualty attacks in schools and public places. High-profile attacks — such as those at Columbine High School (1999), Virginia Tech (2007), Sandy Hook Elementary School (2012), the Pulse nightclub (2016), and Las Vegas (2017, the deadliest in modern U.S. history) — have repeatedly driven national debates about firearms regulation, mental health, and security.

Perpetrators and motives

Perpetrators are overwhelmingly male, and motives examined in research include grievance, revenge, notoriety-seeking, extremist ideology, and mental illness, though most people with mental illness are not violent. Researchers also study media contagion effects — the possibility that intensive coverage of one attack raises the likelihood of subsequent attacks.

Responses and debate

Responses under debate include firearm legislation (background checks, restrictions on certain weapons), threat-assessment and reporting programmes in schools and workplaces, armed security, and changes to media coverage guidelines. The topic remains politically contentious, particularly in the United States.
