Hockey-stick identity

In combinatorics, the hockey-stick identity is an identity involving binomial coefficients. It states that a sum of binomial coefficients lying along a diagonal of Pascal's triangle equals a single binomial coefficient located below and to one side of the last term — so that the summed entries together with the result form a shape resembling a hockey stick.

Statement

For natural numbers r and n with n ≥ r, the identity reads:

Σi=rn C(i, r) = C(n+1, r+1)

where C(i, r) denotes the binomial coefficient "i choose r". In Pascal's triangle, the terms C(r, r), C(r+1, r), …, C(n, r) run down a diagonal (the "shaft" of the stick), and the value of the sum, C(n+1, r+1), sits at the "blade" of the stick. An equivalent form, summing along rows, is sometimes called the Christmas stocking identity.

Proofs

Algebraic proof (induction). Start from Pascal's identity, C(m, k) + C(m, k+1) = C(m+1, k+1). Repeatedly applying Pascal's rule telescopes the sum: C(r, r) = C(r+1, r+1), so the sum C(r, r) + C(r+1, r) = C(r+1, r+1) + C(r+1, r) = C(r+2, r+1), and continuing in this way the partial sums successively become C(r+2, r+1), C(r+3, r+1), and finally C(n+1, r+1).

Combinatorial proof. Count the subsets of size r+1 of the set {1, 2, …, n+1} in two ways. On one hand there are C(n+1, r+1) such subsets. On the other hand, classify a subset by its largest element, say i+1, where i ranges from r to n. If i+1 is the largest element, the remaining r elements must be chosen from the i elements below it, which can be done in C(i, r) ways. Summing over all possible i gives C(n+1, r+1) = Σ C(i, r), proving the identity.

Applications

The identity is used to evaluate sums of polynomial sequences, since sums of binomial coefficients C(i, r) over i yield a closed form of degree r+1. For example, the familiar formulas for Σ i and Σ i² can be derived by writing i = C(i, 1) and i² in terms of binomial coefficients. The hockey-stick identity also appears in counting lattice paths, in the "stars and bars" arguments of enumerative combinatorics, and in computing cumulative binomial distributions. It generalises to sums of multinomial-like coefficients and to the q-analogues known as q-binomial coefficients.
