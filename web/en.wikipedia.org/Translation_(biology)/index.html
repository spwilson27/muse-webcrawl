In biology, translation is the process in living cells in which proteins are produced using RNA molecules as templates. The ribosome reads the sequence of a messenger RNA (mRNA) in three-nucleotide codons and, with transfer RNAs carrying amino acids, assembles a polypeptide chain whose sequence is specified by the gene. Translation is the second major step of gene expression, following transcription.

Stages

Initiation: the small ribosomal subunit, aided by initiation factors, binds the mRNA near the start codon (usually AUG). Elongation: aminoacyl-tRNAs enter the A site, a peptide bond forms between the growing chain and the new amino acid, and the ribosome translocates one codon; typical rates reach about 20 amino acids per second in bacteria. Termination: a stop codon recruits release factors, freeing the completed polypeptide.

The genetic code

The genetic code maps 64 codons to 20 standard amino acids and stop signals; it is nearly universal across life. Transfer RNA adaptors match each codon via their anticodon and are charged with the correct amino acid by aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases.

Regulation and medicine

Cells regulate translation through initiation-factor control, microRNAs, and stress responses. Many antibiotics — including tetracyclines, macrolides, and aminoglycosides — work by targeting bacterial ribosomes and inhibiting translation.
