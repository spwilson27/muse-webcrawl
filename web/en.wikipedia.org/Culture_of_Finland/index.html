Culture of Finland

The culture of Finland combines indigenous heritage, as represented by the national languages Finnish (a Uralic language) and Swedish (a Germanic language), the sauna, and the traditionally widespread rural lifestyle sustained by the nation's large forests and roughly 188,000 lakes, with common Nordic and European cultural influences. Historically, Finland was part of Sweden from the medieval period until 1809 and then an autonomous grand duchy of the Russian Empire until independence in 1917.

Character and customs

Finnish culture is often associated with qualities such as sisu — a concept of determination, resilience, and perseverance in the face of adversity — as well as modesty, honesty, punctuality, and a reserved communication style. The sauna is a central institution: with an estimated two to three million saunas for a population of about 5.5 million, sauna bathing is an integral part of national life, used for relaxation, socializing, and even business.

Literature and arts

The national epic, the Kalevala, compiled by Elias Lönnrot from oral folk poetry and published in 1835 and 1849, played a decisive role in the development of Finnish national identity. Notable Finnish cultural figures include composer Jean Sibelius, architect Alvar Aalto, painter Akseli Gallen-Kallela, author Mika Waltari, and Tove Jansson, creator of the Moomins.

Food and celebrations

Traditional Finnish cuisine draws on fish, game, rye bread, root vegetables, mushrooms, and berries such as cloudberry and lingonberry. Important celebrations include Midsummer (Juhannus), one of the year's biggest holidays, Vappu (May Day), and Independence Day on 6 December. Finland is also known for design brands such as Marimekko and Iittala, for heavy metal music, and for unusual competitions such as wife-carrying and swamp football.
