Porter's five forces analysis

Porter's five forces analysis is a framework for analyzing the competitiveness of an industry and developing business strategy, developed by Michael E. Porter of Harvard Business School in 1979. It draws on industrial organization economics to determine the competitive intensity, and therefore profitability, of an industry.

The five forces

The five forces are: (1) threat of new entrants, (2) threat of substitute products or services, (3) bargaining power of customers, (4) bargaining power of suppliers, and (5) intensity of competitive rivalry among existing firms. Together they determine an industry's structure and the ease with which incumbent firms can sustain profits.

Use

Companies use the framework to assess whether to enter an industry, where to position themselves, and how strategy can shift the forces in their favour. Porter later extended the work in his books Competitive Strategy (1980) and Competitive Advantage (1985).

Criticism

Critics note that the model assumes relatively static market structures, can understate the role of innovation and complementors (sometimes proposed as a sixth force), and was developed for an era of slower change.
