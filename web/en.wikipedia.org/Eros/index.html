Eros (Ancient Greek: Ἔρως, "desire") is a god of love and sexual desire in Greek mythology and, in the earliest Greek cosmology, one of the primordial powers of the universe. In Hesiod's Theogony he emerges after Chaos and Gaia as the force that draws beings together and makes generation possible. In later tradition he shrank into the mischievous winged child of Aphrodite, armed with a bow whose arrows kindle desire in gods and mortals alike; in Roman form he is Cupid.

Mythology

Philosophers as well as poets treated Eros as a cosmic principle: in Plato's Symposium, Socrates, reporting the priestess Diotima, describes Eros as an intermediate spirit between gods and men, and love as a ladder ascending from desire for a particular body to contemplation of Beauty itself. In the myth of Eros and Psyche, told fully in Apuleius' Metamorphoses, the god falls in love with a mortal princess; after trials imposed by Aphrodite, Psyche wins immortality and marries him. Eros was worshipped particularly at Thespiae in Boeotia, where his image was an ancient unwrought stone and later a famous statue by Praxiteles, and he shared a cult with Aphrodite on the Acropolis at Athens.

In philosophy and psychology

The word eros came to denote one of several Greek kinds of love, distinct from philia (friendship) and agape (selfless love). In psychoanalysis, Freud used "Eros" for the life drive — the ensemble of instincts toward life, sex, and creativity — set against the death drive, Thanatos. Carl Jung and later writers also drew on the figure. The adjective "erotic" and the concept of erotica derive from his name.
