Alliteration

Alliteration was the structural backbone of Old English and Old Norse poetry.

Alliteration is the repetition of the same initial consonant sound in a series of words close to one another in a phrase or line of verse. It is a stylistic literary device used in poetry, prose, rhetoric and everyday language, as in the phrase "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers".

History

Alliteration was a fundamental structural principle of early Germanic poetry, including Old English works such as Beowulf and the Old Norse Poetic Edda, in which stressed syllables in each half-line were bound together by matching initial sounds rather than by rhyme. It also appears in Old Irish, Finnish (the Kalevala) and Mongolian epic traditions. In Latin rhetoric, figures such as Quintilian discussed the effect, and the term itself derives from the Latin littera, "letter".

Use in literature

Poets use alliteration to create rhythm, mood and emphasis, and to link images together. Famous examples include Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven" and the tongue-twisters of popular speech. In prose and oratory, alliterative phrasing aids memorability, which is why it is common in advertising slogans, newspaper headlines and political speeches.

Related concepts

Alliteration is distinguished from consonance, the repetition of consonant sounds anywhere in words, and assonance, the repetition of vowel sounds. When the repeated sound is a sibilant, the effect is sometimes called sibilance.
