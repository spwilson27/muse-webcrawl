Field research

Field research, also known as field studies or fieldwork, is the collection of raw data outside a laboratory, library, or workplace setting. The methods of field research are central to anthropology, biology, earth sciences, economics, sociology, and many other disciplines, and range from structured surveys to long-term participant observation.

History

Field research has roots in natural history and early anthropology. Figures such as Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace gathered evidence through extended field expeditions, while Bronisław Malinowski's immersive fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands (1915–1918) established participant observation as the hallmark of ethnographic method in anthropology.

Methods

Common methods include direct observation, participant observation, interviews, questionnaires, focus groups, case studies, and the collection of physical samples or measurements. Field research is typically qualitative, exploratory, and concerned with understanding phenomena in their natural context, in contrast to laboratory experiments that manipulate variables under controlled conditions.

Strengths and limitations

Field studies offer ecological validity — data gathered where phenomena actually occur — and can reveal processes that laboratory studies miss. Their disadvantages include cost, limited control over confounding variables, reliance on the researcher's skill, and ethical obligations toward the communities studied, including informed consent.
