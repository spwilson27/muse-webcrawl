The Mevlevi Order or Mawlawiyya (Turkish: Mevlevilik) is a Sufi order that originated in Konya, Anatolia (in present-day Turkey), from the teachings of the 13th-century Persian poet, mystic, and Islamic scholar Jalal al-Din Muhammad Rumi (known in Turkish as Mevlana, "our master"). The Mevlevis are also known as the Whirling Dervishes after their famous practice of whirling as a form of dhikr (remembrance of God) during the sema ceremony.

History

Though inspired by Rumi (d. 1273), the order was organised by his son Sultan Walad and his successors, with a hierarchy of lodges (tekke or mevlevihane) spreading across the Ottoman Empire — to Istanbul (the Galata lodge, founded 1491), Aleppo, Damascus, Cairo, and the Balkans. Mevlevi lodges became centres of classical Turkish music, calligraphy, and poetry, and the order enjoyed close ties to the Ottoman court and the Janissary corps (shared with the Bektashis). Under the Turkish Republic, a 1925 law closed all Sufi lodges; the Mevlevi mausoleum in Konya reopened as a museum, and sema performances were later permitted as cultural presentations.

The sema

The sema ceremony, which symbolises the soul's journey toward God, combines sacred music — traditionally the reed flute (ney), drums, and chanted settings of Rumi's poetry — with the whirling of dervishes dressed in white gowns (tennure) and tall camel-hair hats (sikke). The dervish turns with the right palm facing upward to receive God's blessing and the left downward to transmit it to the earth. UNESCO proclaimed the Mevlevi Sema Ceremony a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity in 2005 (inscribed on the Representative List in 2008).

Teachings

The order rests on Rumi's vast poetic works, the Masnavi and the Divan-e Shams, emphasising love as the path to union with God, tolerance, charity, and the annihilation of the ego (fana). Novices traditionally underwent a 1001-day retreat of prayer and service, including kitchen service, before full initiation.
