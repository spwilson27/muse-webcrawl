Mesoamerican ballgame

The Mesoamerican ballgame (Nahuatl: ōllamalīztli; Classical Maya: pitz) was a sport with ritual associations played since at least 1650 BCE by the pre-Columbian peoples of Ancient Mesoamerica. The rules varied between places and times, but in most versions players struck a solid rubber ball with their hips, aiming to keep it in play in a masonry ballcourt; in some later versions the aim was to pass it through a high stone ring.

Origins and spread

The earliest known ballcourt, at Paso de la Amada in Chiapas, dates to about 1400 BCE, and rubber balls from El Manatí bog date centuries earlier. The game was played by the Olmec, Maya, Zapotec, Toltec, Aztec and other cultures; more than 1,300 ballcourts have been identified. In the Maya version the game echoed the myth of the Hero Twins in the Popol Vuh, who played ball against the lords of the underworld.

Ritual and sacrifice

The ballgame was far more than sport: it was tied to fertility, warfare and the movement of the sun, and in some cultures losing players — or, in others, the winners — were sacrificed, as suggested by decapitation imagery in the ballcourt reliefs at Chichén Itzá and El Tajín. The game survives today in modified form as ulama in Sinaloa, Mexico.
