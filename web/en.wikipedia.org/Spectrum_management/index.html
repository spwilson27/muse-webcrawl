Spectrum management

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Spectrum management is the process of regulating the use of radio frequencies to promote efficient use and gain a net social benefit. The term radio spectrum typically refers to the full frequency range from 1 Hz to 3000 GHz (3 THz) that may be used for wireless communication. Increasing demand for services such as mobile telephony and many others has required changes in the philosophy of spectrum management. Demand for wireless broadband has soared due to technological innovation, such as 3G and 4G mobile services, and the rapid expansion of wireless Internet services.

Allocation and assignment

Spectrum management operates at several levels: international allocation of frequency bands to services by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) through World Radiocommunication Conferences and the Radio Regulations; national allocation plans; and assignment of specific frequencies or licenses to individual users. Regulators such as the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) in the United States and Ofcom in the United Kingdom administer the spectrum within their jurisdictions.

Methods of assignment

Historically, spectrum was assigned by administrative "command and control" processes. Since the 1990s, many countries have used spectrum auctions to award licenses, an approach advocated argue allocates frequencies to their most valuable uses. Other approaches include license-exempt bands (used by Wi-Fi and Bluetooth), spectrum sharing, secondary markets and trading, and cognitive radio techniques that allow opportunistic use of idle frequencies.

Issues

Key debates include the balance between licensed and unlicensed use, refarming bands from older technologies (such as analog television) to mobile broadband, preventing harmful interference, ensuring spectrum for public safety and scientific services such as radio astronomy, and addressing the "digital divide" through coverage obligations.
