Limonite is a field term for an amorphous mixture of hydrated iron(III) oxide-hydroxides, principally goethite with varying amounts of hematite, lepidocrocite and adsorbed water. It is not a distinct mineral species — the name was discredited as a formal mineral in 2006 — but remains widely used for yellow-brown to dark brown earthy iron ores formed by the weathering of iron-bearing minerals. Its approximate formula is FeO(OH)·nH2O.

Formation

Limonite forms in oxidising environments near the surface, where iron-bearing minerals such as pyrite, siderite and magnetite are weathered. It is the characteristic material of gossans over ore deposits, of "bog iron ore" precipitated in wetlands and lakes, and of the iron-rich horizons of lateritic soils in the tropics.

Uses

Limonite was one of the earliest iron ores used by humans and an important ore of iron and of the pigment yellow ochre. Bog iron supplied much of the iron of the Viking Age and colonial America; large limonite deposits were worked in Cuba, and the mineraloid remains a minor iron ore and a source of ochre pigments.

Identification

Limonite is recognised by its yellow-brown streak — distinguishing it from hematite, which gives a red streak — and by its earthy, often botryoidal or stalactitic habit. Pseudomorphs after pyrite cubes are common and diagnostic of its secondary origin.
