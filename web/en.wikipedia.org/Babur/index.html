Zahīr ud-Dīn Muhammad Bābur (14 February 1483 – 26 December 1530), commonly known as Babur, was a conqueror from Central Asia who founded the Mughal Empire in the Indian subcontinent. He was a descendant of Timur on his father's side and of Genghis Khan through his mother. "Babur" is derived from a Persian word meaning "tiger" or "panther".

Early life and Central Asian campaigns

Babur was born in Andijan in the Fergana Valley (in present-day Uzbekistan). He became ruler of Fergana in 1494 at the age of eleven on the death of his father, Umar Shaikh Mirza II. Throughout his youth he fought to hold Fergana and to capture Samarkand, Timur's former capital, which he took and lost three times. Defeated by the Uzbek khan Muhammad Shaybani, Babur turned south and in 1504 seized Kabul, which became his base for the next two decades.

Conquest of India

From Kabul, Babur launched a series of incursions into northern India. The decisive moment came at the First Battle of Panipat on 21 April 1526, where his small army — employing field artillery and Ottoman-trained gunners under Ustad Ali Quli — destroyed the much larger forces of Ibrahim Lodi, Sultan of Delhi. Babur then defeated the Rajput confederacy under Rana Sanga at the Battle of Khanwa in 1527 and the Afghan chiefs at the Battle of Ghaghra in 1529, establishing Mughal rule over much of northern India.

The Baburnama

Babur is as celebrated for his writing as for his conquests. His memoir, the Baburnama (written in Chagatai Turkic), is regarded as one of the finest works of autobiography in Islamic literature, noted for its candid observations of nature, society and his own fortunes. He also composed poetry in Turkic and Persian and commissioned celebrated gardens, including the garden in Kabul where he was eventually buried.

Death and succession

Babur died in Agra on 26 December 1530 and was succeeded by his eldest son, Humayun. His body was later reinterred in the Bagh-e Babur (Babur's Garden) in Kabul, in accordance with his wishes.
