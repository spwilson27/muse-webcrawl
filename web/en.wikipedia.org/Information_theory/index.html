Information theory is the mathematical study of the quantification, storage, and communication of information. The field was established by the works of Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley in the 1920s, and Claude Shannon in the 1940s, whose 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" founded the discipline. Information theory lies at the intersection of probability theory, statistics, computer science, and electrical engineering, and underlies data compression, telecommunications, cryptography, and many other technologies.

Core concepts

Entropy

The central measure is entropy H(X), which quantifies the average uncertainty or information content of a random variable. For a discrete source, H(X) = −Σ p(x) log₂ p(x), measured in bits. A fair coin flip carries one bit of entropy; a certain outcome carries none. Related quantities include joint and conditional entropy, and mutual information, which measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another.

Source and channel coding

Shannon's source coding theorem states that a source cannot be losslessly compressed below its entropy rate, but can be compressed arbitrarily close to it; this insight grounds practical compression from Huffman coding to modern formats. His noisy-channel coding theorem states that every communication channel has a capacity C (the maximum of the mutual information between input and output): information can be transmitted at any rate below C with arbitrarily small error probability using suitable error-correcting codes, and not above it. These limit results launched coding theory and the design of codes—from Hamming and Reed–Solomon codes to turbo, LDPC, and polar codes—that approach the Shannon limit.

Applications and extensions

Information-theoretic ideas permeate modern science: lossless and lossy compression of text, audio, and video; modems, mobile networks, and deep-space communication; statistical inference and machine learning (cross-entropy loss, information bottleneck); linguistics and neuroscience; and algorithmic information theory, where Kolmogorov complexity measures the information in an individual object by the length of its shortest description. Shannon himself cautioned that the theory concerns the engineering problem of transmitting symbols, not their semantic meaning.
