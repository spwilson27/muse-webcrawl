Pittite

Pittite was a term used in early mineralogy — and retained as a field term in older geological literature — for dark, resinous-looking iron arsenates and related hydrous iron oxide–arsenate mixtures found in the oxidised zones of arsenic-bearing ore deposits. The name derives from its pitch-like appearance (from "pitch", via German Pittizit). Because "pittite" samples proved to be variable mixtures rather than a single stoichiometric phase — frequently intergrowths of scorodite, arseniosiderite, pharmacosiderite, and amorphous hydrous ferric arsenate — the term is not recognised as a valid mineral species by the International Mineralogical Association and survives only as a historical and field designation.

Occurrence

Material called pittite was reported from oxidised arsenic deposits in places such as the Black Forest (Germany), Cornwall (England), and the Cobalt district of Ontario, typically as earthy or botryoidal crusts coating arsenopyrite-bearing veins.

History of the term

Nineteenth-century mineralogists described numerous poorly characterised hydrous arsenates; systematic X-ray and chemical work in the twentieth century showed many of these to be mixtures, and the names were progressively abandoned or redefined. Pittite is treated today as a synonym/variety name only, chiefly of interest to historians of mineralogy and collectors of antique-labelled specimens.
