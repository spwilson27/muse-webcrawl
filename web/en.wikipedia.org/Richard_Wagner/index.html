Wilhelm Richard Wagner (22 May 1813 – 13 February 1883) was a German composer, theatre director, essayist, and conductor, known chiefly for his operas. Through his concept of the Gesamtkunstwerk ("total work of art") and his revolutionary use of harmony and orchestration, he transformed opera and profoundly influenced the course of Western music.

Life

Wagner was born in Leipzig. After early conducting posts in Magdeburg, Königsberg, and Riga — and mounting debts that dogged him for decades — he achieved his first success with Rienzi (1842) in Dresden, where he became court Kapellmeister and composed The Flying Dutchman, Tannhäuser, and Lohengrin. His participation in the May Uprising of 1849 forced him into exile in Switzerland; a warrant for his arrest prevented his return to Germany for over a decade. In exile he wrote his major prose works, including Art and Revolution, Opera and Drama, and the notorious antisemitic essay Judaism in Music (1850).

His fortunes turned in 1864 when the young King Ludwig II of Bavaria became his patron, settling his debts and staging Tristan und Isolde (1865) and Die Meistersinger von Nürnberg (1868). Wagner settled at Bayreuth, where, funded by Ludwig and a network of Wagner societies, he built the Festspielhaus, a theatre designed to his specifications, and there staged the complete Der Ring des Nibelungen in 1876. His final opera, Parsifal, premiered at Bayreuth in 1882. He died of a heart attack in Venice the following year.

Music and influence

Wagner's mature "music dramas" replaced the separate numbers of traditional opera with continuous music organised by leitmotifs — recurring themes associated with persons, objects, and ideas. The chromatic harmony of Tristan und Isolde, with its famously unresolved opening chord, pushed tonality toward its limits and influenced composers from Mahler, Richard Strauss, and Debussy to Schoenberg, who called himself a "conservative forced to become radical" by Wagner's example. His influence extended to literature, theatre design, and film music.

Controversy

Wagner's antisemitism, expressed in his writings and in alleged caricatures in the operas, made him a favourite composer of Nazi Germany — Adolf Hitler was a devoted admirer — and his music remains informally boycotted in Israel's public concert life. Scholars continue to debate the relationship between his prejudices, his art, and his later appropriation.
