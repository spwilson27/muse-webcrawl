Ptolemy

Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100 – c. 170 AD) was a Greco-Roman mathematician, astronomer, geographer and astrologer who lived in Alexandria, Egypt, then part of the Roman Empire. He authored several scientific treatises, three of which were of enduring importance to later Byzantine, Islamic and Western European science: the astronomical Almagest, the Geography, and the astrological Tetrabiblos. His name (Klaudios Ptolemaios) suggests Greek and Egyptian ancestry and Roman citizenship; nothing reliable is known of his life beyond the observations he dated between 127 and 141 AD.

The Almagest

The Almagest (from Arabic al-majisṭī, "the greatest") is the only surviving comprehensive treatise on ancient astronomy. It presents a mathematical model of the motions of the Sun, Moon and planets with the Earth stationary at the centre — the geocentric or "Ptolemaic" system — using combinations of deferents, epicycles, eccentrics and the equant to reproduce observed positions with remarkable accuracy. Its star catalogue of 1,022 stars, largely derived from Hipparchus, and its chord tables and spherical trigonometry made it the authoritative astronomical text for over 1,400 years, until displaced by Copernican heliocentrism in the 16th–17th centuries.

Geography and other works

The Geography compiled the coordinates of some 8,000 places in the known world with instructions for map projections; revived in Renaissance Europe, its maps framed European conceptions of the world, and its underestimate of Asia's size encouraged Columbus. The Tetrabiblos systematised astrology as a natural (if probabilistic) science and remained its standard textbook for centuries. Ptolemy also wrote on optics (measuring refraction), on music theory (Harmonics), on sundials and map projection (the Planisphaerium and Analemma), and proved the theorem on cyclic quadrilaterals that bears his name. The lunar crater Ptolemaeus and the asteroid 4001 Ptolemaeus commemorate him.
