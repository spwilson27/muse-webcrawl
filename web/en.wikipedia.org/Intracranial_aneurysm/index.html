Intracranial aneurysm

An intracranial aneurysm, also known as a cerebral aneurysm or brain aneurysm, is a weak spot on a blood vessel in the brain that balloons out and fills with blood. An intracranial aneurysm can leak or rupture, causing bleeding in the brain (a hemorrhagic stroke). Most brain aneurysms, however, do not rupture, create health problems, or cause symptoms; such aneurysms are often detected during tests for other conditions.

Classification

Saccular ("berry") aneurysms, the most common type, are rounded outpouchings typically arising at branch points of the circle of Willis, especially the anterior communicating artery. Fusiform aneurysms are spindle-shaped dilatations of an arterial segment. Mycotic aneurysms result from infection of the vessel wall.

Signs and symptoms

Small unruptured aneurysms are usually asymptomatic. Larger ones may press on nearby structures, causing eye problems or cranial nerve palsies, classically a third nerve palsy affecting the pupil. Rupture causes subarachnoid hemorrhage, typically presenting as a sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache — often described as the worst headache of one's life — with vomiting, neck stiffness, seizures, or loss of consciousness. Ruptured aneurysm carries a high risk of death and disability.

Risk factors and treatment

Risk factors include hypertension, smoking, family history, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, and connective tissue disorders. Treatment of ruptured or high-risk aneurysms involves neurosurgical clipping or endovascular coiling (or flow diversion), with the ISAT trial showing better outcomes for coiling in many cases of ruptured aneurysm. Small, low-risk unruptured aneurysms may be monitored with imaging and blood-pressure control.
