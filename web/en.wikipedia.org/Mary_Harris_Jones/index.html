Mary G. Harris Jones (c. 1837 – 30 November 1930), known as Mother Jones, was an Irish-born American labour organiser, a prominent figure in the United Mine Workers and a co-founder of the Industrial Workers of the World. Once described by a prosecutor as "the most dangerous woman in America", she became famous for mobilising miners' families and agitating against child labour.

Life

Born in Cork, Ireland, Jones emigrated as a child, first to Canada and then to the United States, working as a teacher and dressmaker. She lost her husband and four children in the 1867 Memphis yellow fever epidemic, and her possessions again in the Great Chicago Fire of 1871. Thereafter she devoted herself to the labour movement, organising strikes and speeches across the country well into her nineties.

Activism

Jones played central roles in major coal conflicts in West Virginia, Colorado and Pennsylvania, repeatedly facing arrest, military detention and injunctions. In 1903 she led the "Children's Crusade", a march of striking child textile workers from Philadelphia to President Theodore Roosevelt's home in New York to protest child labour. Her rallying cry — "Pray for the dead and fight like hell for the living" — became a motto of the American labour movement.
