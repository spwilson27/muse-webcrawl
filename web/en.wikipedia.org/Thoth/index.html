Thoth

Thoth
Egyptian name: Djehuty
Major cult centre: Hermopolis
Symbol: Ibis, baboon, moon, scribal palette
Consort: Ma'at or Seshat (in various traditions)

Thoth (from Greek Thṓth, derived from Egyptian Ḏḥwtj, Djehuty) is an ancient Egyptian deity, god of the Moon, wisdom, knowledge, writing and hieroglyphs, science, magic, and the measurement of time. He was usually depicted either as a man with the head of an ibis or entirely as an ibis, or as a baboon. Thoth served as scribe and counsellor of the gods, and was credited in Egyptian tradition with inventing writing, the calendar, and the 365-day year.

Roles and mythology

Thoth played a central role in many myths. As a mediator, he intervened in the battles between Horus and Set, healing injuries on both sides and arbitrating the succession to the throne of Egypt. In the judgment of the dead described in the Book of the Dead, Thoth recorded the result of the weighing of the deceased's heart against the feather of Ma'at. He was also said to stand by Ra in the solar barque and to defend the sun god against the serpent Apophis.

According to one myth, Thoth gambled with the moon god Khonsu and won five extra days of light, which were added to the 360-day year; during these five epagomenal days the goddess Nut was able to give birth to her children — Osiris, Isis, Set, Nephthys, and Horus the Elder — circumventing a curse laid upon her.

As god of reckoning, Thoth was associated with the moon, whose phases made it a natural marker of time, and was called "reckoner of years" and "lord of time". His main cult centre was at Hermopolis (Khmun, "Eight-Town") in Middle Egypt, where he headed a local cosmogony centred on the Ogdoad, eight primordial deities. Vast ibis catacombs at Saqqara and Tuna el-Gebel contain millions of mummified ibises offered to him.

Greek identification and Hermeticism

The Greeks identified Thoth with their god Hermes, calling him Thoth the thrice-great — hence "Hermes Trismegistus", the legendary author of the Hermetica, a body of Greco-Egyptian philosophical and occult texts that profoundly influenced Renaissance thought. In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates recounts the myth of Theuth (Thoth), inventor of writing, presenting his invention to King Thamus, who warns that writing will weaken memory — an early and famous critique of the technology of text.
