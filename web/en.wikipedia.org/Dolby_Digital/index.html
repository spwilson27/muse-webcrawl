Dolby Digital

Dolby Digital is a family of audio compression technologies developed by Dolby Laboratories, originally known by the name of its core codec, AC-3. Introduced with the film Batman Returns in 1992, it brought digital 5.1-channel surround sound to cinemas and then to home video, becoming the standard audio format for DVD and for digital television broadcasts.

Technology

AC-3 is a lossy perceptual codec supporting from mono up to 5.1 channels — left, center, right, left surround, right surround and a low-frequency effects (LFE) channel — at bit rates typically between 192 and 640 kbit/s. It uses a modified discrete cosine transform and dynamic range control metadata, and allows downmixing of multichannel material for stereo or mono playback. In the home, Dolby Digital soundtracks were carried over S/PDIF optical or coaxial connections and on 35mm film via a digital track printed between the sprocket holes.

Variants

Later members of the family include Dolby Digital Plus (E-AC-3), which supports more channels and higher streaming efficiency; Dolby AC-4, designed for next-generation broadcast and streaming; and Dolby TrueHD, a lossless format used on Blu-ray. Dolby's object-based system Dolby Atmos builds on these channel-based predecessors.
