Classical economics, also known as classical political economy, is the school of economic thought that flourished in Britain from the late eighteenth century until the marginalist revolution of the 1870s. Its central concern was economic growth: how nations accumulate wealth, and how the produce of an economy is divided among wages, profits and rents.

Origins

The school is conventionally dated from Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776), which attacked the mercantilist system and argued that national prosperity rests on the division of labour, widening markets, and the self-interested activity of individuals guided by an "invisible hand". Smith's framework was developed by David Ricardo, whose Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817) gave rigorous form to the labour theory of value and established the theory of comparative advantage in trade.

Main doctrines

Thomas Robert Malthus applied his principle of population — that population grows faster than subsistence — to argue that wages tend toward subsistence levels. Jean-Baptiste Say contributed Say's law, the proposition that supply creates its own demand, which classical writers used to deny the possibility of general gluts. John Stuart Mill's Principles of Political Economy (1848) synthesized the classical system and became its standard textbook. Karl Marx drew heavily on Ricardo's value theory while turning it into a critique of capitalism itself.

Decline and legacy

From the 1870s, the marginal utility theories of Jevons, Menger and Walras displaced the labour theory of value and re-founded economics on subjective choice, inaugurating neoclassical economics. Keynes later revived the label "classical" to characterize the doctrines he attacked in The General Theory (1936). Classical insights into growth, trade and distribution continue to shape modern economics, and the Ricardian theory of comparative advantage remains a foundation of trade theory.
