Eastern Orthodox theology

Eastern Orthodox theology is the theology particular to the Eastern Orthodox Church. It is based on Holy Tradition, which includes the dogmatic decrees of the seven Ecumenical Councils, the Scriptures, and the teaching of the Church Fathers. The Church understands itself to be the one, holy, catholic, and apostolic Church established by Jesus Christ, in unbroken continuity with the faith of the apostles.

Holy Trinity

Orthodox theology confesses one God in three hypostases — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — sharing one essence (ousia). The Father is the single source and cause of the Son (by generation) and of the Holy Spirit (by procession). The Orthodox Church rejects the Latin Filioque addition to the Nicene Creed, holding that the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, and regards the monarchy of the Father as essential to Trinitarian doctrine.

Essence and energies

Following St. Gregory Palamas and the hesychast councils of the 14th century, Orthodox theology distinguishes between God's imparticipable essence and his uncreated energies, through which God is genuinely known and participated in. This distinction undergirds the doctrine of theosis, or divinization: salvation as participation by grace in the divine life, in the words of St. Athanasius, "He was made man, that we men might be made gods."

Salvation and the Church

Orthodox soteriology emphasizes healing and deification rather than primarily juridical categories; sin is understood as sickness and death, and Christ's incarnation, death, and resurrection as the defeat of death and the restoration of human nature. The Church is the Body of Christ, and the sacraments (Holy Mysteries) — Baptism, Chrismation, the Eucharist, Confession, Holy Orders, Marriage, and Unction — convey grace within its life. Scripture is read within Holy Tradition rather than as a separate authority, and the consensus of the Fathers (consensus patrum) guides interpretation.

Eschatology and the saints

The Orthodox Church honors the Theotokos (Mary, the Mother of God) and the saints as members of the Church triumphant, venerates icons as affirmed by the Seventh Ecumenical Council, and prays for the departed. Aerial toll houses and other popular eschatological images are treated as theologoumena (theological opinions) rather than dogma. The Church rejects doctrines it regards as Latin innovations, including papal supremacy and universal jurisdiction, purgatory as a place of temporal punishment, and the Immaculate Conception as defined in 1854.
