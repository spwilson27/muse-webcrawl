Garden Tomb

Garden Tomb
LocationEast Jerusalem, just outside the Old City walls
Identified19th century (popularised 1883 by Gen. Charles Gordon)
Maintained byGarden Tomb (Jerusalem) Association

The Garden Tomb is a rock-cut tomb in Jerusalem, located just outside the Old City walls north of the Damascus Gate. Since the nineteenth century it has been proposed by some Christians, particularly Protestants, as an alternative site of the crucifixion, burial and resurrection of Jesus, in place of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.

Identification

In 1883 the British Major-General Charles George Gordon visited Jerusalem and argued that a skull-shaped rocky escarpment nearby was the Golgotha ("place of the skull") of the Gospels, and that the ancient tomb in the garden at its foot was the tomb of Jesus. The site became popularly known as "Gordon's Calvary." The adjacent tomb, excavated in 1867, is a Jewish burial chamber which most archaeologists date to the 7th–8th centuries BC (Iron Age), with reuse in later periods, rather than the first century AD.

The garden

The site is maintained as a peaceful garden by the Garden Tomb (Jerusalem) Association, a charitable trust based in the United Kingdom. Features pointed out to visitors include the skull-faced cliff, an ancient winepress and cistern, and the tomb with a stone channel in front of its entrance. The garden receives many thousands of pilgrims each year and is a popular place of worship and reflection for evangelical and other Protestant groups.

Scholarly view

Most archaeologists reject the Garden Tomb as the burial place of Jesus on dating grounds, and the traditional site within the Church of the Holy Sepulchre remains the more widely accepted candidate. The association itself presents the garden primarily as an illustrative setting for the Gospel account rather than insisting on its authenticity.
