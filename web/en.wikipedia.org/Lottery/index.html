Lottery

A lottery is a form of gambling in which participants purchase tickets for a chance to win a prize determined by a random drawing. Prizes may be cash or goods. Most modern lotteries are run by governments or licensed operators, with proceeds funding public programs, and are typically structured so that winners are chosen by drawing numbered balls or by computer-generated random numbers.

History

Lotteries date to antiquity — the Chinese Han dynasty used a lottery-like keno to help fund state projects including the Great Wall, and lotteries in Roman times distributed prizes at banquets. In early modern Europe, lotteries funded public works, churches, and wars; in colonial and early America they financed roads, canals, and colleges including Harvard and Yale. Scandals led to widespread bans in the nineteenth century before state lotteries revived in the twentieth.

Modern lotteries

Today's formats include draw games such as Powerball and Mega Millions in the United States and the multinational EuroMillions, instant scratch cards, and daily number games. Multi-jurisdiction games generate jackpots that can exceed a billion dollars. Economists note that the expected value of a ticket is generally less than its price, and debate the regressivity of lottery revenue and its role as a source of public funding.
