Vowel harmony

Vowel harmony is an assimilatory process in phonology in which vowels within a word or phrase agree with each other (are "in harmony") in terms of some phonological feature, such as height, backness, or roundedness. In a typical vowel-harmony language, all vowels in a word must belong to a single class—for example, all front vowels or all back vowels—and suffixes change their vowels to match those of the root. Vowel harmony is characteristic of many agglutinative languages, including the Turkic, Mongolic, and Uralic families.

Features involved

Backness harmony

In Turkish, vowels are divided into front vowels (e, i, ö, ü) and back vowels (a, ı, o, u). A word generally contains vowels of only one class, and suffixes have two forms: thus ev-ler "houses" (front) but at-lar "horses" (back). Hungarian and Finnish have similar palatal (front/back) harmony systems.

Rounding harmony

Some languages, including Turkish in certain suffixes, also harmonize for lip rounding: high vowels in suffixes take rounded or unrounded variants depending on the preceding vowel.

Tongue root harmony

Many languages of Africa, including Akan and numerous Niger-Congo and Nilo-Saharan languages, harmonize vowels according to advanced tongue root (ATR): vowels produced with the tongue root advanced contrast with retracted-tongue-root sets, and affixes alternate accordingly.

Harmony domains and exceptions

Harmony usually applies within a phonological word; compounds may behave as separate domains, and loanwords frequently violate harmony (Turkish kitap "book", from Arabic, mixes back and front vowels). Some vowels may be "neutral" or "transparent", allowing harmony to pass through them—Finnish i and e are well-known examples. In some languages harmony is progressive (root controls suffixes), in others regressive, and in still others a dominant vowel feature overrides the harmony of the rest of the word.

Examples of languages

  • Turkic: Turkish, Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Uzbek, Tatar
  • Uralic: Finnish, Hungarian, Estonian (partially lost)
  • Mongolic: Mongolian (tongue-root and rounding harmony)
  • Tungusic: Manchu and relatives
  • African: many Bantu and Nilotic languages (ATR harmony)
  • Other: Korean historically had vowel harmony, traces of which survive in ideophones and some suffixes.
