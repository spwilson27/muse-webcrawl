Organelle

In cell biology, an organelle is a specialized subunit, usually within a cell, that has a specific function. The name organelle comes from the idea that these structures are parts of cells, as organs are to the body. Organelles are typically enclosed by their own lipid bilayer membrane. They are characteristic of eukaryotic cells; prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles (though they have simpler structures such as ribosomes and carboxysomes).

Major organelles

The nucleus stores the cell's DNA and coordinates activities such as growth and reproduction. Mitochondria generate most of the cell's ATP through oxidative phosphorylation. The endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus synthesise, modify, and ship proteins and lipids. Lysosomes digest macromolecules and worn-out structures; peroxisomes carry out oxidation reactions. Chloroplasts, found in plants and algae, perform photosynthesis. Ribosomes—often counted among organelles though not membrane-bound—translate messenger RNA into protein. Endosymbiotic theory holds that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral cell.

History

Improvements in microscopy in the late 19th century revealed internal cell structures; Camillo Golgi described the apparatus bearing his name in 1898. The term "organelle" entered wide use in the early 20th century as cell biology coalesced, and electron microscopy from the 1940s onward revealed organelle ultrastructure in detail.
