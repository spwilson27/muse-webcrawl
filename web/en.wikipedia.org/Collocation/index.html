Collocation

In corpus linguistics, a collocation is a sequence of words or terms that co-occur more often than would be expected by chance. Collocations are partly or fully conventionalised combinations — such as strong tea, make a decision, heavy rain or fast food — in which the choice of one word strongly predicts the other, even though near-synonyms exist (powerful tea sounds unnatural to native speakers).

Characteristics

The concept was developed by J. R. Firth, who famously wrote that "you shall know a word by the company it keeps". Collocations lie on a spectrum between free combinations, whose elements can be substituted freely, and idioms, whose meaning cannot be derived from their parts. They are typically resistant to substitution and translation word-for-word, which makes them a major difficulty for language learners and a significant topic in lexicography and language teaching.

Extraction

Computational linguistics identifies collocations in large text corpora using statistical measures such as mutual information, t-scores and log-likelihood ratios applied to word co-occurrence frequencies. Collocation dictionaries, such as the Oxford Collocations Dictionary, list common partners for headwords to help writers produce natural-sounding text.
