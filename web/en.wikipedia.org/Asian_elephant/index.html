Asian elephant

Scientific nameElephas maximus
FamilyElephantidae
Height2.4–3 m (8–10 ft) at the shoulder
Weight2,000–5,500 kg
StatusEndangered (IUCN)

The Asian elephant (Elephas maximus), also known as the Asiatic elephant, is the only living species of the genus Elephas and is distributed throughout the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia, from India and Nepal in the west to Borneo in the south. It is the largest living land animal in Asia.

Description

Asian elephants are smaller than African elephants, with a convex or level back, smaller ears that do not cover the shoulder, and a trunk with a single finger-like projection (African species have two). Only males grow tusks, and some males, called makhnas, are tuskless. Females usually have only short tushes. The skin is grey, often depigmented in patches, especially in Sri Lankan animals.

Behaviour and ecology

Asian elephants are highly social and intelligent, living in matriarchal family groups of related females and their calves; adult males are solitary or form loose bachelor groups. They communicate through vocalizations, including infrasound, and through seismic signals. As herbivorous megaherbivores, they consume up to 150 kg of vegetation a day, including grasses, leaves, bark and fruit, and act as important seed dispersers.

Conservation

The species has been listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List since 1986. Wild populations are estimated at roughly 40,000–50,000 and are declining because of habitat loss and fragmentation, poaching for ivory, and human–elephant conflict. Asian elephants have been domesticated for millennia and used in forestry, warfare, ceremony and tourism; the captive population across Asia numbers in the tens of thousands.
