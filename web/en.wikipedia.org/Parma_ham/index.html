Parma ham (Prosciutto di Parma)

Parma ham (Italian: Prosciutto di Parma) is a dry-cured ham produced in the province of Parma in the Emilia-Romagna region of northern Italy. It is protected by a European Protected Designation of Origin (PDO): only ham salted and aged in the hills around Parma, from pigs of specified breeds raised in northern and central Italy, may bear the name and the ducal crown brand fire-stamped into the rind. It is eaten raw, sliced paper-thin, and prized for a delicate sweet flavour with a slightly salty finish.

Parma hams ageing in a traditional curing house in the Parma hills.

Production

Production uses only the hind leg of heavy pigs, sea salt, air, and time. The legs are trimmed, massaged with salt, and rested in refrigerated rooms, then washed, hung, and pre-aged before a coating of lard and rice flour (the sugna) is applied to soften the exterior. Maturation continues in airy ageing rooms, where the dry winds blowing from the Apennines through the region's valleys are traditionally credited with the ham's character. The minimum ageing period is 12 months, and many hams mature for 18–24 months or longer. No nitrites, nitrates, colourings, or preservatives other than salt are permitted.

Consortium and protection

The Consorzio del Prosciutto di Parma, founded in 1963, oversees production standards and branding; each ham is inspected and, if approved, branded with the five-point ducal crown of Parma. The PDO status, granted in 1996, is enforced in the European Union and recognised in many other markets, though long-running disputes over the use of the name "Parma" occur in countries where the term has been treated as generic.

Culinary use

Parma ham is served as an antipasto, classically with melon or figs, draped over grissini, or used in dishes such as saltimbocca and as a topping added after baking on pizza. Its producers recommend slicing it thin and eating it at room temperature, without cooking, to preserve its texture and aroma.
