The Apostles' Creed (Latin: Symbolum Apostolorum) is an early statement of Christian belief widely used in Western Christianity. Despite its name, it was probably not written by the apostles themselves; a later legend held that each of the Twelve contributed one of its articles. The creed developed from the baptismal confession known as the Old Roman Creed and reached essentially its present form by the early Middle Ages.

Content

The creed is Trinitarian in structure. It professes belief in God the Father, creator of heaven and earth; in Jesus Christ, his only Son — conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary, crucified under Pontius Pilate, risen on the third day, ascended into heaven, and coming again to judge the living and the dead; and in the Holy Spirit, the holy catholic church, the communion of saints, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body, and life everlasting.

Use

The creed is recited in Roman Catholic, Lutheran, Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian and many other Western liturgies, and at baptisms. Historically less used in Eastern Orthodoxy, it remains the most familiar short summary of Christian faith in the West, and a frequent ecumenical point of reference.
