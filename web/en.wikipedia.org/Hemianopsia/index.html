Hemianopsia

SpecialtyNeurology, ophthalmology
SymptomsLoss of vision in half of the visual field of one or both eyes
CausesStroke, brain tumour, trauma, lesions of the optic pathways

Hemianopsia, also spelled hemianopia, is a loss of vision or blindness in half of the visual field, usually on one side of the vertical midline. It most commonly results from damage to the visual pathways in the brain — including stroke, brain tumours, and traumatic injury — rather than from disease of the eyes themselves. The pattern of visual field loss helps localise the lesion within the optic pathway.

Types

  • Homonymous hemianopsia — loss of the same half of the visual field (left or right) in both eyes, caused by a lesion behind the optic chiasm, for example in the optic tract, optic radiation, or occipital lobe. A right-sided occipital lesion causes a left homonymous hemianopsia.
  • Heteronymous hemianopsia — loss of opposite halves of the visual field in the two eyes. Bitemporal hemianopsia, loss of the outer (temporal) halves, is the classic result of a lesion at the optic chiasm, classically a pituitary tumour compressing the crossing nasal retinal fibres. Binasal hemianopsia, loss of the inner halves, is rare and usually caused by bilateral lesions affecting the uncrossed fibres.
  • Quadrantanopia — loss of one quarter of the visual field, caused by smaller lesions of the optic radiation; an upper quadrantanopia ("pie in the sky") follows a temporal lobe (Meyer's loop) lesion, and a lower quadrantanopia follows a parietal lobe (Baumgarten loop) lesion.

Causes

Stroke affecting the posterior cerebral artery territory is the most common cause of homonymous hemianopsia in adults. Other causes include tumours, head injury, neurosurgical procedures, multiple sclerosis, and congenital malformations. In children, perinatal stroke and cortical malformations are more frequent causes.

Management

Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible. Rehabilitation includes visual scanning training to teach compensatory eye movements into the blind field, optical prisms that shift images from the blind field into the seeing field, and low-vision aids. Spontaneous recovery occurs in a proportion of cases, mainly in the first weeks to months after the injury.
