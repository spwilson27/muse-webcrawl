The dermis (from Greek δέρμα derma, "skin"), also called the corium, is a layer of skin between the epidermis (with which it makes up the cutis) and subcutaneous tissues, that primarily consists of dense irregular connective tissue and cushions the body from stress and strain. It is divided into two layers: the superficial area adjacent to the epidermis called the papillary region, and a deep, thicker area known as the reticular region. The dermis is tightly connected to the epidermis through a basement membrane. Structural components of the dermis are collagen, elastic fibres, and extrafibrillar matrix. It also contains mechanoreceptors that provide the sense of touch and pressure, heat receptors, hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands, lymphatic vessels, and blood vessels — the blood vessels providing nourishment and waste removal for both dermal and epidermal cells.

Structure

Papillary dermis

The papillary dermis is the uppermost layer, composed of loose areolar connective tissue. Its finger-like projections, the dermal papillae, interlock with the epidermis, strengthening the connection between the two layers and forming the ridges responsible for fingerprints. The papillary dermis contains fine collagen and elastic fibres, capillary loops nourishing the avascular epidermis, and Meissner's corpuscles, which sense light touch.

Reticular dermis

The reticular dermis forms the bulk of the dermis and consists of dense irregular connective tissue with thick bundles of type I collagen and elastic fibres arranged in a woven meshwork, giving the skin strength, extensibility, and elasticity. It houses the deeper structures: hair follicle roots, sweat and sebaceous glands, Pacinian corpuscles (deep-pressure receptors), nerves, and larger blood vessels. The reticular dermis also gives rise to lines of cleavage (Langer's lines), which are surgically important because incisions parallel to them heal with less scarring.

Functions

Beyond mechanical protection, the dermis participates in thermoregulation (through its rich blood supply and sweat glands), sensation, immune surveillance (via dermal dendritic cells and resident macrophages), and wound healing: fibroblasts in the dermis synthesize new collagen during repair, and deep wounds heal with scar tissue poorer in elastic fibres than original dermis. With age, the dermis loses collagen and elastin, contributing to wrinkling and sagging of skin. The dermis of animals, when tanned, forms leather.

Clinical significance

Disorders primarily affecting the dermis include scleroderma (excess collagen deposition), elastosis, some connective tissue diseases such as Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (defective collagen), and dermal tumours such as dermatofibroma and dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans. Because intradermal injections deliver antigen into this immunologically active layer, the dermis is used for tests such as the Mantoux tuberculin test and allergy testing, and for some vaccines.
