Inferno (Italian for "Hell") is the first part of Italian writer Dante Alighieri's 14th-century epic poem the Divine Comedy. It is followed by Purgatorio and Paradiso. The Inferno describes Dante's journey through Hell, guided by the ancient Roman poet Virgil, beginning on the night before Good Friday in the year 1300. Written in terza rima — interlocking three-line stanzas whose rhyme scheme Dante invented — it comprises 34 cantos, one more than the other two parts, the first canto serving as a prologue to the whole poem.

Overview

The poem opens with Dante, lost midway through life ("Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita") in a dark wood, attempting to climb a sunlit hill but driven back by three beasts — a leopard, a lion and a she-wolf. Virgil, sent by Beatrice, Dante's beloved, offers to guide him by a longer road through Hell and Purgatory to Paradise. Passing the gate inscribed "Lasciate ogne speranza, voi ch'intrate" ("Abandon all hope, ye who enter here"), the poets cross the river Acheron into Hell proper.

The nine circles

Dante's Hell is a vast funnel of nine concentric circles descending into the earth, each punishing a category of sin under the principle of contrapasso — the punishment symbolically fitting the crime:

  1. Limbo — the unbaptised and virtuous pagans (including Virgil himself, Homer and Aristotle) dwell in a deficient heaven, grieving without torment.
  2. Lust — the lustful (Paolo and Francesca, Cleopatra, Helen) are blown endlessly by storm winds.
  3. Gluttony — the gluttonous lie in icy slush, guarded by Cerberus.
  4. Greed — hoarders and spendthrifts roll weights against each other; Plutus presides.
  5. Wrath — the wrathful fight in the muddy river Styx while the sullen choke beneath it; the walls of Dis mark the start of lower Hell.
  6. Heresy — heretics, including Epicureans and the emperor Frederick II, lie in flaming tombs.
  7. Violence — against neighbours (murderers boiling in the Phlegethon, a river of blood), against self (suicides turned into trees), and against God and nature (blasphemers, sodomites and usurers on burning sand).
  8. Fraud — the ten Malebolge ("evil ditches") punish seducers, flatterers, simoniac popes, sorcerers, corrupt politicians, hypocrites, thieves, false counsellors (Ulysses), sowers of discord and falsifiers.
  9. Treachery — the frozen lake Cocytus holds traitors to kin (Caïna), country (Antenora), guests (Ptolomea) and benefactors (Judecca); at the centre, the three-faced Lucifer chews Judas Iscariot, Brutus and Cassius while beating the wings that freeze the lake.

Dante and Virgil climb down Lucifer's body, pass the centre of the earth, and emerge beneath the opposite hemisphere to see the stars again — the poem's final word, stelle ("stars"), ends each of the Comedy's three parts.

Influence

The Inferno shaped Western imagery of Hell and remains the most widely read part of the Comedy. Its verses have been illustrated by Botticelli, Gustave Doré and many others, rendered in sculpture by Rodin (whose Gates of Hell draw heavily on it), and echo through works from Chaucer and Milton to T. S. Eliot and modern popular culture.
