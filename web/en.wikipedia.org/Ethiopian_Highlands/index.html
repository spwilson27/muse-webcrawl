The Ethiopian Highlands are a rugged mass of mountains in Ethiopia in the Horn of Africa. They are sometimes called the "Roof of Africa" because of their height and large area. The highlands are bisected by the Great Rift Valley into northwestern and southeastern sections, and form the largest continuous area of their elevation on the African continent.

Geography

The highlands rise from about 1,500 m to peaks above 4,500 m; Ras Dashen, at 4,550 m, is Ethiopia's highest mountain. The Blue Nile rises in Lake Tana in the northwest highlands and carves deep gorges through the plateau. The Simien Mountains, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, are noted for dramatic escarpments.

Climate and ecology

Elevation moderates the tropical latitude: highland Ethiopia has a temperate climate with a main rainy season from June to September. The highlands are a centre of biodiversity and of crop origin — coffee (Coffea arabica) originated in the Kaffa forests, and crops such as teff and ensete are cultivated on terraced slopes. Endemic wildlife includes the gelada, Ethiopian wolf, and walia ibex.

History

The highlands were the heartland of the Aksumite Empire and of successive Ethiopian states; their defensible terrain helped preserve Ethiopian independence. Rock-hewn churches at Lalibela and the stelae of Aksum testify to a long highland civilisation.
