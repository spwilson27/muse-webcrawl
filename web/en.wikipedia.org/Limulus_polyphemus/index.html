Limulus polyphemus

Atlantic horseshoe crab
Scientific nameLimulus polyphemus
ClassMerostomata
OrderXiphosura
ConservationVulnerable (IUCN)

Limulus polyphemus, the Atlantic horseshoe crab, is a marine chelicerate arthropod found along the Atlantic coast of North America and the Gulf of Mexico, from Maine to the Yucatán Peninsula. Despite its name it is not a true crab but is more closely related to spiders and scorpions; it is one of only four living species of horseshoe crab and a classic example of a "living fossil", its lineage extending back some 450 million years.

Anatomy

The body consists of a large front shield (prosoma), a hinged rear shield (opisthosoma) with movable spines, and a long tail spine, the telson, used to right itself if overturned. Underneath are five pairs of walking legs and book gills for respiration. The animal has ten eyes, including lateral compound eyes much studied in vision research. Its blue, copper-based blood (hemocyanin) clots in the presence of bacterial endotoxin.

Ecology and human use

Each spring, horseshoe crabs spawn in huge numbers on sandy beaches, notably in Delaware Bay, where their eggs fuel migrating shorebirds such as the red knot. Crabs are harvested for bait in eel and whelk fisheries and bled for the production of Limulus amebocyte lysate, used worldwide to test medical products for endotoxin. The IUCN lists the species as Vulnerable (2016) owing to harvest pressure and habitat loss.
