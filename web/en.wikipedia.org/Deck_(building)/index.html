In architecture, a deck is a flat surface capable of supporting weight, similar to a floor, but typically constructed outdoors, often elevated from the ground, and usually connected to a building. In the United States and Canada, decks are a common feature of suburban houses, used for dining, entertaining, and recreation in place of or alongside a ground-level patio.

Construction

A typical residential deck is framed in lumber: concrete footings support posts, which carry beams; joists span the beams and are fastened to the house with a ledger board; and decking boards are laid across the joists. Guardrails are generally required by building codes when the deck surface is more than about 30 inches (76 cm) above grade, and a stairway provides access to the yard. Flashing and proper ledger attachment are critical details, since ledger failure is a leading cause of deck collapses.

Materials

Decking is commonly made from pressure-treated southern pine, naturally rot-resistant woods such as western red cedar and redwood, dense tropical hardwoods such as ipe, or manufactured materials. Wood–plastic composite decking, introduced in the 1990s, and solid PVC decking are marketed as lower-maintenance alternatives that do not require staining or sealing, though they cost more per board foot and can become hot in direct sun.

History and usage

The raised wooden deck became widespread in post-war North American suburbs, influenced by California ranch houses and Japanese-influenced modernist design, and by the availability of inexpensive pressure-treated lumber. Rooftop decks are common on urban townhouses, and pool decks surround above-ground and in-ground swimming pools. Unlike a patio, which rests on the ground, a deck can accommodate sloped sites and second-storey access.
