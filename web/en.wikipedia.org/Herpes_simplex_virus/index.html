Herpes simplex virus

TypesHSV-1 (HHV-1), HSV-2 (HHV-2)
FamilyHerpesviridae
GenomeLinear double-stranded DNA, ~150 kb
HostsHumans

The herpes simplex viruses (HSV) are two closely related species of the herpesvirus family, Herpesviridae, that infect humans: HSV-1 (human herpesvirus 1) and HSV-2 (human herpesvirus 2). They are large, enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses and the cause of herpes simplex infections, including oral and genital herpes.

Structure and genome

The virion consists of an icosahedral capsid roughly 125 nm across, surrounded by a protein tegument and a lipid envelope studded with glycoproteins used for cell entry, such as gB, gD, and gH/gL. The linear DNA genome of about 150 kilobases encodes over 70 proteins and is notable for its high guanine–cytosine content and extensive sequence repeats.

Life cycle and latency

HSV enters epithelial cells at mucosal surfaces, replicates, and spreads to sensory nerve endings. Capsids travel by retrograde axonal transport to neuronal cell bodies in the trigeminal or sacral ganglia, where the genome persists as a circular episome in a latent state, expressing mainly latency-associated transcripts. Stress and other stimuli can trigger reactivation and anterograde transport back to the skin, producing recurrent lesions.

Evolution and research

HSV-1 and HSV-2 diverged several million years ago, roughly tracking the evolution of their primate hosts. Because of their large genomes and neurotropism, engineered HSV strains are used as oncolytic viruses in cancer therapy and as vectors in neuroscience and gene delivery research.
