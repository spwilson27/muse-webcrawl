Atlantic herring (Clupea harengus)

Herring are forage fish belonging to the family Clupeidae, most famously the genus Clupea. The Atlantic herring (Clupea harengus) is one of the most abundant fish species in the world, forming enormous schools in the coastal waters of the North Atlantic and Baltic Sea.

Biology

Herring are silvery, laterally compressed fish growing to about 25–40 cm. They feed on plankton — copepods, krill and small crustaceans — filtering them through their gill rakers, and are themselves a vital food source for cod, seals, whales, seabirds and humans. Schools can contain billions of individuals and migrate over great distances between feeding and spawning grounds.

Fisheries and food

Herring have been a staple food in Europe since at least 3000 BC and were of immense historical economic importance — the Dutch herring fishery of the 17th century and the Hanseatic trade were built on them. Herring are eaten fresh and in many preserved forms: pickled herring, rollmops, kippers (smoked), Dutch maatjes, Scandinavian surströmming and herring salads. Their eggs (roe) are also valued. The North Sea herring fishery collapsed in the 1970s through overfishing, leading to a total ban from 1977 to 1981; stocks later recovered under quota management.

In language

The expression "red herring" — something that diverts attention from the real issue — is commonly said to derive from the practice of using strong-smelling cured herring to throw hunting dogs off a scent.
