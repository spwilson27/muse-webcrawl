The Irish Rebellion of 1798 was a major uprising against British rule in Ireland, inspired by the American and French revolutions and organised by the Society of United Irishmen, a republican movement founded in Belfast in 1791 that sought Catholic emancipation, parliamentary reform and ultimately an independent Irish republic uniting "Catholic, Protestant and Dissenter".

Course of the rebellion

Government repression and martial law in 1797–98, including the arrest of most of the United Irish leadership, forced a premature rising on 23 May 1798. Fighting flared in Leinster — above all in County Wexford, where rebels won early victories before their defeat at Vinegar Hill on 21 June — and in Ulster, where Henry Joy McCracken and Henry Munro led risings at Antrim and Down. A small French expedition under General Humbert landed at Killala in August and won at Castlebar ("the Races of Castlebar") but surrendered at Ballinamuck in September. Wolfe Tone, who had lobbied France for support, was captured with a second French force off Lough Swilly and died in custody in November.

Consequences

Estimates of the dead range from 10,000 to 30,000, many in atrocities committed by both sides. The rebellion was followed by savage reprisals and, convinced that the Irish Parliament could not be trusted with Ireland's government, the British administration of William Pitt the Younger carried the Acts of Union 1800, abolishing the Dublin parliament and creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland from 1801. The rebellion became a foundational event in Irish republican memory, commemorated in ballads such as "The Wind That Shakes the Barley" and in the bicentenary commemorations of 1998.
