James Van Allen

James Alfred Van Allen (September 7, 1914 – August 9, 2006) was an American space physicist at the University of Iowa. He is best known for the discovery of the Van Allen radiation belts, regions of energetic charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field, using instruments aboard the Explorer 1 satellite in 1958.

Born: September 7, 1914, Mount Pleasant, Iowa, US

Died: August 9, 2006, Iowa City, Iowa, US

Known for: Van Allen radiation belts

Fields: Space physics

Career

Van Allen earned his doctorate at the University of Iowa in 1939. During the Second World War he worked on proximity fuzes at the Applied Physics Laboratory of Johns Hopkins University. After the war he led high-altitude research using captured V-2 rockets and later developed the "rockoon" — a rocket launched from a balloon — for inexpensive cosmic-ray studies.

Radiation belts

Van Allen designed the Geiger–Müller tube instrument carried by Explorer 1, the first successful American satellite, launched on January 31, 1958. Its unexpectedly fluctuating count rates, interpreted together with data from Explorer 3, revealed belts of trapped radiation encircling Earth. The discovery was one of the signature findings of the International Geophysical Year and helped launch the discipline of space physics. Van Allen went on to design instruments for dozens of missions, including Pioneer 10 and Pioneer 11, and remained a vigorous advocate of small, dedicated planetary probes.

Honours

He received the National Medal of Science in 1987 and the Crafoord Prize in 1989. NASA's Van Allen Probes mission (2012–2019) was named in his honour.
