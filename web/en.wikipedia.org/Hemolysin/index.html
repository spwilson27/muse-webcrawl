Hemolysin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hemolysin
TypeExotoxin / cytolysin
TargetRed blood cell membranes
EffectHemolysis (cell lysis)
Produced byVarious bacteria, fungi, and other organisms

Hemolysins are substances, usually exotoxins produced by bacteria, that cause the lysis of red blood cells (erythrocytes) in vitro and in vivo. The destruction of red blood cells releases hemoglobin into the surrounding fluid. Many bacteria that produce hemolysins are pathogens, and hemolysis can contribute to disease by destroying host cells and liberating iron-containing compounds that bacteria can use for growth.

Classification on blood agar

Microbiologists classify the hemolytic activity of streptococci and other bacteria by their appearance on blood agar plates:

  • Alpha-hemolysis (α): partial hemolysis, producing a greenish discoloration of the agar around colonies, caused by oxidation of hemoglobin to methemoglobin. Seen in Streptococcus pneumoniae and viridans streptococci.
  • Beta-hemolysis (β): complete lysis of red blood cells, producing a clear, transparent zone around colonies. Caused by hemolysins such as streptolysin O and streptolysin S in Streptococcus pyogenes.
  • Gamma-hemolysis (γ): no hemolysis; the agar is unchanged.

Mechanisms

Many hemolysins are pore-forming toxins. They bind to the target cell membrane, oligomerise, and insert a transmembrane pore that allows uncontrolled passage of water and ions, swelling and bursting the cell. Examples include alpha-hemolysin of Staphylococcus aureus and the cholesterol-dependent cytolysins such as streptolysin O and pneumolysin. Other hemolysins act as phospholipases or detergents that dissolve membrane lipids; the alpha-toxin of Clostridium perfringens is a phospholipase C.

Role in disease

Hemolysins are important virulence factors. By lysing red blood cells and other host cells (including leukocytes), they help bacteria evade the immune system, obtain nutrients such as iron, and spread through tissues. The hemolysin of uropathogenic Escherichia coli (HlyA) contributes to urinary tract and kidney damage. Some hemolysins also damage the cells lining blood vessels and organs in severe infections.

Other organisms

Hemolytic substances are not limited to bacteria. Some fungi, plants (saponins), and animal venoms contain hemolytic components. Certain sea anemone and jellyfish toxins lyse red blood cells, and the immune system's own complement membrane attack complex performs a related pore-forming function.
