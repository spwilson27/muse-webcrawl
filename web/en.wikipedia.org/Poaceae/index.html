Poaceae (/poʊˈeɪsiːiː/), also called Gramineae, is a large and nearly ubiquitous family of monocotyledonous flowering plants commonly known as grasses. It includes the cereal grasses, bamboos, and the grasses of natural grassland and species cultivated in lawns and pasture. With around 780 genera and around 12,000 species, the Poaceae is the fifth-largest plant family.

Importance

Grasses are of major economic importance. The cereals — wheat, rice, maize, barley, oats, rye, sorghum and millets — provide the majority of human caloric intake directly and as livestock feed. Grasslands such as savannas and prairies cover about 40 percent of Earth's land area, and grasses dominate many ecosystems. Sugarcane is the world's largest crop by production quantity.

Structure

Grasses typically have hollow stems called culms (except at the nodes), and narrow alternate leaves with parallel veins borne in two ranks. The leaf consists of a sheath clasping the stem and a blade, with a membranous ligule at their junction. The flowers are typically wind-pollinated and arranged in spikelets, each protected by bracts called glumes, lemmas and paleas. The fruit is a caryopsis, in which the seed coat is fused with the fruit wall.

Growth and photosynthesis

Growth in grasses occurs at intercalary meristems near the base of the leaf and stem segments, which allows them to recover quickly from grazing and mowing. Many grasses in warm climates use the C4 photosynthetic pathway, which is more efficient at high temperatures; C4 grasses include maize, sugarcane and sorghum.

Evolution and distribution

Phytolith evidence suggests grasses were present by the Late Cretaceous, possibly eaten by dinosaurs, but grasslands expanded dramatically during the Miocene as climates became cooler and drier. Grasses occur on every continent including Antarctica, where the Antarctic hair grass is one of only two native flowering plants.
