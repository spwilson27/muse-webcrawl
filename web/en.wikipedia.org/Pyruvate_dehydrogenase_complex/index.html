Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex

The pyruvate dehydrogenase complex (PDC or PDH complex) is a large multi-enzyme complex located in the mitochondrial matrix of eukaryotes and in the cytosol of many bacteria. It catalyses the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and carbon dioxide, producing NADH in the process. This reaction is the irreversible link between glycolysis and the citric acid cycle, committing glucose carbons to oxidation or, via acetyl-CoA, to fatty acid synthesis.

Composition and mechanism

The mammalian complex is one of the largest known enzyme assemblies, roughly 50 nanometres across and composed of multiple copies of three catalytic enzymes: pyruvate dehydrogenase (E1), dihydrolipoyl transacetylase (E2), and dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase (E3), together with the E3-binding protein and regulatory kinase and phosphatase enzymes. The reaction proceeds through five coenzymes — thiamine pyrophosphate, lipoamide, coenzyme A, FAD, and NAD+. E1 decarboxylates pyruvate and transfers the remaining two-carbon unit to the swinging lipoamide arm of E2, which passes the acetyl group to coenzyme A; E3 then reoxidizes the lipoamide, reducing NAD+ to NADH. Substrate channelling by the mobile lipoamide domains greatly increases the complex's efficiency.

Regulation

PDC activity is controlled by reversible phosphorylation: pyruvate dehydrogenase kinases phosphorylate and inactivate E1 in response to high levels of ATP, NADH, and acetyl-CoA, while pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatases reactivate it in response to calcium ions and insulin. Pyruvate itself inhibits the kinase, so abundant substrate keeps the complex switched on. This regulation coordinates the complex with the energy state of the cell and with the choice between glucose and fatty acid oxidation.

Clinical significance

Congenital deficiency of the complex, most often from mutations in the X-linked PDHA1 gene encoding E1α, causes lactic acidosis and severe neurological impairment in infancy; management includes ketogenic diets that bypass the complex by supplying acetyl-CoA from fat. Thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency also impairs the complex because E1 requires thiamine pyrophosphate, contributing to the pathology of beriberi and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. The complex is additionally a target of autoimmunity in primary biliary cholangitis, in which antimitochondrial antibodies are directed mainly against its E2 subunit.
