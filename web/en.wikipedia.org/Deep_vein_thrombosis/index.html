Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a type of venous thrombosis involving the formation of a blood clot in a deep vein, most commonly in the legs or pelvis. A minority of DVTs occur in the arms. Symptoms may include pain, swelling, redness, and enlarged veins in the affected area, but some DVTs have no symptoms.

Causes and risk factors

The mechanisms behind DVT are summarised by Virchow's triad: reduced blood flow (stasis), injury to the vein wall, and an increased tendency of the blood to clot (hypercoagulability). Risk factors include recent surgery or hospitalisation, prolonged immobility such as long-haul travel or bed rest, active cancer, pregnancy and the postpartum period, oestrogen-containing contraceptives and hormone therapy, obesity, smoking, and inherited conditions such as factor V Leiden. Long-distance air travel is colloquially associated with DVT through the term "economy class syndrome."

Complications

The most serious complication is pulmonary embolism, in which part of the clot breaks off and travels to the lungs; it can be fatal. A common long-term complication is post-thrombotic syndrome, involving chronic leg pain, swelling, and skin changes caused by damage to the vein's valves. Together, DVT and pulmonary embolism constitute venous thromboembolism (VTE).

Diagnosis

Clinicians estimate probability with scoring systems such as the Wells score. A D-dimer blood test can help exclude DVT in low-probability patients, while compression ultrasonography is the standard imaging test for confirming the diagnosis.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment centres on anticoagulation — initially with heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin, followed by warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant such as rivaroxaban or apixaban — to prevent clot extension and embolism while the body's fibrinolytic system dissolves the clot. Selected severe cases may receive thrombolysis or thrombectomy. Prevention in hospital patients includes early mobilisation, graduated compression stockings, and prophylactic anticoagulants.
