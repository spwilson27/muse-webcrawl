Jarls

[Image: Norse chieftains — jarls of the Viking Age]

A jarl (plural jarls; Old Norse jarl) was a rank of Scandinavian nobility in the early Middle Ages, roughly equivalent to an earl. The word is cognate with the English "earl" and ultimately derives from Proto-Germanic *erlaz.

Role and status

In Norse society, jarls stood between kings and the free landowning class of karls, and above thralls. A jarl typically governed a district or served as a king's lieutenant, commanding fleets and armies, presiding at assemblies (things), and collecting tribute. Some jarls were effectively independent rulers — most famously the Jarls of Orkney and the Jarls of Lade in Norway, who at times rivaled kings in power.

Mythology and later use

The Eddic poem Rígsþula presents Jarl as the mythic ancestor of the warrior aristocracy, born of the god Ríg (Heimdall) and a noble mother. As Scandinavian kingdoms centralized in the high Middle Ages, the title jarl became singular and honorific — often held by the king's chief minister or a royal relative, as in Sweden, where figures such as Birger Jarl (d. 1266) wielded power as regent. The title gradually fell out of use by the late medieval period.
