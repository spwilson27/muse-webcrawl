In Greek mythology, Cerberus (Greek: Κέρβερος, Kérberos), often called the "hound of Hades," is a monstrous multi-headed dog that guards the gates of the Underworld to prevent the dead from leaving. He is traditionally depicted with three heads, a serpent's tail, and snakes protruding from his body. Cerberus is the offspring of the monsters Echidna and Typhon, and the brother of other famous beasts including Orthrus, the Hydra, the Chimera, and the Nemean lion.

Mythology

The twelfth labour of Heracles

The capture of Cerberus was the twelfth and final of the Labours of Heracles (Hercules). Ordered by King Eurystheus to fetch the hound from the underworld, Heracles descended to Hades, wrestled Cerberus into submission with his bare hands — Hades permitted the attempt on condition no weapons were used — and carried him up to the surface, displaying him to the terrified Eurystheus before returning him to his post.

Other tales

Other heroes encountered Cerberus: Orpheus lulled him with music on his descent to retrieve Eurydice; Aeneas, in Virgil's Aeneid, passed him after the Sibyl threw him a honey-cake (in some tellings drugged). Hermes and Psyche also feature in encounters with the hound. In Dante's Inferno, Cerberus torments the gluttonous in the third circle of Hell.

Depictions and legacy

Hesiod described Cerberus as having fifty heads, while later sources standardized three. The constellation Cerberus was an obsolete star pattern once drawn near Hercules. "Cerberus" remains a byword for a fearsome watchdog — applied to guards, security systems, and multi-layered authentication (e.g., the Kerberos network protocol is named after the mythological figure, guarding the gates of a network).
