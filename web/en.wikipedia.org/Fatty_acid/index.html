Fatty acid

StructureCarboxylic acid with a long aliphatic chain
Chain lengthTypically 4–28 carbon atoms
ClassesSaturated, monounsaturated, polyunsaturated
ExamplesPalmitic acid, oleic acid, linoleic acid

In chemistry, particularly in biochemistry, a fatty acid is a carboxylic acid with an aliphatic chain, which is either saturated or unsaturated. Most naturally occurring fatty acids have an unbranched chain of an even number of carbon atoms, from 4 to 28. Fatty acids are a major component of lipids (fats) in plants, animals and microorganisms, and are usually not found free but esterified in triglycerides, phospholipids and cholesterol esters.

Classification

Saturated fatty acids (such as palmitic and stearic acid) have no carbon–carbon double bonds and pack tightly, tending to be solid at room temperature. Unsaturated fatty acids have one (monounsaturated, e.g. oleic acid) or more (polyunsaturated, e.g. linoleic and alpha-linolenic acid) double bonds, usually in the cis configuration, whose kinks loosen packing and lower melting points. Industrially produced trans fatty acids arise from partial hydrogenation of vegetable oils and are associated with increased cardiovascular risk.

Biological roles

Fatty acids are an energy-dense fuel (about 9 kcal per gram), oxidised by beta-oxidation to acetyl-CoA. Certain polyunsaturated fatty acids are essential nutrients because animals cannot synthesise them: linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3) give rise to eicosanoids and to the long-chain omega-3s EPA and DHA. Fatty-acid chains also determine the fluidity of cell membranes via phospholipids.

Production and uses

Industrially, fatty acids are obtained by hydrolysis of fats and oils and used in soaps, detergents, cosmetics, lubricants and biodiesel. Inside cells, fatty acid synthesis is carried out by the fatty acid synthase complex, building palmitate from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA.
