South Sea Company

The South Sea Company
Founded: 1711
Founders: Robert Harley; John Blunt
Purpose: trade with Spanish America; management of the national debt
Famous for: the South Sea Bubble of 1720

The South Sea Company (officially the Governor and Company of the Merchants of Great Britain, trading to the South Seas and other parts of America) was a British joint-stock company founded in 1711, created by Robert Harley as a Tory rival to the Whig-dominated Bank of England and East India Company. It was granted a monopoly on trade with Spain's colonies in the "South Seas" (South America), including the asiento contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America, obtained at the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713.

The Bubble

In 1720 the company proposed to take over most of Britain's national debt, converting government annuities into company stock. Aggressive promotion, insider dealing, bribery of ministers and MPs, and margin lending sent the share price from about £128 in January 1720 to over £1,000 in August — the South Sea Bubble. When confidence broke in September, the price collapsed to near £100, ruining thousands of investors, including Isaac Newton, who reportedly lost some £20,000 and remarked that he could "calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people."

Aftermath

A parliamentary inquiry exposed corruption; Chancellor of the Exchequer John Aislabie was expelled and imprisoned, and Robert Walpole's skilful crisis management — restructuring the debt between the Bank of England and the East India Company — began his two-decade ascendancy. The Bubble Act of 1720, passed to curb rival promotions, restricted joint-stock company formation for a century. The company itself continued whaling and debt management, finally winding up its trading activities and surviving as a financial shell into the 19th century.
